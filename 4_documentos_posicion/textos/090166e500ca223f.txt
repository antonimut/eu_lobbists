CMDI – Review of the Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive (BRRD - 2023/0112) and of the 
Single Resolution Mechanism Regulation (SRMR – 2023/0111) 
 
On April 1 8th, the E uropean Commission presented four new legislative proposals with an aim to 
reinforce the European Union’s crisis management and deposit insurance (CMDI) framework.  
Over the last decade, significant improvements have been achieved as banks have built up Minimum 
Requirements for Own funds and Eligible Liabilities (MREL) and Total Loss Absorption Capacity (TLAC) 
buffers. The Single Resolution Fund (SRF), a sector -wide safety net has reached its final milestone by 
finalising its build-up.   
The CMDI package includes further steps in the good direction, most notably through the extension of 
PIA assessments and their harmonisation, as well as the harmonisation of the least cost test (LCT). 
However, we have several concerns regarding the direction chosen by the European Commission.  
• MREL should remain the first line of defence.  All institutions earmarked for resolution need 
to have sufficient levels of loss -absorption MREL buffers . The package should not build 
disincentives to comply with this norm, as we believe it is the case with the so -called “DGS 
bridge”. 
• The proposals may be strengthened concerning early intervention by authorities.  Earlier 
intervention mitigates the risk of deterioration of the net asset value of the distressed bank up 
to the point where third-party funds are needed to finance the resolution. 
• The modifications to the creditor hierarchy (depositor preference  and single tier  for all 
deposits) are unwarranted in our vi ew and in light of the consequences it would bring, from 
increased pressure on DGSs to impact in terms of ratings for senior unsecured debt.

REVIEW OF THE BRRD and SRMR – KEY ISSUES 
• Public Interest Assessment (PIA)  
• Extension of resolution  
We welcome the changes introduced by the European Commission to the Public Interest Assessment 
(PIA) as today the PIA is implemented very differently across the EU and as the extension of the scope 
of resolution , i.e. a harmoni sed framework to swiftly handle bank failures,  would further enhance 
financial stability. 
Taking into account the impact at regional level in the definition of critical functions (Art. 2(1)(35) 
BRRD), adjusting the resolution objectives , (Art. 31(2) BRRD / Art. 14(2) SRMR) or even procedural 
changes to the comparison between resolution and insolvency (Art. 32(5) BRRD / Art. 18(5) SRMR) 
are positive steps in that regard.   
However, we encourage the co-legislators to consider further measures to strengthen accountability 
and transparency from resolution authorities . Such measures would prevent national resolution 
authorities from assigning a negative PIA that is not necessarily backed by sufficient criteria. The aim 
is to ensure consistent application of the new rules. In particular, in terms of control mechanism, the 
resolution authorities could be asked to disclose the main features of their assessment to the public in 
the case of a negative PIA. This “high-level summary” following a negative PIA decision would promote 
a better understanding for market participants and ensure a consistent approach to resolution in the 
EU. 
• Extension of resolution / public support measures 
While the extension of PIA is a positive step, we note with concern the mention “in particular when 
provided from the budget of a Member-State” in Art. 31(2) point c BRRD. This does not seem relevant 
as state aid may come from resources other than the public budget as long as the support measures 
are imputable to the State and the “private resources” that underpin the measures come under public 
control. The Commission’s proposal gives off the wrong idea that State aid rules depend on the origin 
of the funds.  
• Bail-inable liabilities – status of provisions and contingent liabilities  
We draw attention to Art. 2(1)(71) BRRD which mentions that “bail-inable liabilities mean the liabilities 
including those giving rise to accounting provisions ” as well Art. 36 and 46 which would include an 
explicit reference to contingent liabilities.   
This approach does not seem justified as provisions and contingent liabilities are not bail-inables in 
our view and should not be part of resolution planning. 
Provisions fulfil strict conditions under IAS37. These conditions would continue to be met even after a 
resolution authority would decide to bail-in the provisions. Unless the current liability resulting from a 
past event that led to booking the provision is fully discharged, the bank would have to book a provision 
again and thus the bail -in would be meaningless.  We note that the Commission foresees such a 
discharge, but we think this w ould lead to complex recognition issue s with regards to third -country 
jurisdictions. 
Contingent liabilities are possible obligations whose existence will be confirmed by uncertain future 
events. They also include obligations that are not recognised because  their amount cannot be 
measured reliably or because settlement is not probable.

Contingent liabilities are not quantifiable and are settled in a judicial context without any control on 
the outcome. Therefore, it would not be fit for purpose to require a valuation of these liabilities, which 
is bound to lead to very conservative numbers by valuers.  
• Early intervention measures (EIMs)  
The Group welcomes the Commission’s proposals when it comes to early intervention measures  
(EIMs), in Art. 27 and 29 BRRD. More legal certainty, less overlaps between EIMS and CRD supervisory 
powers (triggers and available measures) mean more applicability and a more operational framework 
for the sector.  
EIMs are beneficial as they enhance the tools available for competent authorities to deal with a crisis 
early-on which is more efficient. It completes the well -established supervisory powers in the Capital 
Requirements Directive (CRD) and the Single Supervisory Mechanism Regulation (SRMR). 
It should be ensured that authorities intervene early enough to avoid further downstream mobilisation 
of third -party funds (DGS/SRF). Efficient communication between resolution authorities and 
competent authorities is essential to trigger such measures in a timely manner. In addition, including 
some well-calibrated quantitative triggers could ensure the EIM s are deployed early  enough in the 
process of failure.  
• Early warning of failing or likely to fail (FOLF) 
Measures on the preparation for the resolution (Art. 30a BRRD and Art. 13c SRMR) are a positive move 
forward as it encourages strengthening cooperation between competent authorities (CA) and 
resolution authorities. The Group is of the opinion that authorities should intervene early, in a timely 
and efficient manner to prevent more severe situations downstream  and reducing the amount of 
losses that would need to be absorbed.  
However, we do not fully understand the reason behind the exemption of DGS preventive measures 
from the qualification of ‘extraordinary public support’ and we would support deleting this measure. 
The proposal of the Commission would create an important breach in the principle that 
extraordinary public support  triggers FOLF.  Similarly, exempting DGS alternative measures from 
such a qualification when the ban k is wound up under insolvency proceedings would create an 
imbalance in the revised PIA, especially when resolution authorities have to compare  the use of 
extraordinary public financial support in the resolution and liquidation scenarios. 
• Use of DGS in resolution  
We refute the argument behind the increased use of DGS in resolution (“DGS bridge”) in Art. 109 BRRD 
/ Art. 79 SRMR as it would lead to several negative consequences. 
First, MREL must firmly remain the first line of defence to prevent moral hazard . The “DGS bridge” 
would enter in contradiction with the bail-in principle and would disincentivise raising MREL for small 
and mid -size banks.  Data and practical cases have proven that small and mid -size banks are in a 
capacity to raise MREL. For instance, in Denmark or Finland, small and mid-size banks have been able 
to raise this necessary capital, as confirmed by an EBA report1. The 8% TLOF requirement for all should 
be preserved and should become a Pillar-1 subordination requirement for all banks with a positive PIA, 
whether they are G-SIIs, top-tier or small and mid-sized banks

Second, in practice, the DGS bridge would create a two-tier rule for access to resolution funds. We 
call to keep the same stringent rules for every bank.  We also note that the Commission proposes to 
drop the 50% cap currently present in Art. 109(5) BRRD for DGS interventions, which we believe is 
essential to limit the risk of depletion faced by DGS.  
Third, we call for realistic and sound market exit strategies to avoid maintaining zombie banks on 
the market. It should not be the role of a DGS to save a non-viable bank from failure, moreover using 
money mainly raised on its competitors and thereby aggravating competitive distortions. Non -viable 
banks should terminate their activities as soon as possible (possibly through resolution and the 
consumption of MREL resources preferably) for the market to welcome o ther viable competitors. 
Effective reduction in production capacity is necessary.  
Fourth, while the Commission argues that it is less costly to use the DGS in preventive /alternative 
measures than in pay-out because the least-cost-test would be met, mobilising the DGS would increase 
pressure on healthy banks with costs finally passed onto the clients. Moreover, the least -cost test 
would be met “artificially” only because the Commission would have changed the frame of reference, 
i.e. the applicable creditor hierarchy. 
• Depositor preference 
The European Commission has proposed, in Art. 108(1) of the BRRD proposal, to modify the creditor 
hierarchy to introduce a single-tier ranking above senior unsecured liabilities that would make covered 
and uncovered deposits pari-passu while non-covered non-preferred depositors would be one rank 
above senior preferred debt. 
We express strong concern regarding this preference given to non -covered and non-preferred 
deposits. This change is not justified , in our view , and would have powerful and unintended 
consequences.  
Among those consequences:  
- The single tier depositor rank would artificially increase the reliance on DGS and reduce the 
contribution on shareholders and creditors that must bear the bulk of the burden in line of the 
principle that bank’s internal resources must remain the first line of defence; 
- Consequences for financial stability  as it may affect the managing o f liquidity. Corporate 
deposits, which are more volatile may replace short and mid-term issuance; 
- It would create moral hazard for depositors. Large corporates with uncovered deposits could 
be under the impression that the level of risk has decreased;  
- Downgrading of s enior debt rating.  Banks’ ratings may be affected and the cost for issuing 
bail-in able instruments may rise; 
- For banks that would still be liquidated in the new framework, costs for replenishing the DGS 
would increase (as recovery rate would be lower due to the loss of the super preference) , 
thereby increasing pressure on healthy banks with a risk that costs would be passed onto the 
client.  
Also, the existing super-preference given to DGS/covered deposits mitigates the cost of DGS 
intervention in the case of liquidation banks for the whole community.  Considering the stakes here, 
we call to maintain the status quo on the creditor hierarchy.

• Irrevocable payment Commitments (IPCs) 
The Commission proposes in  Art. 103(1) to introduce a cash call even when a bank loses its banking 
license before it can retrieve the collateral backing an IPC. This means that whatever the circumstances 
(going-concern or gone-concern), a bank would always lose such a collateral in economic terms.  
We think this proposal would have negative consequences since it would nullify the purpose of IPC in 
countries where the SRF contributions can be paid under such a form. The change here would force 
banks to recognize the stock of IPC as an operating charge.  
• Other measures: 
 
o SRF target level: Article 103 paragraph 3 BRRD which provides more flexibility for SRF 
contributions which would take place when needed and not necessarily every year is a 
positive step. 
o Governance of DGS intervention in resolution: We note that the Commission does not 
spell out clearly how the DGS would interact with the resolution authority when the 
resolution scheme foresees the use of DGS money. We may wonder to what extent a DGS 
should be involved in resolution planning. In our view, such an involvement and the 
incorporation of DGS use in the preferred resolution strategy must lead to sufficiently high 
subordinated MREL requirement for small and mid-size banks on a going-concern basis.

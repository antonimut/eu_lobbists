Introduction  
 
 
The European Commission (the Commission) published a legislative proposal to regulate the activities of 
environmental, social and governance (ESG) ratings providers (the Proposal).  
LSEG is a provider of ESG scor es, which are described as a specific subset of ESG ratings by the Proposal, 
via its subsidiaries Refinitiv and FTSE Russell. Several family types of LSEG’s ESG scores fall in scope of the 
Proposal, given many of LSEG’s ESG scores products provide a measure on either a combined approach to 
‘ESG’ or one aspect of the ‘E, S or G’ factors.  
LSEG’s ESG scores are a quantitative assessment, based on pre-defined rules and formula on how ESG data 
should be aggregated and weighted. They do not involve any analyst discretion and therefore offer a high 
degree of transparency. The traceability of input data and the possibility to deconstruct the scores’ weighting 
model makes LSEG’s ESG scores an open analytical tool. LSEG scores are marketed to investors and not to 
issuers/rated companies. 
This note summarises LSEG’s initial views on the Proposal.  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Introductory Remarks 
 
Good quality ESG assessments are vital to support the transition to net -zero. LSEG is overall supportive of 
the Proposal which will allow for greater transparency in the market, without prescribing ESG assessment 
methodologies. This approach will contribute to a more effective allocation of capital to sustainable activities. 
This paper makes recommendations to ensure that the future regulatory framework achieves it stated objective 
to improve transparency and integrity of the sector without impeding the development of a nas cent market:  
- Legal certainty: the proposed regulation should be clear, precise and unambiguous, and its legal 
implications be foreseeable. It should therefore set clear regulatory boundaries to avoid applying 
requirements to a wide-range of activities when not intended or justified. In light of this, the proposed 
definitions of ESG opinions and scores should be refined, as well as the activities that the regulation 
is seeking to regulate (e.g., issuance, distribution, offering or determination of methodolo gies of ESG 
ratings). We make some recommendations in relation to that in the body of the paper.  
- Independence and diversity of ESG assessments:  the proposed regulation should recognise the 
diversity of opinions and methods in assessing ESG risks or perfor mance, the need for EU users to 
access independent information and the different business models, serving different use cases. For 
instance, LSEG provides ESG scores which are based on pre -defined rules and formulas. This 
quantitatively-driven model provid es a high degree of transparency to the market and should be 
preserved by the proposed regulation.  
- Proportionality: the proposed regulation should avoid creating unnecessary costs for end clients and 
therefore be proportionate to the scale and potential r isks related to ESG ratings. The principles 
proposed by the International Organization of Securities Commissions’ recommendations for ESG 
ratings and data products providers (‘IOSCO recommendations’) 1, which are currently being codified 
in several countri es, provide for the right balance between key governance and transparency 
principles and ensuring enough flexibility for the sector to continue to innovate and grow.  We make 
some recommendations in the paper below to ensure the proposed regulation strikes  a similar 
balance, notably as regards the approach to separation of activities, ESG ratings pricing principles 
and disclosures.

Detailed elements  
 
A- Underpinning principles to regulate ESG ratings 
I. Support diversity of ESG ratings 
ESG ratings as defined in the Proposal cover a broad, highly heterogenous and rapidly innovating diversity of 
ESG assessments products.  
The diversity of ESG ratings and scores reflects the fact that there can be no single “truth” as to how to assess 
E, S or G performance, risks or opportunities, or how to combine them.  Different stakeholders and different 
investors will have different views on the relative importance of different issues and the best way to assess 
them. The market needs creativity, competition and challenge, together with transparency, to meet the diverse 
and evolving needs from the investment and finance sector. 
In addition, time horizon will affect the ratings/scores: some rating users will have a short to medium term time 
horizon, when others will invest on a longer-term basis. Users’ and providers’ approach to materiality will also 
differ. At LSEG for example we consider materiality to be dynamic:  all sustainability issues that have significant 
impact on society or the environment are l ikely at some point in the future to have a financial impact. There 
will be variability in likelihood, time frames and significance but views on these will differ and cannot be precise. 
They may not have direct financial implications for a particular compa ny in the short term (1 -3 years) but are 
more likely to impact over longer time scales. Different ESG scores may therefore reflect different time horizons 
or view the strength of likelihood of these impacts differently, so as to serve varying needs of diff erent users.  
The diversity of ESG assessments is an intrinsic feature of the ESG ratings ecosystem. It should be 
supported and promoted by the EU regulatory regime, to ensure ESG ratings providers can continue 
serving the various needs of their clients. 
II. Proportionate regulation is needed to support a small and innovative sector  
Despite the challenges around reliable and comparable reported ESG data, ESG ratings provide an important 
source of independent information and initial analysis for investors or any  other stakeholders. The size and 
scale of the market of ESG ratings is rather small, especially when compared to similar sectors. 3 ESG ratings 
have been recognised as “ not as systemically important as credit ratings ” in the Commission’s impact 
assessment4 and indeed, are not directly tied to financial products’ valuation like the credit ratings are.  
The new EU regulatory framework should reflect this fact and be proportionate to the size, scale and 
interconnectedness (or lack of) of ESG ratings. In our view, IOSCO recommendations set the right level of 
requirements. Where the Proposal is being more restrictive than IOSCO’s recommendations, it should be 
clearly justified.  
The proposed regulation should not seek to impose a burden on providers that is excessive in 
proportion to the potential risk posed by those providers and their products, as per the proportionality 
principle.  
III. Global interoperability with other regional frameworks to limit compliance costs on end products  
As a company operating global ly, LSEG has already endorsed the Japan FSA Code of Conduct for ESG 
Evaluation and Data Providers, 5 and is preparing for compliance with the SEBI regulation for ESG ratings

conflicting to those in other markets. The m arket needs global standards and approaches rather than 
fragmented or overlapping regulatory initiatives.  
B- LSEG comments on the EU ESG ratings regulation proposal 
This section provides LSEG’s comments on the Proposal’s provisions, with the view of contributing to building 
a sound and effective regulatory framework for ESG ratings providers. 
I. In support of independence of assessments  
IOSCO’s Recommendation 3 highlights the importance of providing ratings “free from political or economic 
interferences”. We st rongly support this principle: it protects the diversity of approaches to assessing ESG 
risks and opportunities and ensures that no specific political opinion can be imposed onto rating or scoring 
providers. The Commission has rightfully referred to this principle in two articles: 
• Article 14(1): “ESG rating providers shall ensure the independence of their rating activities, including from 
all political and economic influences or constraints”; and  
• Article 26: “ In carrying out their duties under this Regulation, ESMA, the Commission or any public 
authorities of a Member State shall not interfere with the content of ESG ratings or methodologies.”   
The approach taken by the Commission to ensure that ESG ratings me thodologies remain fundamentally 
independent protects the integrity of the market. In doing so, the Commission supports ESG ratings providers 
in performing their own research, including by partnering with academic and scientific experts to constantly 
grow the knowledge base, refine E, S or G assessments and challenge pre-conceptions. 
Keeping those provisions in the Proposal and abiding by these when implementing the final regulation via 
delegated acts or level 3 guidance is critical.

II. Increased legal certainty can be achieved by improving scope description and definitions  
The number and nature of ESG products captured by the Proposal could be unintentionally wide given the use 
of several undefined terms in the scope description (Article 2(1)) and in the de finitions (Article 3). Adopting a 
fit for purpose and sound regulatory approach requires clear regulatory boundaries and unambiguous 
identification of the type of products the regulation will be covering. As a general principle, the regulation 
should seek to cover those ESG ratings that present the higher degree of subjectivity and opacity and that are 
used for investment decision-making purposes.  
Considering this, we make the following comments:  
• ESG scores are recognised as a specific type of ESG ratings as per Article 3(3)’s definition. As a provider 
of ESG scores, we support this approach given scores’ distinct rule -based features described above. We 
note however that the proposed definition of scores taken in isolation does not match the definition of ESG 
ratings and could capture any quantitative measure, even purely financial. This is not the stated intent 
of the Proposal. Given the Proposal refers to scores on a standalone basis in some instances (see Articles 
3(4); 3(7) or 16(1)), we recommend adopt ing a definition for “ESG scores” which adequately reflects the 
fact that they are a subset or a type of ESG ratings.10  
• We also suggest clarifying the role of the rating analyst in the definition of “opinion”. We recommend 
referring to the discretionary judgement the analyst has in the application of the rating methodology. This 
would help differentiate between scores and ratings and ensure that any discretionary analysis undertaken 
in addition to a rule-based methodology (e.g., private discussions with the rated entity, qualitative analysis 
contributing to the rating, or any judgement applied on top of the rule -based approach) is appropriately 
regulated.  
• Furthermore, we note a useful definition attempt for a rating or scoring ‘category’ in the MAS draft code of 
conduct for ESG ratings that refers to an “ ordinal rank or score ”.11 We find that this specification further 
helps qualifying the definition of a rating or a score.  
• In light of the above, the drafting we propose for the definitions of ‘scores’ is the following:  
o ‘ESG score’ means an ESG measure derived from data, using a rule-based 
methodology, a defined ranking syst em of scoring categories,  and based only on a pre-
established statistical or algorithmic system or model, without any additional substantial 
discretionary analytical input from an analyst. 
• Given ESG scores’ methodologies are applied in a consistent manner to all entities, they provide a higher 
level of transparency than ratings involving discretionary judgement from an analyst. Policy makers could 
consider differentiating their approach for regulating ESG scores and ESG ratings, notably as regards 
transparency requirements or third country procedures, among others (see recommendations below), for 
proportionality purposes.  
• ESG ratings that are “disclosed publicly” or “distributed” are meant to be in scope of the Proposal (Article 
2(1)). However, the concepts  of “disclosed publicly” and “distributed” are not defined and could 
inadvertently include products that i) are developed for an in-house use by regulated financial undertakings 
(‘RFU’) (excluded from the scope as per Article 2(2)(b)) and made available to  clients for transparency 
purpose or ii) are “publicly disclosed” but have no link to EU clients or rated entities. This could lead 
providers to reduce transparency on those products to avoid them being unintentionally caught within the 
scope of the Regulation. We recommend refining the approach to improve legal certainty by clarifying that:

III. A more risk-based and proportionate approach can support a competitive growth of the sector  
We welcome that the Commission has adopted some of IOSCO’s recommendations’ general principles such 
as independence of research or ensuring adequate systems and controls are in place.   
We note that the Commission stated that the Proposal “complies with the principle of proportionality as set out 
in Article 5 TFEU.”12 In several instances however, the Proposal appears to be more restrictive than IOSCO’s 
principle-based recommendations without providing any substantial justifications in the explanatory 
memorandum or the impact assessment accompanying the Proposal as to why a more prescriptive approach 
was needed.  
The most far-reaching provisions are described below. Some of them, such as the strict separation of activities, 
do not appear in any other EU financial services regulations. Given the relatively small scale and low systemic 
impact of the ESG ratings sector – as recognised in the Commission’s impact assessment – we question 
whether some of the provisions described below are adequate and proportionate. We also recom mend 
alternative options in our below comments that could help achieve the same objectives without adding an 
undue burden on this nascent market. 
a. Management of conflicts of interests : we note that the Commission proposes a strict separation of 
activities between ESG ratings providers and certain other types of services (development of benchmarks, 
consulting services, etc., as per Article 15(1)). Imposing a legal separation of activities would be a unique 
case in the EU financial services regulatory framewor k.  The standard regulatory approach to conflicts of 
interests has indeed been consistently focused on seeking to prevent them, and in case they arise, be 
transparent and demonstrate how those conflicts are effectively managed. This is the approach taken f or 
all financially systemic regulated activities and there are no justifications as to why a stricter option has 
been proposed specifically for the provision of ESG ratings .13 We therefore recommend adopting more 
proportionate policy provisions, consistent  with the approach taken in other EU regulations, 
mandating ESG ratings providers to implement an appropriate conflict of interest management 
framework, rather than a strict separation of activities from the outset.

b. Methodologies: as mentioned above, we strongly support the protection of independence of assessments 
and opinions. However, some provisions could interfere with this principle: 
i) The Proposal requires that ESG ratings methodologies be “rigorous, systematic, objective and 
capable of validation” (Article 14(7) emphasis added). Although the first three principles are part 
of the IOSCO recommendations, we note that IOSCO had purposefully chosen not to refer to a 
principle of “validation”, on the basis that “ statistical validation can be difficult and the 
recommendation should instead focus on promoting the publication of ESG performance reviews 
by the provider”.14 We agree with this statement. Given the diversity of ESG ratings and scores 
that are going to be captured by the regulation (e.g. combination of E, S and G ratings, social 
diversity ratings, sectorial ratings, etc.) and the fact that ESG metrics of success  can vary 
depending on the users’ objectives, we recommend a principle-based approach focusing on ESG 
ratings’ providers periodic reviews and appropriate documentation of the reviews.  
ii) Transparency and disclosure: some disclosure requirements could interfe re with a provider’s 
ESG ratings methodologies. For example, the requirement to disclose whether the assessment 
is “backward looking or forward -looking” could inadvertently force a provider to restrict their 
methodology to meet only one or the other approa ch, while many methodologies are a 
combination of backward and forward -looking elements.  We therefore recommend that the 
mandate given to ESMA on disclosure requirements ensures that the regulatory technical 
standards be principle -based, in order to suppo rt and protect full independence of 
methodologies. 
iii) Furthermore, in the case of " a major new information on a rated entity that has the possibility to 
affect the result of an ESG rating ", the requirement to disclose how ESG ratings providers have 
“taken that information  into account and whether they have amended the 
corresponding ESG rating” (Annex III, 2(e)) could be interpreted as a requirement to amend a 
rating each time a “major new information” is received. This ex -post amendment approach is 
problematic for ESG scores which are built on a rule -based methodology with no additional 
discretionary judgement. The proposed approach would require applying discretionary judgement 
to scores to i) decide whether the new information is “major”; ii) decide on its im pact on the ESG 
score, and iii) amend the score on an ex -post basis. In the context of ESG scores, the 
disclosure requirement should focus on the process, rather than on the score.  Providers 
should be transparent in demonstrating that the methodologies ar e robust enough to 
capture all key information and events impacting the rated entity in relation to the 
measured E, S, or G risk or opportunity.  Requiring that each score be reviewed on an ex-
post and discretionary basis risks undermining transparency for scores’ users.   
c. Pricing: the Proposal introduces the principle of “ fair, reasonable and non -discriminatory” (FRAND) 
treatment of clients when it comes to fees and pricing (Article 25(1)). The same article also requires that 
ESG ratings’ pricing be established “based on costs”, which is a very restrictive condition to operate: 
• This provision would be challenging to implement given how complex it is to assess actual costs in 
technology and data -driven services. Given the breadth of services offered by LSEG,  it is almost 
impossible to allocate costs to specific products. There are a number of different factors which are 
considered when setting pricing for scores, such as research and development technology, 
operational, data and resourcing costs required to e nsure the reliability and quality of the scores. 
These costs apply across the ESG scores business and cannot be easily attributed to each score or 
family of scores.   
• In addition, consideration should be given to the value of the scores provided to customers, who have 
different needs and use cases for the scores. Applying a cost -based pricing model would not 
appropriately reflect the different values the scores generate for the customers and would hinder 
innovation required to cater for the different customer needs and use cases. Providing a differentiated

• In particular, for a technology and solutions provider such as LSEG, the value is always a combination

We therefore recommend removing the requirement to base the pricing of ESG ratings on costs,  
given the complexity of costs assessments and the need for ESG ratings providers to continuously 
innovate and invest in value-driven solutions.  
Furthermore, the Proposal gran ts ESMA the power to withdraw an ESG ratings provider’s license or set 
fines up to 10% of the turnover of the provider, should it find that the fees charged to clients are not based 
on “actual costs”. The proposed amount is disproportionate. This provision  would also indirectly allow 
ESMA to set pricing levels on a private market. We do not believe that ESMA should be empowered with 
a price setting role and would argue that competition matters should be dealt with at the level of the relevant 
competition authorities.  
d. Third country regime: the Proposal introduces an equivalence regime with other third country 
jurisdictions adopting similar regulations for ESG ratings providers. We welcome this approach, especially 
as regards the proliferation of regulations and codes of conducts to which LSEG will be subject. However, 
we also know from experience that even fully equivalent regimes are not necessarily recognised as such 
by the European Commission. We therefore expect the proposed endorsement and recognition re gimes 
to be the main regulatory options for third country providers to be able to continue serving their EU clients.  
In the Proposal, the drafting of the endorsement and recognition options is similar to the  Benchmark 
Regulation (BMR)’s third country regi me. This regime is however currently being reviewed on the basis 
that it may not be fit for purpose and risks preventing EU users from accessing third country benchmarks 
once the ongoing transition period is over. 15 We recommend avoiding BMR pitfalls and e nsuring that EU 
clients can continue accessing ESG ratings regardless of where the providers are located and at a 
reasonable price, given the impact of third country compliance infrastructures have on cost of service. A 
few options to ensure an orderly continuation of service are: 
i) Make the recognition route available to any type of ESG ratings providers . Both 
endorsement and recognition policy options are meant to offer the same level of regulatory 
compliance and supervision. The endorsement route can be ex tremely cumbersome, notably 
because each product needs to be endorsed on a standalone basis (as per below). The 
recognition option is less burdensome, if a third -country provider does not already possess an 
established and functioning business in the EU, while still ensuring that the third country provider 
demonstrates compliance with the entire regulation and is subject to ESMA’s supervision. The 
recognition option seems a more appropriate and proportionate route than endorsement for such 
third-country pro viders to keep the costs to serve EU clients manageable while ensuring full

IV. Transparency requirements to be applied at the level of the scores’ methodology 
As a long-standing promoter of transparency in the ESG scores market, LSEG supports the general focus on 
transparency of methodologies. Given the diversity of views and opinions in the ESG fi eld, adequate 
transparency is paramount to understand what is being measured by ESG ratings.  
We also support the Proposal’s approach to differentiate between public disclosure and private disclosures of 
ESG ratings and their methodologies. Clients and users should have access to more granular disclosures than 
the general public. For ESG scores, we would further recommend the disclosures to apply at the level of the 
methodology rather at the level of the score. Once a methodology is set, it remains the same for all companies 
covered without any discretionary input from an analyst, so transparency of the underlying methodology 
provides all the necessary information on categories and weightings. It would also help making the disclosures 
more workable and avoid  mandating several pages of templates for each score. This would make the 
environment more readable and user friendly for the clients.  
In addition, both the methodologies assessing ESG risks and performance and the type of data collected 
constantly evolve and become more refined. As an example, our climate governance score provided via our 
partnership with the Transition Path Initiative (TPI) would fall either under the “E” or the “G” category. The EU 
disclosure framework should avoid pres cribing one category but should rather enable providers of ratings 
where the line between E, S or G factors is not so clear to demonstrate how those factors impact the overall 
assessment, where relevant. In addition, a provider will continuously refine its models and source of data and 
can, for example, consider using alternative data, such as satellite imagery or weather forecasts. Disclosure 
requirements should not inadvertently stifle such innovation by forcing providers to focus on a specific type of 
data. 
We therefore recommend a clear mandate for ESMA’s level 2 on the transparency requirements for 
them to apply at the level of the methodology for ESG scores and to be principle-based so as to support 
innovation and competition. 
We finally note that Article 46 of the Proposal grants the European Commission the unilateral power to amend 
the Annexes. The Annexes detail information to be provided in the application for authorisation, organisational 
requirements on record keeping and outsourcing as well as disclosure requirements. Both Annex I and Annex 
III are required to be further specified by ESMA regulatory technical standards (as per Articles 5, 21 and 22). 
Any changes to the Annexes would therefore have substantial repercussions on the content of the Le vel 1 
regulation.

V. Need for a realistic implementation timeline 
We note the proposed 6-month application deadline (Article 50). This timeframe appears unusual and is likely 
to be very challenging to achieve in practice.  The standard is usually 18 months as imposing compliance in a 
shorter period of time is extremely difficult operationally and creates undue non -compliance risks. We also 
note that the level 2 requirements, including the details of the application for authorisati on which have 
regulatory technical standards attached to them (Article 5(3)  and of the disclosure requirements (Article s 21 
and 22), will most certainly not be available within those 6 months, making compliance virtually impossible.  
Indeed, as per the ESMA Regulation (1095/2010) Articles 10 to 14, regulatory technical standards need first 
to be drafted by ESMA, then be published for consultation, then adopted and proposed to the European 
Commission, which then has to adopt and publish them as a delegate d act and subject this delegated act to 
the co-legislators’ 3-month scrutiny period. This scrutiny period can be extended for a further 3 months.  
The above process usually takes at least 6 months, if not more. Legal certainty and clarity would require that  
the regulation’s application date does not start before the entire regulatory framework is decided upon, for in 
scope entities to then be able to make an informed decision.  
We therefore recommend setting the application deadline to 18 months.

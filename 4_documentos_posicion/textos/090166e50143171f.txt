Paris | 7 September 2023
 Used textiles collected within the EU are predominantly exported, primarily to African and Asiancountries, without sufficient control over their quality or subsequent fate [1]. For example, only in France,80% of collected textiles (in tons) are exported [2]. The immense quantities involved, the highly uncertainquality of these textiles, and the inadequate local waste management infrastructure, often result in theirdeposition in open landfills, placing significant strain on the environment and affected communities.
It is essential to recognise that the increasing volumes of textiles entering EU markets, due tooverproduction and overconsumption, causes major degradation to the environment (chemical release,microfiber leakage, etc.), while negatively affecting local communities (health hazards, deprivation ofeconomic opportunities, etc.). 
Considering that the volume of used textiles exported from the EU has tripled over the past twodecades, reaching nearly 1.7 million tons in 2019 [3], and with expectations of this proportion continuing torise, it is imperative for the EU's transition towards a circular economy to address the issue of textileexports, ensuring that only quality garments suitable for re-use are exported. 
The fact that the majority of collected textiles are exported also shows the limited ability of the EUeconomic tissue to valorise these products. In the forthcoming years, a substantial influx of used garmentsis expected to enter the European Union (EU) waste management systems as the EU Member States aremandated to collect, sort, and handle the end-of-life of textiles by 2025. In response to these requirements,Member States have started implementing extended producer responsibility schemes (EPR) for textiles toimprove waste management and meet their separate collection targets. 
This fundamental transformation must take place while considering both environmental and socialdimensions without compromising the well-being of workers and communities in third countries.

A. THE WASTE HIERARCHY & THE WASTE STATUS FOR GARMENTS "DISCARDED" BYCONSUMER
1. Consider potential shortcomings of the Waste Hierarchy through an independentenvironmental impact assessment.Recommendation: To better understand and justify the export of used garments and textile waste from theEU, we urge the Commission to conduct an independent environmental impact study on the implications ofshipping textile waste and used garments destined for reuse outside the EU versus recycling in the EU. The waste hierarchy [4], by which reuse is considered the best outcome for discarded products, is a crucialprinciple to the EU's efforts in addressing waste generation in Europe. However, it is vital to acknowledgethe negative environmental consequences of shipping discarded garments and textile waste abroad. For example, when low-quality garments are sent to African countries for reuse, they often end up in openlandfills rather than being recycled into valuable secondary raw materials in Europe. Prioritising reuse at allcosts without assessing the potential environmental impacts can exacerbate the problem. Therefore, we strongly urge the EU Commission to conduct an independent impact assessment on the reuse of usedtextiles as part of the ongoing revision of the Waste Framework Directive (WFD).
2. Establish end-of-waste criteria for textiles, combined with item-based sorting, to enhancethe quality and homogeneity of textiles destined for reuse.Recommendation: Establish consistent EU-wide criteria for end-of-waste and by-product classification inaccordance with the waste hierarchy. These criteria would provide clarity on when sorted textiles, preparedfor reuse [5], should no longer be considered waste. Every collected item going through sorting processesmust be checked for its quality and condition before determining whether it is fit for reuse or requiresrecycling.
To address the issue of textile waste, the EU legislators are currently negotiating the Waste FrameworkDirective (WFD) to set end-of-waste criteria, among others. However, once the end-of-waste (EoW) criteriafor used garments are defined, those deemed suitable for reuse will fall outside the scope of the WasteShipment Regulation, as it purely applies to "wastes". Consequently, it becomes crucial to set sufficientrules for those reuse items destined for export under the revision of the WFD.    Currently, used textiles are often labelled as "fit for reuse" despite their quality and condition beinginadequate for actual reuse. The responsibility for determining which products are suitable for exportcategorised as 2nd grade or B quality (also known as "tropical mix") and subsequently shipped to Asian andAfrican countries lies primarily with the sorters.
To address this issue, alongside quality standards for textiles bales exported (see next section), theimplementation of End-of-Waste criteria becomes crucial. These criteria would establish the standards usedgarments must meet to be classified as "fit for reuse" while ensuring that garments not meeting thesestandards are directed toward recycling processes. Promoting an item-based sorting process, also calledpositive sorting [6], that adheres to clear sorting criteria is equally important. This approach ensures theconsistent and homogeneous quality of sorted items across different sorting facilities.     However, EPR schemes should organise and finance the infrastructure until the end-of-waste status isreached (for example, repair). This would improve downstream traceability and support responsible wastemanagement, as required by the polluter pays principle.

B. EXPORTS OF USED GARMENTS & TEXTILE WASTE: DEFINE EXPORT QUALITY STANDARDS
3. Require quality standards for the composition of textile bales for export, with regular auditsto check compliance, as part of the revised Waste Framework Directive.Recommendation: Common quality standards for textiles bales for export should be set. Such standardsshould be established as part of the revised Waste Framework Directive, complementing the WasteShipment Regulation. 
There are currently no standards for the composition of exported textile bales. Changing styles and needsimply changes in sorting and waste management practices. Consequently, regular verification of theadequacy between textile bales’ composition and clients' expectations on arrival is required. Otherwise,receiving countries end up with textile waste that they can neither reuse nor process, given the lack ofinfrastructure. Simultaneously, the textile bale issuer bears major responsibility in this process and shouldbe held accountable for the quality of the textile bales.     While we support the direction of the EU Commission as part of the proposed revision of the WasteFramework Directive (article 22d) to require information on the quality of used garments ready for shipping,we are convinced that clear quality standards are a more effective tool. To begin with, criteria should coveritems that are not suitable for export (e.g., stained or torn items). Strong collaboration and regular contactbetween clients in receiving countries and used textile exporters can address this issue. Audits comprisingquality checks can be instrumental in closing loopholes once the shipped textile bales arrive in the receivingcountry. Those audits should check the percentage of garments that do not meet the quality standards fortextile bales for export. 
4. Promote stronger accountability and traçability: Accreditation and performance controls ofsorting facilities.Recommendation: Waste sorting facilities should be accredited, and national market surveillanceauthorities should conduct regular performance checks to ensure items’ quality ahead of reuse or recycling.A specific focus should be placed onitems destined for export. 
Traceability and accountability of sorting facilities are needed to ensure that they perform a qualitativesorting process of garments. Since accreditation provides means for national market surveillance authoritiesto perform audits and check at sorting plants, we recommend expanding the accreditation of sorting plantsacross Europe. 
Audits and checks by national market surveillance authorities should be performed upon the study ofsorting quality grades rates (A-C or 1-3)[7] at the national level. More stringent controls should be performedon sorting facilities with a higher percentage of exports of used garments to ensure that qualityrequirements are met before exportation. Therefore, we call on the EU authorities to strengthen the qualityrequirements as part of the revision of the Waste Framework Directive.

C. ENSURE & DEMONSTRATE CAPACITY IN RECEIVING COUNTRIES
5. Ensure the demonstrated capacity of receiving countries to deal with both textile waste andused garments.Recommendation: Exports of textile waste for recovery and used garments for reuse should only beallowed to countries that give their consent and demonstrate their capacity to either (1) process such wastesustainability or have a market for used textiles [8].
To prevent the exportation of used garments in countries without sufficient capacity to deal with such wasteand used textiles, it is paramount to ensure that:• adequate treatment capacity and recycling are available in the receiving country, • there is an actual market for those garments shipped for reuse, • regardless of where that country is. 
However, traceability throughout the entire export chain becomes essential, including intermediarycountries, to ensure that potential loopholes can be closed and that we better understand the issue at hand.
6. Promote mutually beneficial knowledge exchange between EU actors and localcommunities, fostering capacity building in waste management infrastructure and upcycling.Recommendation: A dialogue between local communities and EU actors should be further facilitated andencouraged by the EU Commission. 
Local communities have harnessed rich knowledge on, for example, upcycling of garments that should beleveraged in the EU. Equally, PROs and other independent actors experienced in waste management canhave a significant role in supporting the capacity building of countries that do not have appropriate wasteinfrastructure and are recipients of textile waste. With the EU Commissions' proposal on the targetedrevision of the Waste Framework Directive, we see the opportunity to further strengthen the role of PROs inthis respect. Additionally, we call on the authorities to further promote and encourage such levers. 
FINAL REMARKS
Paris Good Fashion and Vestiaire Collective are eager and committed to engaging in fruitful collaborationwith EU policymakers regarding the crucial matter of textile waste export and relevant EU legislativedevelopments (WFD, WSR, EoW, ESPR, etc.). As advocates for sustainable fashion, we firmly believe in theimportance of addressing the environmental impact of shipping used garments and textile waste overseas.We are ready to contribute our expertise and insights to help shape effective policies that prioritise reuseand waste management within the EU.

ABOUT 
PARIS GOOD FASHION
Founded in 2019, Paris Good Fashion (PGF) is an association under the French law of 1901 which gathers
more than a hundred professional actors (companies, designers, NGOs, institutional ...) committed to
accelerating change and transforming fashion companies' practices. Paris Good Fashion brings all actors
together, whatever their field of expertise (fashion, accessory, luxury, ready-to-wear, brand, distributor...), to
co-construct and share best practices. 
VESTIAIRE COLLECTIVE
Vestiaire Collective is the leading global platform for pre-loved luxury fashion. The company’s mission is to
transform the fashion industry for a more sustainable future, promoting the circular fashion movement as an
alternative to overproduction, overconsumption and the wasteful practices of the industry. Driven by the
philosophy "Long Live Fashion," Vestiaire Collective offers a trusted space for its community to prolong the
life of its most-loved fashion pieces. The platform's innovative features simplify the selling and buying
process and give its members access to one-of-a-kind wardrobes around the world. The company boasts a
curated catalog of five million rare and highly desirable items. Founded in Paris in 2009, Vestiaire Collective
is a Certified B Corporation® and is active in 80 countries worldwide.

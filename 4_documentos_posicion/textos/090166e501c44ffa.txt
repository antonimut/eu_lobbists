The National Association of German Cooperative Bank s (Bundesverband der Deutschen Volksbanken 
und Raiffeisenbanken e.V. – BVR) is grateful for ha ving the chance to comment on the European 
Commission's legislative package to revise the requ irements for crisis management and deposit 
insurance ("CMDI Review"), published on 18 April 2023.  
As a preliminary remark, the BVR is in favour of im proving crisis management for credit institutions. 
However, we are afraid that this welcomed goal will not be achieved by the amendments proposed with 
the CMDI Review. Instead, the legislative proposal would considerably impair the performance of the 
well-established national deposit-based guarantee s chemes and in particular such that are 
acknowledged as institutional protection schemes. U ltimately, the BVR fears that the proposal is a 
threat to the structure of the German banking market and to financial stability as a whole. 
The legislative proposal includes amendments to the  Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive (BRRD), 
the Single Resolution Mechanism Regulation (SRMR) a nd the Deposit Guarantee Schemes Directive 
(DGSD). The BVR rejects the entire legislative pack age. Nevertheless, the following comments focus on 
the proposed changes to the SRMR. Suggested amendme nts to the BRRD and the DGSD will be dealt 
with in the separate comments in the respective Have your say-process. 
In more detail we would like to raise the following concerns: 
1.  No complete abandonment of the insolvency regime. 
If a credit institution encounters financial challe nges, the authorized resolution authority must, in 
accordance with existing laws, determine whether th e institution should undergo liquidation through 
insolvency procedures or if it should adhere to the  specialized resolution framework. The resolution 
mechanism is designed and only suitable for particu larly large credit institutions of systemic 
significance. For credit institutions of moderate a nd small sizes, insolvency-based liquidation presen ts 
a suitable, dependable, and balanced array of tools . The same applies to significant institutions whic h 
are subject to the SSM, but for whom safeguard prog rams for institutions and schemes guaranteeing 
deposits offer a favourable and economically viable means to exit the market.  
Nevertheless, deviating from this established appro ach, the European Commission is currently 
advocating for subjecting virtually all institution s to the resolution regime, regardless of the exten t of 
their systemic importance. This is envisaged by maj or changes to the Public Interest Assessment (PIA) 
whose outcome governs the decision on whether to ap ply the resolution mechanism or the insolvency 
proceeding to a credit institution.  
The BVR rejects this novel strategy for the ensuing reasons: 
 Resolution tools are primarily tailored for institu tions of extensive systemic importance. 
Their complex design is not appropriate for credit institutions whose failure has minimal 
repercussions on overall financial stability.  
 We consider the proposed changes to the PIA pursuan t to Art. 18 (5) draft SRMR as 
inadequate. First, there is no reason why a normal insolvency proceeding shall only be in 
the public interest if it meets the resolution obje ctives more effectively  than the resolution 
mechanism. Public interest should remain considered  as satisfied when resolution 
objectives are met to the same extent , as per the current version of the PIA. Second, th e 
inclusion of costs with a limited view to extraordi nary public financial support disregards 
extensive costs associated with resolution planning  and casts a distorted light on 
insolvency proceedings.  
 The extension of the resolution mechanism will caus e extensive planning and onerous 
reporting obligations, this way imposing significant administrative and financial burdens.  
Against this background, we advocate for a removal of the proposed changes to Art. 18 (5) SRMR.

2.  Co-financing of resolutions by Deposit Guarantee Schemes must remain limited.  
The current legal framework includes provisions for  the involvement of Deposit Guarantee Schemes in 
funding resolution actions. The extent of such fund ing is very limited – for a good reason: to safegua rd 
the financial soundness of the Deposit Guarantee Schemes themselves.  
In contrast to this, the legislative proposal envis ages that Deposit Guarantee Schemes will assume 
unlimited liability going forward. Additionally, fu nds from the Deposit Guarantee Scheme can also be 
employed, for instance, to facilitate the transfer of even those deposits that lack protection. This 
introduces a potential risk of depleting the financ ial resources of the Guarantee Schemes, thereby 
undermining depositor confidence in the effectiveness and functionality of the schemes: 
 In a worst-case scenario, all amassed funds within the Deposit Guarantee Scheme might 
need to be utilized for resolution financing. They would then have to be replenished within 
a maximum of six years. The augmented financial dem and due to a higher likelihood of 
seeking recourse during a crisis is compounded by t he planned expansion of depositor 
protection to previously unprotected deposits (see below), which contradicts the 
designated mandate. 
 If resolution measures co-funded by a Deposit Guara ntee Scheme prove unsuccessful, we 
understand that the same schemes must also fund com pensatory disbursements to 
depositors. Eventually there would be a dual burden for the schemes.  
 Unavoidably, subsequent increases in contributions undermine the competitiveness of the 
institutions. Unforeseen necessity for special cont ributions further erodes profits and 
impacts both competitiveness, financial stability, and customers' trust in the system.  
 As a sidenote it shall be mentioned that the CMDI l egislative proposal also envisages the 
abandonment of the proven preference of Deposit Gua rantee Schemes in the insolvency 
proceedings of credit institutions, which is curren tly provided for in Art. 108 BRRD. This 
would have further critical financial consequences which must be taken into consideration 
here (see Art. 108 of the draft BRRD and our explanations in the respective "Have your say" 
process relating to the BRRD). 
Against this background, we advocate for a removal of the proposed changes to Art. 79 and Art. 27 (7) 
point (a) SRMR (in connection with the proposed cha nges to Art. 109 BRRD, see our comment in the 
respective Have your say-process). 
3.  Alternative private and preventive measures may not be impaired. 
The Commission's legislative proposal associates additional requisites with alternative measures by the 
Deposit Guarantee Schemes which have the purpose of  forestalling an impending default. The BVR 
objects to these propositions for the ensuing reasons: 
 Art. 13c draft SRMR introduces a supervisory evaluation of alternative measures to avert an 
imminent default during an "early warning period". This evaluation relates to their effects 
and timing of implementation, see Art. 13c (2) point (b) draft SRMR. It is understood that the 
proposal does not imply mandatory approval of these  private measures. Nevertheless, the 
newly introduced engagement with the regulatory bod y introduces the potential risk of 
triggering a state-aid situation (especially agains t the proposed incorporation of Art. 18a 
draft SRMR, see below). This would impede swift and efficient action. 
 The introduction of the authority's option to marke t an institution in the phase before an 
FOLTF-status must be seen critically as well (Art. 13c (4) and (5) SRMR draft). This measure 
is a strong infringement of the institute's rights and would happen too early if it is not yet 
even clear that the institution will fail eventuall y. Additionally, the proposal is not 
compatible with measures aimed at averting a defaul t through an IPS (Institutional 
Protection Scheme). Consequently, as an alternative  to the proposed deletion of

paragraphs 4 and 5, an addition is imperative to clarify that the proposed marketing option 
is rescinded if there are alternative measures by a n IPS – with which it fulfils legally or 
officially recognized responsibilities. 
 The wording of Art. 18a (1) point (b) draft SRMR can be misunderstood to mean that in future 
all preventive measures, including those of private  deposit guarantee schemes, are to 
constitute state aid. Since such a comprehensive ch ange in the legal situation is not 
intended, the wording should be made clearer to ensure that it only applies if the conditions 
for state aid are met. 
 The implementation of preventive measures is couple d with the effective execution of the 
institution's resolution strategy (Art. 18 (1) point (b) draft SRMR). It is, however, unclear how 
privately executed measures to prevent an instituti on's failure should align with the 
effective implementation of the - potentially divergent - resolution strategy. The objectives 
of resolution (gone concern) and institutional prot ection (going concern) contradict each 
other so that the latter should not be subject to the first.  
Against this background, we advocate for a removal of the proposed introduction of Art. 13c and Art. 
18a (1) point (b) SRMR as well as of the change to Art. 18 (1) point (b) draft SRMR. If the marketing option 
in Art. 13c (4) and (5) draft SRMR remains there mu st be an exemption to cases where alternative 
measures by an IPS would prevent the failure of the institution. 
4.  Contributions to the Single Resolution Fund (SRF) according to Art. 69 et seq. SRMR 
In terms of contributions to the resolution fund, w e consider it essential to incorporate some 
clarifications. Specifically, once the resolution funds reach their target level by the end of 2023, regular 
contributions should no longer be imposed. However,  it is necessary to explicitly set a non-dynamic 
target level of 1% of covered deposits, as the Commission and the SRB have so far assumed the opposite 
without a legal basis. 
The contributions already collected, particularly for the Single Resolution Fund (SRF), are approximately 
40% higher than originally estimated. If these cont ributions persist, the fund will become even more 
overcapitalized than it already is at the end of 20 23. According to the current legal regulations, 
contributions cease when the target level is reache d. They only have to be reinstated through ex post 
contributions if the target level falls significantly short. 
If the bank levy continues to be imposed despite th is, it's crucial to explicitly state in the legal t ext 
(Art. 69(4) BRRD) that the collection process costs  encompass all expenses incurred by authorities and  
institutions. Focusing solely on the costs related to collection by resolution authorities would make 
suspending the levy improbable. Additionally, suspending the collection process should not only cover 
the contributions themselves but also extend to the  submission of the annual report used for 
contribution calculations. 
Finally, we consider it essential that the provisio n in Art. 8(5) of the Council Implementing Regulati on 
(EU) 2015/81 continues after the SRF has reached it s target level at the end of 2023. Medium-sized 
institutions according to Art. 8(5) Council Implementing Regulation (EU) 2015/81, this means institutions 
whose total assets are equal to, or less than EUR 3 ,000,000,000 €, pay a lump-sum of EUR 50,000 for 
the first EUR 300,000,000 of total liabilities, les s own funds and covered deposits. This arrangement is 
to end when the SRF target level is reached. We do not consider this to be justified. The regulation h as 
proven itself, is risk-appropriate and ensures that  medium-sized institutions are not overly burdened 
financially in terms of the bank levy. Maintaining the regulation is also justified in view of the 
prerequisite of an institution as a small and non-c omplex institution (SNCI) in the CRR (balance sheet  
total limit of EUR 5 billion).

EUROPEN contribution to the ex-post consultation on 
the targeted revision of the Waste Framework 
Directive 
 
EUROPEN, the European Organisation for Packaging and the Environment, fully supports the objective of the 
European Commission proposal for a targeted revision of the Waste Framework Directive (WFD) to establish food 
waste and textile waste reduction targets  with a view to improve the environmental sustainability of the agri-food 
and textile sectors. At the same time, EUROPEN calls for a more ambitious and far-reaching revision of the text to 
ensure better alignment between the WFD and other EU policies and legislative initiatives.  
Through this paper, EUROPEN wishes to provide feedback on the Commission text and suggest elements that the 
revision should incorporate to more broadly contri bute to the policy goals of the European Green Deal, as well as 
the ongoing revision of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive (PPWD). 
 
1. Recognising that packaging contributes to the prevention of products and food waste 
o Packaging plays a key role in protecting products and minimising waste at all stages of the value chain. High 
performance packaging solutions and technologies can preserve food safety by minimising sources of 
contamination and reduce food waste from spoila ge. As an example, the use of aseptic packages, which are 
designed to protect the foodstuff from the effects of light and oxygen, do not require refrigeration, and have a 
shelf life of 6 to 12 months. Innovative packaging designs also help consumers buy an d use food in portions 
that match their needs and reduce food waste from leftovers. Smart food packaging, equipped with sensors or 
radio frequency identification  technologies, enable to monitor the safety and quality of foodstuffs and allow 
improvements in supply chain logistics and management.  
o By protecting products, packaging also prevents waste of raw materials and natural resources that went into 
growing or making goods and the energy used to transport them from the producer to the retailer; ensuring the 
safe delivery of products to European consumers . As also recognised in a recent opinion 1 of the European 
Economic and Social Committee: “Packaging plays a critical role in economic, environmental, and social 
sustainability, but looking at packaging alone might have a negative impact on the regulatory system. In reality, 
the packaging used to preserve things usually represents significantly less resources and has a far lower 
intrinsic value than the product it is protecting. For example, in the food business, 30% of all food produced 
worldwide is lost or wasted throughout the supply chain: as a result, product loss due to poor packing is likely 
to have far more negative environmental consequences. Improved packaging might be one approach to 
minimise this food loss rate.” From a production standpoint, it should also be recalled that food waste has at 
least ten times the environmental impact of packaging waste, and that even without taking into account of the 
impact of methane from decayed food.2  
o In a recent publication, the UN FAO stressed3 that “Packaging plays an important role in not only reducing food 
loss and waste but by extension boosting food security, nutrition and livelihoods too. A circular approach to 
how we package our food can make a big difference to the food security of our communities and the health of 
our planet”. Packaging’s role in securing the resilience of food systems was also highlighted in the European

In addition to food and textile waste, the review of the Waste Framework Directive must address remaining barriers 
and bottlenecks to the creation of an EU market for secondary raw materials, thus supporting the recycled content 
and packaging recyclability objectives of the  forthcoming Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR ), 
and address weak enforcement of existing EU provisions at national level to ensure waste management obligations 
are met by Member States and other operators. 
 
2. Enforcing Member States’ mandatory separate waste collection obligations to achieve 
waste recycling targets and packaging recyclability at scale. 
o Packaging design plays a key role to increase packaging recyclability. However, design ing packaging for 
recyclability alone will not suffice. Effective collection systems, together with sorting and recycling infrastructure 
are essential to ensure that recyclable packaging is recycled in practice and to boost recycling rates.  
o The Waste Early Warning Report 7, published by the European Commission in June 2023, found that 18 
Member States are at risk of missing the target of 55% preparing for reuse and recycling of municipal waste, 
and 10 are at risk of missing the target of 65% recycling of packaging waste by 2025. The report also stressed 
that, in 2020 eight Member States still landfilled over 50% of municipal waste, with three of them reporting rates 
above 70%.   
o Effective separate collection and source segregation are crucial to obtain Secondary Raw Materials (SRMs) of 
quality. EUROPEN supports the introduction in the PPWR of an EU -wide definition of ‘recyclable packaging’ 
and the proposal to assess packaging recyclability against Design for Recycling (DfR) criteria, which the EU 
packaging industry has been developing and applying for some years now. However, to ensure achieving the 
PPWR target of packaging recyclability at scale by 2035, it is necessary that Member States effectively comply 
with their mandatory separate collection obligations under the WFD and that all packaging which are compliant

with DfR are collected for recycling with no exemptions. Therefore, the link between packaging recyclability 
and Member States’ collection obligations must be adequately reflected in both the PPWR and WFD.  
o As a very minimum, separate collection should be implemented in an effective way, and least harmonised at 
country level, where many differences often persist across regions within a single Member State, to ensure at 
least four existing main streams: 1) plastic, metal and beverage cartons; 2) paper and cardboard; 3) glass and 
4) bio-waste. This ambition should however be taken further with a goal to harmonise separate waste collection 
across the EU, in line with the harmonisation of sorting instructions under the forthcoming PPWR. 
o Finally, landfilling of packaging waste should be banned. The landfill restrictions that are to come into effect 
only in 2035, as defined in Directive 1999/31/EC on the landfill of waste, should be anticipated. In countries 
where an outright ban of landfill is not yet implemented, landfill taxes will help dissuade waste managers from 
pursuing this end of life. Similarly to landfill, reliance on incineration should be rapidly reduced to a minimum 
where appropriate, considering existing regulatory requirement s, to ensure waste is brought back into the 
economy as valuable secondary raw materials. High gate fees for incineration will help to avoid that separately 
collected waste is diverted towards incineration. 
 
3. Addressing improper enforcement and diverging transposition of EU rules  
o As highlighted above, w aste collection systems vary across the EU and sub -optimal practices lead to lower 
recycling rates and lower quality recyclates. In this respect, we would like to emphasise the importance of 
ensuring the full transposition and subsequent implementation and enforcement of the provisions of Article 8a 
of the WFD (i.e. “the General minimum requirements for extended producer responsibility schemes”). 
o Well-functioning Extended Producers Responsibilities (EPR) schemes are essential to the achievement of the 
ambitions of the Circular Economy Action Plan and consistent with the polluter -pays-principle. Within the 
context of packaging waste, such schemes are key to fund the separate collection of packaging waste to reduce 
the volume of packaging waste and ensure all packaging are effectively recycled, thus permitting the 
reprocessing of secondary raw materials to replace virgin materials. 
o In a limited number of Member States, ill -construed schemes (outside the control of the obligated industry) 
have failed and national governments are considering schemes with governance models that are clearly 
inconsistent with Article 8a. In such cases, th ere is a risk that fees collected from obligated industry are not 
hypothecated for packaging waste management. EUROPEN therefore calls for  the full and complete 
transposition and subsequent enforcement of the General Minimum Requirements of Article 8a and looks to 
the Commission to monitor and enforce this obligation , including with the development of the pending 
guidelines on the modulation of financial contributions referred to in Article 8a(5). 
o The revision of the WFD is also an opportunity to strengthen the General Minimum Requirements of Article 8a 
to boost recycling across the EU. In relation to packaging waste, we strongly recommend that the governance 
and transparency of EPR schemes is improved to ensure better oversight of EPR fees and their utilisation. As 
an example, financial contributions paid in line with Article 8a should be earmarked to finance the net cost of 
collection, sorting and recycling infrastructures of the packaging type they are paid for. 
o Beyond EPR schemes, diverging measures adopted by Member States across the EU, such as country-specific 
labelling requirements for packaging waste sorting or unilateral national targets obligations, are fragmenting 
the Single Market. Recent examples of such divergent measures include the  Spanish Royal Decree on 
packaging and the packaging bans in troduced by France and Luxembourg. These measures are not only 
undermining Europe’s competitiveness, but also investment predictability and the attainment of circular 
economy goals. Businesses need long -term investment visibility to continue to develop innovative packaging 
and solutions for recycling processes and technologies.

4. Developing a well-functioning EU market for Secondary Raw Materials 
o Packaging can no longer be considered as waste when entering the EU market but rather as a valuable 
resource that must be brought back into the circular economy. 
o Inadequate sorting, collection and recycling infrastructures are structural issues that do not only limit recycling 
but also hinder effective competition between recycled and virgin materials. The revision of the WFD, in 
conjunction with the forthcoming PPWR, needs to provide sufficient visibility for businesses to drive investments 
in innovative recycling technologies that are needed to improve the quality of SRM s, including mechanical, 
organic and chemical recycling. With regards to recycling, clarity is urgently needed on the extent at which 
chemical recycling will be factored in the calculation of future recycling rates and recycled content targets, to 
avoid stalling investment in this new technology. EU calculating rules enabling the use of a mass balance 
accounting method (with a fuel -use exempt model ) to calculate the content of recycled plastic and biomass 
feedstock used in packaging should be adopted in a timely manner to boost plastic recycling. 
o A stronger policy framework is necessary to facilitate and strengthen the transboundary movements of waste 
across the EU to support the creation of a Single Market for SRM. The measures on Waste Shipment proposed 
by the European Commission are a step in th e right direction to increase recycling. It is however important to 
go one step further in supporting the creation of a Union market for secondary raw materials. The WFD should 
also introduce a clear obligation on Member States to ensure that, when they la ck adequate waste sorting 
and/or recycling infrastructure, they would be required to ship their waste to another EU country to ensure that 
packaging waste is effectively recycled, avoiding incineration or landfill. 
 
5. Ensuring full alignment between the objectives of the WFD and PPWR  
o The obligations of the WFD must be effectively enforced and aligned with targets and requirements of the 
PPWR. For example, separate waste collection must be effectively implemented for all waste streams to enable 
the achievement of the PPW R recycling and recyclability targets and to boost the uptake of recycled content 
in packaging. Not incorporating requirements for the improvement of collection, sorting and recycling of waste 
in the WFD would be a serious missed opportunity to deliver on the goals of the EU Green Deal. 
o Similarly, the objective to reduce food and textile waste pursued by the WFD revision should not be contradicted 
by measures that, such as the ban on the use of packaging for fresh fruit and vegetable o r measures on 
packaging minimisation, have not been fully impact assessed against food wa ste and spoilage or disregard 
packaging functionality.

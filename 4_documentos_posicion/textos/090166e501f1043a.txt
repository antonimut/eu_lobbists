Missing Children Europe’s feedback on the Commission proposal on the review of the 
Victims’ Rights Directive 
 
 
Missing Children Europe is the European Federation for Missing and Sexually Exploited Children 
connecting 32 European organisations committed to prevent that children go missing and to protect them 
from any harm that lead to or result from going missing. Aimed at holistic and integrated child protection 
systems across Europe, Missing Children Europe works with professionals who assist children (at risk 
of) going missing and their families. Missing Children Europe coordinates the network of the 116 000 
missing child European hotlines, the free service working with law enforcement, child protection 
services and other relevant actors, that provides immediate response to children as well as psychological 
support, administrative and legal assistance to families.  
Missing Children Europe welcomes the European Commission proposal amending Directive 
2012/29/EU establishing minimum standards on the rights, support and protection of victims of crime, 
and replacing Council Framework Decision 2001/220/JHA (Victims’ Rights Directive)  and accords 
priority to guaranteeing access to justice  and to supporting services for children  considering their  
vulnerabilities and individual needs.  Moreover, Missing Children Europe welcomes Directive’s 
provisions on specialised support services, improved physical protection, court assistance, access to 
compensation and enhanced information sharing . For us, the theme is of utmost importance since the 
hotlines are the initial point of contact with children and offer them the chance to speak up about 
instances of abuse and exploitation, which are thought as the main causes of a missing episode. 
The systematic and prompt appointment of a specialised and trained lawyer to intervene - free of charge 
- for every child victim involved in criminal proceedings is fundamental; however, access to justice for 
children seems not to be fully addressed through the Victims’ Rights Directive; generic support services 
at national level as well as  specialised support services , offered by adequately trained professionals , 
should be put in place, especially for children, who are at high risk of victimisation.  
Missing Children Europe strongly support s anonymous reporting channels as a mean of increasing 
rates of reported potential crime, thus reducing data shortage and impunity. Child hotlines and helplines 
play a vital role in providing psycho-social support while tackling distress and acting as entry-point for 
the subsequent referral to law enforcement authorities and legal aid. For this reason, Missing Children 
Europe welcomes provisions aimed at establishing easily accessible, user friendly, free of charge and 
confidential victims’ helplines, but it also encourages the extension of the platform to real time support 
via a chat-box to enhance reporting.  
Studies demonstrate that children report incidents of violence and abuse one time, regardless of reporting 
services. Therefore, strong referral mechanisms and cooperation between child-friendly services is 
fundamental to ensure children receive the right support. Moreover, mechanisms aimed at enabling 
hotlines and helplines to exchange data and share information should be considered.

individual needs assessment through a coordinated approach - specifically addressing the coordination

In order to  decrease the revictimisation/retraumatisation rates, evidence based procedures and 
procedural safeguards should be promoted and enforced. Aimed at a child friendly justice for children’s 
rights to be heard, informed and protected 1, for example, the contact person as a single professional 
contact to support children during proceedings  - as foreseen by the Barnahus model 2 - should be 
envisioned by the Directive. 
Missing Children Europe also supports the Directive’s provisions aimed at improving victims' access 
to information about their rights  and to better protect those among them who are particularly 
vulnerable to fall victim of crime, such as children (or) victims of organised crime. Crime victims are 
not always aware of their rights nor sufficiently supported or protected, especially in cross-border cases. 
For this reason, Missing Children Europe believes in the implementation of national victims’ support 
strategies and the adoption of comprehensive national support frameworks , effective 
communication and information sharing strategic frameworks, and safe justice systems.  
Regarding restorative justice , Missing Children Europe  highlights the importance to increase 
accessibility and availability of pertinent services and to ensure a balanced approach between the all 
actors. As stated by the Council of Europe Recommendation (Rule 15), restorative justice should not be 
designed or delivered to promote the interests of either the victim or offender ahead of the other. As a 
concept, it creates a neutral space where all parties are encouraged to express their needs to the extent 
possible. Moreover, restorative justice should be included in other relevant EU policies (e.g. child -
friendly justice, combatting hate crime, cybercrime, violent extremism, sexual and gender -based 
violence, environmental crime, child sexual abuse , etc.). Research shows that the right to access 
restorative justice services empowers victims in taking an active role and heal through internal 
conciliation; thus, the use of restorative justice processes in cases involving child victims of crimes 
and the strengthening of justice professionals capacities should be promoted . Engaging with 
children, through Child Advisory Boards, to understand their views and improve the way in which 
crimes are handled in the community are the first steps. 
 
Missing Children Europe strongly believes that  the revision of the Victims’ Rights Directive is 
connected to the general goal of enhancing child protection systems within the EU and supports the 
development and strengthening of integrated child protection initiatives. Enhancing safety measures 
tailored to the specific needs of vulnerable victims, regardless of the outcome of vulnerability 
assessments and from the first contact with a potential victim, and improving the individual assessment 
of victims’ protection needs are our principle, especially when addressing the needs of children victims 
of crime.

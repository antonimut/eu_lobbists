We welcome the opportunity to express our views on the European Commission's ambitious 
proposals to reform the EU customs union. We welcome the ambition to take the EU customs 
union to the next level in order to take advantage of the opportunities created by the 
development of digitalisation and, more generally, trade. Business and customs have a 
common interest in improving and harmonising customs processes and controls , as this 
reduces import processing costs and times, facilitates international cross -border trade and 
minimises delivery delays and unexpected costs for customers. Equally crucial are enhanced 
customs control capabilities and proactive control measures through the use of technology, as 
well as improved customs data to minimise risks to customer security, help combat customs 
and tax fraud and protect and increase revenue for EU Member States. 
However, we have significant reservations about various parts of the Commission's proposals, 
which we have summarised below: 
1. Early consultation with business and implementation timeline 
• The proposal to reform the EU Customs Union is ambitious, but many of the details of 
how it will be implemented still need to be work ed out. For the reform to deliver the 
expected results and be tailored to the realities of business and trade, continued 
consultation with industry is crucial. This public consultation is a first step, but the 
European Commission should consider setting up expert groups to inform the debate 
already during the legislative process. For example, we strongly recommend setting up 
a group of experts from marketplaces and logistics providers, focused on changes 
related to e-commerce. 
• The long deadlines foreseen ar e reasonable given the scale of the reform and may 
even need to be extended further depending on the length of the legislative process 
and further implementation work. Businesses can only start preparing and adapting 
their processes based on the final legi slation and detailed implementing rules and 
guidelines. The proposed timeline for e -commerce-related changes for 2028 may 
therefore require further extension, depending on the pace of the legislative process. 
2. Concerns about the impact of the proposed de minimis duties on businesses, EU customers 
and trade in general  
A study by Copenhagen Economics (2023) on the de minimis threshold clearly shows that the 
removal of the EU de minimis threshold will have a negative impact on businesses (especially 
small and medium -sized enterprises, SMEs), EU customers and more broadly on the EU's 
position in the global trade arena. This recent study does not take into account the EU's 
customs reform proposal (which includes some trade facilitation and simplification measures, 
as discussed below), but we believe it is important to highlight the potential negative 
consequences of removing the threshold, as we doubt that the Commission has actually taken 
them into account in its impact assessment:

• [Removing the de minimis threshold increases the burden on traders, especially 
SMEs, when selling to the EU]: The removal of the de minimis threshold will increase 
the complexity and administrative burden for traders, who rely on reduced bureaucracy 
at the border to keep costs down. This will have a disproportionate impact on SMEs, 
which have limited economies of scale to absorb these costs. The removal will act as 
a barrier for small companies based outside the EU to access the EU customer market, 
leading to higher prices and less choice for EU customers, as well as for SMEs in the 
EU who will face higher costs of supply from imports.   
• [Impact on developing countries] Many developing countries, especially those with 
smaller economies, rely heavily on low-value exports to the EU to stimulate economic 
growth and job creation. Increased costs associated with customs duties and the 
removal of de minimis aid may make it more difficult for them to compete in the EU 
market. This could potentially lead to a decline in export revenues and intensify existing 
development challenges. Furthermore, the removal may further increase the trade 
imbalance between developed and developing countries by placing a disproportionate 
burden on exporters from less economically advanced regions. In this light, the impact 
on the EU's overall immigration policy should also be considered, as this reform of 
customs policy may create less incentive for people to develop economic activities in 
their countries of origin and encourage them to seek opportunities within the EU itself. 
• [Removing the threshold reduces consumer welfare]: Ultimately, the higher trade 
barriers associated with the removal of the de minimis threshold result in a decrease 
in consumer welfare. Increased administrative burdens on importing firms, increased 
delivery times due to time-consuming border processing or limited choice are the main 
factors through which consumers may be disadvantaged. Evidence shows a positive 
correlation between de mini mis thresholds and higher logistics performance.  
Countries with higher de minimis thresholds tend to have better logistics efficiency 
(mainly due to fewer checks), which results in shorter delivery times for customers. A 
recent International Post Corporation (IPC) cross-border survey shows that speed of 
delivery is considered the reason for the most frequent complaints and the factor with 
the lowest consumer satisfaction. Clearly, the higher landed costs (processing + 
duties) caused by the removal of de minimis thresholds would result in either a loss of 
profit for the importing company or, more likely, higher prices for consumers, as 
companies are expected to pass this increase on to consumers. This would be an 
undesirable outcome in times of high inflation. 
• [Removing the threshold increases the burden on customs  authorities]: De minimis 
thresholds bring significant benefits to customs administrations in terms of costs and 
time savings. The automation of border crossing and customs clearance procedures 
has significantly reduced collection costs over the years.  However, the formalities 
involved in cross -border trade are still high and burdensome. Collecting customs 
duties on low -value goods is costly for customs as it requires additional resources, 
staff and increased time resources. Furthermore, increased complexity results from 
processes such as tariff classification or rules of origin. The proposed simplification for 
trading platforms as deemed importers is a first step towards solving this problem, but 
it needs to be elaborated in detail, and simplification should also be envisaged for other 
sales channels.  
• [Impact on the EU's position in the global trade arena]: Removing the threshold would 
have the risk of antagonising trading partners, who could see its removal as a barrier 
to trade and a form of escalating EU protectionism. This could indirectly undermine the 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
European Union's ambition to be 'one of the most outward -looking economies in the 
world' and could potentially create an incentive for other economies to back away from 
trade liberalisation or, 'at the very least', introduce similar measures that would  affect 
EU-based SMEs accessing these markets. The negative consequences of trade 
retaliatory measures do not appear to have been taken into account in the impact 
assessment of the European Commission's proposal, which estimates that the 
benefits of de minimis removal will amount to €13 billion over 15 years. 
• [The question is whether the removal of the threshold will solve the fundamental 
problem of undervaluation of customs value]: The removal of the de minimis threshold 
is presented as a solution to the p roblem of undervaluation  of customs value  by 
removing the incentive for fraudulent operators to unjustifiably declare values below 
€150. While the problem of undervaluation cannot be denied, it is questionable 
whether it is due to the existence of the de m inimis threshold.  The removal of the 
threshold creates an even greater incentive for suppliers of goods with a value below 
€150 to declare a lower value, given that this will have an impact on the amount of 
duty paid. It is therefore highly doubtful that the removal of the de minimis will solve 
this problem, as dishonest operators are left with other opportunities to evade duties 
and taxes (or simply smuggle), even if it is partly compensated by the deemed importer 
scheme. 
3. The new concept of the deemed importer for customs authorities  
• [Proportionality]: We emphasise the need to ensure that market obligations are 
proportionate. Business models for cross -border e-commerce vary widely and sales 
platforms may not be involved in customs clearance or may not have access to the 
data required to calculate duties under current or proposed customs rules. Detailed 
rules on the calculation of customs duties, the determination of the origin of goods, 
valuation, as well as how to link trade data between foreign traders, sales platforms, 
EU customs authorities and consumers, are therefore absolutely necessary for the 
proposed reform to work in practice. We welcome the simplifications proposed by the 
European Commission, e.g. tariff buckets, optional rules of origin, simplified customs 
value, but these need to be further developed in close consultation with the businesses 
concerned for the revised scheme to work. Such changes include e.g.: further limiting 
the number of tariff buckets to a maximum of 3, removing or lowering the highest tariff 
bucket (currently 17%), allowing the use of tariff preferences, limiting the data transfer 
to the EU Customs Data Hub to a basic minimum (7+1 safety and security data plus  
IOSS number plus unique shipment reference number), with all other data being 
shared, transferred or provided separately. 
• [Level playing field and effective enforcement]: The rules must ensure a level playing 
field for all sales platforms, irrespective of  their location, business model, size or 
method of dispatch of goods. The current proposal states that any person involved in 
the distance s ales of goods to be imported from third countries into the customs 
territory of the Union, who is authorised to use the IOSS, is considered to be an 
importer. It can be understood that those sales platforms that are not authorised to use 
the IOSS will not be considered importers. The assumption that - pending the 
implementation of the customs reform - the IOSS authorisation will be valid for sales 
platforms, as proposed by the European Commission in the framework of the VAT 
reform in the digital age (ViDA), is a good first step to ensure a level playing field and 
effective enforcement.  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Of particular concern is ICS2 repo rting, which we understand will continue under EU 
customs reform to ensure that customs authorities receive pre-loading and arrival data 
for customs risk assessments for safety and security. However, we understand that 
not all obliged entities report in th is way. This needs to be addressed to ensure not 
only a level playing field, but also the actual application of the rules on the category of 
deemed importer, as without a reliable link between goods sold online and goods 
crossing borders (unique consignmen t identification number), the whole exercise 
cannot be de facto operationalised. 
• [IOSS needs to be improved first]: The introduction of the concept of a deemed 
importer on a sales platform will put significant pressure on the Import One Stop Shop 
system. The system is already used today by sales platforms to collect and remit VAT 
on third-party imported distance sales (and by businesses selling through their own 
sales channels). Tw o years after the launch of the IOSS system, there are still 
fundamental problems (e.g. double taxation, possibility of misuse of the IOSS number) 
and therefore it is not ready to be extended to the collection of customs duties. These 
issues need to be addressed urgently before extending it to custom duties, as this will 
only intensify existing problems.  
• [Other customs collection systems should be implemented and improved first]: The 
same applies to ICS2 and a lot of other systems at national and EU -wide level (such 
as Single Window), which are still in various phases of implementation and not fully 
implemented in all EU Member States and which may not be implemented until 2028, 
when the new systems become operational. Furthermore, even before full 
implementation, systems such as ICS2 would have to be changed to handle, for 
example, the shipment reference number, a concept that has not even entered the 
discussion phase. 
• [The opportunity for partnerships with sales platforms]: The reform is an opportunity to 
promote partnerships between sales platforms and other actors involved in cross -
border distance s ales, to share data and resources on risk management and to 
combine efforts to combat fraud and bad actors. To promote greater cooperation on 
these issues, the  EC should consider clarifying legal standards and obligations, in 
particular for deemed importers, to promote transparency and cooperation with custom 
authorities on these common objectives, and should also consider other options for 
greater facilitation, such as through enhanced AEO or Trust & Check facilitation for e-
commerce models. While customs enforcement is necessary, enforcement focused on 
e-commerce should be risk-based with clear public guidance on enforcement criteria, 
and enforcement policies should be calibrated to promote cooperation. 
4. The concept of deemed importer should not be extended to non-fiscal obligations 
Regarding non-fiscal obligations on products imported into the EU, the European Commission 
has spent the last 5 years building up the acquis on e-commerce products originating outside 
the EU. The obvious choice for these non-fiscal measures is to focus on having a 'responsible 
person' in the EU. This will now be the case for all products covered by the General Product 
Safety Regulat ion (GPSR). This approach recognises the appropriate role of platforms in 
ensuring vendor compliance through their interface and proportionate regulated obligations to 
promote compliance. EC Vice President Vestager confirmed during a public exchange with the 
European Parliament on the Digital Services Act that the EC does not place responsibility on 
sales platforms for sellers and their products. The current customs reform is a method of 
enforcing these national rules at the border and should be consistent with this approach to 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
allow third country sellers to simply comply with the rules. As part of the reform, consideration 
could be given to how to improve the professionalisation of the  'responsible persons' and to 
establish a database of these service providers for up-to-date cross-checks. 
5. Extension of VAT liability to high-value imported consignments  
Platforms that have chosen to use IOSS currently already collect and remit VAT on imported 
distance sales up to a value of EUR 150. The proposed extension of the VAT liability of 
imported consignments above EUR 150 will further ease the burden on sellers a nd improve 
the experience of EU customers for high -value consignments. However, it is crucial that the 
problems associated with the functioning of the IOSS system in its current scope (e.g. double 
taxation and risk of fraud) are addressed first, as expandi ng it will only increase the existing 
problems. 
6. EU Customs Data Hub / data sharing  
• [The need for mutual data sharing]: We stress the importance of data sharing rules, 
which must be proportionate, effectively enforceable also for non -EU companies and 
provide a level playing field. E -commerce platforms play an active role in securing 
borders against counterfeiting and unsafe products. In general, they are obliged to 
collect data and information and make it available to customs authorities in order to 
protect European consumers and reduce fraud. However, they would require clearer 
guidelines and reciprocity to ensure that data collection is targeted and meaningful and 
that it adequately addresses the issues that the customs reform proposal attempts to 
address.  
• [Data minimisation]: Data transfer to the EU Customs Data Hub should be limited to 
the basic minimum required for goods to cross borders efficiently, with all other data 
provided in a separate mode, via direct access to traders systems as proposed by the 
EC. It is equally important to ensure that only key data elements (safety and securi ty 
data plus the IOSS number and unique consignment reference number) are made 
available/through the EU Customs Data Hub when goods cross EU borders, reducing 
the amount of information that needs to be processed quickly, making the process 
work without delay and reliably.  
• [Data security]: This is extremely important for businesses and should be protected for 
all data shared with the EU Customs Data Hub. 
7. Trust and Check traders status 
• [Availability of Trust & Check trader status for SMEs]: The EC proposal provides 
insufficient trade facilitation measures for companies, in particular SMEs. Only large 
companies with sufficient resources will be able to meet the requirements of the newly 
introduced Trust & Check status, in particular the requirement t o provide real -time 
access to data. Companies unable to meet the Trust & Check Trader conditions will 
face a significant administrative burden related to standard customs procedures.

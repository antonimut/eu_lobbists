Amendments to the Waste Framework Directive and Extended Producer 
Responsibilities schemes for textiles 
A position paper from Humana LT - October 2023  
 
Humana LT supports the European Green Deal and joint efforts to fight climate and environmental crises. 
Being a circular economy company, we advocate for the proposals made by the European Commission and 
other co-legislators on extending the lifecycle of products, making them more sustainable and reusable.  
 
The recent amendments to the Waste Framework Directive, incl uding a proposal to introduce mandatory 
Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) schemes for textiles by the Commission on 5 July, is not an 
exception. In general, we support the ambition and vision of the Commission on the proposed transformation 
of the industry. 
 
We firmly believe these amendments will be crucial in encouraging manufacturers to take long -awaited steps 
and transform the industry into a more sustainable and environmentally friendly one. At the same time, we 
support joint efforts to extend the  life cycle of textile products as it brings new potential for growth to a green 
and circular economy as well as enables crucial changes in consumer consumption patterns.  
 
Nevertheless, from our vast experience and knowledge in the textiles industry, espec ially in terms of 
preparation for reuse and recycling, certain parts of the proposed amendments could be improved to fully open 
the potential of circular textiles. 
 
In our opinion, key considerations that would improve new amendments to the Waste Framework Directive 
and would support the EU's ambition for a green and circular economy are the following:  
 
● It is crucial to introduce separate targets for reuse and recycling in the textile se ctor to gain 
the best environmental, economic, and social effects from EPR schemes 
 
Reusing textiles is far superior to recycling in terms of its environmental footprint. Recycling processes followed 
by the production of new clothes use significant amounts  of energy, chemicals, water, and other resources, 
although it is less energy and resource intensive than the production of new clothes from virgin materials. The 
use of these additional resources is spared when clothes are reused rather than recycled.  
 
Moreover, prioritizing reusability would unlock new opportunities in social and economic dimensions. For 
example, to create more new green jobs all over the EU, including in the regions, increase employment 
opportunities for vulnerable social groups as a big part of sorting companies are social enterprises, and create 
a market for more affordable good quality clothes and other textile products both in the EU and globally.  
 
Even though we strongly support the path of the Commission and its willingness to make EPR schemes work 
in line with the EU waste hierarchy, we believe that separate targets for reuse and recycling are the best way 
forward to promote reuse instead of recycling and ensure that all sorting facilities aim for the maximum amount 
of reuse possible. 
 
We believe that 75% of all collected textile streams should be kept in the economy by reusing or recycling, 
and at least 50% of the stream should be reused as second -hand textile products. These targets would be 
ambitious yet realistic. From our exper ience, 75% of all collected textiles could be reused immediately after 
washing or minor refurbishment. Targets should be constantly reviewed and adjusted to the situation in the 
market. For e.g. if sorting capabilities in the EU were more developed and the  quality of clothing changes, 
targets for reuse and recycling could then also be adjusted accordingly.

● Financial allocations will be needed to expand the sorting capabilities of the EU, but they are 
not a silver bullet to increase the sorting capacities and maximize reuse  
 
Whilst Humana LT strongly supports the Commission's proposal to collect textile waste separately from other 
waste streams from 2025, it is important to highlight that current sorting capabilities in the EU are insufficient 
to manage collected textile streams sustainably. Thus, significant EU investment or blended p ublic-private 
investment will be needed to expand sorting capabilities and kick -off the sustainable functioning of EPR 
schemes in the short term. 
 
Moreover, several other issues might burden the implementation of the policy. First, different from some 
industries, the sorting process in the textile industry benefits from a detailed approach, yet it lacks the potential 
for digitalisation and automation. This means that sorting in the foreseeable future will remain to be a manual 
and thorough process. 
 
Secondly, Europe is experiencing growing tension in the labour market, and sorting facilities already face a 
challenge in attracting employees. This means that the human potential to increase sorting capacity is limited. 
In addition to that, the sorters need to w ork in a system with comprehensive knowledge of textile categories, 
possibilities to refurbish used clothes, and many more aspects. This means that the sorters must be skilled, 
and it takes months to train new employees. 
  
The only realistic option to implement proposed policies in the set timeframe would be sorting collected textiles 
outside Europe, whether original or presorted items. We agree with the intention of the Commission and that 
of other co-legislators, which is that the EU should take care of its waste. Nevertheless, with rapidly increased 
quantities of collected textiles and current capabilities, the EU cannot deliver the European Green Deal 
ambition in this area in a genuinely sustainable manner. 
 
To avoid potential problems, we encourage the Commission to undertake a study on current manual sorting 
capabilities in the EU and their compliance with estimated quantities of collected textiles from 2025. Findings 
of the study should be used to create a system with simplified exports of collected textile products to audited 
sorting facilities outside the OECD. Otherwise, the mismatch between collected clothing and sorting 
capabilities in the EU would need to be solved at the later stages of implementation .  
 
● The synergy between the Waste Framework Directive and upcoming criteria on the End -of-
Waste will be crucial to the implementation of the policy 
 
As noted in the amendments of the Waste Framework Directive, the Commission will develop sorting 
requirements as part of the harmonized Union end -of-waste criteria for reusable and recycled textiles. The 
synergy between these policy papers will be critical to the implementation of EPR schemes.  
 
It is essential that the criteria for End -of-Waste does not become a backdoor to force reusable clothes to be 
recycled by establishing unrealistic demands and requirements on textiles for reuse and include clothes with 
minor or repairable defects within its framework. It is therefore critical that the criteria include a definition for 
what constitutes ‘repairable clothes’ and recognises how clothes are sold on the global reuse market.  
 
Moreover, the End-of-Waste criteria should make it possible to move collected clothes after simple presorting 
in a mix of reusable and rec yclable clothes as goods. In our view, the way this could be done is to collect 
clothes, presort them and remove the material that is defined as waste which in our experience is a low 
percentage, and separate shoes and handbags. The leftover material (clot hing) should, by definition, not be 
'waste' anymore.  
 
. 
These clothes would then be sent as a product for sorting in preparation for reuse and, after that, sorting in 
preparation for recycling via EU -approved facilities in third countries, including non -OECD countries. These 
facilities should be obliged to report figures prepared for reuse and recycling back to the presorting entity within 
the EU. Regarding the legal basis, there is a need to define this 'semi-manufactured' mix of clothes that could 
be traded as goods between the EU and third countries with sufficient sorting. This definition should include 
at least a 2% tolerance for mistakes of defined waste. 
 
This type of process would make better economic and environmental sense. It would facilitate a much simpler 
and cheaper presorting process followed by transfer to EU -audited facilities. In the facilities, experts would 
make informed decisions on the best reuse options and the best recycling optio ns for the sorted clothes. 
Moreover, exported goods would not contain waste taken out in the presorting stage.  
 
● The Extended Producer Responsibility scheme should be shaped in a way that decreases the 
impact of textiles on the climate and environment globally 
 
In a recent report by the European Environment Agency, it was noted that there has been an increasing 
demand across Africa for affordable, used clothes from Europe. In many cases, good quality second hand 
clothing (SHC) is preferred to new items. However, the export burden placed on good quality SHC and bundled 
clothing exported for sorting in non -OECD countries has resulted in unintended consequences - an increase 
in the sale of cheap but low-quality textiles in developing countries. 
 
The Waste Fra mework Directive rightly stresses that 'most environmental impact from textiles production 
occurs in third countries, but also affects the EU due to global climate and environmental impact.’ Whilst recent 
research shows that textile waste in landfills acco unts for only about 0.5% of all waste, it is still important to 
create tools to tackle the textile waste issue in developing countries, especially when current trends show that 
the textile waste stream might increase due to increasing production and the use of cheap, low-quality clothing. 
This means that EPR schemes and other policies of the EU should be balanced and shaped to decrease the 
production and use of low-quality textile products globally and promote the reuse of textile products worldwide.  
 
In a ddition, the Commission, together with Member States and EPR organisations, based on multilateral 
agreements, could facilitate re -export of textile products that have reached the end of their life cycle in non -
OECD countries to recycling facilities in the EU or elsewhere. 
 
EU and / or Extended Producer Responsibility funds could also be used to set up textile collection centres for 
end-of-life textiles in developing countries, which could feed into the local up -scaling industry and sorting for 
various recycling products. This would improve the circularity for end -of-life clothes stemming from clothes 
sold as new in the local countries or imported second-hand clothes. 
 
● The Extended Producer Responsibility schemes should not include second-hand textiles 
imported from the third countries 
 
As a supporter of the development of the global reuse market, Humana LT advocates for current amendments 
to the Waste Framework Directive proposed by the Commission, which do not include used textiles imported 
from third countries in the Extended Producer Responsibility schemes. We strongly believe that it is a wise 
and balanced approach due to a few reasons. 
 
The Waste Framework Directive aims to promote reuse instead of other ways of managing waste. As the price 
of second-hand textile products is small already, additional taxation would decrease the competitiveness of 
good quality used clothes and disturb the development of both European and global reuse markets.  
Moreover, with the current sorting capacity in the European Union, the market will not be able to achieve the 
proposed circular economy targets. Hence, sufficient capabilities will have to be built in the European Union. 
Additional taxation on circular economy companies will decrease their profitability and financial capabilities to 
expand their sorting facilities.  
 
Finally, the inclusion of imported textiles in Extended Producer Responsibility schemes would not be logical 
from an environmental perspective. Production is the most harmful part of the product's lifecycle, so eco -fees 
should target producers and original sellers. As the reuse of used textiles aims to decrease pollution and brings 
more benefits from environmental perspective, it would not be fair to tax importers and sellers of second-hand 
clothes. 
 
● Citizens should be informed about the tremendous environmental benefit of reuse compared 
to recycling 
 
The proposal on the Waste Framework Directive sets out extensive instructions for Extended Producer 
Responsibility organisations regarding consumer information criteria. Nevertheless, it is essential to inform 
consumers about the reuse benefits over recycling. 
 
As already not ed in this response, reusing textile items delivers more environmental, social, and economic 
benefits than recycling. As a result, an enthusiastic advert about a producer's recycling efforts may mislead 
consumers to buy new clothes and not consider repairing or buying second-hand textile products. 
 
Concluding remarks 
 
Humana LT supports the Commission’s proposal on the Waste Framework Directive and the intention to 
disrupt the current market mechanism and linear economic model. Humana LT remains committed to sharing 
its experience and knowledge on implementing circular economy models in the textiles industry and to assist 
in defining end-of-waste criteria.

 
 
EU Soil Monitoring Law  
Feedback to draft act | October 2023 
 
On the 5 th July 2023, the European Commission unveiled its legislative proposal for a Directive 
on Soil Monitoring and Resilience (so-called Soil Monitoring Law). In line with the position paper 
“Solid grounds for agriculture: a young farmer perspecti ve on soils” published in January 2022, 
CEJA reiterates the commitment of young farmers to preserve and restore soils in the EU and 
stands ready to discuss concrete and pragmatic policy and market enablers going that direction.  
Soils across Europe are cha racterised by a diversity of biological, chemical, physical and 
pedo-climatic parameters, making the strength and richness of EU agriculture but also posing 
many challenges to policymaking  and decision -making on the farm . As a consequence, any  
policy related to soils must avoid promoting the “one size fits all” approach and give space 
to local realities.  
General thoughts  
• The Directive should put forward an adaptable soil health monitoring framework, able to 
fill data gaps as well as to match the diversity of needs and soil profiles across Europe. 
In concrete terms, it means: 
o a health check based on the multifunctionality of soils and latest scientific 
evidence, aiming to encourage sustainable soil management practices rather 
than penalising soil managers; 
o a governance centred around the principle of “local challenges, local 
solutions”, but in a realistic manner; 
o a set of concrete tools at the disposal of young farmers, including options for 
financial support, enabling land planning policies, access to justice in relation to 
land take cases  and clearer synergies with other tools currently discussed , 
particularly the carbon removals certification. 
 
Positive developments  
• More data on soils – The proposed monitoring framework would allow to fill significant 
data gaps and facilitate decision-making at farm level, which should be the first step of 
any policy framework on soils. To reinforce the uptake of sustainable soil management 
practices, young farmers need to be aware of their local realities and able to assess their 
own soils in the first place. On the operational side, it requires free access to soil testing, 
independent advisory services and the multiplication of peer-to-peer learning initiatives, 
always in the respect of data ownership and privacy. The proposition to build synergies 
with programmes such as Horizon Europe or LIFE is also very positive to avoid ending up 
with overlapping data streams.

• Measures for land take monitoring and mitigation – Addressing land take (Article 11) 
is a positive signal given to the agricultural sector, both in terms of its capacity to deliver 
ecosystem services and in terms of access to land for young farmers. Public authorities 
must commit  legally to preserving agricultural land in the face of urbanisation, 
artificialisation and other industrial activities. CEJA would have wished the Directive to 
align with the ambition of the EU Soil Strategy of ‘no net land take by  2050’. Article 11 
constitutes a solid basis for further work on preventing and mitigating such phenomenon. 
Areas for improvement  
• Methodology for health assessment – By opting for a system “one out, all out” in soil 
health criteria for descriptors of the categories A and B (i.e., if a soil scores ‘unhealthy’ in 
one category, the soil is considered unhealthy across the board), the proposal for a 
Directive requires farmers to optimise their performance in every single one of the soil 
functions. Such system is biased for several reasons: 
o It is scientifically complex for one soil to achieve top performance in every one 
of the soil functions, as demonstrated in recent years by major Horizon 2020 and 
Horizon Europe projects (e.g., LANDMARK, BENCHMARKS) . M ore specifically, 
improving one soil function can have trade-offs for others, making it necessary to 
establish priorities at the level of the plot; 
o It doesn’t have any risk-based dimension, although soils are also affected by 
changes independent of the farmer’s willingness, such as trade-offs between soil 
functions or climate adverse events;   
o It is susceptible to send a negative signal  to those soil managers trying to 
implement change on the ground. From a young farmer perspective, the 
proposal does not take into consideration soil health history and legacy , 
particularly in those cases when young farmers just set up on the land.  
 
• Soil d istricts – Besides lacking clarity in the proposal made by the European 
Commission, soil districts risk to overly simplify the otherwise complex diversity of soils 
in Europe.  While it is understood that  it would create  some kind of benchmarking 
between sim ilar districts , defining districts as  homogenous areas seems to be a 
superficial approach. It might also lead to soil districts of very different sizes  from one 
country to another . As seen in other legislative frameworks, the use of districts has 
conducted certain Member States to choose the least constraining option, with entire 
countries considered one district. This seems at odds with the realities of farms, where 
several soil types can co-exist.  
 
• List of sustainable soil management principles in Annexe III – Listing such principles 
also seems to be lacking compatibility with the principle of an adaptable framework. 
Given the diversity of soil types on farms, and the pace of innova tion in sustainable soil 
management practices, requiring a national law to list what is considered sustainable 
entails the risk of not fitting to and lagging behind local realities. 
 
• Voluntary soil health certificate – CEJA calls for a removal of the provision related to 
voluntary soil health certificates in the absence of a proper assessment, particularly 
when it comes to its impacts on land markets. While it would potentially bring an added 
value to those already owning la nd, the increase in prices would directly affect young 
farmers who already struggle to access this important working tool. Such certificate 
would also rather benefit landowners than soil managers, knowing that about half of the 
utilised agricultural area ( UAA) in the Union is currently under leasing contracts . It is 
therefore essential to measure the risk of capitalisation of such certificates.  
 
• Tools surrounding the Directive  – Finally, for the Directive and subsequent national 
laws to  properly work out on the ground, certain tools are needed to support young 
farmers in their soil health endeavour, including: 
o Options for financial support:  the Staff Working Document proposed by the 
European Commission and listing all funds available to support soil health is a 
positive instrument, which will need to be replicated at national level . It is 
fundamental to get away from the thought that the Common Agriculture Policy 
(CAP) can support  the magnitude of investments required from so il managers 
themselves to restore soils across Europe;  
o Enabling land planning policies: sustainable soil management will not work out 
without long -term access to land and strong objectives of  preservation of the  
agricultural land. Article 11 on land take mitigation should also be followed up 
with the uptake of clear measures at national and regional levels; 
o Access to justice for farmers : as Article 21 puts forward principles for the 
access to justice of civil society, farmers should also be entitled to raise cases 
related to land take without sufficient compensation or soil contamination by 
nearby industries;   
o Clearer synergies with other policies:  
▪ Common Agriculture Policy (CAP): It is necessary to make the proposed 
Directive complementary with the CAP by aligning the tools envisaged in 
the proposal with those already provid ed in the agricultural policy, e.g., 
eco-schemes, enhanced conditionality. It is of paramount importance to 
avoid overlapping rules, as it would only add administrative burden to the 
current system.  
▪ Carbon removal certification: Similarly, rather than creating a new 
certificate, the draft proposal should explain more clearly  synergies with 
the currently discussed proposal for a Regulation on an EU certification 
for carbon removals.

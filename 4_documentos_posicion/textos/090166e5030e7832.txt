Stora Enso is a renewable materials company that

a total area of approximately 2 million hectares. In the 
EU, we own forest land in Sweden, and have shares

Sustainable forest management is at the heart of our 
business, and maintaining and enhancing the 
capacity of soil to provide its ecosystem services is 
one of its key principles. We take this responsibility 
seriously. Without healthy and resilient soils, we 
would not be able to secure the vitality and growth of 
forests and, consequently, deliver renewable 
products that store carbon and provide low -carbon 
alternatives to fossil-based materials. 
 
Need for a n ambitious EU framework, which 
enables localised action. 
We fully support the EU ambition to achieve healthy 
soils across the EU  by 2050 and view the proposed 
Directive on Soil Monitoring and Resilience  as a 
positive step in th is direction. We welcome the fact 
that priority is given to the establishment of a common 
soil monitoring framework  to improve the availability 
of robust and reliable data on soils. This will be a key 
first step towards obtaining an adequate picture of the 
condition of soils throughout the EU  and will be 
instrumental in informing effective policymaking.  
 
We also applaud the recognition that soil conditions 
and uses vary greatly across the EU . Consequently, 
the proposal rightly concludes that Member States 
are best placed to identify and implement the right 
measures and practices that are tailored to the 
specific situation, type, use, and condition of the soil, 
local, climatic and environmental conditions, and the 
history of land use for each landscape.  
 
However, we also see room for improvement in some 
parts of the proposed text and would like to ask  the 
legislators to consider our recommendations as the 
legislative work on this Directive continues. 
 
 
 
 
 
The determination by Member States of what does 
and does not constitute a sustainable soil 
management practice m ust be site-specific, 
informed by a prior soil assessment, and based 
on scientific evidence. 
We believe that the definition  by Member States of 
what does and does not constitute a sustainable soil 
management or regeneration practice, as stipulated 
in Article 10, is a crucial aspect of the proposed 
Directive. As this action will be the determining factor 
for the future health of EU soils, Member States must 
be equipped with accurate data, comprehensive 
information, and appropriate tools  to be able to 
identify and implement the right  measures. It is 
therefore imperative that this process be underpinned 
by several key elements. The process should 
therefore: 
 
- Be site-specific. The heterogeneity of soils and 
local conditions demands a site-specific approach. 
Attempting to prescribe one size fits all policies or 
practices would be inherently ineffective a nd lead 
to serious unintended negative consequences.  
Different locations  and ecosystems, at different 
times and for different purposes , will require  
distinct approaches . While s ome practices (i.e., 
avoiding leaving soil bare) might be needed and 
beneficial in one place and in certain ecosystems, 
they might yield unfavourable outcomes in others.

- Be i nformed by the outcomes of a soil 
assessment. Conducting a soil assessment is an 
indispensable preliminary step to provide 
policymakers with  the necessary information to 
identify any soil health issue and comprehend the 
different factors influencing its condition . Without 
the resulting  data, it will not be possible  to 
determine the most appropriate  and effective  
course of action. 
 
- Be based on scientific evidence. Any decision-
making on soils should be grounded in relevant, 
commonly agreed scientific knowledge. While 
much interdisciplinary research on soil  and soil 
health is already available, building further 
knowledge, i.e. , on soil carbon,  and a data 
repository, would be highly beneficial to support 
the objectives of this Directive . We therefore 
welcome the additional EU funding announced as 
part of the EU Mission ‘A Soil Deal for Europe'1. 
 
- Consult with landowners and managers. Given 
their extensive on-the-ground knowledge and 
experience in soil management , landowners and 
managers, such as farmers and  forest owners , 
offer invaluable insights that would ensure that any 
measure taken is tailored to the specific soil 
needs. Involving them in this process not only 
ensures that decisions are rooted in practical 
knowhow but also engenders a sense of 
ownership and support for the measures taken as 
a result. 
 
- Take into account the  relevant soil 
management principles  listed in Annex III . 
However, while the proposed principles are 
undeniably robust, they are not universally 
applicable. Member States should therefore 
respect, when defining sustainable soil 
management and regeneration practices, only 
those principles t hat are of relevance for local 
contexts and site specificities.

The proposed definitions need to be accurate, 
clear and c onsistent with established EU 
legislation and globally recognised terminology. 
Every new legislative framework needs to rely on 
robust definitions to ensure legal certainty. However, 
some of the proposed definitions within Article 3 of 
this proposal fall sh ort of being clear, accurate, or 
aligned with established EU legislation and globally 
recognised terminology.  
 
Specifically, the definition of ecosystem services fails 
to capture the entirety of their contributions to human 
well-being and societal functions. It only mentions  
indirect benefits, neglecting the crucial direct 
contributions such as food, water or wood. Rectifying 
this omission is essential for a comprehensive and 
accurate representation of the multifaceted 
advantages that ecosystems offer. This would not 
only harmoni se the definition with established EU 
legal terminology 2 but also align  it with the UN 
Convention on Biological Diversity (1992)3. 
 
In addition, sustainable soil management  is defined 
as a soil management practice that is not detrimental 
to other properties of the environment . However, the 
term ‘detrimental’ introduces a subjective element 
that may lead to varying interpretations. Moreover, it 
is often unav oidable for many sustainable soil 
management practices, aimed at maintaining and 
enhancing soil’s ecosystem services, to have an 
impact, albeit temporary and reversible, on ‘other 
properties of the environment ’. Considering these 
nuances, and to ensure leg al clarity , the word 
‘detrimental’ should be replaced by ‘causing 
significant (or irreversible) damage’.

Optimising soil measurement s by using existing 
national tools can alleviate the administrative and 
economic burden, without compromising the 
quality and availability of data. 
It is important to acknowledge that the new s oil 
measurement obligations, introduced by Article 8, 
while necessary, will inevitably create a considerable 
administrative and economic burden . Moreover, 
many Member States have already established their 
national soil monitoring frameworks  and have been  
conducting regular measurements as part of their 
national inventories. 
 
Thus, r ather than mandating a fixed five -year 
measurement cycle, the Directive could instead 
propose a more flexible system, allowing Member 
States to maintain their existing systems or tailor the 
timing to the specific circumstances, provided the 
overarching objective of having regularly updated and 
accurate information on soil condition  across the EU  
remains intact. Such an approach would promise  
substantial gains in terms of cost and time efficiency, 
eliminating redundancies and streamlining efforts , 
without compromising the quality and availability of 
data. 
 
The suggested five-year measurement interval could 
also be seen as unnecessarily excessive  from the 
perspective of forested areas, where changes to soil 
occur at a very slow pace over extended periods of 
time. However, in other ecosystems the degradation 
may happen very fast, which is why a more flexible 
approach would be preferable. 
 
Conclusion: Fostering exchange of best practice 
and collaboration will be key to ensure success 
The proposed Directive on Soil Monitoring and 
Resilience marks an important milestone  towards 
establishing the much -needed common soil 
monitoring framework, as well as enabling Member 
States t o take action to enhance the health and 
resilience of their soils and further tackle 
contaminated sites . It is essential  to acknowledge, 
however, t hat this endeavo ur does not start with  a 
blank slate and other relevant legislation has a crucial 
role to play in ensuring soil health . Many effective 
actions and measures on  soil measurement and 
management have already been taken at local and 
national level s. Hence, it is important to build upon 
existing experiences and knowledge , and foster the 
exchange of best practice that transcends borders. 
 
To ensure the successful implementation of this 
Directive, close collaboration between the authorities 
and the private sector is indispensable. Actors 
involved in soil and land management have a pivotal 
role to play in achieving the objective of healthy soils 
across the EU: their expertise and resources can and 
should be leveraged to support the implementation of 
effective soil management measures. We therefore 
strongly urge policymakers to ensure that landowners 
and managers are involved in further developments 
and transposition of this critical legislation, as well as 
in the development and adoption of subsequent 
secondary acts.

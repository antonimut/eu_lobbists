   Response	to	the	EU	Waste	Framework	Directive	The	EU	Waste	Framework	Directive's	(WFD)	intentions	are	commendable;	however,	the	revision’s	focus	on	waste	prevention	is	too	narrow,	missing	significant	opportunities	to	inform	and	improve	delivery	of	the	EU’s	environmental	strategies	in	the	fashion	and	textile	industry.	 Overproduction,	 overconsumption	 and	 the	 need	 to	 divert	 clothing	 from	 waste	 streams.	Globally,	we	are	producing,	consuming,	and	wasting	materials	at	an	unsustainable	rate,	and	the	fast	 fashion	 industry	 is	the	lead	 driver .	Synthetic	 ;ibres	 represent	 69%	 of	 all	 materials	 used	 in	textiles1, a	 ;igure	 grossly	 underestimated	 in	 the	European	 Commission’s	 Impact	Assessment	Report2. Between	2002	and	2015,	global	clothing	production	more	than	doubled	with	the	average	consumer	buying	60%	more	but	keeping	each	garment	for	half	as	long.3	Research	suggests	some	garments	are	discarded	after	7	to	8	uses4,	indicative	of	the	mindset	around	‘disposable’	garments	and	fast	fashion’s	links	to	high	product	turnover ,	low	product	quality,	and	short	lifespans.			Increased	 durability	 in	 clothing	 does	 not	 necessarily	 lead	 to	 reduced	 production	 or	consumption.	The	primary	driver	for	purchasing	new	clothing	in	the	EU	is	not	the	wear	and	tear	of	existing	items.	In	fact,	research	indicates	that	only	a	minority	of	clothing	purchases	are	made	to	replace	worn-out	items.	Evidence	for	this	lies	at	the	heart	of	the	fast	fashion	business	model,	which	is	dominated	by	synthetic	garments.	The	fact	that	cheap	synthetic	clothing	has	been	the	enabler	of	 fast	 fashion5,	yet	 it	 has	 the	 highest	 physical	 durability,	underscores	 the	 importance	 of	 not	relying	primarily	on	physical	durability	as	a	means	of	extending	clothing	lifetime.		Improving	textiles	legislation	through	a	better	understanding	of	the	clothing	use	phase.	Instead,	a	comprehensive	understanding	of	how	long	a	garment	is	kept	in	wardrobes	and	how	many	times	it’s	worn	is	vital	to	achieve	the	EU’s	ambition	to	put	“fast	fashion	out	of	fashion”.	This	is	paramount	for	understanding	the	longevity	and	utilization	rate	of	clothing	items.	Such	metrics	can	serve	as	a	foundation	to	set	clear	policy	targets	for	sustainable	development,	encompassing	waste	volumes,	textile	usability,	and	age.		Importance	of	waste	audits	and	comprehensive	data	collection.	Data	generated	at	products’	end-of-life	can	better	inform	duration	of	service	(DoS)	predictions	for	new	products.	Legislating	to	put	the	year	garments	are	produced	on	clothing	labels	will	enable	the	identification	of	‘real-life’	DoS	as	clothing	comes	through	waste	streams.	This	will	improve	and	facilitate	the	assessment	of	an	expected	duration	of	service	for	similar	new	products	coming	onto	the	market.	This	knowledge	will	allow	for	more	effective	ecodesign	rules	to	be	developed	that	truly	extend	clothing	lifetime	and	help	overcome	the	misunderstanding	that	physical	durability,	especially	in	synthetic-based	garments,	leads	to	longer	clothing	lifespans. Clothing	 waste	streams	audits	are	essential	and	 much	 more	 effective	to	 derive	 realistic	 DoS	metrics,	 whilst	 also	meeting	the	WFD’s	call	 for	 robust	 data.	The	 use	 of	waste	 audits	for	 data	acquisition	is	also	crucial	for	the	WFD’s	alignment	with	other	EU	strategies	and	directives.				Successful	waste	audits	require	high	quality	data.	Vital	information	must	be	captured	on	clothing	labels	(and	 later	Digital	 Product	 Passports	 (DPP)	to	enable	this	 quality	 data	collection	and	improve	the	delivery	 of	the	 Circular	 Economy	 Action	 Plan,	including	year	 of	 production,	subcategory	type,	raw	material	content,	size	and	brand	name.	This	data	can	then	be	collected	in	waste	audits	through	artificial	intelligence	and	photo	recognition,	with	the	introduction	of	DPPs	enabling	much	simpler	data	collection	at	lower	cost.			 Addressing	fast	 fashion	 and	 overproduction	through	 a	targeted	 EPR.	The	WFD	does	 not	adequately	tackle	the	core	issue	of	overproduction	in	the	clothing	sector.	The	quantities	produced	globally	far	surpass	the	population's	potential	to	wear	them	out,	making	many	Circular	Economy	tools	 ineffective.	Extended	 Producer	 Responsibility	 (EPR)	lacks	 a	 focus	on	 waste	 prevention,	prohibiting	it	from	meaningfully	reducing	overproduction.	The	current	eco-modulation	system,	which	 determines	 fees	 based	 on	 the	 weight	 of	 textile	 products,	 inadvertently	 favours	lighter	synthetic	textiles	over	natural	fibres,	ignoring	the	fact	that	garments	made	from	natural	fibres	are	longer	 lived	 in	wardrobes,	 more	 readily	 recyclable	 and	 biodegradable.	This	 weight-centric	approach	does	not	adequately	incentivize	recyclability	or	the	production	of	clothing	that	is	kept	in	 use	 for	 longer,	 nor	 does	 it	reflect	 the	 genuine	 environmental	 footprint	 of	 textiles	 from	production	to	disposal.		This	can	be	rectified	by	eco-modulating	the	fee	placed	on	producers	based	on	waste	audit	data,	including	the	quantity,	DoS	and	recycling	costs	of	textiles	according	to	the	EU	waste	hierarchy.	By	using	 a	 now	 proven	 waste	audit	method	 for	 fee	 modulation6,	the	 twin	 goals	 of	 ensuring	 waste	reduction	while	putting	fast	fashion	out	of	fashion	can	be	achieved.	The	EPR	fee	must	be	set	high	enough	to	positively	influence	brand	behaviour.	By	making	it	unpro;itable	to	place	goods	on	the	market	which	are	discarded	after	little	use	or	due	to	poor	quality,	and	which	are	expensive	to	treat	as	waste,	changes	to	the	fast-fashion	business	model	can	;inally	be	achieved.	 Recommendation. The	 significant	 potential	of	 the	 WFD	to	 improve	 delivery	 of	a	 suite	 of	EU	environmental	strategies	cannot	be	realized	with	its	current	narrow	focus.	Instead	of	relying	on	insufficient	evidence	and	assumptions,	the	EU	must	ensure	appropriate	information	is	captured	on	clothing	labels	and	use	the	data	from	clothing	waste	stream	audits	to	inform	textile	policies.	WFD	can	use	data	from	waste	streams	as	a	determinant	for	EPR	fees,	instead	of	an	over-reliance	on	 sales	 data.	 This	 approach	 would	 not	 only	 provide	 more	 accurate	 data	 but	 also	 significantly	reduce	administrative	burdens.

DIGITALEUROPE’s Response to the 
Revision of the Union Customs Code  
 
 
 Executive summary 
DIGITALEUROPE supports the objectives of this ambitious reform and the aim 
of modernising the Union Customs Code in the wake of structural changes 
induced by the digitalisation of trade and of business models. We also welcome 
the proposed improvements for a coherent and uniform interpretation of the 
Code, as well as reformed mechanisms striving for enhanced trade facilitation 
for economic operators.  
This paper serves as the first generic feedback on several aspects of the reform 
relevant to DIGITALEUROPE members, while the Association will still be 
collaborating closely with the Institutions and business partners on the details 
of the proposal during the legislative process ahead to ensure that the new 
UCC is better adapted to the realities and needs of business, with specific 
attention to the digital sector. 
 Need for early and continued consultation with business and lead time 
The EU Customs reform proposal is ambitious, but many details on how the 
reform will be operationalised still need to be worked out. To ensure the reform 
meets its desired results and is adapted to the realities of business and trade, 
continued consultation with industry is key. This public consultation is a first 
step, but the EU Commission should consider  setting up expert groups to 
inform the debate already during the legislative process. For example, we 
would highly recommend setting up an expert group with m arketplaces and 
logistics service providers, focused on the e-commerce-related changes.  
 Timeline 
The long timelines foreseen are justified given the scale of the reform and might 
even need further extension depending on the duration of the legislative 
process and further implementation work. Businesses can only start preparing 
and adapting their proce sses based on final legislation and detailed 
implementing legislation and guidelines. In addition, learning from the delayed 
implementation of the 2021 E-commerce VAT package, sufficient lead time is 
also needed to adapt customs systems and processes. The proposed timeline 
of 2028 for the e-commerce-related changes might therefore even need further 
extension, depending on the speed of the legislative process.

 Trust and Checked Trader 
 
The new Trust and Check Trader status builds on existing AEO program and 
aims at delivering a series of enhanced simplifications for economic operators 
against increased and real-time access to the operator’s customs, commercial, 
accounting and transport records and systems. While we strongly support the 
aim of simplification, we believe that the foreseen timeline for the 
implementation of the Trusted and Checked Traders (as linked to the 
deployment of the Data Hub from 2032) is too much of a stretch (by 20 37).  
Some elements of the new system, in particular self -assessment, can be 
deployed earlier for the benefit of traders, especially in consideration of the fact 
that self-assessment already exists under the current UCC but is never fully 
enjoyed by operators. 
In addition, the Trust and Check Trader status is only accessible if an operator 
is resident or registered in the EU. Under the current and future legislation, 
companies that are not registered in the EU cannot apply for trade facilitation. 
The same applies for the importer of record. As trade is global, EU Institutions 
should investigate possibilities for a “trusted status” and importer of record 
status for non -EU registered entities as the import of non -EU companies is 
responsible for a big part of the traders. The trusted trader might create a trade 
barrier for these companies for doing business in the EU. Mutual recognition is 
only possible for safety and security (AEOS). Legitimate businesses should be 
offered a similar status. One suggestion could be to unlock mutual recognition 
for customs (AEOC). 
 
Customs Data Hub 
DIGITALEUROPE welcomes and supports the establishment of the EU  
Customs Data Hub. We believe the objective of a coherent and uniform 
digitalisation is fundamental to overcoming existing procedural fragmentation 
across the Union and delivering simplification for businesses. With this in mind, 
we believe that more can be done to reduce the timeline for a full deployment 
of the Data Hub. 
We think it is an important and useful initiative, but it will have to be carefully 
thought through to take into account any local Member State 
requirements/recommendations. It is the only way to effectively simplify/unify 
clearance requirements across Member States. 
Moreover, in a fully -fledged EU customs information environment digitization 
requires Business Continuity Processes (BCP) in case IT customs systems 
fails. At the moment, the operational responsibilities/availability of Member 
States’ electronics systems ( BCP) for the trader is the responsibility of the

different MS. The European Commission should take the lead in “EU “ensuring 
safeguards that traders can leverage (BCP) to deliver a “true Customs Union 
acting as one.” 
We take note of the new definitions in the proposal, and we fully support the 
effort for legal certainty in the application of EU customs law. However, we are 
of the view that such definitions, in particular , those of “exporter”, “importer” 
and “deemed importer”, need to be treated with great care and should align 
with relevant terms used in other areas of EU legislation, so that there is no 
incompatible impact on the application of the Union Customs Code and the 
obligations corresponding to these roles (e.g., the Dual-Use Regulation, digital 
services act or product-related legislation).  
Last but not least, as far as information sharing is concerned, in art. 25 & 61, 
the proposal requests real-time commercial information to be shared by Trust 
and Check Traders in the Data Hub. While we understand the intention of the 
provision, we also believe that such obligation can eventually be of problematic 
application in practice, even with the best cyber security in place, and taking 
trade secrets and business confidentiality into account. 
 
Data sharing - EU Customs Data Hub 
Our other key concerns regarding data sharing are:  
 [Need for reciprocal data sharing]:  We highlight the importance of 
data-sharing provisions which are proportionate, effectively enforceable 
also against non -EU businesses and ensure a level playing field. 
Marketplaces play an active role in securing borders against 
counterfeits and unsafe p roducts. In general, they are committed to 
collecting and sharing data and information with customs authorities 
with the aim of protecting European consumers and reducing fraud. 
However, they would require clearer guidelines and reciprocity to 
ensure the data collection is targeted and meaningful and adequately 
addresses the issues the Customs reform proposal is trying to address.  
 [Data minimization]: The data submission to the EU Data Hub shall 
be limited to the core minimum required for smooth entry through the 
borders, with all other data provided in a separate mode, through direct 
access to traders’ systems as per the Customs proposal. Equally 
important is to ensure that only the crucial data elements (safety and 
security data plus IOSS number plus unique consignment reference) 
are shared with/through the EU Data Hub at the moment the goods are 
crossing the EU borders limiting the volume of information to be swiftly 
processed, so that the process works fast and reliable.  
 [Data security]: It is hugely important for businesses and this needs to 
be safeguarded for all data shared with the EU Customs Data Hub.

 [Harmonized data sharing and documentation requirements ]: the 
data sharing mechanism and any documentary requirements need to 
be fully harmonized, removing the need for countries to comply with 
EU27 systems and requirements. 
DIGITALEUROPE welcomes that prohibitions and restrictions on goods 
can be justified, inter alia, on the grounds of “the protection of industrial or 
commercial property” and “goods infringing certain intellectual property 
rights,” as stated in recital 8. However, we do call for harmonizing P&R rules 
across the EU as the current divergence is one of the root causes for double 
VAT taxation on IOSS shipments which is still happening today and needs 
to be urgently addressed before the scope of the IOSS is expanded further 
(see below). 
 
Marketplace deemed importer concept for customs: 
 
 [Proportionality]: EU Customs Code shall be adherent to existing 
Union legislation and ensuring consistency with transparency and 
accountability framework under other EU -wide regulations (especially 
in such fields like market surveillance, product safety, liability, or 
sustainability). It can be a right tool to strengthen the compliance 
enforcement mechanisms currently in force through better use of data 
within existing liability schemes. We emphasize the need to ensure that 
marketplace obligations are proportional. Cross-border e -commerce 
business models vary significantly, and marketplaces may not be 
involved with customs clearance (as they may not directly carry  an 
importation) or have access to data required to calculate duties under 
either current or pr oposed customs rules. Specific rules on customs 
duty calculation, determination of origin of goods, valuation, as well as 
ways to connect trade data between foreign sellers, marketplaces, EU 
customs and consumers, are therefore absolutely crucial to ensure the 
proposed reform works in practice and that the ‘deemed importer’ 
obligations are proportional. We welcome the simplifications proposed 
by the EU Commission, incl. the tariff buckets, optional origin rules, 
simplified customs value, but these need to b e further worked out in 
close consultation with impacted businesses to make the revised 
scheme work. For example: a further limitation of the number of tariff 
buckets to maximum 3, deleting or lowering the highest tariff bucket 
(currently 17%), allowing th e use of preferential schemes in the 
solution, limiting the data submission to the EU Data Hub to core 
minimum (safety and security 7+1 data plus IOSS number plus unique 
consignment reference), with all other data accessed, submitted or 
delivered in a separate mode.

 [Level playing field and effective enforcement]:  The rules need to 
ensure a level playing field between all marketplaces regardless of their 
place of establishment, business model, size, or shipping method. The 
current customs proposal states that deemed importer is defined as any 
person involved in the distance sales of goods to be imported from third 
countries into the customs territory of the Union who is authorised to 
use the IOSS. This could be understood that these marketplaces, which 
are not authorized to use the IOSS, will not be deemed importers. 
Assuming that – by the time the customs reform is implemented – the 
IOSS authorization will be mandated for marketplaces, as proposed by 
the EU Commission under the VAT in the Digital Age reform, is a good 
first step to ensure a level playing field and effective enforcement. 
 [Need for improvement of IOSS first]: The introduction of the deemed 
importer concept for marketplace will put considerable pressure on the 
Import One Stop Shop system. The system is today already used by 
marketplaces to collect and remit VAT on third-party imported distance 
sales (and by busines ses selling through their own sales channels). 
Two years after its go -live, there are still fundamental issues with the 
IOSS system (e.g., double taxation, potential for IOSS number misuse) 
and is therefore not ready to expand to cover the collection of duties. 
These issues need to be urgently addressed to expand it to customs 
duties as this will only exacerbate existing problems.  
 [Need to implement and improve other customs data intake 
systems first]:  The same  applies to ICS2 and a plethora of other 
systems at national and EU-wide levels (like the Single Window), which 
are still under various deployment phases, not fully implemented across 
the EU, and which may not be implemented by 2028 when the new 
systems would start kicking in. I n addition, even before full 
implementation, systems like ICS2 would need to be amended to – for 
example – provide for the possibility to convey the unique consignment 
reference number, a concept which has not even entered a discussion 
phase.  
 [Opportunity for partnership with marketplaces]: These reforms are 
an opportunity to promote more of a partnership between marketplaces 
and other actors involved in cross -border distance sales, to share risk 
management data and resources, and to combine efforts to combat 
fraud and bad actors. To promote  more collaboration on these points, 
the EC should consider clarifying legal standards and responsibilities, 
particularly for Deemed Importers, to promote transparency and 
cooperation with Customs authorities towards those shared goals, and 
should also consider other opportunities for greater facilitation, such as 
through enhanced AEO or Trust & Check facilitation benefits for e -
commerce models. While customs enforcement is necessary, e -
commerce-focused enforcement should be risk-based with clear public

 De-minimis issue 
The abolition of the 150 Euros de-minimis threshold raised diverging opinions 
within the business community, not least within the ICT and digital sectors.  
On one hand, some DIGITALEUROPE members support the Commission’s 
proposal to abolish the threshold: 
 The existence of the threshold contributes to the importation and 
placement on the internal market of counterfeit goods, which not only 
infringe on the intellectual property of those operators (with related 
economic damages) but also constitute a safety is sue for consumers. 
Undervaluation is also a problem attributed to the existence of the de -
minimis threshold. 
 By not having a threshold, customs would rely on a method that can 
work, that is, stopping a certain % of imports based on statistics/profiling 
(e.g., 2 or 3%) rather than declared value, as is done today at container 
ports. In the view of some DIGITALEUROPE’s members, this would be 
a more dissuasive method and would ensure that there are higher 
chances of counterfeit products being stopped. 
 A value-based approach to not stop fakes is not consistent with the fact 
that all counterfeit products are illegal. The manufacturing, supply and 
trade of these goods are considered a criminal office under EU and 
international law, regardless of the value of the product.  
On the other hand, other DIGITALEUROPE members are not in favour of the 
Commission’s proposal to abolish the threshold: 
 The removal of de-minimis may increase complexity and administrative 
burdens for small and medium economic operators (SMEs) who rely on 
reduced red tape at the border to keep costs low. It would affect not 
only EU SMEs but also those from developing countries, particularly 
with smaller economies, and thus contradict general EU trade and 
immigration policies. 
 Countries with higher de minimis thresholds tend to have better logistics 
performance (due mainly to reduced clearance processing), which 
translates into shorter delivery times for customers.  
 There is no evidence that undervaluation is triggered by the threshold; 
some of DIGITALEUROPE’s members believe that actually undervaluation 
will be even more pronounced with removal of de-minimis, as increased 
duty rates would provide additional incentives for it.

The policy challenge for the legislators is to find a solution which would reduce 
the scope for misuse and at the same time ensure the benefits of trade 
facilitation. Several options, not excluding others, are possible: 
 Maintain the current system. 
 Fully remove the threshold. 
 Lowering the threshold or providing for a flat duty rate system and using 
new control tools such as the use of technologies like AI to scan against 
potential infringements. 
All members support the implementation of the proposed trade facilitation 
measures and simplifications to eliminate as much as possible red tape and 
smooth-out logistics.  
 EU Customs Authority 
DIGITALEUROPE strongly support the creation of an EU Customs Authority 
with the aim of assisting in the simplification endeavour and ensuring a uniform 
application of the reformed UCC across the Union. In particular, amongst the 
tasks of the Authority, we welcome the inclusion of “ providing support and 
expertise to the Commission for the resolution of complex classification, 
valuation, and origin cases, and monitoring of decisions and the application of 
the decisions in this regard” as referred to in Art. 2 08. We further encourage 
the Institutions to consolidate, within the Authority, wider powers, and quicker 
mechanisms for a resolution of classification controversies across the Union, 
considering the fast technological advancement of goods traded by the digital 
and ICT industry.

About DIGITALEUROPE 
DIGITALEUROPE is the leading trade association representing digitally transforming industries in 
Europe. We stand for a regulatory environment that enables European businesses and citizens to 
prosper from digital technologies. We wish Europe to grow, attract, and sustain the world’s best digital 
talents and technology companies. Together with our members, we shape the industry policy positions 
on all relevant legislative matters and contribute to the development and implementation of relevant EU 
policies, as w ell as international policies that have an impact on Europe's digital economy. Our 
membership represents over 45,000 businesses who operate and invest in Europe. It includes 105 
corporations which are global leaders in their field of activity, as well as 41 national trade associations 
from across Europe.

 
  
Our views on 
The European Commission proposal for a soil monitoring and resilience 
Directive COM(2023) 416 
 
 
Bayer welcomes the European Commission proposal COM(2023) 416 for a Directive on soil monitoring. 
Soil monitoring is a crucial foundation for sustainable soil management and sustainability. Bayer believes 
this Directive can contribute to sustainable farming and remediation of contaminated sites. Soils provide 
the basis of our agricultural production systems and healthy agricultural soils not only benefit the 
production of goods and services but can deliver co-benefits such as climate change mitigation, 
adaptation, and biodiversity conservation. Managing soils sustainably, restoring degraded soils and 
remediating contaminated sites to bring all soil ecosystems in a healthy state by 2050 is a vision we can 
support.   
 
When it comes to agriculture it is our experience that soil health is top of mind for farmers. Soil is a 
crucial resource for farm productivity, affecting both the quality and quantity of farm produce. 
Managing soil health is a challenging exercise influenced by soil type, climatic conditions, market 
realities, soil ownership, and the regulatory environment. Concrete challenges for farmers are nutrient, 
soil organic carbon, water, pest, disease, and weed management as well as prevention of soil 
degradation due to erosion, compaction and salinisation. Farmers need to be able to address these 
challenges. 
 
Bayer provides farmers with tools, practices, and knowledge they can use to create integrated crop 
management (ICM) systems that deliver on social, environmental, and economic agricultural 
sustainability objectives. These tools and practices concern seeds, pesticides, digital solutions, and 
services. Bayer supports all farmers such as conventional, organic, regenerative, and conservation 
farmers.  
 
With a focus on supporting regenerative practices, such as minimal tillage, crop rotation, and cover 
crops, and the creation of carbon farming opportunities, soil health is important to our business. It is a 
key consideration when we develop products for farmers. Pesticides are tested for potential effects on 
certain soil organisms and soil functions, seeds are developed for performance under different soil 
conditions, and digital solutions offer new opportunities for farmers to manage soil health. We are 
committed to supporting farmers in reducing their greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and to reducing the 
environmental impact of our pesticides by 30% in 2030. We support farmers in the responsible use of 
our products. Pollution prevention is the standard for our production locations, and we remediate soils 
that were contaminated in the past. 
 
We appreciate the opportunity to provide feedback on the proposal. Our feedback is focused on soil 
health in relation to agriculture and remediation of contaminated sites. We hope to contribute to a 
constructive and meaningful discussion. Herewith our comments:

General comments 
 
1. Link soil health to soil function 
Soil health is interlinked with the use or function of the soil. Soils in agriculture need to be healthy but 
soils are also a means to deliver agricultural sustainability outcomes. The Directive should therefore 
recognise the agricultural function of agricultural soils by linking the proposed soil health indicators with 
the desired social, environmental, and economic agricultural sustainability performance. Sustainability 
indicators for objectives such as yield, productivity, product quality, social and economic wellbeing of 
farmers, climate change mitigation and adaptation, conservation and restoration of biodiversity, and 
conservation of water resources should be included in the Member State’s assessment on the health of 
an agricultural soil to ensure that the use of soil health indicators contributes to agricultural 
sustainability.    
 
2. Include agricultural land use 
The proposal of the Commission defines natural, semi-natural and artificial land. The category of 
agricultural productive land is missing. Agricultural productive land is an important land-use category 
delivering on agricultural sustainability objectives. Agricultural productive land or an area of land where 
the physical, chemical, and biological condition of the soil has been optimised to sustain or increase 
agricultural production with the aim to fulfil social, environmental, and economic agricultural 
sustainability objectives should therefore be included in the proposal. 
 
3. Define soil health pragmatically 
In the current proposal, a soil is healthy only when all pre-determined values of given soil health 
indicators are met. This strict  ‘one out all out approach’ does not reflect the reality of the quality and 
sustainability performance of soils under agricultural practice. Soil quality and performance are not 
black and white. This is certainly the case for agricultural productive soils, where soil health and 
agricultural sustainability performance are interlinked. A gradual score reflecting both soil health and 
sustainability performance would be more effective since it brings nuance, it enables focus on those 
soils that need most attention and it invites farmers to improve their soil health and sustainability 
performance. 
 
4. Create outcome-based sustainable soil management  
The proposal is based on the Member States drafting lists with sustainable soil management practices 
that must be implemented on the entire surface of the Member State. Lists with practices that have 
been proven under specific conditions to contribute to sustainable soil management are useful, but 
mandatory lists are not an effective and desirable way to increase the sustainability of soil management 
since:  
 
▪ The sustainability of a soil management practice depends on the quality of implementation. 
Prescribing practices does not incentivise effective implementation. It risks turning soil management 
into a tic box exercise. 
▪ Management of agricultural productive soils is more effective in the hands of the agricultural 
professional. Member States should decide for the farmer. Farmers are capable of ICM, including 
soil management, that delivers on social, environmental, and economic sustainability objectives.  
3 
 
▪ Soil management needs flexibility to align to specific farm conditions. Choices in soil management 
differ from farm to farm due to farm structure, soil type, climatic conditions, markets, and the 
farmers’ specific landscape and community context.  
▪ Innovation is crucial to improve sustainable soil management. New practices should find their way 
to EU farmers easily and not get stuck in a registration process to get on a Member State’s list.  
▪ Enhancing soil health and sustainability performance needs a diversity of public and private 
incentives. Mandatory lists limit opportunities to incentivy farmers.  
 
Instead of mandatory lists with practices, Member States should incentivise farmers to reach the desired 
combination of soil health and sustainability outcomes and leave the practice of sustainable agriculture 
in the hands of the agricultural professional. 
 
5. Ensure c ost effectiveness and transparency on soil data  
Sustainable soil management needs accurate and accessible data on soil health and farm sustainability 
performance. Currently the costs of measuring and monitoring of many soil health and farm 
sustainability indicators is a limiting factor for the necessary data generation. The current proposal could 
be strengthened by challenging the European Commission and Member States to provide cost-effective 
measuring and monitoring for those indicators where the costs are still too high. These data and existing 
data, such as data collected under the CAP or other soil health relevant data collected by Member States 
and the European Commission, should be made available to farmers and to public and private actors 
supporting farmers on sustainable soil management. With access to the right data farmers can more 
effectively strengthen their ICM systems on soil health and farm sustainability performance. 
 
6. Harmonise with room for innovation  
The proposal gives room to Member States to set values for indicators, define sustainable soil practices 
and develop monitoring. This is understandable considering the national nature of soils. However, we 
would like to stress that harmonisation across the EU is important, to prevent a patch work of different 
soil standards, monitoring and or management requirements. This could create hurdles to roll out 
sustainable soil management innovation at scale across the EU.  Harmonisation should however not 
stifle innovation in measuring and monitoring of soil health. It should not be too prescriptive regarding 
the use of monitoring methodologies blocking innovation in more effective and efficient technologies. 
Interesting novel technologies currently under development include the use of remote data, like NIR, 
MIR and satellite imagery. These technologies may be more effective and efficient compared to existing 
methods.

therefore recognise the agricultural function of agricultural soils by linking the proposed soil health 
indicators with the desired social, environmental, and economic agricultural sustainability performance. 
Sustainability indicators for objectives such as yield, productivity, product quality, social and economic 
wellbeing of farmers, climate change mitigation and adaptation, conservation and restoration of 
biodiversity, and conservation of water resources should be included in the Member State’s assessment on 
the health of an agricultural soil to ensure that the use of soil health indicators contributes to agricultural 
sustainability.  
 
An assessment of the health of a soil should not only include the values from Annex IA and B, but also the 
values for these objectives. See the proposed Annex IIIA for the social, environmental, and economic 
objectives that should be considered when determining the health of an agricultural productive soil. 
Deviations from the values of the indicators in Annex IA and B can be considered acceptable if a desired 
sustainability performance is achieved. The soil should not be considered unhealthy if this is the case.

Soil health is interlinked with the use or function of the soil. Soils in agriculture need to be managed 
sustainably but soils are also a means to deliver agricultural sustainability outcomes. The Directive should 
therefore recognise the agricultural function of agricultural soils by linking th e proposed soil health 
indicators with the desired social, environmental, and economic agricultural sustainability performance. 
Sustainability indicators for objectives such as yield, productivity, product quality, social and economic 
wellbeing of farmers, climate change mitigation and adaptation, conservation and restoration of 
biodiversity, and conservation of water resources should be included in the Member State’s assessment on 
the health of an agricultural soil to ensure that the use of soil health indicators contributes to agricultural 
sustainability. This new Annex IIIA introduces relevant agricultural sustainability objectives in the 
Commissions proposal.

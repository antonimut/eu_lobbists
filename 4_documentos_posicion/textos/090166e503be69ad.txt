Build Europe 
Response to European Commission’s call for feedback on 
Soil health – protecting, sustainably managing and restoring EU soils 
 
Build Europe, the association representing European developers and homebuilders, 
accounting for more than 60% of the EU’s homebuilding capacity, agrees with the European 
Commission on the importance of soil protection and the need for pragmatic standards to 
ensure sustainable soil use and restoration. However, it is crucial for the Soil Health Law to 
recognise the role of both brownfield and greenfield development in promoting biodiversity 
and restoring natural habitats. 
 
The Soil Health Law should provide national and regional authorities with flexible policy tools 
that consider the specific conditions and needs of their areas and populations. It is important 
to avoid implementing a uniform one-size-fits-all policy that inadvertently reduces land 
availability across the EU, thus driving up housing prices for European citizens and families, or 
difficulties in accommodating economic and industrial development in local communities.  
Singling out urbanisation as the only cause o f soil artificialisation is not in line with the 
scientific reality of soil evolution, and leads to strong opposition to many local projects 
deemed of public utility, just as Europe is committed to relocating essential industries for its 
independence. 
 
While only 0.6% of the EU's geographical space changed land use between 2000 and 2018, 
the No Net Land Take initiative has already negatively impacted the supply of developable 
land, leading to increased land prices  and development difficulties for many businesses . A 
well-balanced EU Soil Health Law should not impose new restrictions on the construction of 
new housing  or businesses, especially for Near Zero Energy Buildings or Zero Emission 
Buildings. A law on soil health must include the health of all soils, including the health of 
agricultural soils and, of course, urban soils in which a significant proportion of the soil can be 
used for urban vegetation (street trees, parks and gardens, etc.) or even  urban agriculture, 
which have an essential effect on the climate of cities, the physical and mental health of their 
inhabitants and the pleasure of living in different urban forms. Limiting land use must not be 
allowed to reduce the quality of cities and exacerbate social and urban inequalities.

Therefore, we believe that a successful Soil Health Law should recogni se that new housing 
construction can meet the demand for decent and affordable homes while contributing to 
biodiversity restoration in the urban environment through the integration of soil functions 
into the city itself . We believe that the qualitative integration of soil functions into urban 
planning is more likely to provide a solution than an arithmetical regulatory approach . Soil 
protection should not hinder the housing and employment  needs of a growing and aging 
population, which is influenced by factors such as migration flows due to war, poverty, and 
climate change. It is important to remember that land prices represent a significant portion 
(20% to 50%) of property prices, and a law that further limits available buildable land will only 
exacerbate housing affordability challenges , or imbalances in industrialisation and 
employment between countries or continents. 
 
Finally, recent developments in French regulations to limit the artificial development of land 
demonstrate the need for a sufficiently flexible law that takes into account both natural 
constraints and the ambitions of Member States  in other sectors , including past efforts to 
reduce the artificial development of land, in order to reconcile soil health with the issues of 
the economic attractiveness of regions (employment, housing, services, amenities, etc.), the 
energy transition, and the regaining of agricultural and industrial sovereignty.  Failing that, the 
law on soil health is bound to generate economic tensions, particularly over land prices, but 
also social tensions (exclusion of users from certain areas). 
 
The situation in France serves as a stark example of how a soil protection law can lead to 
unintended negative consequences without delivering its intended benefits. The No Net Land 
Take law, enacted in July to curb urbanisation, has had such adverse effects on local regions 
and the housing sector that numerous local officials, including Cristophe Rivenq, the head of 
a coalition of 72 communes in Occitanie, have openly stated their intention to disregard it, as 
well as Mr Wauquiez, President of the larger R hône Alpes Auvergne region . Even Philippe 
Sorez, the signatory of the Zero Net Land Take bill, acknowledged its shortcomings and sees 
the need for refinement. 
 
Finally, we would like to highlight the fact that defining 'land take' accurately is vital to avoid 
confusion. The proposed Soil Monitoring Directive requires Member States to establish soil 
districts based on LUCAS land use and cover data (Article 4). They must then monitor land take 
using specified indicators (Articles 6 -7). 'Land take' refers to the conversion of natural and 
semi-natural landscapes into artificial land, with terms like 'natural landscape' and 'semi -
natural landscape' also being defined. Notably, gardens and parks fal l under 'semi -natural 
landscape'. Several definitions, including 'negative land take', are in Annex I. 
 
The Directive intends to adapt the LUCAS program accordingly, with the Commission set to 
update the LUCAS program to complement national monitoring (p.5). Various departments 
have roles in adapting LUCAS to the Directive's requirements (pp. 19-20). It is essential to align 
the Eurostat concept of 'settlement area per capita' (based on LUCAS) with the Directive's 
'land take' definition, especially noting that gardens and parks are not considered as land take. 
 
We are confident that the European Commission will carefully consider these aspects when 
shaping the new Soil Health Law.

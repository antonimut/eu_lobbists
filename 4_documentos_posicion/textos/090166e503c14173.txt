Europe’s metals and minerals sector  views on the Soil Monitoring Law 
(Directive) proposal. 
Euromines is committed to protecting our valuable natural resources by prioritizing sustainable soil 
management. The mining industry is dedicated to reducing its environmental impacts and fully supports the 
European Green Deal. Soil protection remains a top priority for our industry, and we adhere to the latest 
technologies and procedures to comply with European and national legislation fr ameworks. We recognize 
the value of European soil as a precious natural resource, and we are committed to its maintenance and 
protection. 
Below are our key considerations: 
1) Regulation Reconciliation and Clarification:  
We support the need for a clarification within the Soil Monitoring Law concerning its relationship with existing 
European legislation focused on soil protection, investigation, and remediation. The proposal should ensure 
coherence between the national and EU legislation to avoid conflicting or double requirements. 
To address concerns of potential duplication and ensure regulatory coherence, we suggest including a 
regulation clarifying that specific provisions included in other EU directives should precede the future S oil 
Monitoring Law.  Such specific provisions are i.e., the Industrial Emissions Directive (Directive 2010/75/EU) and 
Mining Waste Directive (Directive 2006/21/EC), when relevant. This will help prevent conflicting requirements 
and create a unified regulatory framework for soil management. 
For instance, the Industrial Emissions Directive already includes provisions for the industrial production sites 
on soil to ensure that this resource is fully protected, and that contamination is avoided during the life of the 
permit. These provisions include background information on the current state of the soil e.g., details on former 
activities and potential contamination. However, this only gives an indication of the current physical condition 
of the soil but does not assess any risk that may be caused by any soil contamination. Hence, the data collected 
as required by the Industrial Emissions Directive (IED) could be usable for the monitoring requirements of the 
soil monitoring law. 
2) Objective (Article 1) in conjunction with Evaluation and Review (Article 24):  
In consideration of the directive's objective to bring all soils into a healthy state, a more differentiated 
approach needs to be taken those accounts for the diverse soil types and land uses across Europe.  
Moreover, considering the experiences, challenges, and unsolved issues with the EU Water Framework in 
achieving its ambitious environmental goals, we urge the inclusion of appropriate derogations possibilities in 
the upcoming EU soil monitoring law . Furthermore, we believe that these mechanisms will help tailor soil 
management to the unique conditions and requirements of different regions.

3) Definition of Soil (Article 3) - Extraction of Raw Materials: 
We consider the definition of Soil in Article 3 (1) to be very broad and it covers materials from land surface to 
bedrock, which could be tens or hundreds (or more) meters thick. 
Article 3 (1) says: "the top layer of the Earth’s crust situated between the bedrock and the land surface, which 
is composed of mineral particles, organic matter, water, air, and living organisms “.  
We strongly emphasize that the Soil Monitoring Directive should not encompass the extraction of raw 
materials. This broad definition categorizes raw materials, insofar as they align with the definition, as "soils." 
Consequently, the extraction of raw materials would be directly subject to the regulations of the Soil 
Monitoring Directive. 
This potential co-regulation of raw material extraction at the EU level leads to a conflict with the subsidiarity 
principle, as the competence to regulate mining and raw material extraction primarily rests with the EU 
Member States. Raw material deposits are inherently local, immobile, and typically not cross -border. 
Furthermore, many Member States have already established comprehensive national regulatory frameworks 
that effectively govern the extraction of raw materials while ensuring soil protection. 
The proposed EU Soil Monitoring Directive must not encroach upon the existing competence of Member 
States, particularly about the regulation of raw material extraction. To regulate the extraction of raw materials 
via the Soil Monitoring Directive and subject it to additional bureaucratic requirements would not only run 
counter to the subsidiarity principle but also conflict with  the EU's commitment to promoting domestic raw 
material extraction under the Critical Raw Materials Act. This is crucial for expedi ting approval procedures, 
enhancing investment security, and reducing the EU's reliance on raw material imports. 
Therefore, we recommend that the EU Soil Monitoring Directive explicitly state that raw material deposits 
and their extraction are exempt from its regulations to preserve the competencies of Member States and 
prevent any hindrance to the promotion of domestic raw material extraction within the EU. 
Likewise, as the Soil definition also includes water, to avoid confusion and maintain clear differentiation from 
European water legislation, we recommend making the clarification that water aspects will be regulated by 
the existing EU water policies. It is important to highlight that all waters in the EU are already subject to specific 
and comprehensive water legislation. This modification will help create a distinct and unambiguous definition 
of soil within the directive. 
4) Soil districts (Article 4): 
The proposal should better account for the natural variation in soils across the EU. 
Although the goal is to seek homogeneity within each soil district regarding soil type, climatic conditions, 
environmental zone, and land use, soil districts will never be 100% homogeneous in soil types and soil 
properties. Therefore, any thresholds for so il health indicators must still consider site -specific conditions 
(pedo-climatological, land use, …). All indicators need carefully selected reference values, considering the 
natural variety in soil types and parent materials.

5) EU Commission's Operational Sampling Tasks (Article 6):  
We share concerns regarding the EU Commission's empowerment to carry out operational sampling tasks. 
Industry input and oversight are essential to maintain the integrity of soil assessments. We strongly advocate 
for meaningful involvement of industry stake holders in the decision-making process to ensure balanced and 
scientifically rigorous soil monitoring. 
6) Assessment of Soil Health (Article 9):  
We raise concerns about the criteria for "healthy soil” and their comprehensibility. According to Article 9(2), 
soils are considered healthy if the values of all soil descriptors listed in Annex I Part A, and Part B are met. 
Conversely, the soil is already considered "unhealthy" if one of the criteria mentioned is not fulfilled based on 
a “one -out-all-out-principle” (as in the case of the assessment of the status of water bodies in the Water 
Framework Directive). Depending on the number of indicators applic able, it is challenging to comply with all 
those descriptors at the same time and the situation in which one descriptor is slightly exceeding the set value 
should not necessarily lead to the conclusion that soil is unable to provide ecosystem services. 
The directive should prioritize the establishment of a clear, risk -based approach for assessing soil health. 
Furthermore, it is crucial to consider and account for pre -existing pollution in soils, both geogenic and 
anthropogenic, as part of the directive's framework. This will enable a more accurate evaluation of soil 
conditions and the development of effective remediation strategies. 
7) Flexibility in Land Use Priorities (Article 11):  
We align with the perspective that sustainable land use is of paramount importance. Article 11, which 
addresses "no net land take" raises concerns. Member States should retain the autonomy to establish their 
own priorities for land use, as land use needs may differ significantly across regions. 
We therefore suggest that Article 11 be considered for outright deletion from the proposed amendments. The 
primary rationale behind this recommendation is that sustainable soil management should take precedence 
over the concept of "no net land take." Sustainable soil management is critical for preserving and improving 
the quality of soil, which in turn has wide-ranging ecological and economic implications. By allowing Member 
States to make autonomous decisions in this regard, the directive can better addres s the diverse 
environmental challenges they face. 
8) Risk-Based Approach (Article 12) and Public Participation (12.4):  
Euromines supports the principles of risk -based and sustainable risk management of industrial sites and 
welcomes their inclusion in the Directive.  
While we support public participation in defining and applying the risk -based approach, we recognize the 
challenges associated with extensive public involvement in identifying and investigating potentially 
contaminated sites as well as managing contaminate d soils. To address this concern, we propose the 
exploration of alternative, more streamlined approaches that still ensure public input without causing 
impractical delays or complications in the decision-making process.

9) Publication Obligations and Data Privacy (Article 19):  
We share concerns regarding the publication obligations, especially related to the "digital soil health data 
portal." Data privacy is a paramount concern, particularly when it comes to internal company data. We strongly 
advocate for the implementation of r obust safeguards to protect sensitive business information and 
intellectual property rights. The directive should ensure that data sharing is balanced with the protection of 
proprietary data. 
10) Access to Justice (Article 22):  
We acknowledge the importance of access to justice provisions within the directive. It is crucial that these 
provisions are fair and balanced, taking into consideration the impact on businesses and stakeholders. A 
transparent and equitable framework for ac cessing justice will contribute to a more effective and just 
implementation of the Soil Monitoring Directive. 
It would be advantageous to include a clear reference to the Aarhus Convention regarding access to justice 
(Article 22) within the directive. By referencing the Aarhus Convention, the directive can affirm its commitment 
to harmonizing with international agreements, reinforcing the principle that access to justice for different 
involved stakeholders is a well-established and protected right under this convention. This alignment with the 
Aarhus Convention not only ensures consistency with international legal norms but also facilitates a smoother 
and more coordinated approach to environmental justice. 
We believe that this addition would further strengthen the directive by providing clarity and consistency in its 
approach to access to justice.  
11) Annex I (Soil Descriptors and Criteria for Healthy Soil Condition):  
We critically assess the inclusion of salinization and the descriptor "electrical conductivity" in Part A. Salinised 
soil does not necessarily pose a risk to human health and the environment, it may only be restricted in its 
agricultural use. We recommend a revaluation of their relevance, ensuring that they align with the specific 
goals and objectives of the directive.  
Moreover, salt-affected areas may have greatly increased conductivity values compared to the values of the 
descriptor "conductivity" from Annex 1 for a healthy soil condition. With the exception of natural saline areas 
and areas affected by sea -level rise,  this condition is already taken into account. However, elevated salt 
concentrations also affect regions where salt is found underground and has reached the surface and the 
surrounding soils through centuries of tectonics or mining. Geogenically salinated groundwater can sometimes 
lead to salinisation of the overlying soils through evaporation and capillary rise in the soil. These cases should 
also be considered accordingly under the aspect of "salinisation". A return to a healthy state, as defined in 
Annex 1, would neither be reasonable nor proportionate for such soils. 
Additionally, we advocate for the addition of criteria related to nutrient undersupply in Parts B and C to create 
a balanced perspective. Clarity is essential, and the directive should specify the spatial unit for evaluating soil, 
facilitating a clear understanding of when a target failure occurs.

Euromines questions the added value of a minimum list of pollutants  in Part B . The contaminants to be 
considered should depend on the site -specific conditions (e.g., type of potentially polluting activities, ...). For 
naturally occurring substances such as metals, a risk-based approach is the only scientifically sound approach 
to address contamination issues, as the presence of a contaminant as such cannot be used as an indicator of 
potential problems. Thresholds must consider the effect of site -specific conditions (e.g., pH, organic matter, 
cation exchange capacity, ...) on the bioavailability and toxicity of contaminants. For metals, it is important to 
consider the spatial variability of natural background concentrations in soils when considering the 
contamination status of soil. Clear guidance should be available on how to derive the natural background 
level of naturally occurring soil contaminants to identify significant anthropogenic contributions.  For 
instance, there are already heavily polluted soils in nature such as the heavy metal turf in the Harz 
Mountains, and such geogenic background pollution must be adequately considered in the Directive. 
It is crucial to introduce relevant derogations for naturally mineral -rich areas. Failure to do so may lead to 
misleading results, as natural geological conditions can influence soil health assessments, especially in the 
context of soil contamination (Annex 1 Part B). 
12) Legal basis falls under Special Legislative Procedure 
We also wish to draw attention to the legal basis under which the EU Soil Monitoring Directive should fall. The 
Commission proposal appears to advocate for the ordinary legislative procedure under Article 192(1) of the 
Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU). However, it is evident that th e Soil Monitoring 
Directive directly impacts land use, as exemplified by Article 11 on "Land take mitigation principles." 
Pursuant to Article 192(2)(b), third indent, TFEU, the special legislative procedure is the appropriate framework 
for measures affecting land use, in which the European Parliament is consulted, and the Council decides 
unanimously. Despite the EU-COM's assertion to the contrary, without providing reasoning, it is apparent that 
the Soil Monitoring Directive significantly influences land use, thus necessitating the use of the special 
legislative procedure. 
To ensure compliance with procedural and participation rights, especially for Member States in the Council, 
and to avoid any infringement of European primary law, it is imperative that the Soil Monitoring Directive is 
adopted in accordance with the special legislative procedure. 
Conclusion 
In conclusion, we appreciate the opportunity to provide our input on the Soil Monitoring Law Proposal. Our 
organization remains committed to promoting soil health and environmental protection, and we believe that 
a well-balanced and scientifically rigorous  directive will contribute to achieving these shared goals. We look 
forward to further discussions and collaboration with the European Commission and stakeholders to ensure 
the successful development and implementation of the directive.

Soil Monitoring Law 
 
 
 
 
Introduction  
Safeguarding soil resources is vital for multiple reasons. Soil is essential for 
ecological systems, agriculture, and human well-being. It serves as the foun-
dation for sustaining life by providing essential nutrients and water to plants, 
thereby supporting global food security. Preserving soil integrity is essential 
for biodiversity conservation and ecological balance, as numerous plant and 
animal species rely on this foundational medium. Delving deeper into the 
intricacies of soil's role, it emerges as a central player in the global carbon 
cycle, efficiently sequestering carbon and significantly contributing to the re-
duction of greenhouse gas emissions, crucial for mitigating climate chang e 
impacts. Simultaneously, soil acts as a natural reservoir, proficient at storing 
and purifying water, serving as a critical defence against flooding and ensur-
ing water quality, which is of paramount importance, especially for industrial 
facilities in vulnerable areas.  
 
Soil health is inherently linked to the industry, impacting for example the 
availability and quality of raw materials. In an era marked by an increasing 
emphasis on sustainability and environmental stewardship, preserving soil 
health is integral to environmental protection. From an industrial standpoint, 
maintaining soil quality is a long -term strategy for sustainability, resource 
access, and environmental mitigation.  The German industry recognizes that 
responsible and sustainable soil resource utilization is not just an economic 
necessity but a moral obligation, ensuring that future generations inherit the 
same opportunities as the current generation. 
 
However, to navigate this terrain successfully, a judicious connection be-
tween industry and sustainability policies at both the European and national 
levels is essential. The European Commission must foster a deep understand-
ing of the unique challenges faced by European businesses in soil protection. 
Striking a balance between regulatory requirements and maintaining indus-
trial competitiveness is vital to ensure that European enterprises do not en-
counter undue disadvantages in pursuing sustainable practices.  It is of para-
mount importance that policymakers create an environment that allows a 
thriving European industry to coexist with sustainable environmental prac-
tices. Vigilance is needed to prevent new directives from inadvertently lead-
ing to protracted site assessments and extended authorization procedures that 
could hinder progress. In summary, the preservation of soil health and quality 
is a collective endeavour resonating across diverse sectors, reflecting a shared 
commitment to protect this vital natural resource.

1. Article 1: Objective and Subject matter – in conjunction with 
Article 24 (1): Evaluation and review 
The goal of restoring all soils to a healthy state must be examined in a more 
differentiated manner. This should be clearly reflected in the wording of Ar-
ticle 1. Deviation possibilities (less stringent objectives) and an appropriate 
exception regulation s hould be provided. In doing so, the experiences and 
existing challenges from the implementation of the ambitious goals of the EU 
Water Framework Directive should be adequately taken into account. 
For example, the target of achieving a healthy soil cannot apply to: 
• Contaminated sites according to Chapter IV 
• "Artificial land" according to Article 3 (16): Often a healthy soil condition 
cannot be achieved by proportionate means, this applies in particular to sites 
with anthropogenic fill materials or urban agglomerations (e.g., Ruhr area). 
In these cases, there must be exceptions to the goal of achieving a healthy 
soil status. The legal principle applies that the impossible must not be de-
manded. Otherwise, the same situation threatens as in water protection – the 
good chemical status remains unattainable (Hg problem).  
• Land that does not fall into the category of "contaminated sites", but that is 
heavily altered due to human activities – including fill material – and a re-
turn to a good condition is not possible or proportionate.  
• Locations that do not meet the criteria outlined in Annex I but do not fall 
into the category of contaminated sites . There  should be an exemption 
clause formulated for them.  
• Natural, anthropogenically unaltered soils (e. g. marshland soils in northern 
Germany) – because these can already have an "unhealthy" soil quality by 
nature. It must be made clear here that the political goal of a healthy soil 
condition cannot be striven for or achieved here. A distinction should be 
made between natural elements and contaminants or natural background 
levels on one side and anthropogenic inputs on the other. This means that 
just because, for example, chromium is present in the soil in concentrations 
above the precautionary value, this soil is not necessarily anthropogenically 
contaminated, but only originated on a chromium-rich parent rock. 
Article 24 goes beyond the objective of Article 1. The goal of a healthy state 
of all soils by 2050 is set here as a benchmark for the definition of further 
concrete requirements. Article 1, on the other hand, is formulated in a much 
more open manner regarding the objective. There are analogies to the process

2. Article 2: Scope 
It should be stipulated in the Directive that the extraction of raw materials is 
not subject to the Directive. According to Article 2 of the COM proposal, the 
scope of the EU Soil Monitoring Directive covers "all soils in the territory of 
the Member States". The COM proposal does not contain exceptions or lim-
itations to the scope. The COM proposal then defines soil in Article 3 No. 1 
as "the top layer of the Earth’s crust situated between the bedrock and the 
land surface, which is composed of mineral particles, organic matter, water, 
air and living organisms".  
This means that raw materials, insofar as the above definition applies to them, 
are also considered "soils" and would be subject to the regulations of the Di-
rective. Not only the soil material, which includes raw materials stored un-
derground, would be soil according to the new EU Directive, but also the raw 
material itself, so that the extraction of the raw material would be an activity 
directly affected by the new EU Soil Monitoring Law.  
Thus, the EU Soil Monitoring Directive would also co-regulate the extraction 
of raw materials at EU level. However, such regulation at EU level on raw 
material extraction would clearly contradict the subsidiarity principle be-
cause the competence to regulate mining and raw material extraction lies with 
the EU Member States. This is based in particular on the fact that raw material 
deposits are inherently local, immobile, and typically not cross -border. Fur-
thermore, it is noteworthy that numerous Member States have already estab-
lished specialized national regulatory frameworks that comprehensively gov-
ern and conclusively oversee the extraction of raw materials while concur-
rently safeguarding soil integrity (e.g., as exemplified by Germany's mining 
law). It is imperative that the proposed EU Soil Monitoring Law does not 
encroach upon or limit the existing jurisdictional competence of Member 
States in this regard. 
To co-regulate the extraction of raw materials via the EU Soil Monitoring 
Law and to expose it to additional bureaucratic requirements would also con-
tradict the EU's concern to use the Critical Raw Materials Act to promote the 
extraction of domestic raw materials in the EU – with a view to accelerating 
approval procedures and investment security aspects in order not to further 
worsen the EU's dependence on imports in the raw materials sector and ide-
ally to improve the security of supply of domestic raw materials in the EU.

The EU Soil Monitoring Directive should therefore stipulate that raw mate-
rial deposits – opencast mines, underground mines, excavations – and their 
extraction are not subject to the regulations of the Directive. Therefore, a cor-
responding provision should be included in the Soil Monitoring Law. 
The present Commission proposal lacks a provision with regard to the rela-
tionship with other European legislation concerning soil protection, soil in-
vestigation and soil remediation. These include, for example, the Industrial 
Emissions Directive (Directive 2010/75/EU), the EU Landfill Directive, the 
Mining Waste Directive (Directive 2006/21/EC), and the Directive on the 
Prevention of Pollution from Nitrates from Agricultural Sources (Directive 
91/676/EEC). These EU Directives are more specific and should th erefore 
take precedence over the provisions of the Soil Monitoring Law. Further-
more, duplication of regulatory measures is mitigated.  
3. Article 3: Definitions 
The following definitions require clarifications:  
• No. 1 "Soil": In the context of a distinct demarcation from European water 
legislation, it is advisable to omit the term "water." It is imperative to 
acknowledge that all bodies of water within the European Union are already 
subject to distinct and comprehensive regulat ory frameworks outlined in 
European water legislation. 
• No. 10/20/21 "Contaminated site/soil contamination/contaminants": It is 
unclear which pollutants are to be used for the assessment of "contaminated 
sites". The term "contaminants" is only mentioned in connection with or-
ganic pollutants in Annex I Part B. However, this Annex is not mentioned 
in Chapter IV.  
o Which pollutants come into consideration is relevant to the question 
of whether the systematics of German soil protection law is congru-
ent with the systematics of the categories proposed here: "poten-
tially contaminated sites ", "contaminated sites", "contaminated 
sites requiring further actions".  
o In the present draft, contaminated sites with a proven unacceptable 
risk to humans and the environment are conceptually equated with 
sites for which such a risk is merely possible. Both are defined as 
"contaminated sites". However, a corresponding distinct ion is ur-
gently needed.

• No. 15 "Semi-natural land": It is unclear whether this includes all agricul-
turally used land. Specifically, the question is how the criterion "high value 
in terms of biodiversity and the ecosystem services it provides" is inter-
preted. It is questionable whether this could mean an exclusion for inten-
sively used farmland.  
• No. 15 "semi-natural land" and No. 16 "Artificial Land": Describe very well 
the special soil conditions at industrial sites ("Brown Fields"). Due to long 
prior industrial use, conversion, filling and the consequences of two world 
wars, the affected soils have been anthropogenically altered or planned 
standards regarding compaction, biodiversity , carbon content, etc. are not 
applicable. Unfortunately, those definitions remain an "artefact" in the fur-
ther text of the Directive. The consideration of the special framework con-
ditions of such land uses in the definition of assessment and measure criteria 
is unfortunately missing here. Uses of previously unused areas ("green 
fields") should be treated differently or provided with other value standards.  
In any case, the parameters to be used for the classification of healthy/un-
healthy soils ("Soil Districts") are not congruent with the parameters for con-
taminated sites (Chapter IV).  What effect do "contaminated sites" have on 
the evaluation of the condition of a "Soil District"? 
A definition for the term "topsoil" is missing. In the Annex I, the "topsoil" 
quality is described, but there is no definition of "topsoil". It is therefore up 
to each member state to decide to what depth "topsoil" is present and there-
fore the definition of measures to be taken.  
4. Article 6: Soil health and land take monitoring framework  
Article 6 (4) empowers the EU Commission to carry out operational tasks in 
the form of sampling. We reject this power of the EU. This is an approach 
which, especially in the case of "contaminated sites" according to  Article 4, 
raises questions regarding access to private property, security etc. but also 
regarding the confidentiality of the data generated. 
Article 6 to article 8in conjunction with Article 19 - Digital Soil Health Por-
tal: It is unclear at what scale the data will be published. Parcel -related data 
should not be published here, especially if they are privately owned. Publica-
tion of information at aggregated scale levels such as Soil Health Districts or 
sub-areas would be acceptable where appropriate.

5. Article 9: Assessment of the soil health  
It is unclear to which spatial unit (soil district, sub-unit, parcel) the evaluation 
of the soil refers. Thus, it is also not clear when a target failure occurs. In 
paragraph 4, there is a reference to " areas within each soil district with un-
healthy soils." Again, there is no specification of this. At least this gives an 
indication that the Soil District is not the reference unit for target achieve-
ment. In the Water Framework Directive, the water body is the reference unit 
(and not the river basin district;  furthermore, the assessment is based on the 
principle of "representative monitoring sites").  
According to Article 9 (2), soils are considered healthy if the values of all 
soil descriptors listed in Annex I Part A and Part B are fulfilled. Conversely, 
a soil is already considered "unhealthy" if one of the criteria mentioned is not 
fulfilled.  
• Some of the criteria set out in Annex I are not comprehensible from a soil 
protection point of view. Please refer to the comments provided in Annex I 
below.  
• Additionally, it is imperative to highlight the existence of extensively con-
taminated natural soils, such as the prevalence of heavy metal -rich peat in 
the German Harz Mountains, resulting from geogenic factors. The Directive 
should duly consider and addre ss such inherent background pollution 
through the formulation of pertinent regulations. This consideration should 
extend to soil regions that have already incurred pollution, often due to his-
torical industrial operations.   
6. Article 9 (5): Soil health certification 
The requirement for Member States to establish a mechanism for a voluntary 
soil health certificate is expected to effectively transform the certificate into 
a standard practice. Such requirements would mean a high additional burden 
on land transactions due to soil data that may have to be collected first. This 
is particularly the case for transactions where such data is not required under 
the risk-based approach ( e.g., "greenfield"). Otherwise, each party can de-
mand ground data today before a transaction, or contractually regulate corre-
sponding risks with proven means. In light of this, we advocate for the re-
moval of this regulation.  
This provision may become redundant for industrial sites, considering the 
forthcoming establishment of the “Register of contaminated sites and poten-
tially contaminated sites, ” which is expected to publicly disclose all site -

related information. This development contrasts with the existing situation in 
Germany, where such data is not consistently available in the federal state 
registers of contaminated sites. The critical factor in this context will be the

The proposed "Voluntary Soil Health Certification" mechanism ( Article 9,

explicitly applies the risk-based approach and since it is also not clear who is 
to be obliged to carry out such investigations.  
Moreover, this would lead to duplication for industrial installations that im-
plement the mandatory requirements of the IED ( “baseline report”, regular 
monitoring of soil). Article 14, No. 2 only provides for the option that Mem-
ber States may use existing data and results of “soil monitoring” carried out 
on the basis of the IED – an option that is formulated far too weakly in this 
context. This applies in particular to the requirements for informing the pub-
lic (Article 19) – the baseline report from the Industrial Emissions Directive 
(IED) is explicitly excluded from the obligation to be published. Industrial 
installations subject to the IED should be excluded from the requirements for 
the proposed Voluntary Soil Health Certification in order to avoid duplication 
of regulations (subsidiarity).  
Critical in this context are also the publication obligations provided for in 
various parts of the draft and the opportunities for participation granted to 
"environmental NGOs" (Definitions, No. 19). Article 19 sets out further re-
quirements for informing the public – in particular a "digital soil health data 
portal".  
We are very concerned about the threat of publication of internal data. In this 
context, we would like to refer to the IED's exemplary regulation on the Base-
line Report on soil and groundwater, which must be submitted to the author-
ities but does not have to be published.  
Many aspects of the proposed Soil Monitoring Law, at least in Germany, are 
already covered by the Baseline Report in the authorization procedure. This 
may also apply to other EU countries and needs to be acknowledged in order 
to avoid duplications of efforts.

7. Article 12: Risk-based approach  
The risk -based approach of Chapter IV, Article 12 - 6 for "contaminated 
sites" is welcomed . The influence of the "contaminated sites" on the "soil 
districts" introduced in Article 4 remains open. The statement made in recital 
number 2 that 60 - 70% of the soils in the EU are not "healthy" by the stand-
ards applied fills us with concern. How can a "healthy" state be achieved by 
2050 (see page 3)? Article 15, "Risk Assessment and Management of con-
taminated Sites", with the introduced term "unaccepta ble risks for human 
health or the environment", only gives indications for an assessment. It is left 
to the Member States to develop such specifications (Article 15, No. 2). The 
requirement to conduct a case-by-case assessment for each site (Article 15, 
No. 3) is welcomed, as it respects the actual conditions in each individual 
case.   
• Article 12 (4): The inclusion of the public in the implementation of the risk-
based approach, including the definition of suspected contaminated sites 
and the management of contaminated sites, is a matter of critical concern. It 
is our position that such extensive public involvement, as described, is not 
advisable. Similarly, with regard to the establishment of the risk -based ap-
proach, it is essential to recognize that engaging the public in t hese pro-
cesses may not be prudent due to the inherently complex a nd highly tech-
nical nature of the issues at hand. 
8. Article 13: Identification of potentially contaminated sites - in 
conjunction with Article 16: Register  
Seven years following the enactment of the Directive, all suspected contam-
inated sites are to be identified and entered in the register pursuant to Article 
16. Whether this requirement is met by the registers of contaminated sites 
kept in the German federal states is questionable. In addition, the effects of 
this requirement for industrial sites in Germany depend on whether suspected 
contaminated sites, harmful soil changes and contaminated sites are congru-
ent with the terms "potentially contaminated sites" and "contaminated sites". 
In the most unfavourable scenario, an entire industrial site could potentially 
be classified as a suspected contaminated site. It is of utmost importance to 
emphasize that, in accordance with Article 14, the stipulation that all sus-
pected contaminated sites must be documented within seven year s necessi-
tates the conduction of soil investigations on these identified sites.

9. Article 16: Register  
The full-scale publication of the register is a matter of fundamental signifi-
cance. In the case of registers of contaminated sites in the German federal 
states, unrestricted access to individual site data is not possible. Given that 
the register encompasses private land, we oppose the idea of providing unre-
stricted public access to all data. 
10. Article 23: Penalties 
The provision of Article 23 constitutes a permissible supplement to Directive 
2008/99/EC of November 19, 2008. However, it significantly extends beyond 
the requirements of Directive 2008/99/EC, which governs the criminal pro-
tection of the environment. While this is permissible, it is advisable, for the 
sake of coherence among EU directives, to reject this aspect in one respect. 
According to Article 23 (3) (b) of the proposed Directive, Member States 
shall ensure that intentional or already negligent offences are sanctioned. 
However, Directive 2008/99/EC on environmental criminal law only sanc-
tions "intentional or at least grossly negligent" offences against the environ-
ment (according to Article 3, Recital (7) Directive 2008/99/EC). It is imper-
ative that the Directive be adapted to the effect that only those who act inten-
tionally or at most with gross negligence commit unlawful acts.  
Sanctions may only be imposed in cases of gross negligence, which means 
when the level of care required in ordinary conduct is severely breached. 
Gross negligence is generally understood to occur when, for example, even 
the simplest and most obvious consid erations are neglected, or when what 
should have been evident in the given circumstances is ignored. Sanctions 
should not come into effect solely when the required level of care is disre-
garded. Minor and thus easily excusable negligent breaches of duty are  in-
tentionally excluded from the scope of application of EU environmental 
criminal law, Directive 2008/99/EC. Moreover, proving gross negligence is 
more accessible than proving mere negligence. Particularly in the context of 
demonstrating environmental damages, the burden of proof should not be un-
duly and disproportionately placed on the potential affected party, especially 
when small and medium -sized enterprises (SMEs) might be subject to the 
sanctions provision. Consequently, Directive 2008/99/EC on envir onmental 
criminal law rightly sanctions only gross negligence. 
Infringements are only to be sanctioned if they have been committed inten-
tionally or with gross negligence. The scope of application of Article 23 is

thus to be limited to "gross negligence", which is only then coherent with 
Directive 2008/99/EC. 
Furthermore, Article 23 introduces the concept of linking the severity of a 
penalty to a company's turnover. This approach constitutes a novelty within 
environmental law. Unlike other legal domains, environmental damages are 
often quantifiable, and restora tion is to some extent feasible. Therefore, in 
environmental law, there is no compelling rationale for tethering penalties to 
a company's turnover. Additionally, it remains unclear under what circum-
stances this principle should be applied. Even in cases of  negligence that do 
not qualify as gross negligence or minor offenses, this principle would have 
to be applied. This approach appears problematic and merits further exami-
nation. The specifics of the sanctions should be left to the discretion of the 
Member States. In this regard, it suffices, as in Directive 2008/99/EC, to 
merely describe the sanctions as effective, proportionate, and deterrent (see 
also Article 79 of EU Directive 2010/75/EU). 
11. Annex I : SOIL DESCRIPTORS, CRITERIA FOR HEALTHY

Part A : Salt-affected areas may show strongly elevated conductivity values 
compared to the values of the descriptor "conductivity" from Annex I for a 
healthy soil condition. With the exception of natural saline areas and areas af-
fected by sea-level rise, this condition is already taken into account. However, 
elevated salt concentrations also affect regions where salt is found underground 
and has reached the surface and the surrounding soils through centuries of min-
ing and tectonics. Geogenic ally salinated groundwater can sometimes lead to 
salinisation of the overlying soils through evaporation and capillary rise in the 
soil. These cases should also be considered accordingly under the aspect of "sal-
inisation". A return to a healthy state as defined in Annex I would not be propor-
tionate for such soil. 
Part B and C : From a fundamental point of view, the question arises why 
nutrient oversupply is used as an aspect, but not nutrient undersupply – nu-
trient undersupply should also be included.  
The limit value for extractable P (30-50 mg/kg P soil is oriented towards the 
lower to middle range of content class C (VDLUFA: 31 - 60 mg/kg; CAL 
extract). Note: Methods are not congruent (VDLUFA; CAL method, Soil 
Monitoring Law: ISO 11263:1994).

12. Annex III: SUSTAINABLE SOIL MANAGEMENT PRINCIPLES 
c: The statement is articulated in a highly generalized fashion.  Clarification 
would be desirable to the effect that the input of substances is based on the 
quantity or threshold values are defined. Reference should also be made to

e: The primary emphasis on recycling solutions that augment soil organic

In general, the avoidance of an undersupply of nutrients could also be more

13. Annex VI : PHASES AND REQUIREMENTS FOR SITE -SPE-
CIFIC RISK ASSESSMENT 
The characterisation of pollutants should include a distribution in soil and 
groundwater. In our opinion, the latter is not anchored in German soil protec-
tion law. The German Soil Protection Ordinance only describes the seepage 
prognosis and, if applicable, the mixing prognosis. Against this background, 
the relationship between the EU Groundwater Directive and the Soil Moni-
toring Law must also be clarified. Annex VI provides for groundwater inves-
tigations under number 2 and 3. This significantly expands the  scope of the 
Soil Monitoring Law and extends it into the area of the Groundwater Di-
rective. A basic determination of the distribution of pollutants in groundwater 
is to be rejected and should only be carried out in justified cases (e. g. on the 
basis of the results of a leachate prognosis). Duplicate regulations or compet-
ing sets of regulations must be avoided.

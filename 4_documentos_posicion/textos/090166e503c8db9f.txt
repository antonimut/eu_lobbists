Europe’s non-ferrous metals sector feedback to the new Soil 
Monitoring Law (Directive).  
 
The non-ferrous metals industry recognises the need to take soil protection, the sustainable functions and use 
of the soil and restoration of soils to a balanced level within the EU.  
We are committed to continuously improve our production performance to prevent, control, reduce and eliminate pollution 
as far as possible to protect human health and the environment. We support the process regulated under the New Soil 
Strategy. As a sector, we are actively engaged at all levels, political, and with the Member States, to improve soil health 
through improved science and better monitoring. 
We welcome the opportunity to provide feedback on the recent Commission proposal for a directive of the European 
parliament and of the Council on Soil Monitoring and Resilience (Soil Monitoring Law). Below are our feedback and 
recommendations to ensure the science robustness and transparency of this new legislation: 
Key recommendations:  
• Soil health indicators should not be described by a single value or criterion within a soil district; instead, they 
must be dependent on site-specific soil properties to account for natural variations and soil heterogeneity.  
• The "one -out-all-out" principle  (as seen in the Water Framework D irective) implied in the definition o f 
unhealthy soil definition should be avoided in the new soil monitoring law. 
• Adopt a risk-based approach for addressing soil contamination issues, tailoring contaminant considerations 
to site-specific conditions.  
• Consider the importance of assessing the spatial variability of natural background concentrations for metals 
in soils and provide clear guidance on deriving these levels.  
• Expand the definition of soil health beyond contamination, incorporating considerations for soil mineral 
deficiencies, essentiality, and nutrient loss. 
• Publicly available monitoring framework for soil need to have standardised data collection and analysis at 
the EU level, ensuring comparability of national or regional databases. Monitoring databases  must be  
accessible for all stakeholders involved in assessing soil threats.  
• Exclude industrial installations already subject to the Industrial Emissions Directive (IED) from soil 
monitoring requirements to prevent regulatory overlaps and ensure coherence among legislations.  
• Reconsider the proposed standard analytical method (ISO 17586:2016) for metal concentrations, favouring 
the inclusion of the ISO 11466:1995 method. The latter is in line with current threshold concentrations and 
toxicity data and facilitates comparability with existing standards at national and EU level.

• Clarify the EU Soil Monitoring Directive to explicitly exclude raw material deposits and their extraction from 
its provisions to align with the EU's objective to promote domestic raw material extraction and improve the 
security of supply. 
• Delegate the responsibility of risk assessment and management for contaminated sites to companies, 
aligning with practices in other EU regulations thereby avoiding regulatory duplication.  
• Define unacceptable risks based solely on current land use in the Soil Monitoring Law, excluding 
consideration of future land use due to potential uncertainty. 
• Adopt the special legislative procedure, as outlined in Article 192(2)(b), third indent, of the Treaty on the 
Functioning of the European Union, for the Soil Monitoring Law. 
1) Soil health & Soil district 
We welcome the flexibility left to the members state to assess soil health, we also noted the introduction of soil 
districts, for which we express some reservations. Although the goal is to seek homogeneity within each soil 
district regarding soil type, climatic conditions, environmental zone and land use, soil districts will never be 100% 
homogeneous in soil types and soil properties.  Therefore, any thresholds for soil health indicators must still take 
into account site-specific conditions (pedo-climatological, land use, …).  
All indicators need carefully selected reference values, considering the natural variety in soil types and parent 
materials. In addition, soil types are generally based on common properties of sub- surface soil horizons, and 
topsoil properties, which are generally the first indicators for soil health, can still vary significantly for a single soil 
type.  
Therefore, even within a single soil district, soil health indicators should not be described by one single value 
or criterion, but soil health indicators must be dependent on site- specific soil properties (e.g. , toxicity of many 
metals is dependent on the cation exchange capacity of the soil and equations are available to normalize toxicity 
thresholds to site-specific soil properties).  
In addition, the definition of "unhealthy soil" implies that if one of the criteria is not met, the soil is considered 
unhealthy on a "one out of all out" principle. Given all the arguments above, this will prove very difficult and is 
already a known problem in the WFD, and therefore should be avoided in the new soil monitoring law.  
2) Definition of Soil Health and criteria in Annex I 
Eurometaux questions the added value of a minimum list of pollutants. The contaminants to be considered should 
depend on the site- specific conditions (e.g., type of potentially polluting activities, ...). For naturally occurring 
substances such as metals, a risk -based approach is the only scientifically sound approach to address 
contamination issues, as the presence of a contaminant as such cannot be used as an indicator of potential 
problems. Thresholds must consider the effect of site- specific conditions (e.g., pH, organic matter, cation 
exchange capacity, ...) on the bioavailability and toxicity of contaminants. For metals, it is important to take into 
account the spatial variability of natural background concentrations in soils when considering the conta mination 
status of a soil.

Clear guidance should be provided on how to derive the natural background level of naturally occurring soil 
contaminants in order to identify significant anthropogenic contributions. In addition, the essential nature of some 
metals listed in Annex I raises important questions about how this list was established in the first place (e.g., Zn). 
Finally, the definition of soil health cannot include soil contamination alone but  must go hand in hand with 
consideration of soil mineral deficiencies and nutrient loss. 
3) Monitoring Framework 
Eurometaux welcomes the implementation of a, publicly available, monitoring framework. Data collection and 
analysis (soil properties, contaminant concentrations, …) is preferentially standardized at EU level to improve 
comparability of national or regional databases and there use in risk assessments. Monitoring databases (e.g., 
LUCAS database) should be completely available for all actors in the assessment for a soil threat . 
Databases with measured contaminant concentrations  and soil properties affecting bi oavailability (e.g., pH, 
organic carbon content, texture and cation exchange capacity)  at regional/national/European level should e.g. , 
be available for registrants of risk assessment dossiers under various European Regulations (PPP, BPR, REACH, 
…).  
However, it is also important that coherence among legislations should be ensured to avoid double requirements. 
Industrial installations already subject to the Industrial Emissions Directive (IED), should be excluded from the 
soil monitoring requirements, including monitoring frequencies, as  provisions are already set in Art. 14 (Permit 
conditions) and Art.  16 (Monitoring requirements). Furthermore, strict rules are set on the state of soil and 
groundwater contamination via IED art. 22. To this end, and in order to avoid double regulation and/or regulatory 
overlaps, we ask that a clear line be drawn between the new Soil Monitoring Law and the IED .  
4) Restoration and remediation  
 
Remediation should focus on sustainable risk reduction and risk management based on site- specific standards 
and remediation goals. Remediation should not necessarily focus on complete removal of contamination, which 
is often neither realistic nor the most environmentally friendly option. Member States should have the flexibility to 
set priorities and remediation targets. The Commission's role should be to monitor performance to ensure that all 
Member States meet the overall targets set in the Soil Monitoring Directive, while post -activity remediation is 
already covered by the IED. 
5) Methodology proposed to assess metals in annex II 
We welcome the standardisation of analytical methodologies for the main soi descriptors but  question the 
selection of the proposed standard method for metals concentrations in soil. The proposed method in Annex II 
(ISO 17586:2016) is based on the extraction of the potential environmental available metal content based on a 
dilute nitric acid extract. Most thresholds for metals in soils are however currently based on total, or concentrated 
acid (e.g., aqua regia) extractable metal concentrations. We agree that this total metal concentration can be a 
poor descriptor of toxicity and the bioavailable metal fraction in soil but for several metals bioavailability models 
have been derived linking total metal concentrations with toxicity to several soil organisms  by considering the 
variability in soil properties

Because the vast majority of toxicity data for metals in soil are also based on total metal concentrations, monitoring 
data based on dilute nitric acid extracts cannot be directly compared with these toxicity data for metals in soil, nor 
with most threshold concentrations currently used at national or EU level.  Moreover, there is currently no proof 
that a dilute nitric acid extract yields better estimates of toxicity and the available metal fraction in soil because so 
far, no specific extractable metal fraction could yet be identified as the best predictor of the bioavailable fraction 
across metals and soil organisms.  
It is therefore requested to include the standard ISO 11466:1995 method (extraction of trace elements 
soluble in aqua regia) as a standard method for metal analysis in soils. 
6) Extraction of raw materials should not be subject of the Directive. 
According to Article 2 of the EU Commission proposal, the scope of the EU Soil Monitoring Directive should cover 
"all soils in the territory of the Member States ". The Commission proposal does not contain any exceptions or 
limitations to the scope. The proposal then defines soil in Article 3 (1) as "the uppermost layer of the earth's crust 
between the bedrock and the land surface, which is composed of mineral particles, organic matter, water, air and 
living organisms". 
This means that raw materials, to the extent that the above definition applies to them, are also considered "soils" 
and would be subject to the provisions of the Directive. Not only would the soil material containing the raw 
materials stored underground be considered soil under the new EU directive, but also the raw material itself, so 
that the extraction of the raw material would be an activity directly affected by the new EU soil monitoring law.  
Thus, the EU Soil Monitoring Directive would also co-regulate the extraction of raw materials at EU level. However, 
such regulation of mineral extraction at EU level would clearly contradict the subsidiarity principle, as the 
competence to regulate mining and mineral extraction lies with the EU Member States. This is due in particular 
to the fact that raw material deposits are local, immobile and usually not cross-border. In addition, many Member 
States already have specific national regulations that comprehensively and conclusively regulate the extraction 
of raw materials and also ensure soil protection. This existing competence of Member States must not be 
restricted by the proposed EU Soil Directive. 
Co-regulating raw material extraction via the EU Soil Monitoring Directive and subjecting it to additional 
bureaucratic requirements would also run counter to the EU's concern to use the Critical Raw Materials Act to 
promote raw material extraction in the EU by accelerating approval procedures and investment security aspects 
in order to at least not further increase the EU's dependence on imports in the raw materials sector and, ideally, 
to improve the security of supply of domestic raw materials in the EU. 
The EU Soil Monitoring Directive should therefore stipulate that raw material deposits and their extraction 
are not subject to the provisions of this Directive. 
7) Risk assessment and management of contaminated sites (art. 15): 
In Article 15 of the Soil Monitoring Directive, it is the responsibility of the Member States to lay down relevant 
provisions (Article 15(2)) on what constitutes an unacceptable risk to human health, and a site- specific 
assessment must be made for each contaminated site to determine whether the site poses an unacceptable risk 
to human health and the environment. However, in the other EU regulations, companies assess the risk as they

have all the relevant information on the contaminants and the site plans. The Soil Monitoring Law should therefore 
leave the risk assessment and management of contaminated sites to companies to avoid regulatory duplication.  
Furthermore, in the in the definition of unacceptable risks, the future land use should not be considered  because 
it may not be known. Reference should only be made to current land use. 
8) The EU Soil Monitoring Directive falls under the special legislative procedure: 
The ordinary legislative procedure pursuant to Article 192(1) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European 
Union (TFEU) chosen by the EU-COM is not relevant because the EU Soil Monitoring Law is a measure affecting 
land use. Therefore, according to Article 192(2)(b), third indent, TFEU, the special legislative procedure has to be 
applied, in which the European Parliament is only consulted, and the EU Council decides unanimously. 
The Soil Monitoring Directive affects land use because this is precisely the aim of, for example, Article 11 on " 
Land Take Mitigation Principles", which requires Member States to avoid, reduce or compensate as far as 
technically and economically practicable land take. Such provisions clearly affect land use within the meaning of 
Article 192(2)(b) TFEU. 
Therefore, the Soil Monitoring Directive can only be adopted in accordance with the special legislative procedure. 
If this is not observed, the procedural and participation rights of the Member States through the EU Council will 
be breached, and the Directive will be in breach of European primary law. 
 
Overall, Eurometaux continues to emphasise the commitment of the non-ferrous metals industry to achieve good 
environmental and chemical status of soils in the EU and to improve the assessment of risks from metals in soils 
through the most up-to-date scientific knowledge and methodologies. As an industry, we are actively engaged at 
all levels, both at policy level and in Member States, to improve soil health through better science and monitoring. 
Thank you for the attention you will give to our comments, we welcome any opportunity for us to provide 
additional information.

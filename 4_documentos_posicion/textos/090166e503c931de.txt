Feedback to the Commission´s proposal  
 
for a Directive on Soil Monitoring and Resilience (Soil Monitoring Law, SML)  
 
The Association of German Potash and Salt Industry e.V. (VKS) represents mining companies that 
produce salt products and potash fertilisers with about 12.000 employees (EU transparency register 
number: 85717948337-22). Potash and salt extraction in Germany takes place under the highest 
environmental, climate, energy, safety and labour standards worldwide, and stands for sustainable 
mining as well as continuous improvement of environmental performance. The industry is committed 
to the "EU Principles for Sustainable Raw Materials".  
 
In this context it should be pointed out that the potash and salt industry is regulated by a dense set 
of European and national legislation. These regulations include specific national mining regulations 
(i.e. in Germany: Bundesberggesetz) as well as a wide range of environmental regulations including 
a detailed and ambitious nat ional soil protection law. Therefore , in the salt and potash industry 
environmental including the protection of  soil is already fully ensured. Considering this from our 
perspective the need for the proposed SML could be questioned beforehand.  
 
Regardless this fundamental question, we would like to take the opportunity to comment on the 
proposed SML.  
 
 
General remarks:  
 
• Relationship Soil Monitoring Law to other EU Directives must be clarified 
 
It is unclear how the new Soil Monitoring Law (SML) should relate to other European legislation 
that is partly also regulating aspects of soil policy (i.e. protection).  
 
This concerns in particular environmental legislation such as: Industrial Emissions Directive (that 
is currently under revision),  the Directive on Waste, the Landfill Directive or the Mining Waste 
Directive. The mentioned EU Directives are to be considered as specific legislation regulating for 
particular matters environmental protection including soil protection. As specific legislation they 
should take precedence over the regulations of the future SML. This must be clarified in the 
Scope (Article 2) of the SML.  
 
Moreover, it is also unclear how the SML will relate to the EU raw materials strategy and the 
future Critical Raw Materials Act.  
 
• Soil Monitoring Law should not include “Water”  
 
In the Commission´s proposal t he definition of soil (Article 3, No. 1) also includes “water”. It 
should be considered that all aspects of (surface and groundwater) water protection are already 
fully regulated by the European water legislation (i.e. EU Water Framework Directive and the EU 
Groundwater Directive). Therefore, there is no need to include water protection in the future SLM. 
The word “water” should be deleted from the soil definition.

• Extraction of raw materials lays in the competence of member states  
The COM proposal defines soil in Article 3 (1) as " the top layer of the Earth’s crust situated 
between the bedrock and the land surface, which is composed of mineral particles, organic 
matter, water, air and living organisms“. According to this definition also raw materials could fall 
in the scope of the new SML.  
 
In the consequence, this means that the SML would at EU level also regulate the extraction of 
raw materials. However, the competence to regulate raw materials extraction lies with the EU 
Member States . This is based in particular on  the fact that raw material deposits are local, 
immobile and, as a rule, not cross-border. Moreover, in many Member States there are already 
special national regulatory regimes that comprehensively and conclusively regulate the 
extraction of raw materials also ensuring soil protection. A regulation at EU level on raw material 
extraction would affect this competence and in the end contradict the subsidiarity principle. 
 
Moreover, a European regulation on  the extraction of raw materials would also stand in 
contradiction to  the Critical Raw Materials Act and its aim to promote the extraction of raw 
materials in the EU with a view to accelerat e approval procedures and investment security 
aspects in order to improve the security of supply of domestic raw materials in the EU. 
 
Considering the above-said it needs to be ensured that the extraction of raw materials doesn´t 
fall under the regulations of the SML. This should be done by a corresponding clarification in the 
SML (i.e. in article 1 or 2).  
 
Detailed comments 
 
Regardless the general comments above, we would like to make the following detailed comments:  
 
• Article 1 and article 24 - Objective (achievement of healthy soils)  
Regarding the objective to b ring all soils in the EU into a healthy state, a more differentiated 
approach needs to be taken considering the high diversity of soil types and the variety of different 
land uses that can be found across Europe.  
Moreover, the experiences, challenges, and unsolved issues with the EU Water Framework in 
achieving its ambitious environmental goals should be considered also for the future SML. This 
said, we urge in particular the inclusion of appropriate derogations possibilities in the upcoming 
SML. 
• Definition of “Soil health” criteria and criteria in annex I  
 
- Annex I, Part A: Salinization 
 
We critically assess the inclusion of salinization and the descriptor "electrical conductivity" 
in Part A. Salinized soil does not necessarily pose a risk to human health and the 
environment, it may only be restricted in its agricultural use. Therefore, the aspect of 
salinization should be reassessed regarding its relevance for the achievement of the 
directive´s goals.  
 
Regardless the above-said it needs to be considered that salt-affected areas may have 
greatly increased conductivity values compared to the values of the descriptor 
"conductivity" from Annex 1 for a healthy soil condition. Natural saline areas and areas 
affected by sea-level rise are already excluded from achieving the criterion “conductivity”. 
However, elevated salt concentrations in soils occur also in regions where salt naturally

occurs in the underground and reaches the surface through tectonics. Salt tectonics, the 
mechanical mobilisation of underground salt through differences in density between the salt 
and the surrounding rock, moves salt to the surface. Geogenically salinated groundwater 
can sometimes lead to salinisation of the overlying soils through evaporation and capillary 
rise. Accordingly, these cases should also be considered under the aspect of "salinisation". 
Moreover, centuries of salt mining in the EU might have affected surrounding soils. These 
historic/anthropogenic impacts must also adequately be taken into account when determining 
the state of soils. 
  
Altogether a return to a healthy state, as defined in Annex 1, would not be proportionate for 
such geogenic or anthropogenic influences soils. That means that any thresholds for soil 
health indicators must adequately take into account all site-specific conditions (i.e.  land-
use, geogenic conditions etc.)   
 
 
- In general, the objective of bringing all soils into a healthy state should be considered in a 
more differentiated way. We are reluctant that eu- wide thresholds are appropriate to 
adequately define healthy soils as soil properties and characteristics vary extremely between 
member states and even from region to region. Therefore, all soil health indicators – including 
those mentioned in annex I, part A - should be defined at national level.

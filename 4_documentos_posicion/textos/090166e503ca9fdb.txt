EUSTAFOR1 feedback on the Commission proposal for a Soil Monitoring and 
Resilience Directive 
 
European State Forest Association (EUSTAFOR), which represents a coalition of 37 
European State Forest Management Organizations (SFMOs), welcomes the 
opportunity to express its views on the Commission proposal for a Soil Monitoring and 
Resilience Directive.  
 
EUSTAFOR would like to outline the following 5 key points: 
 
1. Evidence-based analysis:  
Addressing soil degradation effectively can only be adequately planned  by using 
credible, fact-based sources of data for assessing the current state of soils and setting 
the targets for potential improvements.  Current estimate in the proposal which shows 
that about 60 to 70% of soils in the EU are unhealthy is mostly based on assumptions2 
related to  land-use activities, with a strong focus on agricultural soils. Since the 
management of soils is very different for different land -uses and potential pressures 
and long-term impacts are very different too, EUSTAFOR would like to call for: 
- Clear identification of specific soils related issues for different land-uses, 
- Land-use-based approach when defining measures within the law, and 
- Consideration of countries’ specificities when setting the targets.   
2. Soil health assessment:  
EUSTAFOR does not see appropriate the approach set in  Art 9 3 of the proposal 
suggesting the ‘one out all out’ method to assess the health of soils. With such 
methodology, reaching ‘healthy soil’ will almost never be possible . As a result,  all 
potential improvements on specific criteria are invisible and watered down to a one 
singular negative assessment. Furthermore, focusing on one overall assessment blurs  
specific problems and impedes the planning process on the necessary measures for 
improvements. Instead, developing a soil health gradient or index would fit better. 
EUSTAFOR wishes to emphasize that such assessment must be realistic and feasible. 
Namely, managed soils provide many different social and economic benefits 
compared to the intact nature that cannot necessarily offer the same level of such 
benefits. EUSTAFOR recommends this must be considered in any future ecosystem 
assessments, including those related to soil health.

Monitoring and assessment interval should be adjusted to the needs of different  
ecosystems and land-uses so  the dynamics of processes ongoing in soils . EUSTAFOR 
believes that the p roposed 5 years  interval is too short to show  trends in forest 
ecosystems. In any case, t he interval should be scientifically sound  and practically 
feasible. Thus, the minimum interval for assessing any significant trends in forest soils 
should also be consistent with forest management planning, e.g. 10 years, especially 
for those descriptors for which field data collection is necessary. Moreover, it's essential 
to have a clear understanding of the constraints associated with employing remote 
sensing for data acquisition, particularly in order to accurately evaluate the human 
and financial resources required for data collection and monitoring. Obtaining all the 
necessary data exclusively through remote sensing is unfeasible  (for instance, 
determining soil thickness  as an important basic characteristic of soil  cannot be 
accurately assessed through remote sensing).  
  
4. Financing:  
EUSTAFOR recommends that before proceeding with accepting legal obligations,  
Members States’ experts together with the Commission should set up a clear action 
plan outlining activities that are needed under the scope of the Soil law, followed by 
a thorough assessment of financial gaps, and a clear identification of financial sources 
to be used. Due to the increasing number of EU Green Deal initiatives, a general 
compilation of EU funding sources  is inadequate and will ultimately lead to 
unsuccessful implementation. EUSTAFOR would also like to express its doubts about 
setting yet another certification system (i.e., soil health certification suggeste d by the 
proposal) that will certainly pose additional financial and administrative burden 
without a clear added value.   
5. Stocktaking:  
In the realm of Sustainable Forest Management (SFM)  and the existing certification 
schemes, long-standing measures are being de veloped and applied to reduce soil 
disruption when managing forests. For instance, f ollowing timber harvesting, state 
forest management organizations are obligated to promptly restore the site, usually 
within 2 -5 years . This restoration process aims to facilitate a rapid regeneration, 
enhancing the coverage of the soil, and prevent erosion. Furthermore, t here is a 
growing trend in employing less impactful machinery.  State forests are actively 
engaged in restoring peat soils, which play a crucial role in water purification, serve as 
water reservoirs, and aid in carbon emissions stabilization.  In addition, many MS work 
actively with restoring semi -natural landscapes. This means often fellings to open up 
the landscapes, also having cattle on these landscapes, which might affect the soil 
coverage, but on the other hand, these areas are important for biodiversity.  These 
considerations must be thoroughly compiled and taken into account before 
concluding the legislation and proposing potential theoretical issues and their 
corresponding solutions.

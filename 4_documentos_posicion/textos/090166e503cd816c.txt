 
 
Directive on Soil Monitoring and Resilience - Metsä Group views 
 
 
 
 
Metsä Group welcomes the proposal for a Directive on Soil Monitoring and Resilience.  We also 
welcome the overall objective of the EU to maintain healthy and resilient biodiversity across Europe. 
Soil is an inherent part of this objective, and it plays significant importance for forest industry as the 
wood production capacity of the European forests is highly dependent on the soil quality. Metsä Group’s 
strategic objective is to enhance the vitality of forests and nature, as healthy and resilient ecosystems 
enable the competitiveness of forest-based industry. 
 
The EU needs a solid and coherent framework for the monitoring of its soils, and we support the aim 
for a continuous improvement of soil health, with a view of achieving healthy soils by 2050. The 
promotion of soil resilience supports the forest sectors’  objectives to combat climate change and  
biodiversity loss. Moreover, sufficient wood raw material procurement in the long term cannot be carried 
out without healthy and resilient soils. 
 
Healthy soil is a fundamental part of all land use, including sustainable forest management. Sustainable 
forest management practices give high importance to soil’s capacity to provide several different  
ecosystem services in the long term. Finland has a long history of collecting data from forests, including 
soil, and Metsä Group wants to share the following insight:  
 
A predictable legislative framework, close to the EU territories and their specificities 
  
• We welcome the Commission’s proposal that the Soil Health Law should take the form of a 
directive. The land use history, natural ecosystems and soil condition vary widely across the 
Union. Therefore, the directive leaves room for the Member States to take the national 
specificities into account. 
• However, the proposed directive states that the Commission has the power of imposing 
additional requirements and supplementing the directive with delegated acts . The 
Commission proposes inter alia delegated powers to amend the measurements and 
methodologies (Article 8) as well as the sustainable soil management principles (Article 10). 
• We understand the potential need for improvement in the future  in cases where actions 
taken by Member States are not sufficient. We still insist that the Commission should not be 
the one to unilaterally update and add to the original requirements via delegated act. One-
size-fits-all solutions should be avoided at the time of initial drafting but also when it comes 
to updating and improving requirements.  
• We are calling for greater clarity in Article 23 provisions, which requires Member States to 
lay down the rules on penalties applicable in case of breaches of national provisions . 
Provisions are unclear as far as their grounds and targeting are concerned and will have to 
be amended to ensure clarity and specificity. 
 
Differences between forest and agricultural land 
 
• Soil management on f orest land differs essentially from  agricultural land . Unlike on 
agricultural soils, for example, measures affecting the forest soil are only carried out a few 
times in a hundred years in an actively used forest. The changes to the forest soil occur very 
slowly over a long term, while the short term  changes can be significant.  Therefore, the 
legislation should encourage long-term work to promote good soil condition. This will enable 
the implementation of best practices enhancing the long-term resilience of soils at local level. 
• Soil monitoring systems should be based primarily on existing Member State systems and 
the expertise of their national research institutes. I n the case of forest soils, national forest 
inventories are an excellent starting point and relevant place to include the new and 
additional monitoring data.  This would ensure both cost -effectiveness and a better 
assessment of the cause -and-effect relationships between different measurement results 
obtained from forests and forest soils.

Defining the right criteria for soil monitoring and data distribution  
 
• Sustainable forest and soil management is complex, and it is important to consider the 
different dimensions of sustainable activity, i.e., ecological, social and economic 
perspectives, and the interdependencies between them. 
• One of the cornerstones of the directive proposal is the list of the principles of sustainable 
soil management. In order to ensure the necessary flexibility and coordination of different 
objectives, the list in Annex III to the proposal should be exemplary in nature.  
• Significant uncertainties may also arise. An example of these is soil organic carbon (SOC) 
in organic soils, the amount of which is difficult to measure and will affect on the reliability of 
reporting. An extensive Soil Health Benchmarks research project has been commenced in 
Europe with the aim o f developing such indicators and limit values for different soil 
ecosystems in cooperation with stakeholders. For this reason, the criterion indicating change 
in the amount of SOC in organic soils should only be outlined when the investigation has 
been brought to completion. 
• In addition, the proposal requires that the data collected under it must be public. In order to 
guarantee the property rights  and safeguard Member States ’ monitoring systems, the 
degree of publicity of data should also be re-considered. 
• Finally, we want to highlight that allowing for various method of land use is key to reaching 
the EU 2050 goals, whether it is the emissions and nature restoration objectives, or in terms 
of strategic autonomy and self-sufficiency in renewable raw materials.

PAN Europe Feedback on the European’s Commission's proposal for a Directive 
on Soil Monitoring and Resilience    
 
Pesticide Action Network Europe (PAN Europe) very much welcomes the European 
Commission’s proposal for a Directive on Soil Monitoring and Resilience (Soil Monitoring Law, 
SML). Soils, largely hidden from our field of view, have been unjustifiably overlooked in 
legislation, being treated as inferior to air, water and marine environments. This while soils are 
estimated to harbour about 59% of Earth’s species, and possibly more. For example, 90% of 
fungi, 85% of plants and 50% of bacteria are living in soils, and provide the basis for healthy 
ecosystems and human health. The EU Soil Strategy for 2030 must rectify this, with the Soil 
Law as its cornerstone.  
Healthy soils provide a wide variety of ecosystem services, which are also central to human 
health, such as biodiversity, nutrient cycling, sustainable plant production, natural pest control, 
good water quality, water retention, carbon stor age and erosion management. Soils are 
characterised by highly complex processes and interactions, of which many still need to be 
further explored.  
Pesticide use is a major source of widespread and long -lasting soil pollution, harming soil 
organisms, overall soil health and ecosystem functioning. Pesticide pollution in soils also leads 
to contamination of other matrices, such as water resources and air, and can harm citizens 
through direct (ingestion or inhalation) and indirect (e.g. food, house dust) exposure.  
The SML proposal currently lacks the needed ambition and provisions to protect and restore 
soils, and ensure that they are used sustainably. The proposal in its current form is unlikely to 
achieve healthy soils by 2050. The lack of legally binding targets and the absence of focus 
on soil biodiversity  are among the outspoken gaps. A major shortcoming in the 
proposal, is the largely absent topic of diffuse pollution, including pesticides. This while 
the soil strategy’s objective aims to reduce soil pollution to a level that is no longer harmful to 
people’s health or ecosystems by 2050. Diffusive soil pollution is linked to extensive impacts 
on soil hea lth, and the environment in general.  An eff ective Soil Law is a prerequisite for 
reaching the European Green Deal objectives, as effective soil protection is directly linked with 
reversing the collapse of biodiversity, achieving zero pollution and climate neutrality. Current 
EU risk assessment does not sufficiently protect soils. EU legislation on soils, including binding 
and effective EU-wide rules, is hence long overdue.   
The name of the Law, which would first be the ‘Soil Health Law’, is indicative of its lack of 
ambition. A Soil Law for Europe must not only lead to increased monitoring of soils, but must

Following points are key for uptake in further negotiations on the SML, and their inclusion is a 
prerequisite for an effective soil law.  
1. Overall ambition and Targets: The SML should include a clear framework with legally 
binding medium- and long-term targets (2030, 2050). Targets should include amonge 
more:  
- reducing pesticide residues and other pollutants in soils, to meet binding 
thresholds for total concentrations of pollutant groups (e.g. total concentration 
of pesticide residues) , as well as for a set individual pollutants. The key list 
should include heavy metals, pesticides, contaminants linked to sewage sludge 
and compost, microplastics, veterinary products, pharmaceuticals, 
antimicrobials, POPs, PFAS, PAHs, polychlorinated biphenyls, PCBs, mineral 
oil, VOCs and contaminants of emerging concern 
- restoring soil biodiversity 
- reducing erosion to a minimum 
- reducing topsoil and subsoil compaction 
- increasing humus buildup 
- achieving zero net land take 
- restoration of peatlands  
 
2. National plans : The SML should oblige Member States to provide national action  
plans, which report on taken and planned measures to reach the obligations and 
objectives of the Directive.  
3. Soil health monitoring and assessment: The SML should include scientifically robust 
endpoints of soil biodiversity, which should be part of the soil health assessment.  
4. Polluter pays principle : The SML should lead to application of the polluter pays 
principle, by taking up a pollution levy (e.g. pesticide tax).  
5. Mandatory practices : The SML should impose mandatory sustainable soil 
management practices, rather than merely guidelines. These mandatory practices 
should be aligned with conditionality rules of the CAP, requirements of EU Pesticide 
Regulation (Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 and the future SUR Regulation), the Water 
Framework Directive, Groundwater directive and other relevant EU legislation.  
6. (Diffuse) soil pollution : The SML should set mandatory EU -wide monitoring 
requirements and thresholds for key pollutants for contaminat ed sites and for diffuse 
pollution. Apart from an EU-wide set of key pollutants, Member states should be obliged 
to monitor and set thresholds for an additional set of nationally relevant pollutants, as 
well as to carry out screenings for a wide ran ge of pollutants, including contaminants 
of emerging concern.  
 
Restoring healthy soil ecosystems by 2050 is essential. We provide the European institutions 
with our the further feedback below, to consider during the further negotiations on  this vital 
piece of legislation.

Zooming in on pesticides, monitoring, assessing and addressing pesticide pollution 
across Europe’s soils is essential for numerous reasons. Most of these reasons also hold 
for other types of diffuse pollution: 
 
 
• High need for soil monitoring data on cocktails of pollutants in soils, to address 
extensive data gaps.   
 
- Pesticides represent a high share of diffuse pollution, and large scale 
monitoring of pesticide residues in soils has been long overdue. The sampling 
studies that have been carried out show that mixtures of pesticide 
residues in agricultural soils are the rule rather than the exception, with 
83% of soils containing one or more residues, while about 58% contain 
mixtures. Currently, mixture effects of pesticides are not taken into account in 
EU risk assessment, while models of the transport routes and fate of  pesticides 
also don’t take wind transport of soil bound pesticides particles adequately into 
account. Hence, we are still largely in the dark about 1) which mixtures of 
pesticides or other pollutants are found in our soils, and 2) which impact 
these mixtures have on the environment, ecosystem services and human 
health. In the ongoing EU SPRINT project on soils , researchers compiled 
hazard properties of found residues, which “showed that more than 50% of the 
residues detected in the environment were harmful for soil or water organisms. 
Likewise, 60% were described to have a possible effect on reproductive 
development, and 30% were considered to be possibly carcinogenic”. 
 
- Co-formulants must be taken into account in soil monitoring. Only in 2023 
has the Commission published a regulation to regulate co-formulants. It will take 
many years until they undergo a regulatory screening. Furthermore, there is a 
major lack of information on the risk they pose to soil health. Many of them are 
very toxic and others increase the toxic effect of the active ingredient in the 
pesticide formula. 
 
• High need for soil monitoring data to contribute to protective risk assessment of 
pesticides.  
 
- In Risk Assessment procedures of EFSA, PECs (predictable environmental 
concentrations) are an exposure proxy for soil organisms. Representative 
pesticide uses and application schemes are used to calculate these values. 
However, they need validation with field data, under different climate and 
soil conditions.  Currently, the latter tend to demonstrate that the PEC 
calculation regularly underestimates reality. Furthermore, historical pollution of 
other pesticides or other pollutants is not accounted for in the approval process. 
This leads to significant underestimations of the real risk. Knowing which

concentrations of pesticide and other pollutant mixtures exist in soils is 
essential to carry out assessments on the impact of diffusive pollution on 
(soil) organisms,  and to allow for robust pesticide risk assessments . 
Information on pesticide cocktails in major cropping systems, in combination 
with ecotoxicity testing and soil biodiversity indicators, is needed to assess 
which mixtures pose a high risk to soils, and w hich pesticides should be 
restricted or banned.  
 
- Current risk assessment of pesticides only includes a small fraction of 
species living in soils. Monitoring of pesticide pollution in combination 
with assessment of biodiversity indicators is needed.                                                             
Toxicity for soil organisms is only assessed for a few EFSA test organisms, 
representing a very small fraction of millions of species living in soils. It is 
therefore needed to have systematic monitorin g in place, to link contaminant 
mixtures to other soil health indicators, among which soil biodiversity indicators. 
For example, community shifts of organisms are not taken into account in 
current risk assessment, while that can have a large impact on soil ecosystem 
functioning and crop health. Combined effects of residue mixtures on different 
taxa and functioning of the community should be monitored. taken into account.  
 
- Very frequently, pesticide active substances have been shown to persist 
much longer  in the soil than predicted by EU risk assessment . For 
example, Clothianidin, a neonicotinoid, toxic for bees,  and to many other non-
target organisms, was shown to persist for more than a decade in soils, much 
longer than the data put forward by the indust ry in regulatory dossiers. 
Abamectin, highly toxic to birds, bees and soil life, is also an example of a 
pesticide which persists much longer than assessed upon authorisation. 
Atrazine, banned in the EU in 2003, was shown to persist in soils for decades. 
 
- Residues often accumulate in the topsoil, and particle bound pesticide 
residues are easily transported by wind, leading to exposure of humans 
and animals through inhalation , directly entering the bloodstream via the 
lungs. Also children are more at risk for exposure to soil pollution, through both 
ingestion and inhalation, while crawling and playing. Pesticides have been 
shown to accumulate in household dust, and should therefore also be 
monitored in dust.. 
 
- Pesticides can enter the food chain and impact f ood quality and human 
health. EU -harmonised MRLs for pesticide residues only exist for active 
substances and a few metabolites, and also don’t exist for total pesticide 
content. Monitoring of residues of pesticides in soils (active substances, co -
formulants, metabolites) is needed to contribute to the development of 
protective MRLs.

- Contamination of soils plays an extensive role in not mee ting surface, ground 
and drinking water quality goals.  
- Soil pollution also leads to air pollution.  
Therefore, mandatory monitoring of and the setting of  binding threshold for soil 
pollutants is urgently needed.  
Binding monitoring requirements and quality thresholds for an EU list of priority 
pollutants should be included in the proposal. The priority list should inlcude heavy metals, 
pesticides, contaminants linked to sewage sludge and compost, microplastics, veterinary 
products, pharmaceuticals, antimicrobials, POPs, PFAS, PAHs, polychlorinated biphenyls, 
PCBs, mineral oil, VOCs and contaminants of emerging concern. Monitoring should include 
residues, metabolites and by-products. High importance should be given to CMR, EDS and all 
persistent and bioaccumulative chemicals. Chemicals that should not be present in a soil 
considered healthy include chemicals identified as ecotoxic, persistent, bioaccu mulative, 
mobile and toxic, by any EU regulation. For this priority list, soil quality thresholds should be 
set and/or developed, with exceedance of thresholds leading to concrete action.  Quality 
thresholds should be set or developed taking into account m ixture and cumulative effects on 
human health, ecosystems and ecosystem functioning. This list and thresholds should be 
included in Part A of Annex I.  The list and thresholds should be developed taking into 
account existing and to be set thresholds e.g. f or water quality (Water Framework, 
Groundwater, Drinking water and EQS directive) and air quality. Thresholds for individual 
substances as well as for total concentration of a group of pollutants should be set (e.g. 
individual and total concentration thresh olds for pesticides, PFAS , …). A priority list of 
pollutants and accompanying EQS list set at EU level should not replace existing monitoring 
networks, risk assessment and thresholds at member state level, but will rather build upon 
existing EU (e.g. LUCA Soi l survey) and national monitoring networks . It should provide an 
overarching quality assessment network to evaluate progress and effectiveness of 
measurements taken, in view of achieving healthy soils  in the EU. The priority list should be 
revised over time as nee ded, based on progressive scientific insights in the impact  of 
pollutants, including mixture and cumulative effects , on human health and ecosystem 
functioning. 
In addition to an at EU level defined priority list of pollutants and thresholds, member states 
should defin e an additional set of key pollutants which are of specific relevance and 
concern at national level , and set or develop thresholds for these pollutants. Pollutants 
should include substances from the groups of contaminants listed above. This list and 
thresholds should be included in Part B of Annex I.  
An additional set of pollutants which are not included in Part A and B of Annex I,  and for which 
thresholds can’t be defined yet, but for which monitoring is key in terms of assessing 
trends and possible risks, should be taken up in Part C of Annex I. Part C can then involve 
screening requirements for large groups of pollutants, to identify new possible risks 
and e.g. emerging pollutants.

In framework of monitoring and risk assessment of large groups of pollutants, generic 
chemical screening approaches based on for example chromatography with full scan mass 
spectrometry should play an important role and are effective of screening samples for a very 
high number of pollutants, in an economic way, to also pick up  unexpected pollutants. Unlike 
with conventional targeted methods, samples, e.g. soil samples, can be analyzed 
comprehensively, after which, for example for contaminants of emerging concern, specific data 
can be retrieved later. Other important advantages are the possibility of retrospective detection 
of additional EPs without the need for re -sampling/re-analysis of the sample, and detection 
and identification of unknowns.(Geissen et al., 2015). 
It is essential that the option is maintained to update and re view established lists of 
priority and key pollutants and thresholds in the soil directive , to be further developed 
over time, based on progressive scientific insights, for example on cumulative and synergistic 
effects on human health, biodiversity and ecosystem functioning.  
 
Systematic monitoring of diffusive pollution, such as pesticide residues, in soils is, given the 
above, also essential to account for the shortcomings in current risk assessment. Monitoring 
data resulting from systematic monitoring should also directly feed into tackling these 
shortcomings. On the other hand, monitoring results should be linked to concrete 
actions to tackle pollution and restore soil health.  
 
PAN Europe also fully supports and was consulted on the EEB assessment of the European 
Commission's proposal for a Soil Monitoring Law . A soil law for Europe must both 
ambitiously contribute to filling in extensive knowledge gaps, and provide a clear, 
binding framework to lead to effective restoration of soil health.

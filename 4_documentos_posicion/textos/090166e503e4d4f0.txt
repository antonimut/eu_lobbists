Concawe Feedback on the proposal for a Directive 
on Soil Monitoring and Resilience  
(Soil Monitoring Law) 
 
 
1. Introduction 
Concawe welcomes the opportunity to respond to the OPC related to the proposal for a Directive on Soil Monitoring and 
Resilience and to contribute to developing a consistent sustainable approach for managing contaminated sites  in line with 
international best practice with insights from scientific research to enhance the effectiveness of the proposed Directive  
 
2. Scope of Annex 1 Criteria for healthy soils 
The proposal encompasses two primary domains of soils. Firstly, it addresses the significance of soils in agricultural 
production and soil ecosystem services such as carbon sequestration and water regulation. Secondly, it aims to regulate the 
management of contaminated sites. These represent very different areas characterized by differing levels of regulatory 
development and varying spatial extents. Agricultural and natural soils may span extensive portions of a nation, whereas 
contaminated sites are typically more confined in scale, and the extent can be limited to a single parcel of land. 
 
The proposal aims at setting criteria to define a healthy soil which may make sense for agricultural or natural soils but may 
not be applicable for built-up, man-made sites with ‘artificial soils’ as defined in Chapter 1. Applying the Annex 1 criteria to 
‘artificial soils’ or made ground will in many cases lead to a classification as ‘unhealthy’ and while the Annex 1 criteria may be 
relevant for agricultural production and natural soils, they do not recognize that built-up sites may have other functional 
requirements which can conflict with many of the Annex 1 criteria. In urban or built-up areas, soils provide a wide variety of 
ecosystem services which include provisioning services such as providing physical support for human activities and 
infrastructures 1,2. The functional requirements for these ecosystem services may be very different to those for agricultural 
activities. Setting criteria for soils should ideally recognize all ecosystem services that may be offered by soils.  
 
For example, soils may be compacted to allow construction of buildings or facilities3. Through ground improvement 
techniques the bulk density of soils is deliberately increased to obtain sufficient geotechnical strength and bearing capacity 4. 
In order to achieve a sufficient compaction, the bulk density will in most cases be higher than those specified in Annex 1. 
Although we realize that these criteria are probably intended for use on a ‘soil district’ level , this is not explicitly stated in the 
proposal and could lead to regulators enforcing criteria which cannot be practically achieved for a site and which could even 
conflict with the desired soil properties required for a site for construction.  
 
For this reason, it is likely that agricultural soil health indicators published in scientific literature exclude urban or soils  or 
made ground. Reference is made to the Open Soil Health Framework 5 and the Soil Health Index 6 which both only map 
agricultural land. Developing soil health indicators for wider applications should consider the function of soil (or land use) as 
highlighted by both Lehman et al. 7 and Ros et al.5. Practically, it would imply that the soil health indicators in Annex 1 A which 
are relevant to crop productivity or nature (for example bulk density or soil organic matter) apply only for agricultural or 
natural soils and are not applicable for built-up (often urban) sites where other criteria may be relevant.  
 
3. Follow a science-based risk-based management of contaminated sites applying the principles of sustainable remediation 
The proposal requires member states to develop a risk-based approach for the identification, investigation and management 
of contaminated sites. Concawe considers that a risk-based approach is the best practice for management of contaminated

sites and we therefore welcome this requirement. The sustainable management of soil is a valuable component of the 
proposal (Chapter III) and it would make sense to link this to Chapter IV (article 15) by making reference to principles of 
sustainable remediation (see for example ISO 18504:2017 on Sustainable Remediation).  
 
It should further be recognised that managing contaminated sites is not new and that best practices have been developed 
over the past decades. These approaches have been standardised in different ISO standards and guidance documents. It 
would be valuable if the Commission supports member states who need to develop this framework to implement a risk-
based approach by leveraging the products and standards that have been developed in the past. In particular, we would like 
to reference the following standards and guidance documents:  
 
- ISO 18400 - series Soil Quality - Sampling 8 
- ISO 21365:2019 Soil quality - Conceptual site models for potentially contaminated sites 9 
- ISO 19204:2022 Soil quality - Procedure for site-specific ecological risk assessment of soil contamination 10 
- ISO 18504:2017 Soil quality - Sustainable remediation11 
- ASTM E2081-22 Standard Guide for Risk-Based Corrective Action12 
- ASTM-E1739-95 Risk-based Corrective Action Applied at Petroleum Release Sites 13 
 
In order to support the development of the Soil Strategy and the proposal for a directive, the European Commission 
established the EU Soil Observatory and the Extended Soil Expert Group. Concawe has participated in these over the past two 
years and Concawe would welcome the continuation of these groups (or their successor), using a model such as the Common 
Implementation Strategy under the Water Framework Directive. Such groups can support member states with implementing 
the Directive and also develop further science-based guidance documents. 
 
4. Healthy Soil Certification: Parameters 
The proposal includes provision for voluntary soil certification based on the Annex I criteria. The parameters listed in Annex 1 
are suited for agricultural and natural land and are not suitable for the case of 'artificial land' or made ground. In case 
voluntary soil certification would also apply to for artificial land / made ground, Annex 1 should be revised to include the 
specificities of such lands.  
 
5. Register of contaminated sites: Technical Terms 
The register of contaminated or potentially-contaminated sites as described in Chapter 16 and Annex VII does not describe 
the process to remove from the register a site that has undergone remedial work and /or has been assessed as not 
contaminated and not posing any risk any more. Such process (either removing from the register or defining as no-longer 
contaminated) should be integrated in the directive in order to provide legal certainty to the owner of the site.  
6. Definitions  
Chapter 1 contains a definition of soil as follows: 
 
' ‘soil’ means the top layer of the Earth’s crust situated between the bedrock and the land surface, which is composed of 
mineral particles, organic matter, water, air and living organisms' 
 
This definition implies soils could reach a thickness of several 100s of meters depending on the depth to bedrock. We 
recommend to adopt a more fitting definition of soil for example the definition proposed by the ISO standard on soil 
quality14: 
'Soil is the 'upper layer of the Earth’s crust transformed by weathering and physical/chemical and biological processes and 
composed of mineral particles, organic matter, water, air, and living organisms organized in generic soil horizons' 
 
It is further noted that several concepts are used in the proposal but lack a clear definition:

- 'Point-source activities': This is used in the definition of 'contaminated site' but is not specified;  
- 'Restoration': This term is used an important subject and aim in the explanatory memorandum but not used  or

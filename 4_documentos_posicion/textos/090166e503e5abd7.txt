eBay’s Feedback on the reform of the Customs Union  
*** 
INTRODUCTION 
We welcome the opportunity to share feedback on the package recently brought forward by the 
Commission, composed of a proposal for a new Regulation on the Union Customs Code, EU Customs Data 
Hub and EU Customs Authority, a proposal for amending the Regulation on the system of reliefs from 
customs duty and the tariff and statistical nomenclature, and a proposal for amending the VAT Directive on 
its provisions related to distance sale of imported goods. 
While we support the EU’s ambition to make customs processes and controls more efficient and 
harmonized, we think that this important evolution should be carefully conducted and carry proportionate 
obligations and responsibilities for  all players involved, including online marketplaces like eBay. We also 
challenge the proposal made by the European Commission of entirely removing the 150 EUR de minimis  
exemption on customs duties, for reasons further detailed in this document. 
As one of  the first online marketplaces to be launched over 25 years ago, eBay has in- depth long-time 
experience connecting buyers and sellers online. Today, eBay gathers more than 132 million active buyers 
and millions of sellers globally, both individuals and businesses, which include a large number of small and 
medium-sized companies (SMEs). At any point in time there are around 1.9 billion listings live on eBay 
platforms around the world. eBay empowers SMEs to create their own digital storefront and to control their 
strategy, customer relationship, product marketing and delivery. In fact, as a pure third- party marketplace, 
eBay never sells its own products and never competes with its sellers. Moreover, eBay does not hold, ship 
or deliver any of the goods sold via its platform.  
 
EBAY’S FEEDBACK ON THE EU CUSTOMS REFORM – KEY RECOMMENDATIONS 
● Explicitly rule out any non-financial liability stemming from the deemed importer status 
● Implement good faith clauses to protect deemed importers against miscategorized goods  
● Align the date of application of the deemed importer regime with the availability of the EU 
Customs Data Hub 
● Rescind the removal of the 150 EUR de minimis

1. The role of online marketplaces in the reformed Customs Union 
The proposal seems to sustain the preconceived expectation that all e- commerce players are by nature 
involved in the logistics and customs clearance processes. This is, however, not the case for a pure third-
party marketplace like eBay.  
 
a. Implications of the deemed importer status 
As reformed in 2017, the Directive 2006/112/EC (or EU VAT Directive) created the so- called “ deemed 
supplier” regime. Through its new Article 14a, the Directive states that a taxable person that “ facilitates, 
through the use of an electronic interface such as a marketplace” distance sales of goods imported from 
outside the EU in consignments of value <150 EUR, or the supply of goods within the EU by a taxable 
person established outside the EU to a non- taxable person, then said facilitating person (marketplace 
provider) “shall be deemed to have received and supplied those goods himself”.  
Although the concept of supplier appears in other areas of EU legislation, such as consumer law or 
corporate due diligence, the deemed supplier status assumed by online marketplaces under the VAT 
Directive does not extend to those areas as its sole objective is “to ensure effective and efficient collection 
of VAT”, as the European Commission clarified in its own Explanatory Notes on VAT e- commerce rules. 
The same Explanatory Notes reiterate it in the most explicit way: “the deemed supplier provision is a ‘fiction’ 
created for VAT purposes. The deemed supplier is usually not in possession of the goods and the transfer 
of ownership of the goods occurs  between the underlying supplier and the customer. Hence, the deemed 
supplier will often (…) depend on the accuracy of the information provided by the [underlying] suppliers”. 
The situation is not at all as clear when it comes to the “deemed importer” concept introduced by the present 
reform. First, the role of the traditional importer within the Union Customs Code already goes beyond 
taxation, unlike that of the traditional supplier within the VAT Directive. Second, the Commission has so far 
not provided any explicit clarification of the fact that the deemed importer obligations would be strictly limited 
to the financial aspect of the customs import process. In fact, it has even stated in its Impact Assessment 
accompanying the reform package: “ The concept of ‘deemed importer’, however, would go beyond the 
financial liability for the collection of customs duties and VAT and would also cover non- financial aspects, 
within the limitations of the Digital Services Act” (page 129). 
Yet, imposing liability for non-f inancial risks on third -party marketplaces like eBay would infringe the 
principles of intermediary liability enshrined in the e-Commerce Directive and upheld by the EU institutions 
as part of the Digital Services Act. Further adverse effects could be expected, such as the over-blocking of 
sellers even for involuntary mistakes and following abusive reports (e.g. of ill-intentioned competitors), given 
marketplaces’ inability to independently assess the illegality of products. In the meantime, actual bad 
players would be further disincentivized from abiding by the rules, with compliant marketplaces having to 
shoulder the legal and financial hit of non-compliant product sales in their place
1. 
The proposal needs to clarify that the deemed importer status would carry implications strictly limited to the 
financial aspect of importer obligations, namely the effective and efficient collection of customs duties.

b. Challenges with declaring as a deemed importer 
● Unavailable or unreliable goods information 
Recital 14 of the proposed Regulation states that “ the deemed importer should provide to the customs 
authorities not only the data necessary for the release for free circulation of the goods sold to consumers 
in the EU, but also the information that they must collect for VAT purposes.” 
This information includes, as per Article 88 paragraph 3(a), information such as the manufacturer, or the 
product supplier where different from the manufacturer. This information may be challenging to obtain for 
a deemed importer like eBay, especially from sellers of second- hand goods who may simply not have the 
information readily available. In the same logic, it would be important to clarify that the origin as required 
on the declaration refers to the origin of the shipment (which may be communicated by the seller to the 
marketplace) as opposed to the actual origin of the good (i. e. where it was manufactured, for example).  
As per the same Article, the deemed importer would have to provide a “ tariff classification” and a 
“description of the goods”. An online marketplace may of course be able to relay the description as provided 
by the third-party seller offering the goods on its platform. However, if the online marketplace is to become 
the deemed importer, we cannot allow for an erroneous description (or resulting erroneous tariff 
classification) to generate any liability to the platform. In a pure third-party marketplace business model, the 
platform cannot inspect products physically. As a result, the  proposal should include a “good faith clause” 
whereby the liability of the deemed importer should not be incurred, unless it can be proven that the deemed 
importer has failed to exert due commercial care. Such a provision is already included in the European 
Commission’s Explanatory Notes on VAT e- commerce rules for the application of the current “deemed 
supplier” framework: “The deemed supplier rule should not put a disproportionate burden on electronic 
interfaces and does not aim to require a wide-spread standard of checks to be performed for every supply”. 
Finally, Article 21 paragraph 3
2 refers to making available the “proof of exit” , within the returns process for 
goods initially imported under the deemed supplier / deemed importer regime. We believe t his provision 
may be very difficult to implement in practice, as the online marketplace may never be in a position to prove 
the exit (re-export) of the goods, unless the underlying importer and/or the buyer should agree to share with 
us all relevant export documentation for the goods (including copies of submitted export declarations, carrier 
documentation and waybills, etc.). We consider it in fact very probable that underlying importers or buyers 
would recurrently fail to do this, which would then make it  impossible for the deemed importer to abide by 
Article 21 paragraph 3.  
 
●  Absence of the EU Customs Data Hub 
According to the current proposal’s Article 265 paragraph 2(c), the deemed importer regime would start to 
apply as of 1 March 2028. However, the EU Customs Data Hub would only become available for economic 
operators (which we understand to include deemed importers) as of 1 March 2032 (Article 265 paragraph 
4). This four -year gap raises the question as to where deemed importers would be expected to f ile their 
customs declarations in the absence of the EU Customs Data Hub. Recital (74) does enunciate that 
“deemed importers will be supervised by the Member State of their establishment”.  
However, goods may not necessarily be imported in the Member State where the online marketplace has 
its establishment. If the platform is expected to file import declarations in every Member State where goods 
are imported, we fear this may be impossible to comply with, given that location of import is generally 
dictated by carrier flows, something that the deemed importer has no visibility nor control over.

This would also run counter to the simplification objective pursued by the proposal 3 as it would potentially 
fragment deemed importer responsibilities and processes over 27 different customs interfaces.  
As a result, we believe it would be highly preferable to delay and thus align the application of the deemed 
importer regime with that of the EU Customs Data Hub’s availability on 1 March 2032. Alternatively, deemed 
importers should be authorized and enabled to file all relevant declarations through their Member State of 
establishment, regardless of the location of import of the goods into the EU, until the EU Customs Data 
Hub becomes available. 
 
● Simplified Tariff Treatment 
According to the Explanatory Memorandum on the Regulation proposal for the simplified tariff treatment 4, 
goods subject to “EU harmonised excise duties” as well as  to “commercial policy measures, such as anti -
dumping, anti-subsidy duties or safeguard measures” are to be excluded from the simplified tariff treatment. 
Yet, goods subject to anti -dumping or anti -subsidy measures can change depending on the country of 
origin, as well as international policies at any specific point in  time. This raises questions as to the 
responsibilities of the deemed importer in relation to these goods. For example, goods could have shipped 
at a moment when no anti-dumping rates were in place, and later clear customs once new rates are applied. 
These can also be specific to the country of origin, which the online marketplace cannot assert for certain. 
For example, since August 2023, anti-dumping tariffs in place against Chinese imports of fibre cables range 
from 44- 88%, whereas they ranged from 19- 44% from November 2021 to August 2023. The proposal 
should therefore clarify how deemed importers are expected to deal with goods that fall in these exclusions 
from the simplified treatment, should they be sold on their platform. 
 
●  Equal treatment principle 
Finally, we would like to highlight the risks presented by an unbalanced application of the deemed importer 
regime. While we recognize and accept our potential classification as a deemed importer under this reform, 
we already observe other large multinational businesses that provide platform services for online retailers 
and payment systems, that are not currently required to meet collect and remit obligations for EU VAT. This 
could encompass businesses that offer e-commerce website tools, digital storefronts or payment services. 
Although there are differences in the services our businesses offer users, from a tax collection perspective, 
these businesses are no different to eBay and should be similarly capable and required to collect and remit 
VAT from users of their services, and by extension customs duties, should this reform be adopted.  
As a global business, eBay has seen consideration of these issues in respect of other tax regimes which 
apply consumption tax on low value imported goods. For example, in 2021, the Australian Board of Taxation 
(BoT) reviewed arrangements in relation to the application of Goods and Services tax collection on imported

2. Measuring the impact of the reform on European small businesses 
and consumers 
As a pure third- party marketplace, eBay is directly invested in the success of its users, whether they use 
our platform to sell and grow their business, or to buy items they need or simply love. As a result, we deeply 
care about the impact that the EU Customs reform could have on our European users, in particular small 
and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and consumers. 
 
a. Avoid undue double taxation 
First, we are able to draw on recent national experience to raise concerns about the risks presented by the 
reform to European consumers, should the appropriate safeguards not be adopted along with it. Our 
experience comes from implementing a French law which made marketplaces liable for collection of VAT 
on imports above 150 euros
6, thus going beyond the EU deemed supplier model adopted in 2017. Through 
that experience, we have noticed that the lack of synchronization between customs data systems and those 
of carriers, in particular postal services operators, when it came to processing information related to 
shipments of goods which had been sold on our platform.  
As per our legal obligation, we would collect the VAT due on the goods upon purchase (much like we do 
throughout the EU for shipments under 150 euros since 2021). As the shipment would not be covered by 
the EU IOSS, we would give our own VAT number to the seller so that they could affix it to their customs 
declaration, in order to prove its status with regards to paid VAT upon import. We would of course also 
consign the sale and report it to the French tax authority as per our VAT declaration. Upon arriving at the 
border, however, the information would not have been shared with the carrier who, ignoring that the VAT 
had already been paid on the shipment, would go on to claim it from the recipient (as per a standard import 
process without deemed supplier). This would result in double taxation which would either go unnoticed by 
the recipient, or against which he would protest, ultimately turning back to us, the platform, for a refund.

b. Rescind the removal of the 150 EUR de minimis 
Our main concern within the reform applies to the proposed removal of the 150 EUR de minimis, which we 
would strongly encourage the EU to rescind. 
 
● Hurting European SMEs 
Historically, the global trading system and institutions have focused on large enterprises, as they were the 
only entities with the capacity and resources to utilize global supply chains and markets. However, in recent 
years, barriers to accessing larger and more robust markets are receding and small businesses and 
entrepreneurs are beginning to leverage technology and global marketplace platforms to reach consumers 
in new ways. Platforms like eBay are allowing small businesses, and even micro businesses, to open with 
minimal start-up costs and compete in global markets. This is possible because technology, and specifically 
platforms, have dramatically reduced the cost of distance. 
Recent eBay research has shown that nearly all European small businesses selling on eBay are exporting, 
at a much higher rate than their traditional equivalents. For example, our June 2023 report on Promoting 
inclusive economic opportunities across Germany
8 shows that 95.9% of the German small businesses on 
eBay are exporters, with the comparable export rate of traditional businesses being 10.5%.  
Conserving or even expanding de minimis thresholds promotes trade and economic opportunities for small 
businesses by reducing the time, cost, and uncertainty of shipping packages across borders. Higher de 
minimis levels counter excessive per transaction costs for customs authorities.  
Currently, European small businesses benefit from important duty de minimis when they export into: 
- the United States: 753 EUR (800 USD) 
- Australia: 605 EUR (1000 AUD) 
- New Zealand: 560 EUR (1000 NZD) 
- Israel: 470 EUR (500 USD) 
- the United Kingdom: 155 EUR (135 GBP) 
Should the EU bring down its de minimis to 0 EUR, it is to be expected that these major EU trade partners 
would reciprocate and lower their thresholds as well, hurting the capacity of European SMEs to export. In 
fact, legislation pending before the U. S. Congress, proposed by Senator Cassidy of Louisiana, would 
impose “reciprocal” de minimis treatment on goods entering the U. S. In 2021, Germany alone shipped over 
25.5 million de minimis shipments to the United States
9. Presumably a large portion of these are from SMEs 
leveraging the United States as a major market. Should the U. S. reciprocate the de minimis removal, this 
would effectively cut off an important market for European exporters. Yet, this financial impact is not being 
considered by the European Commission in its impact assessment, even though it may largely eat away at 
the additional 1 billion EUR per year that the EU would hope to collect from the removal itself.

●  Hurting EU customs authorities and their budget 
Another financial impact of the de minimis removal is the incremental burden that it will generate on customs 
authorities. Additional officers will in fact be needed to process the increased shipments volumes and to 
collect and enforce the duties. This will be crucial to avoid creating backlog and aggravate congestion and 
distract from more pressing security and revenue operations at the border. Yet, this will again eat away at 
the additional 1 billion EUR per year that the EU was hoping to collect from the change in the first place.  
In addition, we consider it important to dispel the misconception that de minimis shipments might constitute 
a “loophole” in terms of customs enforcement. The U. S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) itself 
clarified that it scrutinizes low-value shipments just as it does formal entries
10, something that EU customs 
authorities can already do as well. 
  
● Hurting European consumers 
Last but not least, the additional tax burden to be shouldered by European consumers cannot be 
understated, especially as annual inflation more than tripled in the EU in 2022
11, measured at 9.2% 
compared to 2.9% in 2021.  
A concrete example can help shed light on just how impactful the reform may be for the average European 
online shopper. As such, a pair of sneakers bought by a French customer from a UK small business tod ay 
at 60 EUR + 15 EUR of shipping would come at 90 EUR (including 0 EUR of duties and 15 EUR of VAT at 
20%). With the reform, tomorrow they would cost 105.3 EUR (including 12.75 EUR of duties at 17% and 
17.55 EUR of VAT at 20%).  
According to the European Commission, 20% of European consumers had bought something online from 
outside the EU in 2022, a huge increase from 8% in 2014
12. With this proposal, the EU is therefore exposing 
these European consumers to an increase of up to 17% on the price of every 1 out of 5 of their online 
purchases. Yet, this price increase would not necessarily result in benefits for the public budget, as 
explained in the previous sections.

The Union Customs Code Reform 
 
Introduction 
EuroCommerce is the voice for six million retail, wholesale, and other trading companies that engage 
in a full range of cross -border supply chain activities, both physically and within eCommerce. These 
range from SME entrepreneurs importing fashion items through to major corporates in cutting edge 
retail and wholesale. Consequently, EuroCommerce has a crucial interest in reforming EU customs 
into a simpler, more reliable structure that fully harnesses the opportunities of the single market, both 
within the EU and in reaching export markets. 
 
As highlighted our position paper, ‘ The Future of EU Customs For a modern, frictionless customs 
environment’, published in July 2022, EuroCommerce strongly supports ambitions to take the EU to the 
next level through making the Union’s customs processes simpler with more effective, efficient, and 
harmonised controls. In that respect, we welcomed the work of the ‘Wise Persons Group’ and many of 
their conclusions contained in the report, ‘Putting More Union in the European Customs:  Ten proposals 
to make the EU Customs Union fit for a Geopolitical Europe ’. We have argued for many years that a 
frictionless, predictable, and responsive customs environment is crucial for the competitiveness of EU 
business at home and on the global stage, especially  for our members in omni -channel retail and 
wholesale sectors with extensive EU and international operations and global supply chains. Yet 
inconsistent interpretation and distortions in the application of the law at the operational level have 
been a persistent problem for EU business on the ground since 1993. Such inconsistencies remain and 
are, possibly, the greatest barriers to the EU’s economic vision for taking the Union to the next level. 
 
General  
The Union Customs Code provides a framework and structure for autonomous Member State 
authorities. EuroCommerce believes the timing for launching the Customs Reform Package is an 
exceptional and pivotal moment for the EU Single Market and businesses operating within it. The tone 
of the reform package is both ambitious and aspirational and advocates fundamental change. But such 
change must fundamentally enhance the current situation for all stakeholders. We cannot improve 
the conditions of one section to the inadvertent detriment of another.  
 
Whilst welcoming this reform, there are questions and concerns when it comes to details and timeline. 
We are especially concerned these proposals do not fully address the interests of SMEs nor some of 
the consequences to international trade. We note that the different elements and concepts were not 
tested with trade at the time the proposals were written. But we are keen to address this during the 
dialogue actively turning this work into functioning law in the Delegated and Implementing Acts and 
in the desi gn of systems and processes. We recall our position paper where we discussed the 
legislative process at the working level. For a reform of this magnitude, we must ensure that 
timetables are challenging but realistic. The process should avoid situations where trade is forced ‘to 
make it work’ against their best counsel. Using pilot schemes and other testbed facilities should be 
employed to help move the process forward and allow access to some of the simplifications earlier.  
 
We strongly urge that trade is included as full partners on a default basis during the dialogue to achieve 
the desired outcomes on entry into force of the new processes and procedures. Otherwise, there are 
concerns the EU will continue to operate with proc esses and procedures that have just shuffled the 
problems around or created new operational issues.

Specific Comments on the Proposal 
 
1. Trust & Check (T&C) Traders 
 
1.1. We welcome plans to extend simplifications/ facilitations to all traders, especially SMEs, under 
the new ‘Trust & Check Trader’ facility, together with the ‘Datahub’ concept. There is some 
support for these notions. But system security and business continuation are real issues for our 
members, especially as the proposals draw other agencies’ systems under the customs umbrella.  
 
1.2. Obtaining T&C status should not be an onerous hurdle, especially for SMEs. It must be 
remembered that SMEs and micro-SMEs are really not just scaled down ‘large companies’. They 
are different, not just in turnover, and have different dynamics and challenges. Large companies 
have more resources at their disposal, with ‘Compliance’ offices or teams. So, gaining T&C status 
is likely to be easier for them than for a small/ medium sized businesses or micro -SMEs. SME 
compliance operations are normally one pe rson handling a raft of operational functions, i.e., 
business regulation such as REACH and now CBAM, national, local corporate and social taxes 
obligations, application of the customs code rules and often basic accounting requirements. Thus, 
it is not poss ible to compare like with like and they face unfair competition compared to their 
large business counterparts.  
 
1.3. Yet, the conditions for becoming a T&C trader appear more difficult to meet than Authorised 
Economic Operator (AEO). T&C is mostly about giving customs real-time access to traders’ internal 
systems. In practice, this may prove a very difficult requirement for many, and es pecially micro 
and small traders, if not impossible and holds real cost implications. Visiting Special Report 
13/2023 of the European Court of Auditors: "Authorized Economic Operators - A solid customs 
program with untapped potential and uneven implementation", is encouraged. 
 
1.4. Adhering to the conditions and rules is paramount, regardless of profile. But SMEs need the 
simplest access and obligations to help them thrive. Yet they may still be disadvantaged and forced 
to use the most difficult and cumbersome standard processes for clearance simply because they 
are small businesses. That would be a serious an d unintended consequence of the reform. The 
result could distort competition between SMEs and larger businesses that have access to 
economies of scale and bulk buying.  
 
1.5. Therefore, the obstacles facing SME access to “Trust & Check” status should be examined, 
and methods to facilitate their access should be devised. A possible, transitional option may be to 
maintain AEO, together with current simplifications, which should not be controlled in a 
transactional manner as already intended. T&C status itself, could then be treated as a ‘next level’ 
simplification. In such a way SMEs could move up without making them worse off. 
 
1.6. Additionally, a key component of a T&C authorisation is the propriety of an applicant and 
whether they have contravened any rules or had action taken against them. The definitions 
relating to propriety only reflect deliberate circumvention/ fraud and negligence committed by a 
trader. We question if this covers all eventuali ties in the modern business world and whether 
current definitions fully reflect growing situations where businesses have acted in good faith and 
applied all reasonable due diligence but are victims of criminal activity and unwittingly contravene 
rules. Currently investigations can take place in the background and appear based on a 
presumption of guilt, not innocence and discriminate. It is therefore vital that the context of any 
transgression is duly considered, especially as customs is policing ever wider legislation, increasing 
the scope of what may be considered non-compliance.

1.7. In view of the strict requirements of Article 25, we believe the EU customs authorities should 
inform a T&C trader as early as possible when an investigation is opened on suspicion of 
circumvention of anti -dumping measures. This would offer the T&C trader the opportunity to 
actively engage in uncovering a fraud that has impacted on them unless the investigating authority 
has provable reasons to believe that the trust and check trader is actively complicit in such fraud. 
In our view, this would be a practical upgrade of the proposed trust and check trader mechanism. 
 
1.8. Additionally, a ‘checklist of ‘due diligence’ efforts a T&C trader could take to safeguard against 
false origin statements  would be useful. Also, the proposed EU Customs Authority could play a 
role in developing the criteria for "knew or ought reasonably to have known". We also believe that 
the New Authority’s  risk management tools could be employed to alert traders importing non -
preferential origin goods listed under trade protection measures. 
 
2. The Datahub 
 
2.1. In relation to the datahub, data -sharing provisions should be proportionate, and ensure a 
level playing field and be effectively enforceable against non -EU businesses as well. Overall, the 
data sharing mechanism and any documentary requirements need to be fully harmonized, 
removing the need to comply with EU27 systems and requirements.  The data submission to the 
EU Data Hub should be limited to the core minimum required for smooth entry across the borders, 
with all other data provided in a separate mode, through direct access to traders’ systems as per 
the Customs proposal. Equally important is to ensure that only the crucial data elements (safety 
and security data plus IOSS number plus unique consignment reference) are shared with through 
the EU Data Hub the moment the goods cross the EU borders, limiting the volume of information 
to be swiftly processed, so that the process works swiftly and reliably. 
 
2.2. We also call for reciprocity with customs authorities to share infringement related information 
with economic operators . This would  allow them to  upgrade their systems to prevent further 
infringements, in order  to collaborate with authorities to take down bad actors , and those 
providing false origin information or violating trade protection measures. 
 
2.3. We should like to see a clear and detailed plan for the security of operations and business 
continuity for all businesses. Traders are keen for reform to offer fluid and frictionless imports, 
movements, and exports. But the datahub will be a gateway for other surveillance/ licensing 
authorities that will have to be ready for when the system goes live. This could cause operational 
conflicts. Customs is a 24/7 environment, but that is not the same with other authorities or 
agencies involved. So, a clear roadmap is needed for the reform to be successful and not cause 
costly obstructions to real time operations. 
 
3. eCommerce/omni-commerce  
 
3.1. eCommerce/omni -commerce is still a  relatively new frontier for retail. Cross -border 
eCommerce business models vary significantly from small specialist retailers through to high-end 
marketplace platforms. We appreciate the work that has taken place already to address this 
modern market through devising rules and adjusting tax regimes to reflect this new sales channel, 
with the work on abolishing the minimum VAT threshold and the Import One Stop Shop.  
 
3.2. We believe the synchronisation between taxation regimes (VAT and Excise) has not been as 
addressed to all traders as it should have been in these proposals. The advent of Centralised 
Clearance for Import (CCI) will go some way to tackle this, but only for those able to access the 
facility. The reform is overall ‘light’ on the notion of improved alignment with other regimes to 
avoid duplication of data obligations and burdens. Such alignment is desirable for busi nesses of 
all profiles where there are overlaps. The Import One Stop Shop (IOSS) has operated reasonably 
since its launch three years ago. However, our position paper highlighted there are still

proposed reform works as envisaged and that the ‘deemed importer’ obligations are proportional. 
 
4. De Minimis Threshold 
 
4.1. A key proposal is to remove the 150  euros customs Low Value Consignment (LVC) threshold 
or ‘de-minimis’ to create a level playing field as well as revenues. We have seen the proposed draft 
directive to enact this proposal. Some of our members firmly support these proposals. They 
consider the abolition of the VAT minimum and implementation of the IOSS does not seem to 
have relieved what these members consider unfair competition. There are also real concerns 
there has been an influx of unsafe , non-compliant products  onto the EU market that puts 
consumers at risk; something that EuroCommerce wants stopped . Supporters of abolishing the 
LVC threshold strongly believe their competitive advantage is undermined by imports from certain 
third country markets because consumers can buy individual goods direct from them under the 
LVC threshold. But an EU company importing the same goods should normally need to import 
them in quantit ies that exceed the d e minimis, making the EU concern’s unit prices less 
competitive. Consequently, they welcome both the proposed removal of the 150 Euro threshold 
for Customs duties and the creation of ‘Deemed Importer’ as steps forward. A synthesis of those 
views suggests these proposals would support the ‘Circular Economy’ and protect consumers, as 
well as  help the enforcement of proposed EU regulations under the ‘Green Deal’ agenda ; 
especially where unsustainable materials  are involved that cannot be easily repaired, reused, or 
recycled. 
 
4.2. On the other hand, it was noted also that the abolition of the current d e-minimis would not 
necessarily solve the problem of under -declaration of the value of goods, which can happen in 
direct shipments from third countries. Additionally, a Copenhagen Economics study published this 
year on Customs duty de minimis ( https://copenhageneconomics.com/publication/study-on-
customs-duty-de-minimis/) concludes the removal of the LVC threshold could impact businesses 
and undermine the EU’s position in the global trade arena. 
 
4.3. The proposal to establish an EU Customs Authority may provide a solution. But it is unclear if 
the Commission had taken the Copenhagen Study into account in its Impact Assessment or 
examined other, perhaps more focused risk -based, options that could of fer economic and 
consumer market protections without impacting legitimate trade, benefitting from the LVC rules 
before proposing to eliminate the threshold? 
 
5. The EU Customs Authority 
 
5.1. The proposal for an EU Customs Authority is positive and has merits. The authority could play 
a positive role applying an effective and system -based control solution to ensure a level playing 
field and curb evasions, such as under -declarations, provided it is equipped with sufficient staff 
and resources.  
 
5.2. Better coordination for the future challenges will prove crucial. We foresee possible 
disruptions during the implementation phase due to changes in the existing IT infrastructure. This

5.3. But members are concerned that the introduction of an EU Customs Authority could 
potentially overlap with Member States' customs authorities’ responsibilities, creating an 
additional layer that causes conflicts impacting on traders’ operations. This situation could lead to 
duplication o f efforts, jurisdictional conflicts, and inconsistencies in the enforcement of 
regulations, underscoring the need for clear definitions of roles and solid coordination 
mechanisms. Currently, local customs authorities see m to have little autonomy outside of 
following the European Customs Authority’s instructions. So, they may be also limited in providing 
binding replies to trader’s questions – therefore there must be a mechanism for traders to discuss 
their problems and clarify questions direct, before, during and after any given transaction.  
 
5.4. There are also trade facilitation concerns over the proposals to deem a request ‘denied’ if a 
customs authority does not respond by a deadline. Apart from encouraging inefficient practices, 
we believe the default should be made the other way around. A trader may consider an 
application ‘authorised’ unless they are informed otherwise by a legal deadline, which follows the 
legal principles that they have acted honestly. 
 
5.5. We believe the current planned trajectory for the Authority would result in a significant 
missed opportunity. The Authority’s envisaged mission statement seems to fall short of what EU 
trade has called for since 1993 to correct practical operational an omalies and distortions in 
application. EuroCommerce anticipated such an authority would play a role that would improve 
inconsistencies of interpretation and distortions of treatment ‘on the ground’. The reform process 
offers the greatest opportunity for uniform application of EU customs rules across the union. This 
process should aim for  a minimum viable product , with  sufficient teeth to ensure  that best 
practice and processes are levelled upwards to that of the  member states with most advanced 
facilitation. 
 
5.6. Yet the plans to make this service exclusive of trade means it will operate at arm’s length from 
the very people it will impact direct. The dialogue so far suggests that the authority will operate 
outside of the legislative process and trade will have no means of direct engagement. We 
appreciate it will have a significant role to play in risk management and it is right and proper that 
it works behind firewalls in that regard. But it will also be working on elements of legislation that 
have direct bearing on the competitiveness of EU businesses and daily operat ions, such as 
valuation and origin questions and we strongly encourage these proposals are re-thought to allow 
a pathway for direct exchanges. 
 
6. Export 
 
6.1. The thrust of the reform proposals is focused on import of goods. However, we ask for more 
clarity on how these reform proposals will facilitate export operations and where they may tie 
more closely to systems in key export markets, such as the USA and the UK. 
 
7. Business Continuity 
 
7.1. The work on the reforms is planned to start while systems planned under the modernised 
UCC are still being implemented or have been launched recently. These include NCTS Phase 6, CCI 
Phase 1 and Phase 2, ICS2 and plethora of other systems at national and EU-wide level, including 
the Single Window. Many are still under various deployment phases, not being fully implemented 
across the EU, and may not be implemented by 2028, when the new systems would start kicking 
in. In addition, even before full implementation, systems like ICS2 would need to be amended, for 
example, to provide for the possibility of conveying a unique consignment reference number, a

8. Other issues 
 
8.1. Article 9: Annulment of favourable decisions. 
We feel guidance from the Commission is appropriate on the correct interpretation of the 
condition (b):  "reasonably should have known that the information was incorrect or 
incomplete". This is the common wording for release for free circulation under the second 
subparagraph of Article 77(3) of the Union Customs Code. Such guidelines should clarify which 
verifiable due diligence measures could be deemed as sufficient for satisfying the fo rmulation 
"reasonably should have known" (e.g., presentation of certificates of origin, issued by a particular 
national authority of the respective country of origin or a legally certified paper by  a particular 
chamber of commerce in the same country; physical inspection by the European importer in the 
respective third country before the EU import etc....).  
 
8.2. The development of such a guide would greatly assist importers in correctly fulfilling their 
obligations under Art. 20(1)(c): "...to ensure that goods brought into or out of the customs 
territory of the Union comply with the relevant other legislation appl ied by the customs 
authorities and to establish, retain and make available appropriate records of such compliance;" 
 
8.3. Article 11: Decisions taken without prior application. 
Additionally, some clarification is required for the instances where a customs authority acts as a 
judicial authority, Article 6(4), (5), (6), (7), Article 7(7) and Articles 8, 9 and 10 shall also apply to 
decisions taken by the customs authorities without  prior application by the person concerned. 
Due to the continuously increasing number of EU trade defence measures such as anti -dumping 
procedures, it would be advantageous for T&C traders, for example, to be assured that the 
customs authority acts as a ju dicial authority only after the official imposition of a penalty 
payment/additional customs duty and not earlier. 
 
8.4. Temporary Storage 
This is an important facility for the flow of goods. Currently, goods may be stored for 90 days, yet 
it is proposed to reduce that period to just 3 days. That could impact significantly on operational 
flows and chains, and we strongly urge the 90-day period to be restored. 
 
8.5. Dangerous Goods 
In relation to simplifications, full Customs declarations are required for dangerous goods for B2C 
shipping, for instance between the EU and UK. Full Customs declarations are costly, and shipping 
is not competitive. It is not so much an issue on larger B2B  transactions as the Customs cost is 
relatively a lot lower. To alleviate the costs, one member suggests the costs could be lowered 
without increasing risks by allowing T&C Traders to import such goods on simplified declarations, 
where all the safety and security requirements are met.  
 
9. Conclusion  
 
9.1 Successful EU trade, both at home and in third country markets is the creator of wealth for the 
European Union. A well -designed customs system  should recognise that competitiveness is just as 
important to the EU as a customs system designed to ensure effective controls. The key is to ensure 
legitimate traders are allowed to enact fluid and frictionless trade at import, in trans EU -movements 
and at export. 
  
9.2. We strongly urge officials to ensure trade is involved in all discussions to find solutions over the 
details to make this reform package a reality and to listen to trade’s counsel. The process should employ 
realistic timetables and the infrastructure should not replace systems that have recently been installed.

9.3 The legislative process should employ best practice and novel ways should be devised to test

9.4 The results of this reform will reset the direction of customs evolution for the next thirty years or 
more. Therefore, we must ensure this fundamental reform fundamentally enhances the current 
situation and the essential position of EU trade overall.

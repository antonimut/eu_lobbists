Introduction 
 
AEGIS Europe appreciates the opportunity to provide comments to the European 
Commission proposed Regulation establishing the Union Customs Code and the European 
Union Customs Authority and repealing Regulation (EU) No 952/2013 (hereinafter the 
“Revised UCC Proposal”).  
The EU's customs union is the bedrock of the single market and plays a crucial role in EU’s 
involvement in international trade, thus adopting and implementing appropriate rules is of 
tantamount importance. Hereinafter you may find the AEGIS Europe observat ions and 
suggestions to improve the existing legislative framework. 
The EU industrial sectors grouped within AEGIS Europe represent the entire value chain 
ranging from commodities such as ferro -alloys, non -ferrous metals or ceramics to end 
products like railway equipment, bicycles or solar panels. Our members account for a n 
annual turnover of €530 – €550 billion and 1.65 – 1.7 million of jobs across the EU.   
1. AEGIS Europe’s take on the current inward processing (IP) regime  
The applicable provisions of the current Union Customs Code provide that IP authorisations 
“shall be granted only where […] the essential interests of Union producers would not be 
adversely affected by an authorization for a processing procedure (economic 
conditions)”1.  
However, the way IP requests are currently examined undermines the practical effect of this 
provision. Member States are in charge of examining IP requests and in case one has 
evidence that the essential interests of Union producers are likely to be advers ely affected 
as a result of an IP request, it is required to submit it to the EU Customs Expert Group to have 
an examination of the economic conditions at EU-level.  
However, a Member State will only examine an IP request and its potential impact in the 
light of its national industry, thus avoiding an examination of a potential impact on Union 
producers located outside its territory. In case this Member State has no lo cal industry for 
that product, it is not able to perform an appropriate examination of the IP request in 
accordance with Article 211(4)(b) of the current Union Customs Code. Furthermore, Union 
producers cannot help the Member State to make a proper examina tion of the IP request 
and its potential impact, as IP requests are not public.  
Union industries are thus only entitled to provide their views on IP requests in case a Member 
State asks for an examination of the economic conditions at the EU -level by the Customs

Taking into account the fact that the interests of the Union producers have not been 
factored in properly in practice when assessing the economic conditions, AEGIS Europe and 
its members have been advocating for: 
• Increasing procedural transparency for applicants and EU producers. This 
should notably include a full and easy access to the non-confidential IP 
requests lodged via the he CSD (Customs Decisions System)  
• Establishing a database where non -confidential information about 
applications and authorisations are accessible to the public and interested 
parties and ensuring that EU industries can provide their views in time before 
any authorizations.  
• Facilitating the EU test also through the involvement of industry associations;  
• Establishing a threshold above which it is considered that any request for 
inward processing would prima facie  fulfil the economic interest test 
requirements. 
2. The IP regime under the Revised UCC Proposal 
Article 102 of the Revised UCC Proposal basically copies the existing rules in what concerns 
inward processing and thus the economic interests of Union producers will continue to be 
overlooked.  
Request to include the inward processing within new Article 102(4)  
AEGIS Europe has noticed a slight improvement in Article 102(4) when it comes to factoring 
in the economic interests of Union producers, whose current wording provides  that 
customs authorities, when assessing whether granting an authorisation for an outward 
processing (OP) procedure adversely affects the essential interest of the Union producers, 
they should request ex ante an opinion of the EU Customs Authority if evidence exists that

the essential interests of Union producers of sensitive goods are likely to be adversely 
affected. However, this provision should also cover inward, not only outward processing.  
Furthermore, the additional condition that there should be evidence that essential interests 
of Union producers are affected without giving the possibility to the latter to get involved 
renders the provision toothless. 
Including a ceiling cap above which any IP request is prima facie considered to affect the 
economic interest of the Union industry  
In what concerns the provisions of Article 102(5) AEGIS Europe considers that there is room 
for improvement. First a ceiling cap should be established at EU level beyond which there is 
rebuttable presumption that the Union producers’ economic interests would be affected in 
case an IP/OP authorization is granted. Second, the decision of the EU Customs Authority is 
not compulsory since the national customs authorities may disregard it provided that they 
motivate their decision, and such decision cannot be cha llenged by the interested parties 
(i.e. Union producers).   
There are also several positive developments like the fact that the Commission will be 
entrusted with the power to adopt delegated acts setting out (i) the conditions to grant 
authorisations and (ii) the cases where the economic nature of the processing justifies that 
the customs authorities assess whether granting an IP authorisation adversely affects the 
essential interest of the Union producers without the opinion of the EU Customs Authority.  
Another provision that AEGIS Europe welcomes is the fact that the Commission holds the 
competence to specify by means of implementing acts the procedural rules for granting IP 
authorisations and respectively for the EU Customs Authority to provide its opin ion in IP 
authorisation proceedings when asked to do so.  
3. The new EU Customs Data Hub  
AEGIS Europe welcomes the idea of setting up a new EU Customs Data Hub since it 
facilitates the exchange and combination of information in a single central environment 
managed by the EU Customs Authority.  
Inferring from recitals 18 & 22 of the Revised UCC Proposal, it appears that economic 
operators should have access to the EU Customs Data Hub, but it is not clear. Thus, we 
consider that in order to ensure a level playing field within the internal market f or all 
economic operators, they should be granted access to the EU Customs Data Hub.  
Union producers must be able to get access to the IP requests (not only authorizations) in 
order to exercise their rights of defence and put forward arguments that their economic 
interests might be adversely affected in case IP authorizations are granted. And the new EU 
Customs Data Hub could be conceived with a feature that includes access to IP requests 
for Union producers.  
This can be done through an easy access to the database and through non -confidential 
summary of IP requests to be prepared by each operator based on a number of objective 
conditions, to ensure that the Union industry may fully exercise its right of defence .

4. Compliance of imported products with EU regulations 
The Commission needs to ensure that the imported products placed on the EU market fully 
comply with all the EU regulations. Currently, the monitoring of products’ compliance with 
EU regulations is handled by Member States market surveillance authority and, at the 
border, by customs under the authority of market surveillance authorities, with some 
variations amongst the Member States.  
The non -food product safety mainly consists of the following two sets of legislative 
instruments which are also organizing the cooperation between Member States authorities: 
(i) Regulation 2019/1020 on market surveillance and compliance of products3, which covers 
harmonised products, and (ii) Directive 2001/95 on general product safety 4, which applies 
to any product intended for consumers and which became fully applicable in the EU on 15 
January 2004. These instruments are primarily focused on the question of products safety. 
The Rapid Alert Systems – which were established by Directive 2001/95 on general product 
safety - are mechanisms for reporting issues with food, feed and non -food consumer 
products. The Rapid Alert System for Non -Food Consumer Products (or RAPEX) covers 
unsafe, non -food, consumer products as well as professional products (e. g. machinery, 
lorries), that pose a serious risk to the health and safety of citizens and to the environment.  
It ensures that information about unsafe products withdrawn from the market and/or 
recalled from consumers anywhere in the EU is quickly circulated between Member States 
and the European Commission, so that appropriate action can be taken everywhere in the  
EU. Participation is mandatory for all Member States. The European Commission is notified 
of the details of products found to present a risk and therefore banned from the market in 
any of the Member States. Then, the Commission relays this information to all Member 
States. 
Despite the development of a regulatory environment, t he complex framework resulting 
from this juxtaposition of instruments hinders coordination and common market 
surveillance.  
Imbalances between Member States in customs controls result in goods being diverted 
towards the weakest entry points of the EU customs territory to avoid detection. Overall, an 
in-depth analysis shows that (i) market surveillance rules for industrial products are 
complex and experience in various fields demonstrates that they are not efficient; (ii) 
enforcement of regulations is fragmented and not fully effective; (iii) the Commission’s role 
is limited; and (iv) stakeholders play no part in products’ compliance monitoring.

In practice, there are many products placed on the market which do not meet the basic 
Union rules and standards. In addition, it is very important to emphasize that operators are 
not afraid to place on the Union market goods which do not meet Union require ments 
because there are no real sanctions attached to these infringements.  
This situation which has been criticized for long by the industrial sectors has been 
recognized in the report of the Wise Persons Group on the reform of the EU Customs Union 5 
which indicates in particular that “Customs cooperate with Market Surveillance Authorities 
[which] are a constellation of more than 600 bodies, placed under the responsibility of a 
dozen concerned national Ministries – from environment to industry or health - with a 
regulatory framework that depends on the topic, and with limited cooperation among 
them. […]. In the non -food sector – less uniform – controls are generally carried out by 
Customs, and Market Surveillance Authorities are notified by Customs of suspected non -
compliance and serious risks and take a decision that will then be enforced by Customs. 
Customs control priorities at EU borders remain defined at national level and there is no 
prioritization at the EU level”6.  
This report confirms the findings of a recent report by the EC JRC on the future of Customs 
in the EU 2040 which underlines in particular that the need for ensuring preparedness for a 
broadened role of Customs in protecting the European Union’s environment could go hand-
in-hand with other customs’ missions and more efficient operations in relation to trade and 
the controls of goods7. 
This situation is likely to be amplified in the near future, notably as a result of the 
environmental requirements the EU will impose on products. While the EU companies are 
subject to strong verifications for environmental considerations, it is likely tha t imported 
products – based on self-declarations – will be imported without verifying if they meet the 
Union’s environmental requirements.  
A central management function at EU level should be established  
This structure would be given investigative and enforcement powers on the control of 
imported products’ compliance with EU regulations. It would work directly with national 
customs authorities and act as a single point of contact for the submission of comp laints 
by EU economic operators, consumers’ associations or Member States.  The cooperation of 
EU economic operators is key to provide prima facie evidence that the EU Commission or 
Member States may not directly have.  
This structure could be hosted by DG TAXUD in coordination with DG GROW and DG TRADE 
or via a dedicated EU agency as proposed in the Wise Persons Group report. Member States 
would be fully associated with the functioning of this structure according to the existing

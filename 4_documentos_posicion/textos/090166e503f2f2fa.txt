ELIA GROUP CONTRIBUTION TO THE EC’s PUBLIC CONSULTATION ON THE UNION’s 
CUSTOMS REFORM 
Introduction 
Elia Group encompasses two electricity Transmission System Operators, Elia Transmission Belgium 
and 50Hertz in the northeast of Germany. Acting in the interest of society, and in line with its tasks of 
developing a secure, reliable and efficient transmission system with due regard to the environment, 
Elia Group strongly believe s the EU’s initiative to review the EU customs framework  – setting a 
pragmatic and simplified approach  - can be a strong tool to support the EU’s energy transition and 
its decarbonisation objective.  
 
In this contribution, Elia Group provides a view on some concerns which could be approached in the 
context of the EU’s customs reform, while formulating when possible, some first proposals . In 
summary, Elia Group’s main messages are the following: 
1. Requirements under Union customs law should not impede appropriate  and efficient operation 
and maintenance of offshore infrastructures located in the Exclusive Economic Zone of Member 
States and/or slow down fur ther build-out of needed offshore infrastructures . Consequently,  a 
flexible (and simplified) customs law regime supportive of the efficient and secure operation of 
offshore electricity in frastructures as well as of the further significant development of such 
infrastructures is called for in the context of the EU’s customs reform; 
 
2. Union customs law should, where not yet the case,  (further) facilitate the transfer and trade of 
electricity between Member States (‘MS’) and should avoid administrative burdens related to such 
activities. Accordingly, Union customs rules should unambiguously, if not yet the case, clarify that 
electricity may temporarily leave the U nion Customs Territory (‘UCT’), in those cases where said 
electricity is being transported between two MS, and this without resulting in the loss of status as 
a Union good so that no customs related requirements would apply; 
 
3. In respect of Union customs law, guidance should be provided as to how electricity volumes 
coming from outside the UCT should be quantified/measured for customs purposes considering 
that the volumes produced outside the UCT may not be equal to those which actually enter the 
UCT at its border. A lack of clarity on this point may lead to inefficient and discrepant practices; 
 
4. Union customs law should ensure TSOs are not in general to be considered as customs declarant 
as regards to the release for free circulation of electricity imported in the  UCT, knowing that they 
are not those who  produce nor, in general, either purchase or use said electricity for their own 
needs.

Main observations and proposals 
1. Current requirements under Union customs law may impede appropriate and efficient operation 
and maintenance of offshore infrastructures located in the Exclusive Economic Zone (‘EEZ’)  of 
Member States and/or slow-down further build-out of needed offshore infrastructure s, at a time 
where such infrastructures are key enablers to the EU’s decarbonisation strategy 
 
Under current Union customs rules bringing equipment/mater ials/tools/spare parts/etc. to/ from 
offshore electricity infrastructures located in the EEZ of a MS from/to the Union Customs Territory  
(‘UCT’) – e.g. for operation, maintenance and construction needs - may entail the performance of 
specific customs related requirements and hence numerous administrative/bureaucra tic burdens.  
Indeed, it can be derived from the current Union Customs Code (‘UCC’) that the EEZ, which is adjacent 
to the territorial sea of a MS, does not fall within the scope of the UCT. As a result, offshore electricity 
infrastructures, such as offshore electricity transmission cables, substations, platforms, energy islands, 
etc. located in the EEZ of MS are not considered as being within the scope of the UCT.   
Consequently, it is our understanding that customs requirements may have to be carrie d out and 
complied with, when (possibly a number of times during a same day) spare parts, materials, tools or 
equipment are transported from inland to offshore electricity infrastructures located in the EEZ (and 
inversely). In practice, such regime could lead to “undesirable” administrative burdens/ bureaucracy  
entailing unnecessary loss of time  to perform (urgent) offshore interventions  as well as some 
associated costs. Considering the need to guarantee effective, efficient and secure operation , 
including maintenance, of offshore electricity infrastructures, as well as to facilitate the development 
and deployment of offshore renewable electricity, it is questionable if the imposition of customs 
related requirements – and hence related bureaucracy - is adequate and proportionate.  
Indeed, compliance with such customs requirements, does not seem adjusted/adapted to the realities 
of offshore electricity activities/operations. Timely (and sometimes urgent) intervention is required to 
ensure effective and secure operations (Operation and maintenance  – “O&M”) of offshore electricity 
infrastructures and may not allow for  (full) performance of related  customs requirements (prior 
declarations, inspections, etc..). In view of the strong weather dependence of offshore activities (a.o. 
interventions), last minute changes are not uncommon and required. Moreover, aside from O&M 
aspects, the future build-out of new offshore electricity infrastructure could also be heavily impacted 
by unsuitable customs related requirements/excessive bureaucracy. 
Considering the need to facilitate the decarbonisation and electrification of the EU economy, a flexible 
(and simplified) customs law regime supportive of the efficient and secure operation of offshore 
electricity infrastructures as well as  of the further significant development of su ch infrastructures is 
called for in the context of the EU’s customs reform.

2. Union customs law should, where not yet the case, (further)  facilitate the t ransfer and trade of 
electricity between Member States and avoid administrative burdens related to such activities.  
Offshore electricity infrastructure is being developed and deployed across Europe to support the 
decarbonisation and climate neutrality ambitions of the EU. In Germany, 50Hertz has for example 
been involved in cross-border projects with the Danish transmission system operator to connect the 
German and Danish power grids across the Baltic Sea. This has resulted in the deployment of both 
the “Kontek” interconnector and the “Kriegers Flak – Combined Grid Solution1￼. 
A feature of these interconnectors is that electricity may be transported from one MS to another MS, 
but when being so temporarily leaves the UCT while crossing the EEZ of both Denmark and Germany. 
Based on current EU customs rules, it cannot be excluded that once leaving the UCT and crossing the 
EEZ of both these countries, the transported electricity loses its status as Union good and may hence 
be subject to Union customs law requirements and related bureaucracy (and this although in the end 
the electricity re-enters the UCT). 
Considering the need to facilitate the integration of renewable electricity in the European energy 
market any disproportionate administrative burdens (whi ch will generate costs a.o. in terms of 
efficiency and resources) should be avoided.  
Consequently, Union customs rules should unambiguously, if not yet the case, clarify that electricity 
may temporarily leave the UCT, in those cases where said electricity is being transported between 
two MS, and this without resulting in the loss of its status as a Union good so that no customs related 
requirements would apply. 
 
3. The quantification methods related to  the importation of electricity volumes from offshore 
production plants and/or installations located outside the Union Custom s Territory should be 
clarified. 
 
Ambitions for offshore wind production are ever increasing. Over the years, various offshore win d 
farms in various MS  have been connected to the inland transmission grid via various maritime 
transmission infrastructures. As regards to  current customs laws the transmission of electricity from 
offshore windfarms located outside the boundaries of the Union Customs Territory (e.g. the EEZ) is a 
relevant activity. We understand that for customs purposes imported volumes of electricity produced 
from such offshore installations need to be quantified. 
 
Theoretically, one could  assume measurement of the quantities  which enter the UCT would be 
performed through relevant equipment located at the border between the EEZ and the UCT . 
However, due to location of such border in the maritime area, performance of measurements through

specific dedicated equipment installed at such location is neither technically practical (e.g. Permitting) 
nor economically reasonable  or efficient . Aside from this option, other quantification/metering 
locations could be considered although these may not be optimal: 
 
 Onshore-side metering: The offshore connections end in an onshore substation, where 
measured values are recorded. However, this metering point may be geographically located 
several kilometers away from the entry point of the transported electricity in the UCT. It should 
also be noted that technical network losses – which cannot be avoided and which are simply 
a result of the laws of physics – will occur. Consequently, the quantities that are delivered 
inland (and measu red inland) will most likely not correspond to the amount of electricity 
entering the Union Customs Territory at its border; 
 
 Quantification as from offshore platforms: under this approach, volumes could be measured 
on the offshore platform before crossing the EU customs border and thus grid losses  for 
example would not be taken into account. 
 
With respect to this specific issue, guidance and clarification as to how electricity volumes should be 
quantified/measured for custom s related purposes  would be required , also considering that such 
regime may also have implications for electricity being imported from an interconnector with a 3 rd 
Country located outside the UCT . Any proposed  solution – for which consultation with relevant  
stakeholders, including relevant infrastructure operators would be recommended - should allow for 
a pragmatic and cost-effective approach, avoiding performance of disproportionate administrative 
or compliance burdens for the concerned parties.  
 
4. With respect to electricity imported from outside the UCT, Union customs law  should ensure 
Transmission System Operators , which do not  produce said electricity  nor in general either 
purchase it or use it for their own needs, are not considered as declarant for the release for free 
circulation of such electricity in the UCT. 
According to the information available to us, currently a lack of uniformity exists as to which 
actors/parties are required by national (customs) authorities to act as the customs declarant for the 
release for free circulation of electricity imported from outside the UCT . In this respect, TSOs which 
are not producing such electricity, nor  in general purchasing the imported electricity or using it for 
their own needs, and are merely performing their regulatory task  of transporting electricity injected 
in the network by  third parties, should, in our view, not  be responsible for lodging such customs 
declarations.  
In order to allow for such a result in the future, and considering the scope of the TSOs activities, Union 
customs law, should ensure that in respect of the above, TSOs are not  in general to be qualified as 
declarant for the release for free circulation of such electricity in the UCT .

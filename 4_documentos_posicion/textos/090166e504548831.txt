PROPOSAL FOR REVIEW OF THE WASTE FRAMEWORK DIRECTIVE 
This note intends to comment on the proposal for amendments of the Waste Framework Directive, submitted to public 
consultation by the European Commission on the 7th of July to the 17th of November 2023. 
Reference: 2023/0234 (COD) – 2008/98/EC 
Date: 22 Sept 2023 
Auteurs: Olivier Van Volden (ovanvolden@essenscia.be – +32 478 491 556) 
 
essenscia position addresses three issues: 
1. material neutral approach 
2. the proper use of the legal instrument of Extended Producer Responsibility, 
3. the alignment of the food waste reduction strategy with the requirements of the currently discussed proposal for 
Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation.

1 Introduction 
As the Belgian Federation for Chemicals, Life Sciences and Polymers, we have the unique distinction of covering both very 
demanding users as well as manufacturers of plastics. essenscia covers the entire value chain, from basic substances to 
recycling, including chemicals, detergents, healthcare products and food contact materials. Our coverage is therefore very 
broad.  
The EU has implemented a dedicated action plan to reduce waste, including both regulatory and non -regulatory actions, 
as part of the Circular Economy Action Plan and the Green Deal.  essenscia strongly believes that a systemic approach to 
assess the objectives of this plan is paramount to avoid unexpected shift of environmental impact or undesired harm to 
the sustainable development goals.  
For these amendments, the European Commission proposes to act respectively on textile waste and food waste.  
1.1 Textile waste management 
The reduction and valorisation of textile waste is mainly depending on the legal instrument of Extended Producer 
Responsibility as defined in Article 8 of the Waste Framework Directive  (WFD). The proposal extends the scope of waste 
management to consumer textile, in a harmonised way.  
It should be noted that textile was already covered as the Directive is targeting any type of waste, since a holder of an item 
expresses an intention of discard this item as waste. The amendment is therefore  only providing some clarifications on 
what type of textile waste should be addressed first by the Member States in charge of implementing these amendments. 
Amendments for textile waste should be consistent with the ongoing talks on Ecodesign for Sustainable Product 
Regulation (ESPR - 2022/0095(COD)), defining the requirements for textile to be placed on the market. To our knowledge, 
such consistency check has not been carried out yet.  
Aside of this harmonising intent , the proposed amendment  introduces product norms concepts , which constitutes an 
important issue.  
1.2 The reduction of food waste  
In the proposal, reducing food waste is mainly about setting ambitious targets. The implementation of these targets is left 
to the discretion of the Member States, and therefore to the discretion of the regions if they are responsible for these 
issues. On the face of it, this already goes against the food policies at European Union level, including Farm to Fork or the 
Common Agricultural Policy, as the proposal to reduce food waste also covers agricultural waste. 
The proposed amendment is claimed to be based on scientific evidence, which suggests that an impact assessment and a 
regulatory alignment with all related regulatory proposal, including packaging and packaging waste regulation, is 
paramount. Such impact assessment, to our knowledge, does not exist.  
Moreover, the proposed amendment is based on economical simulations, concluding that the reduction of food waste may 
have a benefit, which is obvious from the start.  
The reduction of food waste is not about the targets but about the way to achieving the targets without jeopardising food 
supply. The way to achieve the target must be explained in the text. 
2 Material neutrality 
WFD is a material-neutral piece of legislation that focuses on any wasted item. While the WFD can specifically target some 
product categories (such as textiles or food waste), the instrument is not suited to focus on a specific type of material 
family that ends up in different product categories. In this respect, the PPWD was designed to address packaging and 
packaging waste as a product category. The waste aspect of PPWD and PPWR is and should be therefore consistent with 
the material neutrality of WFD. 
There is a rising trend to shift the purpose of product-based legislation to material-based legislation. In particular, the EC 
assumes that only plastics and polymers deserve special attention by restricting their use based on recyclability or 
recycled content.

This assumption is based on the partly proven statement that other materials are already recycled and reused in products. 
However, this can be challenged in terms of product categories, material properties or recycling requirements. An 
important aspect of such a challenge is that many 'other' materials are used in closed-loop recycling, or their closed-loop 
recycling is tied to a single use, shape, etc.  
This does not apply to polymers and plastics, which are versatile and to their wide range of recycling technologies 
available:  
• mechanical recycling to recover plastics,  
• physical recycling to extract additives and to recover both polymers and substances,  
• chemical recycling allowing partial depolymerisation to shorter polymer chains and oligomers or full 
depolymerisation to monomers or the recovery of substances suitable for other applications. 
By not being neutral, legislative proposals ultimately promote material shifts (as observed in the interpretation of the 
Single Use Plastic s Directive), raise hygiene and environmental concerns due to the lack of impact assessments, and 
ultimately do more harm than good to sustainable development goals. 
Therefore, we call for  maintaining a material -neutral approach to the waste -related framework and avoiding 
proceeding as indicated in the proposed amendments. 
In other words, we call for that any requirement either on product or waste item must be material neutral, i.e., applied 
to all materials in the same way, with the same demands to demonstrate compliance. 
3 Extended producer responsibility 
We would like to express our concern in the fact that the proposal sets a precedent for future Extended Producer 
Responsibility schemes (EPR) when including the principle of the eco-modulation fee.  
The EPR scheme aims at valuing waste  by avoiding its destruction in accordance with the basic principles set out in the 
Waste Framework Directive. The EPR scheme is defined under the WFD and therefore requires the acquisition of waste 
status.  
In this respect, it should be recalled that the Lansink's ladder ranks ways to avoid destruction of waste, among which are 
avoiding discarding an item as waste (=prevention), reusing a wasted item (reuse before becoming waste is not part of the 
WFD), recycling, etc. 
The EPR system is foreseen under Article 8 of the Waste Framework Directive as a proportional instrument involving 
producers, public authorities, and end users including consumers. This instrument is driven by the reduction of the waste 
environmental impacts, which allows it to not strictly follow the Lansink's ladder.  
Article 8 of the Single Use Plastics Directive, (EU) 2019/904, also refers to Article 8 of the Waste Framework Directive but 
is surprisingly interpreted as a non -proportional instrument by some Member States and to our best knowledge, by the 
European Commission itself, on the contrary of the law. This may be explained by a confusion between the placing on the 
market and the valuation of waste. 
Although the charge for EPR is levied at the time of placing on the market (e.g. packaging) or at the time of purchase (e.g. 
electronic equipment), it is only linked to the end of the product's life and it is not a characteristic assigned on the product 
itself. It is however based on its nature or composition.  
The proposed eco modulation is addressing aspects of products such as recyclability and recycled content.  
• the term recyclability is not yet defined, as the EC has just asked the JRC to provide more insights on the possible 
criteria to assess recyclability.  
• recycled content is, per definition, the proportion, by mass, of recycled material in a product . Undoubtedly this 
applies to a product placed on the market. Furthermore, there is no  known advantage for waste management if 
a recycled content is present: it does neither improve collection, nor sorting nor recycling.  
The EPR instrument cannot be used to set up eco-modulation schemes based on recycled content, which is applicable to 
the placing on the market of an item, before any consideration of its end-of-life.  
Furthermore, there are already various schemes in place between Member States without boundaries as the European 
Commission failed to clarify how to assess the proportionality of the EPR schemes, between stakeholders and between 
Member States. In the worst-case scenario, unreasonable EPR schemes may be considered as a tool for banning products 
from the national market, which is in infringement of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, TFEU.

Belgium EPR scheme on household packaging waste has introduced a prohibitive fee based on technical and 
scientific evidence that a packaging is posing issues in sorting or recycling, leading to contamination of other 
streams. This double normal fee is used to promote better design for sorting and design for recycling. And it 
is not proportional to unknown criteria or related to product norm requirements. 
Packaging EPR schemes are market dependent. When products are more easily recycled, or when there is a 
high demand for the waste stream, the net cost of the EPR scheme will be lower, which will result in a lower 
contribution fee to the EPR for that kind of products. Being recycled is therefore in the economic interest of 
the producer placing the item on the market.  
These considerations are important as the EC is re -defining the EPR principles in several Green Deal related proposals 
such as ESPR, ELV, and PPWR, while still referring to the WFD, with which the new principles are not consistent. 
We strongly call for a re-evaluation of the new parameters of the EPR scheme and the exclusion of product standards 
(recycled content) from its establishment. 
4 Food waste reduction versus packaging and packaging waste regulation  
Any new policy on food should be consistent with the existing regulatory framework. In the proposal introduction, the EC 
recalls its intention to address food, packaging and textile waste in the Green Deal. While the link between textiles and 
packaging may not be straightforward (with the exception of B2B transport, e-commerce or dry cleaning packaging), there 
is a clear link between food and packaging. 
On the one hand, the EC recognizes the importance of food in the PPWR by defining a specific packaging scenario for it 
under the concept of "contact sensitive". On the other hand, the regulatory package on food and beverages is quite large, 
including food law, drinking water, fruit juice directive, food contact materials, etc. 
Looking at the intent of both the PPWR and the food waste reduction targets, there are several divergences (such as 
avoiding preserving fruit and vegetables with packaging) that suggest regulatory misalignment. 
In the Setting the Scene document 1, JRC points out the complexity of food waste generation . The scientific document 
states the fact that the largest amount of food waste is generated at the consumer. The scientific assessment does not 
acknowledge the fact that not every part of 'food' is edible by humans, which ultimately leads to some waste. Furthermore, 
current data show that more than 50% of the food waste comes from fruit and vegetables. The proposed targets are 
considered as the results of the JRC study which are actually outputs of an economical analysis using a model suitable 
for agriculture and food. This impact assessment is clearly insufficient to assess the systemic feasibility of the targets.  
Moreover, in the current waste framework, it should be remembered that compost is a waste in the first place. So the 
'composting requirement' option will not reduce food waste at all , although some are considering this option as a food 
waste reduction option. 
These considerations underline the fact that the EC has not chosen to address the issue of food waste in the broadest 
sense of its complexity, but only by setting some rules (such as the reduction target) to be implemented at the discretion 
of Member States. 
We ask that consistency is ensured and that the obvious links between targets are assessed. Only in this manner will MS 
understand the routes to achieve the targets set by the EC in an effective and sustainable manner. 
We call for a review of consistency: 
• at scientific level, 
• with other food legislation, 
• with the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation, where food is the content of the packaging, 
• with climate change mitigation.

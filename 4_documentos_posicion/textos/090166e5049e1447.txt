Textile Industry's Feedback on the Proposed Revision of the Waste Framework 
Directive 
Amsterdam | 21 November 2023  
 
The Policy Hub – Circularity for Apparel and Footwear, uniting more than 700 brands, retailers, 
manufacturers, and other stakeholders within the industry and representing more than 50% of the apparel 
and footwear sector welcomes the Commission's efforts to  revise the Waste Framework Directive (WFD). 
This paper outlines the key recommendations to improve the targeted revision further.  
 
Executive summary  
The proposed revision of the Waste Framework Directive (WFD) acknowledges several fundamental points 
for the success of the legislation. These include the call for harmonisation of Extended Producer 
Responsibility (EPR) requirements, ensuring alignment with other relevant regulations, the inclusion of 
Research and Development (R&D) investments, and restrictions on the export of products (used and waste 
textiles). However, the textile industry has identified areas for improvement, such as:  
1. proper enforcement,  
2. the role of online platforms and fulfilment service providers,  
3. re-evaluating the exclusion of micro-enterprises,  
4. mandating eco-modulation alignment,  
5. harmonising reporting rules,  
6. refining PRO reporting requirements, 
7. ensuring clear and measurable definitions,  
8. harmonising the criteria for sorting in relation to preparation reuse and preparation for recycling. 
 
To address these concerns and promote effective implementation, the Policy Hub calls for collaboration 
between the industry and policymakers to foster an efficient and scalable market for secondary raw textile 
materials and to meet the objectives of the WFD. 
 
We specifically welcome the outlined points below: 
● Harmonisation: We welcome the call for harmonisation of requirements across the EU regarding 
the Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) scope, definitions, waste hierarchy operationalisation, 
and cost coverage. This is a crucial element to ensure the functioning of the single market, improve 
the effectiveness of EPR schemes, and ease the administrative costs for businesses operating across 
Europe. The proposal can be strengthened by harmonising fee structures and eco -modulation, 
reporting (rules and guidelines), Member States targets, and KPIs.  
● Coherence with other files: We support the alignment with other relevant regulations, such as the 
Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR) and Waste Shipment Regulation (WSR). In 
this regard, implementation timelines should also be considered ; otherwise, there is the risk of 
implementation gaps and loopholes inadvertently causing unstable market dynamics that result in 
delayed investments in large -scale capital projects crucial to achieving much higher levels of 
recycling capacity. 
● Cost Coverage: We appreciate the inclusion of Research and Development (R&D) investments in 
the proposal. However, we emphasise the need for careful implementation to ensure it is fit for 
purpose and build on a market-driven approach. 
● Export of Textiles: We consider it a positive addition that only actually reusable garments should 
be allowed to be shipped outside the EU (see point 9 ) to address better those used garments that

1. Enforcement: Closing potential loopholes & creating a level playing field 
Administrative and enforcement challenges may arise for businesses and competent authorities concerning 
the requirements set under the Extended Producer Responsibility. These could become specifically relevant 
in the context of identifying the producer obligated (see art. 22c). To ensure that EPR fees for a product are 
paid in the country where the items will be managed as waste, we urge authorities to consider the following 
elements related to identifying the obligated producer: 
a) Administrative challenges  for businesses, for example, when selling a product through various 
channels such as retail, directly online, or through physical shops. The harmonisation of reporting 
requirements will be critical in addressing this issue. 
b) Enforcement challenges for the authorities since, for example, monitoring the compliance of a 
multitude of economic operators making products available on EU markets and products being sold 
through various channels (see above) will be resource intensive. 
The correct designation of producers obligated is crucial to secure efficiency and simplicity to address these 
challenges, which in turn can only be fully tackled through sufficient oversight and enforcement 
mechanisms.  
 
Ensuring proper compliance with the EPR requirements will create a level playing field and incentivise the 
obligated producers to live up to their obligations. It will also ensure that those entities receiving 
contributions from the EPR scheme to handle us ed and waste textiles are properly using the funding to 
deliver improvements in the management of end-of-life textiles. Otherwise, there might be a risk of creating 
potential loopholes for producers to avoid their EPR obligations. This is specifically relevant in cases of non-
EU-based producers without a legal entity in Europe. Therefore, we urge the EU authorities to consider 
mandating non -EU-based producers to designate a legal representative in the EU (like the Market 
Surveillance Authority Regulation and the Digital Services Act). 
      
2. The Role of Online Platforms and Fulfilment Service Providers 
We appreciate the inclusion of online platforms and distance sales under the producers' obligated (22a,7b). 
However, we suggest further detailing online platforms' obligations vis-à-vis their sellers. Inspiration could 
be drawn from  the French approach, wherein marketplaces must check their sellers' registration number  
before putting a product on the market. If the seller has not provided the ID number, the platform bears 
the responsibility on their behalf. And consequently, must fulfil the EPR requirements  for those sellers 
(including the notification of the sold quantities and payment of the accruing EPR fees).

Under French law, the marketplace is responsible for creating and maintaining a register containing the 
identification details of each seller in the French market, their VAT number, their unique ID number, and 
the quantities of products covered by EPR schemes by category sold by each seller. Fulfilment service 
providers should additionally be covered under the scope of these obligations. This will be critical to ensure 
proper enforcement of those requirements otherwise, an equal level playing field is at risk.  
 
3. Exclusion of Micro-Enterprises 
Regarding the economic operators in scope for EPR, we understand the need to exclude certain businesses 
from EPR obligations due to potentially negative economic implications. However, such exclusion could 
create loopholes in an attempt of certain actors to escape EPR fees (see point b). These could be 
addressed using a different metric, such as the volumes placed on the market or revenue. 
There is a lack of data to support the exclusion of micro/social enterprises . Therefore, we recommend 
conducting a study on micro -enterprises contribution to waste generation and revising the exemption 
based on the findings. Additionally, a phased implementation approach might be better suited to set the 
right incentives for microenterprises. 
4. Management of textile waste: A stronger focus on recycling infrastructure 
EPR is fundamentally an instrument designed to address the systemic issues in textiles' end-of-life collection 
and management. 1 Supporting the development of high -quality recycling is one of its key objectives to 
address the insufficiencies regarding collection, sorting, and preparation for recycling. 
At the same time, a recent McKinsey study 2 finds that the entire textile value chain dealing with the end -
of-life of products (i.e., collection, sorting, and recycling) still requires significant investments. Specifically, 
fiber-to-fiber recycling is considered immature. McKinsey (2022) estimates that investments in the range 
of €6 billion to €7 billion would be needed by 2030. While reuse is a critical element to prevent waste and 
the core of the waste hierarchy, we also recommend a stronger focus of the objectives of the EPR on 
recycling (and the related infrastructure). This also relates to the End -of-Waste Criteria, which are to be 
developed for both reuse and recycling . Through determining when a waste ceases to be waste, the work 
of recyclers will be simplified in dealing with such material streams, as waste legislation (such as WSR) will 
no longer apply. Additionally, the quality of feedstock for recycling will be improved. 
Further, the focus on recycling could be achieved by setting adequate targets aligned with the list of 
activities under the EPR schemes (e.g., collection, sorting, and recycling). In this context, harmonisation of 
targets on the EU level is key to avoiding market fragmentation. The definitions and methods for calculating 
these targets must be harmonised at the EU level. 
Companies’ take-back schemes can be a valuable way to complement EPR schemes in helping Member 
States achieve their separate waste collection targets. They can be a central tool to incentivise more 
responsible consumer behavior. With the  European Commission’s  proposal, we see the risk  that such 
schemes might no longer be accepted. We, therefore, urge the EU authorities to pave the way for such 
take-back schemes. A way to further incentiv ise those schemes is , for example, through ecomodulation 
bonus, for companies that implement such schemes.

5. Cost Coverage and Eco-modulation of fees 
The EPR fees should not exceed those necessary to ensure the circularity of textile products in a cost-
efficient way. EPR schemes should only cover the costs of collection, sorting, and preparation for reuse and 
preparation for recycling in line with the objectives of EPR.  
 
We support the inclusion of R&D as part of the costs covered under the EPR schemes. Yet, the proposed list 
of activities (article 22a (4)) covered under the revision of the Waste Framework Directive needs more 
clarity. It is unclear whether the preparation for recycling and recyc ling are covered under the cost. This 
uncertainty creates a major risk, given the urgent need for investments to have scalable options for textile 
recycling (see point 2). Therefore, we call for an unambiguously formulated list of activities that should be  
covered under the EPR schemes for textiles, including the costs for collection, sorting, preparation for 
reuse, and preparation for recycling. We support a marked-driven approach as the most effective way to 
create an efficient market for secondary raw textile materials, and we urge EU Member states to prioritise 
this in implementing the EPR scheme. Furthermore, EPR schemes could  support the development 3 of 
recycling infrastructure through targeted investments in innovation and research that  address 
challenging waste feedstock compositions. 
We welcome the proposal's focus on the eco -modulation of EPR fees in line with the future ESPR's 
ecodesign requirements and assessment methods or other Union law establishing harmonised 
sustainability criteria and measurement tools for textile products, ESPR priorities, and the waste hierarchy. 
However, we recommend clarifying to mandate this alignment through an implementing act.  While the 
ESPR will set the minimum requirements that a product needs to fulfil to be placed on the market, eco -
modulated fees need further differentiation (e.g., identifying the characteristics of the best -performing 
products). Even if based on the ESPR,  the member states can still develop their own approaches to fee 
modulation (e.g., differences in documentation), which will result in little to no incentive for companies that 
sell on various EU markets to meet those requirements. Therefore, harmonisation of fee eco-modulation 
principles, priorities, and documentation (i.e., reporting) is urgently required.  
6. Harmonised Reporting Requirements for Companies to the PROs.  
To maintain and encourage a harmonised approach across the single market, stronger emphasis should be 
placed on the harmonisation of reporting rules  for companies , including timing, frequency, and 
operationalisation. Such a harmonised reporting mechanism would help save costs, streamline the process, 
and motivate participation. 
 
Additionally, harmonising reporting requirements should further consider the interoperability and 
alignment of data and digital systems for smooth data exchange between public and private players. 
 
7. The Role of Producer Responsibility Organisations (PROs) 
Strong coordination between Member States' PROs can foster the PROs' effectiveness, economy of scale, 
and joint investments. This could be ensured via an EU-wide PRO consortium. 
 
Additionally, there should be more leverage for the national PROs and the Member States authorities to 
decide what and how information to consumers should be communicated. Pre-determining communication 
activities in the revision of the Directive could be counterproductive in identifying actions that drive impact.

Paragraph 22c (13) -(d) requires PROs to report on 'fast fashion' practices. However, this requirement is 
unclear and confusing due to the lack of a clear definition of 'fast fashion' . A clear and measurable 
definition is essential to achieve successful implementation and effective consumer engagement.  
 
The revisions made in the proposal include new definitions that align with the provisions of the Packaging 
and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR). These definitions, such as 'making available on the market,' 
'producer responsibility organisation,' and 'online platform,' align with the PPWR. However, there is a slight 
difference in the definition of "consumers," as it mentions 'craft' and natural persons acting for purposes 
outside their trade, business, or profession. These  aligned definitions ensure consistency and coherence 
between the proposed regulations and the PPWR. 
9. Harmonised criteria for sorting in relation to preparation reuse and preparation for recycling. 
Currently, sorting companies are responsible for determining what textile products shall be prepared for 
reuse or recycling based on their (potential) economic benefit. In practical terms, sorters can classify any 
type of garment as ready for 'reuse'.  Today, used garments can be exported that (1) are not fit for reuse; 
or (2) for which there is no market in the export destinations . Since exporting ‘reusable’ garments is more 
cost-effective than feeding these materials into European recycling streams, it is likely that this trend will 
persist. In this context, the proposed additional information requirements for shipping documentation of 
reused textiles will not significantly change the current status quo. 
To ensure sufficient and reliable feedstock for a functioning textile recycling industry in the EU, we call for 
harmonised definitions and standards on what is to be deemed 'prepared for reuse/recycling', as well as 
further clarification on the sorting obligations and collection points. This is a necessary pre -requisite for 
developing the End-of-Waste criteria.  
Conclusion  
In conclusion, the recommendations put forth by the textile industry highlight the importance of addressing 
key concerns in the proposed changes to Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) regulations. If 
implemented correctly, there is significant potential  for building an economy of scale for textile recycling 
and promoting a circular economy. We invite policymakers to collaborate with the textile industry in 
refining and implementing these regulations effectively, ensuring transparency, accountability, and  
sustainable practices in managing textile waste. Together, we can work towards a more sustainable and 
efficient approach to textile production.

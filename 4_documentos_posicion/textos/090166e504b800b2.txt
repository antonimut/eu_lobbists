Feedback regarding the proposal on food waste as 
part of the Waste Framework Directive 
Introduction  
EuroCommerce welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback to the  Commission proposal to set 
legally binding food waste reduction targets for Member States to achieve by 2030, as part of the 
revision of the Waste Framework Directive . If set proportionately, these targets can accelerate the 
reduction of food waste along the food supply chain and simultaneously, advance the EU’s efforts in 
meeting Sustainable Development Goal Target 12.3 (SDG Target 12.3). This, in turn, can have a positive 
impact on enhancing the resilience and sustainability of food systems.  
 
Retailers and wholesalers have been proactive and successful in reducing food waste1. Some have set 
their own ambitious targets to reduce food waste within their operations and publicly report on 
their progress. Several others have made concrete commitments to preventing food waste under the 
EU Code of Conduct  on Responsible Food Business and Marketing Practices 2. Acknowledging the 
urgency of addressing food waste as a pressing global issue, retailers and wholesalers support and 
participate in international initiatives, such as the Consumer Goods Forum Food Waste Resolution3, 
under which leading food manufacturers and retailers have pledged to halve the amount of food 
waste within their operations by 2025 and World Resources Institute's 10x20x30 initiative 4, where 
over 10 global retailers have committed to engaging and guiding at least 20 of their suppliers to halve 
food loss and waste by 2030.  Moreover, EuroCommerce has been an active member of the EU 
Platform on Food Losses and Food Waste  and has contributed to the development of 
Recommendations for Action in Food Waste Prevention5.  
 
The effectiveness of the sector in tackling food waste is evident not only through the Champions 12.3’s 
assessment of SDG Target 12.3 on Food Loss and Waste in the 2022 Progress report6 which highlights 
the active role of the sector and in some respects, its leadership in reducing food waste. It is also 
reflected in both the first (released in October 2022) and second (released in September 2023) EU-
wide monitoring  reports of food waste published by Eurostat 7, which reveal that the retail and 
wholesale sector is the sector generating the least amount of food waste, accounting for only 7% of 
the total food waste generated in both 2020 and 2021.

Detailed comments 
▪ Baseline  
According to Article 9 of the Waste Framework Directive8, Member States need to annually report on 
their levels of food waste, using 2020 as a reference year. We believe that 2020 is not an appropriate 
reference year , considering it is the  year marked by the start of the COVID -19 pandemic. It 
significantly disrupted the normal course of everyday life, with food services being obliged to remain 
closed for an extended period of time. The large majority of retailers and wholesalers continued their 
operations, managing higher sales volumes due to the closures in the  food services sector . 
Remarkably, even during that challenging period, the retail and wholesale sector remained resilient 
and managed to generate the least amount of food waste. While the Commission suggested that some 
of the COVID-19-related effects on the food supply chain were balanced out, such as the reduction in 
restaurant consumption being offset by higher household consumption, these mitigating 
circumstances have not yet been adequately analysed. 
Moreover, retailers and wholesalers started implementing initiatives to reduce food waste before 
2020. As such, the proposed baseline will not reflect the different starting years and extensive efforts 
undertaken by these actors for more than a decade. The proposed baseline also stands in contrast to 
earlier baselines mentioned in both SDG Target 12.3 and the European Parliament’s resolution on the 
Farm to Fork Strategy9.  
We support the Commission’s flexibility that allows Member States to derogate from the 2020 
baseline and choose an earlier one. Nonetheless, this flexibility is granted exclusively to Member 
States and would depend on the data collected by national authorities. It may be the case that some 
of them lack data to support an earlier baseline year. On top of that, it remains unclear how much 
data Member States would need to provide to justify a different baseline year. We, therefore, strongly 
advocate for the acknowledgment of the efforts and progress made so far by actors who possess valid 
data. In the event of choosing an earlier baseline year, any concerns regarding data comparability 
should be addressed to avoid potential disruptions in the operations of retailers and wholesalers 
functioning across multiple Member States.  
 
In general, choosing 2020 as the baseline year risks neglecting the efforts and progress made by the 
sector for more than a decade. Therefore, flexibility is essential to take into account the respective 
baselines in each Member State and the progress that has already been achieved by the retail and 
wholesale sectors.  
 
▪ Targets 
  
o Targets covering the whole food supply chain, from farm gate to the final 
consumer 
 
Food waste occurs all along the food supply chain : on the farms  (9%), during processing and 
manufacturing (21%), in restaurants (9%), in retail (7%) and at home (54%)10. This proposal offers the 
possibility to establish an enabling environment for all food actors to monitor, report, and reduce food 
waste. By omitting setting targets for the primary production, we miss the chance to fully address the 
food waste challenge. SDG Target 12.3 itself states that food losses along production, including post-
harvest losses, should be reduced. This point is echoed by the European Parliament, which suggested 
that “binding targets at every stage of the supply chain, including primary production, pre -retail and

o A joint target for retail, food services, and households could be problematic 
 
EuroCommerce would like to express its reservations regarding the proposed reduction by 30% per 
capita, jointly in retail and other distribution of food, in restaurants and food services and in households 
by 2030. More than half of the food waste is generated at household level (54%), whereas the retail 
sector accounts for the least food waste generation (7%). Despite the significant difference in their 
contributions to food waste, they are included in the same target . Implementing, enforcing, and 
measuring such a joint target could likely present challenges and risks of holding sectors accountable 
without proper recognition of their efforts.  
 
Retailers and wholesalers aim to prevent food waste from occurring in the first place . They achieve 
this by adhering to the waste hierarchy and implementing measures within their supply chain, in their 
shops, and beyond . They invest in technological solutions to improve their forecasting systems, 
ensuring a better alignment of demand and supply , and explore alternative uses for unsold food 
products, such as repurposing fruits and vegetables into soups and jams. The sector is actively 
engaging with consumers via different channels to raise awareness on how to avoid food waste. This 
includes sharing recipes for leftovers, offering tips on proper food storage and removing date marking 
from specific products, such as fruits and vegetables, to address consumer confusion regarding date 
labels. Where there are food surpluses, retailers and wholesalers take action by donating to food 
banks and charitable organisations. 
 
The sector is fully aware of its responsibility and actively takes measures within its control to combat 
food waste.  However, the factors contributing to its generation differ significantly across various 
segments of the supply chain. At the household level, for instance, food waste can occur during various 
phases, including planning, storing, shopping, preparing, and consuming. In fact, consumer food waste 
results from a complex set of different behaviours, including psychological, socio -cultural, and 
emotional factors ,12 and reducing it requires different approaches and measures . Consequently, 
exerting complete influence over household behaviour  is beyond the sector’s control , making it 
essential to differentiate between these two sectors and measure food waste separately for each 
stage, as suggested in Article 1 of the Commission Delegated Decision on a common methodology13.  
 
The retail and wholesale sectors and households only share two common methods for measuring food 
waste: direct measurement and composition analysis 14. When it comes to quantifying food waste 
retained for home composting within households, there is currently no comprehensive overview of 
applicable methodologies 15. This situation raises questions about how these two sectors are 
methodologically interconnected and reinforces the need to measure food waste separately.

Furthermore, it is not clear what the terms “per capita” and “ jointly” mean in this context. Some 
retailers and wholesalers measure food waste per square meter of sales and distribution space  in 
comparison to food products sold and therefore, it is unclear how they can contribute by “per capita”. 
 
• Proportionate accountability  
 
According to the Commission’s impact assessment, proposing a joint target for the retail, food services 
and households offers Member States the flexibility to address food waste hotspots based on their 
national circumstances. While this approach is advantageous for accommodating the specificities of 
each Member State, it is essential to ensure that the proposed targets and potential national initiatives 
for preventing food waste are proportionately designed, taking into account the amount of food 
waste generated by each sector. Any disproportionate burden on retailers and wholesalers in their 
efforts to reduce food waste should be avoided . Disproportionate regulatory and administrative 
burdens could present substantial challenges for small and medium -sized enterprises (SMEs), as 
acknowledged in the impact assessment report17. 
▪ Policy Enablers  
o Supporting Incentives 
 
Article 9a(1)(c) of the proposal invites Member States to take appropriate measures to encourage 
food donation; an effective way to further reduce food waste.  While the EU VAT Committee has 
provided guidance enabling Member States to grant VAT reliefs for food donations under specific 
conditions, not all national governments have opted to enact such  measures18. As a result, business 
operators in several Member States  encounter financial and administrative obstacles when 
redistributing surplus food. Implementing supportive incentives  at national level , such VAT 
exemptions and deductions, could significantly facilitate food donations and contribute substantially 
to the reduction of food waste.  
▪ Coherence and Consistency with other EU policies 
The proposal should be coherent with the upcoming and existing food and non-food-related legislative 
initiatives. The recently adopted Delegated Regulation on marketing standards for fruit and 
vegetables19 exempts fresh produce sold locally and directly by producers to consumers from adhering 
to marketing standards, thereby aiding in the reduction of food waste. However, there remains an 
issue where retailers and wholesalers may need to reject fresh produce to meet aesthetic 
requirements established by these marketing standards. Additionally, it is important to thoroughly 
evaluate potential side effects on food waste arising from the Packaging and Packaging Waste 
proposal.

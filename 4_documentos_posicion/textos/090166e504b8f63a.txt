DM&T feedback to European Commission public consultation on the WFD proposal     Executive summary DM&T welcomes the proposal for a targeted revision of the Waste Framework Directive (WFD) and the EU regulatory efforts to solve the textile waste problem, both to reduce waste and to enable textile waste recycling. The 2025 mandatory separate textile waste collection in the EU requires coordination and investments to build a European textile recycling value chain which could recycle 2.2 min tons by 2030. The proposal seeks to achieve harmonisation on key features of extended producer responsibility (EPR), which is highly welcomed as a harmonised framework is essential for companies to operate in the EU. DM&T would also like to stress the need to use EPR-generated funds to support the circular transition for the sector’s companies, especially the SMEs.   Below we have outlined our main considerations for improvement of the proposed legislation and for ensuring proper and effective implementation, whilst achieving textile circularity goals and preserving the competitiveness of EU businesses in a level playing field:  1. EPR harmonisation is essential for achieving circular economy goals, a level playing field and reducing unnecessary burdens for companies. 2. Micro enterprises should be included in the scope with simplifications.  3. Make online marketplaces jointly liable to ensure proper compliance and a level playing field. 4. Effective market surveillance is needed to eliminate free riders. 5. Exclude PPE from the EPR scope and include leather goods.  6. Common EPR fee structure is needed, including harmonised eco-modulation criteria based on the ESPR. 7. Refrain from setting obligatory targets for recycling of textiles, market data is currently insufficient to inform better regulation. 8. Boost the development of the recycling value chain. 9. Provide sufficient time for implementation to develop EPR schemes tailored to national specificities and a smooth transition.  10. Ensure equal treatment of all EPR stakeholders and shared responsibilities. 11. Prioritise development of EU-wide end-of-waste criteria.  12. Provide a holistic approach to EPR to ease compliance, and uniform registration. 13. Ensure a consistent legal framework and effective consultation with stakeholders.   1. Drive harmonisation in EPR to advance circular textiles in a level playing field. DM&T considers that EU-wide harmonisation in EPR schemes is essential to achieve the environmental benefits they seek, by ensuring that the obligations and incentives are aligned across EU Member States. Implementation of national schemes with diverging coverage or objectives risks serious inefficiencies in delivering a well-functioning system for textile reuse and recycling in Europe. A common framework for EPR will avoid variations in compliance requirements which will result in increased administrative burdens through lack of consistency and inefficiency, leading to additional costs for companies. This concerns particularly SMEs that do not have the necessary capacities to comply with different requirements when operating in several Member States. At the same time, it should be recognised that specificities among national waste management systems exist, and flexibility should be maintained to accommodate country-specific operational solutions (e.g., logistics).   2. Include micro enterprises in the EPR scope with simplifications. DM&T recommends including micro enterprises in the scope of EPR. Acknowledging that there is not yet complete data for all Member States, these enterprises may cumulatively introduce significant quantities of products into the EU market, thus creating important competitive asymmetries and risks for losing relevant market information. In addition, they could also be used to circumvent the provisions of the Directive, especially regarding third country producers categorised as ‘micro enterprises’. At the initial stage of implementation, micro enterprises should therefore be included in the scope, based on volumes of products placed on the EU market, rather than on the size of the company. We consider that the proposed volume-based approach is an effective way to support, among other things, the management of administrative burdens and assessment of the impacts of the legislation. In this regard, it is important to ensure that Member States introduce effective simplifications and facilitations regarding at least administrative requirements and payment of contributions at national level for micro, small and medium-sized enterprises. This will help to avoid unjustified bureaucratic burdens and streamline compliance.     3. Hold online marketplaces jointly and severally liable for EPR compliance. The inclusion of online sales from third countries is critical to ensure a level playing field for responsible EU companies and contribute to the overall objectives of EPR and is strongly welcomed by DM&T. The control by the national competent authorities on online sales is challenging, given the high number of operators active on online marketplaces and product flows. It is therefore extremely complex to obtain ex-post fulfilment of obligations by foreign companies that have operated illegally, as proposed by the current revision.  Therefore, to prevent large quantities of non-compliant products from entering the EU market, DM&T considers that it is necessary to require online marketplaces to pre-verify operators selling products online to EU consumers, ensuring their continuous registration in producers registers and payment of EPR fees. Additionally, DM&T considers that it is necessary to attribute joint and several liability for online sales - either via own dedicated e-commerce channels or via multi-brand marketplaces (platforms) - together with the third country economic entity that sells products to EU territory through these intermediaries. This should be done by means of uniform EU-wide rules to ensure effective compliance.  4. Ensure effective market surveillance for elimination of free riders. DM&T highlights the importance of ensuring that each company that places products concerned on the EU market, including through online sales from third countries, complies

with the legislation and pays its EPR fees. National market surveillance authorities should be equipped with the necessary capacities and infrastructure to ensure that there are no free riders undermining the objectives of EPR and disrupting the fair competition in the EU Single Market. A level playing field for EU companies is essential for competitiveness.   5. Exclude all PPE and include leather goods. DM&T welcomes that the proposal covers household textiles, articles of apparel, clothing accessories and footwear, and no technical textiles. However, the products and their CN codes listed in the proposed Annex IVc may still cover personal protective equipment (PPE). For instance, code 62101092 stands for surgical gowns and 62101098 for surgical drapes. PPE must be excluded from the scope of EPR, as they may present difficulties for collection and recycling and may also obstruct EPR by contaminating other apparel and home textiles if collected and treated together. Used products shall also be excluded from EPR schemes, to support reuse and textile circularity (referring to CN 6309). DM&T also suggests including leather goods falling under codes CN 4202 and 4205, alongside 4203. DM&T recognizes that the implementation of the EPR and setting up PROs requires, among other things, considerable resources and therefore, the scope of EPR should be focused, well-defined and clear. This will help avoiding overcomplicating the system and contribute to directing efforts more effectively towards the development of the circular economy goals and value adding results. In addition, it will help the sorting at source which is also an important step towards efficient collection systems and processes.    6. Seek a common fee structure with uniform eco-modulation criteria. A common EPR fee structure is needed, including harmonised eco-modulation criteria based on the Eco-design for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR). The proposal refers to ‘other Union law establishing harmonized sustainability criteria and measurement methods for textile products’ which is unclear and leads to confusion on the future objectives. DM&T recommends removing any confusion from the legislation.   DM&T considers that the proposal lacks ambition on the issue of eco-modulation, as it mentions that the Commission ‘may’ set specific criteria for eco-modulation in the future to avoid differences in the application of the ESPR criteria. DM&T supports further specifying of the criteria for eco-modulation that aligns with and advances ESPR objectives. Therefore, stronger commitments should be made on eco-modulation. Modulated fee criteria must be applied uniformly and consistently across all EU Member States, towards greater impact and creating real incentives for producers to adopt more circular design. Additionally, the EPR fee paid on each product should be made visible to the final consumer. This is to provide better transparency on the efforts and investments of the European industry to achieve the goals of and obligations arising from the green transition. This will also contribute to awareness raising in consumers and their ability to make better informed and responsible purchasing decisions. Consumers will be made aware of the sustainability performance of products due to eco-modulation and become more conscious when discarding products after knowing the end-of-life management costs paid for their products. To that end, however, it is important to increase the knowledge of consumers about EPR and its relevance for better understanding of the fees they pay and avoiding confusion.

7. Refrain from setting targets for textile recycling at this early stage. DM&T welcomes that the proposed legislation does not set specific targets for recycling of textiles, as there is currently not sufficient reliable data on the EU market capacities and recycling infrastructure to inform regulatory developments mandating realistic targets. The current focus of policy making should be on promoting and advancing circular goals including development and implementation of functional, cost-effective business models and recycling technologies, as well as increasing consumer awareness, rather than setting binding targets prematurely. However, recognising that some Member States might want to set a national target, we recommend that the EU to initiate the process of developing the necessary definitions, metrics, and methodologies for the Member State target-setting process to ensure harmonization among those Member States that do want to make targets.    8. Support the development of the recycling value chain. DM&T welcomes that the proposed legislation enables financing operations to build the reuse and recycling infrastructure. It also supports funding for research and development of recycling technologies, particularly fibre-to-fibre recycling. These are necessary to help build the recycling value chain in the EU to recycle up to 2.2 million tons of waste that will be available for recycling in 2030. DM&T believes that EPR should finance the transition to circular textiles, where waste should be transformed into new raw material. EPR should not only be limited to research and development in recycling, but it should finance the establishment of the full recycling value chain, meaning collection, sorting, preparation for reuse and recycling, and recycling. In this transition, it is important to support SMEs, which may lack necessary capacities and resources, in pursuing innovation and technological development. To be effective, the legislation must be comprehensive and solution-oriented, and it should support the circular economy’s systemic change throughout the entire value chain. DM&T notes that the proposed Article 22a (4)(a) point 3 seems to omit one element in the value chain, namely ‘preparation for recycling’, and should be amended to include both preparation for re-use and recycling.  9. Provide sufficient time for implementation and a smooth transition. The setting up of a national EPR scheme is a complex process that involves various stakeholders. It therefore needs to be ensured that the legislation establishes a feasible timeframe to properly design and implement the rules, tailored to the needs of the national waste management system, based on proper consultation with all relevant parties. As the mandatory separate collection of textile waste comes into force on 1 January 2025, DM&T is concerned about the time gap until fully fledged EPR schemes will be in place to support the proper functioning of textile waste management, as the proposal would require producers to finance the waste management costs of products after entry into force and implementation deadline. It must be clarified how the system will be organised in practice, and who will take care of the products that are now already on the market. DM&T calls for utmost efforts to ensure a smooth transition and effective management of waste in the implementation period.

10. Ensure equal treatment of all EPR stakeholders and shared responsibilities.   DM&T believes that the legislation should set equal treatment for all EPR stakeholders to participate and benefit from the opportunities offered by the transition to circular economy. This is essential for building cost-effective national EPR schemes that drive business competitiveness. DM&T considers that producers should not bear the costs of textile waste management for ‘social enterprises’ and ‘other non-waste operators’, which may operate and compete in the same market. Currently, social enterprises are responsible for sorting the waste they generate and for bearing the costs of their own waste management. It is also important that social enterprises are required to report on the waste they handle, as it is needed to have all the data necessary to assess the infrastructure needed for recycling. These entities shall make publicly available data about the weight collected and the rate of reuse, preparation for reuse, recycling, specifying separately the rate of fibre-to-fibre recycling, and on the rates of other recovery, disposal, and exports. DM&T stresses that the legislation should clearly define terms used and the actors concerned. The proposal provides no definition for ‘social enterprises’ and ‘other non-waste operators’ that appear on numerous occasions in the proposal. To be able to assess the impact of the legislation, a clear understanding is needed of who these actors are and how they influence the development of circular economy business models. Additionally, regarding the proposed obligation for producers to cover the costs of carrying out compositional surveys of collected waste, DM&T stipulates that household mixed waste remains - by law - the responsibility of municipalities, and that such surveys should be within their responsibilities.   11. Prioritise development of EU-wide end-of-waste criteria DM&T welcomes the ongoing work by the Commission and JRC to define the end-of-waste criteria of textile waste and reusable textiles and highlights the urgency for its development and implementation. The setting of harmonized end-of-waste criteria is essential in the transition to circular textiles. Without clear end-of-waste criteria, it will be very difficult to recycle textiles into fibres and use those fibres again as raw materials. Moreover, if these criteria are not harmonized at EU level, there will always be challenges and additional burdens when using recycled fibres in another Member States.   12. Create a holistic approach to EPR schemes and uniform registration. A system of separate national producers’ registries will allow authorities to better control registrations and the application of EPR, which ultimately remains a task for Member States. However, DM&T stresses the need to avoid diverging registration procedures per Member State, in terms of time and related costs. It is essential to provide concrete and uniform rules for a level playing field preferably on the possibility to register through one uniform procedure for all Member States where a producer/importer is active, also relating to the rules on public access for better transparency.  In addition, the proposal encourages the establishment of several Product Responsibility Organisations (PRO) per Member state, and while we are not against a free competition between PROs, we would like to emphasize the critical need for harmonization and

coordination across PROs in case several PROs exist in a Member State. Furthermore, collaboration between PROs across the Member States and the EU is absolutely critical, also when it comes to investing in sorting and recycling facilities. There is currently a need for large investments in sorting and recycling technologies and facilities in the EU in order to make it commercially profitable, and we are concerned that too much fragmentation at PRO level will hinder such investments.  Finally, DM&T stresses that a link should be established between the different EPR schemes that countries develop, e.g., for plastics, paper, textiles, as it would be extremely difficult for SMEs to create and maintain records in so many different schemes at the same time. A tool should be created, especially for SMEs, that would enable the reduction of the administrative burden and make it easier for companies to manage information and their obligations.   13. Ensure a consistent legal framework and effective stakeholder consultation.  DM&T notes that EU companies already struggle to navigate through all the policy and legislative initiatives currently under development. It is therefore imperative to establish a clear and coherent legislative framework that provides predictability and minimises administrative burdens and costs for businesses. In the preparation of ongoing and future legislation, it is essential to enable a constructive dialogue with all relevant stakeholders and to consider whether the obligations and requirements are such that companies, and especially SMEs, will be able to cope with. Effective stakeholder consultation will make it possible to identify the needed support and potential solutions to manage impacts on EU businesses and their continued competitiveness.

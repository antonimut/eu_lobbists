Key points of the contribution: 
 
● Article 3. The definition of “plastics” in article 3 (9) of the draft ELVR should be 
amended to cover more plastics, including polyurethanes. This can be done by 
using the definition of plastics from the food contact regulation (Commission 
Regulation (EU) 10/2011). This will allow polyurethanes, the second most  used 
plastic in vehicles, to contribute to the objectives of the ELVR.  
 
● Article 6 on recycled content of the draft ELVR should be amended to: 
o Specifically recognize chemical recycling as a recycling technology 
complementary to mechanical recycling , as well as  mass balance 
accounting for circular/recycled content.        
o Create a second target for so -called circular plastics (made with bio, bio -
circular, non -plastic recycled content or carbon capture technology) to 
allow more feedstocks than just post- consumer plastics to contribute to 
the sustainability of plastics in vehicles, especially in vehicle interiors.  
o Allow also materials replaced during the lifetime of a vehicle to count as 
feedstock for recycled content for the closed- loop target (automotive to 
automotive). These items are also post -consumer waste and need 
recycling too, even if not present in the ELV itself.  
o In upcoming secondary legislation, take into account existing certification 
schemes for recycled/circular content in plastics.

About EURO-MOULDERS and Polyurethanes 
 
EURO-MOULDERS is the European Association of Manufacturers of Moulded Polyurethane 
Parts for the Automotive Industry. Our members are Tier 1 and 2 manufacturers of 
moulded polyurethane parts for the automotive industry, ranging for example from car 
seating to acoustic insulation and dashboards. Our members operate the majority of the 
moulded foam plants for automotive seating in Europe.  
 
Polyurethanes are the second most used plastic in vehicles, accounting on average for 15% 
of the weight of plastics in vehicles 1. While some polyurethanes are thermoplastics, the 
vast majority of them are  so-called thermosets. Contrary to most other plastic parts that 
are made by converting plastic pellets, polyurethane parts are produced by chemical 
reaction at the same time as the part is produced. This means that for integrating recycled 
content in vehicle interiors , the polyurethane industry relies on using high quality 
chemicals made with recycled content , for example via mass balance accounting, 
combined or not with chemical recycling
2. Mechanical recycling of polyurethanes is mostly 
suited for lower added- value applications and is limited to some non -visible parts in 
vehicles (carpet underlay, filling materials…). 
 
Our comments on the draft ELV Regulation focus on the definition of plastics (article 3),      
on the integration of recycled content in plastics  (article 6) and on substances of concern 
and legacy substances . Considering the importance of polyurethanes in vehicles, it is 
essential that these points are corrected so that the polyurethanes industry can contribute 
to fulfilling the objectives of the draft Regulation.      
 
Definition of Plastics (Article 3 (9)) 
 
The draft Regulation defines plastic as “a polymer within the meaning of Article 3, point (5), 
of Regulation (EC) No 1907/2006, to which additives or other substances may have been 
added”. 
Looking at the article of the REACH Regulation which the draft Regulation refers to, this 
means that plastic is defined as:  
“a substance consisting of molecules characterised by the sequence of one or more types 
of monomer units. Such molecules must be distributed over a range of molecular weights 
wherein differences in the molecular weight are primarily attributable to differences in the 
number of monomer units. A polymer comprises the following: (a) a simple weight majority

of molecules containing at least three monomer units which are covalently bound to at least 
one other monomer unit or other reactant; (b) less than a simple weight majority of 
molecules of the same molecular weight”. 
By suggesting this definition in the draft ELV Regulation, the European Commission more 
or less follows the same logic as in the draft Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation, 
where article 3 (43) defines plastics as “‘ a polymer within the meaning of Article 3(5) of 
Regulation (EC) No 1907/2006, to which additives or other substances may have been 
added, and which is capable of functioning as a main structural component of packaging, 
with the exception of natural polymers that have not been chemically modified”. 
The issue however is that plastics used in packaging are essentially thermoplastics 
(characterized by the sequence of one or more types of monomer units ) and not 
thermosetting plastics or cured resins. By replicating the definition from the packaging 
legislation, the definition used in the draft ELV Regulation basically excludes polyurethanes, 
elastomers, rubbers and reinforced carbon fibres from the definition of plastics. Oddly, this 
definition seems to cover brake fluids, which would be counted as “plastic”.  
In our understanding, the consequences of this definition do not reflect the original 
intention of the European Commission. The Joint Research Centre of the European 
Commission in its report “Towards recycled plastic content targets in vehicles” (July 2023) 
is suggesting to inc lude polyurethane  foams into the scope of the Regulation but not 
elastomers, rubbers or reinforced carbon fibres.  
To correct this, we therefore suggest to use the definition of plastic and polymers used in 
article 3 of Commission Regulation (EU) 10/2011 on plastics intended to come into contact 
with food:  
“(2)  ‘plastic’ means polymer to which additives or other substances may have been added, 
which is capable of functioning as a main structural component of final materials and 
articles; 
(3) ‘polymer’ means any macromolecular substance obtained by: 
  (a) a polymerization process such as polyaddition or polycondensation, or by any 
                other similar process of monomers and other starting substances; or 
  (b) chemical modification of natural or synthetic macromolecules; or 
  (c) microbial fermentation”; 
 
This definition is broadening the scope of article 3 to include more plastics than the current 
text, notably polyurethanes, and is better aligned with the Joint Research Centre’s 
proposed scope of plastics to be covered by recycled content obligations. Considering that 
our industry is investing significantly to improve the sustainability of polyurethanes, and 
because polyurethanes are the second most used plastics in vehicles, our industry believes 
it should be included in the scope of new ELV Regulation so  that also polyurethanes can 
contribute its objectives. However,  such an inclusion also requires that article 6 be 
amended on a number of items.   
 
Recycled Content for Plastics (Article 6, § 1 and 2) 
 
The said article is currently drafted as follows: 
 
“1. The plastic contained in each vehicle type that is type-approved as of [OP: Please insert 
the date = the first day of the month following 72 months after the date of entry into force 
of the Regulation] under Regulation (EU) 2018/858 shall contain a minimum of 25 % of 
plastic recycled by weight from post-consumer plastic waste. 
 
At least 25 % of the target set out in the first subparagraph shall be achieved by including 
plastics recycled from end-of-life vehicles in the vehicle type concerned. 
 
2. By [OP: Please insert the date = the last day of the month following 23 months after the 
date of entry into force of this Regulation], the Commission shall adopt an implementing 
act in accordance with Article 51(2) to supplement this Regulation by establishing the 
methodology for the calculation and verification, for the purposes of paragraph 1 of this 
Article, of the share of plastics recovered from post -consumer waste, and from end- of-life 
vehicles respectively, present in and incorporated into the vehicle type”. 
 
We see the following issues with this article in its current drafting: 
 
● Limiting the “recycled content” to “post -consumer plastics” is overly restrictive in 
terms of feedstock. The plastics industry at large has been investing heavily over 
the past years to develop circular plastics made with bio -based, non-plastic waste 
or with carbon capture raw materials. This is why – in alignment with Plastics 
Europe – we suggest to introduce a secondary target to support and encourage the 
use of such plastics in vehicles. This is critical for polyurethanes, for which none of 
the circular raw materials currently available on the market for the automotive 
sector are made with post-consumer plastic waste, notably due to the fact that the 
automotive sector is for us the sector with the strictest requirements and 
specifications. Opening feedstocks is also comparable with amendments adopted 
by the European Parliament on the draft Eco -design for Sustainable Products 
Regulation and the draft Plastics Packaging Waste Regulation.   Keeping the text as 
is would deter investments and research in recycling non -plastic fractions, such as 
tires, waste oils, or mixed shredder residue. In turn, this would mean that such 
fractions risk being relegated to energy recovery, rather than being used as a source 
of high -quality recyclates that could be used in the production of vehicle 
components. 
 
● It fails to specifically recognise mass-balance accounting and chemical recycling as 
enablers for providing high quality recycled or circular content for plastics used in 
vehicles. A key difference between plastic packaging and parts used in vehicles is 
that the latter are often complex high-performance composites, especially in 
vehicle interiors, that need to last for the whole life-time of the vehicle. They need 
to meet safety, durability, and light weighting standards and, therefore,  often 
require the highest quality of polymers available3. Mechanical recycling alone does 
not allow to handle such complex composites, which need to be treated with other 
technologies. Mass balance accounting with clear rules is critical for recording the 
share or recycled or circular content in a complex supply chain going a ll the way 
from the producers of basic chemicals to car makers. It will also be a key enabler of 
compliance with the closed loop requirement introduced in article 6.1.   
 
● The closed -loop requirement for recycled content (automotive to automotive) 
faces the same issue as emphasized above in terms of feedstocks, namely  overly 
limited feedstock options. Numerous types of waste, not only plastics, from End-of-
Life Vehicles (ELV) can be recycled to produce plastics. The recycling of such waste 
should be counted towards both the closed- loop requirement and towards the 
recycled content target or towards the circular plastics target proposed by the 
plastics’ industry and should be actively encouraged. 
 
● The closed -loop target fails to take into account the fact that materials used in 
vehicles can be replaced during the lifetime of a vehicle and that these need 
recycling too. In the current drafting, only materials recovered from ELVs count 
towards the closed-loop target. This would lead to the relatively absurd situation in 
which a bumper replaced and recycled during the lifetime of a vehicle would not 
count towards the target, but the same bumper removed and recycled from an ELV 
would indeed. The provision on closed- loop should therefore be extended in such 
a way that  materials recycled from end -of-life parts and components , removed 
during the use-phase of a vehicle, count towards the target too.  
 
● It is fully understandable that secondary legislation will need to be drafted to 
implement the ELVR. For recycled /circular content in plastics a number of 
certification schemes are however already in place. When drafting rules for the 
implementation of article 6.1, the European Commission should assess these  
schemes and where appropriate seek to set rules that can make use of them .

Taking the above into account, we suggest that article 6 of the draft ELVR could be 
amended as follows: 
 
1° The plastic contained in each vehicle type that is type -approved as of [OP: Please insert 
the date = the first day of the month following 72 months after the date of entry into force 
of the Regulation] under Regulation (EU) 2018/858 shall contain a mini mum of 25 20% of 
plastic recycled by weight from post -consumer plastic waste based on a combination of 
mechanical, physical and chemical recycling and 5% of circular plastics by 
weight produced from recycled, bio-based, or carbon capture (CCU) feedstock. 
 
At least 25 % of the targets set out in the first subparagraph shall be achieved by including 
plastics recycled materials from end-of-life vehicles in the vehicle type concerned or from 
end-of-life parts and components in vehicle use. 
   
2° By [OP: Please insert the date = the last day of the month following 23 months after the 
date of entry into force of this Regulation], the Commission shall adopt an implementing 
act in accordance with Article 51(2) to supplement this Regulation by establishing the 
methodology for the calculation and verification, for the purposes of paragraph 1 of this 
Article, of the  respective shares of plastics recovered from post -consumer waste,  circular 
plastics, and of recycled materials from end-of-life vehicles respectively or from end-of-life 
parts and components in vehicle use , present in and incorporated into the vehicle type 
concerned.  Where appropriate, the European Commission shall consider existing 
certification schemes for recycled content in plastics when doing so 
 
This is of course just a proposal for wording of the legal text. The matter of the most 
importance is that the issues highlighted above on article 6 are addressed in the best 
possible way. We therefore welcome a ny upcoming discussion of the best wording of the 
said article.   
 
Substances of Concern and Legacy Substances 
 
The European Union has one of the most comprehensive chemical legislations in the world: 
the REACH Regulation and the CLP Regulation. Substances of concern or chemical safety 
should wherever possible be handled under that legislation, which foresees clear 
procedures for the assessment of chemicals. Including further provisions on hazardous 
substances in the ELV would create a second layer of legislation that would create legal 
uncertainty and affect the proper implementation in Member States.  
      
In the context of the ELV however, the mandatory use of recycled plastics and closed-loop 
requirements may create issues with legacy substances. Vehicles recycled today are usually 
10+ years old. Plastics in those vehicles may not all comply with current chemical legislation 
and may contain substances that have been prohibited since the production date of the 
vehicle. The proposal should therefore take into account ways in which innovative recycling 
technologies (e.g chemical recycling) offer possibilities to address such legacy substances.  
 
We hope our contribution can be taken into account and remain at the disposal of the 
European Commission, the European Parliament and the Council of the European Union to 
discuss the matters it contains further.

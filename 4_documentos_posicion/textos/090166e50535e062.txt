Driving circularity in the automotive industry The BMW Group is committed to the goals of the Paris Agreement and is leading the way in circularity within the automotive industry. We underpin our commitment with ambitious targets for the use of secondary raw materials as well as with principles including “Secondary First”, which prioritizes the use of recyclates in development processes, and “Design for Circularity”, which takes a vehicle’s recyclability into account as early as the development stage. We also operate the largest vehicle recycling center in Germany: the BMW Recycling and Dismantling Center in Unterschleißheim. Meanwhile, with the Car2Car project, we have initiated an industry consortium for the ongoing development of vehicle recycling. With this experience under our belts, we welcome many aspects of the further development of the EU’s current End-of-Life Vehicles Directive but firmly reject any measures that distort the market or drive red tape.    Background  July 13, 2023 saw the EU Commission publish its draft revision to the End-of-Life Vehicles Directive as a “regulation on circularity requirements for vehicle design and on management of end-of-life vehicles”. The new regulation is intended to replace the existing End-of-life Vehicles Directive (adopted in 2000) and the 3R Type Approval Directive (reusability, recyclability and recoverability, adopted in 2005). The legislation focuses on improving vehicle recyclability, preventing waste, introducing a digital vehicle passport, connected vehicle registration and deregistration in Europe, addressing the issue of unknown whereabouts and the stringent organization of take-back systems and vehicle recycling. For the first time, the regulation is also set to include motorcycles, trucks and buses.     The challenge While there are already functioning and self-sustaining markets for vehicle recycling in the EU, not all vehicles are recycled properly, with EU figures suggesting an unknown whereabout of a third of the vehicles. The challenge, therefore, is to create regulation that promotes transparency and optimization for vehicle recycling without jeopardizing market-based structures. In doing so, it is also possible to target improvements to vehicle circularity. In the long term, however, the circular economy should contribute not only to raw material resilience, resource conservation and carbon reduction, but also to the economic strengthening of Europe. As such, the current draft regulation fails to give sufficient weight to economic considerations, in particular.

BMW Group position: Yes to circularity, no to red tape.   Promising steps in the right direction The BMW Group welcomes:   The inclusion of motorcycles, buses and trucks.  Effective certificates of destruction to ensure professional recovery.  The (EU-wide) export ban on non-roadworthy used vehicles.  The clear delimitation of dismantled components from waste in order to facilitate the cross-border movement of goods.   We also back digital “circularity vehicle passports”, provided, technologically speaking, they are harmonized with existing systems such as the Battery Passport or IDIS.   The spare parts business & mandatory dismantling: upholding functioning market structures (Art. 30–34)  The existing EU End-of-Life Vehicles Directive already stipulates that all OEMs must, and at no cost to the last owner, take back and recycle any vehicles they have placed on the market that are brought in their entirety to a collection facility. In practice, almost every end-of-life vehicle retains sufficiently high residual value for a well-established vehicle recycling market to have developed. A common business model employed by dismantling facilities is to remove reusable components and feed them into the spare parts market. However, the latest draft from the Commission seriously disrupts this market, drastically broadening the number of mandatory non-destructive dismantling operations that must be carried out on end-of-life vehicles. The draft further stipulates that any assessment of whether there is even a market for reuse should only take place after dismantling has occurred, driving costs and going against the market-driven reality of the spare parts business. As such, the BMW Group is calling for a number of points:   It must be possible to choose between non-destructive or destructive dismantling (assessment of reuse before dismantling!). This way, when it comes to components that cannot be reused accordingly, it will still be possible to work with coarser tools, in turn massively cutting dismantling costs. Generally speaking, we support better separation and sorting of materials as a way to ensure high-quality recycling and therefore high-quality recyclates.   The scope of mandatory dismantling must be reduced, not least for control units and wiring harnesses. In contemporary vehicles, these components are distributed throughout the entire vehicle, making total dismantling highly uneconomical. A significant target reduction would also be logical for material recycling using the shredder route, where the focus should fall on the materials present. Not every control unit is of the same high quality in terms of its material composition. We support removing engine control units and battery management systems separately.   Alternatively, it must be possible to use efficient post-shredder technology in material recovery for all mandatory dismantling processes to improve the supply of economically competitive and high-quality recyclates. Restrictions should apply only to components where shredder technologies cannot be used for reasons of occupational health and safety and environmental protection.   The extended scope of mandatory dismantling should, in the first instance, apply only to type-approved vehicles from 2013. It should still be possible to handle all previously type-approved vehicles in keeping with the dismantling regulations currently in force. For one thing, because the market for spare parts for older vehicles is negligible, and secondly as the principles of circularity were not sufficiently taken into account in their design.

Vehicle take-back: electric vehicles must include their high-voltage battery (Art. 24)  The draft regulation compels OEMs to take back electric vehicles free of charge even if the high-voltage battery has already been removed. This arbitrary requirement further weakens the market-driven business models of dismantling companies, as it removes a component with a potentially high market value. In addition, the removal of the battery by unqualified persons poses a considerable risk to both health (high-voltage electric shock) and the environment (improper recycling). End-of-life vehicles and their traction batteries are a single type-approved system and should always be handed over as a single unit at specific, environmentally certified collection points (e.g. dismantling facilities). This is also where the decision on potential second-use should be made.   Extended producer responsibility: no need for government-run, bureaucratic fee models (Art. 20–22)  The above-mentioned new requirements also render today’s functioning, market-driven vehicle recycling structures no longer economically viable. The intention is to introduce a new bureaucratic instrument, a government-run fee model for vehicle recycling, at the expense of the OEMs to remedy this situation. This is not in line with the aim of a strong, competitive and value-creating industry in Europe. As such, the BMW Group calls for the above-mentioned non-market and uneconomic requirements to be designed in a way that ensures vehicle recycling remains a self-sustaining industry moving forward and one which is not dependent on government fee modelling.   Recyclate quotas for new vehicles: calls for flexibility and overarching targets (Art. 6)  According to the draft regulation, vehicles must in future contain a legally prescribed minimum proportion of recycled materials. The first step will see the regulation of plastics, while a number of Delegated Acts will also regulate steel, aluminum, magnesium, rare earths and boron. With the existing requirements in place from the Batteries Regulation, this will then see around 90% of vehicle weight regulated with recyclate quotas.   The BMW Group has been a passionate proponent of the use of recyclates in its vehicles for many years now, both in order to reduce carbon emissions and conserve raw materials as well as to minimize its environmental footprint. Certainly, the use of recyclates plays a key role in achieving carbon neutrality for vehicles. With this experience in mind, we do not believe that a detailed regulation per material is logical. OEMs are able to achieve the greatest contribution to vehicle material circularity when they are given the freedom to steer both related carbon emissions and costs in the most efficient way. As such, the BMW Group calls for recyclates to be considered across the board. With regard to the primary vehicle materials (steel, aluminum, magnesium, plastics), OEMs must continue to be given entrepreneurial freedom as to where the use of recyclates makes the most sense and is most economically feasible. In particular, it must be possible to guarantee the ongoing availability of material flows. The BMW Group envisages an overarching target for the materials mentioned that would also enable the usability of industrial waste. The Commission’s proposal on post-consumer waste and closed-loop requirements is one-sided in its focus and fails to offer the consistent material quality needed for automotive requirements that would enable long-lasting and safe product design.   Motorcycles: Integration into article 5 and sector-specific Annex  As part of the new End-of-Life Vehicles Regulation, motorcycles should be included in the scope of Article 5 and a motorcycle-specific Annex should be developed.   This request is grounded in the technical realities and environmental exposure unique to motorcycles and their components. We emphasize the necessity for clear rules and specific time-bound exemptions for certain substances, crucial for motorcycle functionality.  The operational demands of motorcycles, often subject to extreme conditions such as high temperatures and strong vibrations, make substances like lead indispensable. Our proposal for a motorcycle-specific Annex under Article 5 reflects a balanced approach, accommodating the technical requirements of the sector while upholding our commitment to environmental stewardship and circularity. Such specific provisions would apply more stringent requirements considering hazardous substances for motorcycles, while allowing for application-based substitution deadlines, and meeting the ELV Regulation’s objectives.

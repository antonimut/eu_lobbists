MOL Group’s position on the Commission proposal for a regulation on End-of-life Vehicles 
  
MOL Group is committed to the EU’s circular economy objectives and is transforming to adapt to a circular 
and low -carbon world. We welcome the Commission proposal for an En d-of-life Vehicles  Regulation 
(ELVR) as it has the potential to improve circularity in the design of vehicles while tackling negative impacts 
on environment from their waste.  
Transition to a more circular economy in this sector still faces many challenges , and the ELVR should 
ensure that Member States can maintain some flexibility of their waste management systems to address 
the specific regional challenges in their own way . Below we summarised our key points related to the 
proposed regulation that we believe will be crucial to achieve these objectives. 
1. Member States  should be allowed flexibility to organise their waste management systems , 
including EPR systems , taking into account their national or regional circumstances to achieve 
better environmental results (Articles 16-21). 
2. We support a cost allocation mechanism between PROs for vehicles becoming end-of-life vehicles 
in another Member State (Article 22) 
3. More feedstock flexibility should be allowed in recycled content targets (including pre-consumer 
waste and post -consumer waste, and a less restrictive closed loop model ) to ensure greater 
circularity of the sector (Article 6) 
4. The timing of t argets should be aligned as they are interlinked (i.e. design for recycling 
requirements need to show results before new plastic recycling / closed loop recyclate targets are 
feasible) 
5. The exact rules and methodologies on defining recyclability and recycled content in vehicles must 
be elaborated as soon as possible (Art 4, 6)  
 
Article 4 - Reusability, recyclability and recoverability of vehicles  – material choices can significantly 
contribute to this objective 
When adopting the implementing act establishing a new methodology for the calculation and verification 
of the rate of recyclability, it is important to take note of the materials present in  the vehicle and their 
respective recyclability (e.g. multi -material combinations or single material solutions; conscious choices 
about plastic fillers etc.). 
 
Article 6 – Minimum recycled content in vehicles – More feedstock flexibility needed 
We welcome the Commission’s intention to include a recycled plastics content target for newly type-
approved vehicles in the ELV Regulation. We agree that there are challenges in the wide adoption of post-
consumer recyclates in vehicle manufacturing, and that regulatory action could help increase activities in 
this area.  
However, we believe that there should be recognition for pre-consumer recyclates too, as both areas 
need significant improvements to increase the replacement of virgin raw materials and the reduction of 
the CO 2 footprint of the automotive sector.  Recycling of pre -consumer plastic waste can increase the 
circularity of the automotive sector already today . Excluding pre-consumer recyclates does not consider 
the chemical behaviour of polymers: the plastics used in a car degrade significantly over time and after a 
few recycling cycles, quality can be significantly damaged which does not allow the use of the recyclate

again in automotive applications. Pre-consumer recyclates can help improve the quality of post-consumer 
recycled feedstock and ensure homogeneous , virgin -like properties of the new compounds , fulfilling 
various technical specifications.  Therefore, at least  safety-related parts should be exempted from the  
restriction to post-consumer waste.  
We also believe that the target that at least 25% of the plastic recycled content target shall be achieved 
from plastics recycled from end -of-life vehicles “in the vehic le type concerned” is too restricting. While 
the incentivization of some closed loop ELV treatment is a good direction,  the requirement for the 
feedstock to come from the same vehicle type is too strict , commercially unviable and limits end -use 
applications for the recyclate which prevents the creation of a dynamic recyclate market . The model-to-
model approach does not take into account the innovation regarding car structures during the lifecycle of 
a car (design and expected mechanical properties can change significantly during a 15-year-lifetime). This 
very limited closed-loop approach does not suit the characteristics and global nature of the automotive 
industry. The different models of the OEMs are produced in dedicated plants across the globe so the end-
of-life vehicle status is usually achieved in a different country than the country of production: to include 
the recycled material in the exact model, the material would need to be shipped to long distances, which 
can be counter -effective (we would lose w hat we gain on circularity due to the carbon emissions of 
transport). This very strict closed-loop approach would also result in unnecessary cost increase (collection, 
disassembling by model, tracking system of parts), and therefore should allow vehicle-to-vehicle loops or 
at maximum refer to manufacturer-to-manufacturer loops.  
Regarding the timing for such targets, closed loop targets should come at least 5-10 years after the plastic 
recycled content target (which is foreseen 6 years after entry into force). This is because the availability 
of feedstock for closed loop targets will depend on other design related elements of this regulation, such 
as design for recyclability (Article 4); design to enable removal and replacement of certain parts and 
components in vehicles (Article 7), which would also only be compulsory after 6 years. These newly 
designed vehicles need t o become end-of-life vehicles before being able to provide sufficient feedstock 
for the closed loop target and meet  the above -mentioned quality  and durability standards of end -
applications.  
We welcome that the methodology for the calculation and verification of recycled content needs to be 
published within 2 years from entry into force. In the methodology it should be confirmed if plastic  
contained in vehicles includes the full weight of parts made of plastic compounds which in the automotive 
industry can have a share of fillers ranging from 5-40%, depending on the application. Plastic compounds 
are essential to guarantee optimal performance and quality of certain automotive parts such as strength 
and toughness required by the industry . Also, the chemical industry is now going through the circular 
transition and is currently investing in significant chemical recycling capabilities. For boosting the circular 
economy, it is important to have a supporting legal framework clarified as soon as possible – including a 
robust and transparent mass balance chain of custody system  based on a fuel -use exempt allocation 
model, creating a level playing field for all technologies –, as major investment decisions depend on having 
this legal certainty. 
Moreover, it should be clarified whether tyres are included in the recycled content targets. The regulation 
should acknowledge the efforts and work being done regarding tyres in the ESPR regulation to avoid 
double regulation.

Article 9 – Circularity strategy – a forward-looking approach is welcome 
We welcome that 3 years after entry into force of this regulation manufacturers shall draw up a circularity 
strategy, as this can help material manufacturers to prepare in advance to meet upcomin g needs. To 
create a successful circular strategy, however, comprehensive collaboration across the entire value chain 
needs to be prioritised. The current approach, as outlined in Annex IV part A, is fragmented and linear, 
mainly focusing on interactions f rom suppliers to the end -of-life stage. Manufacturers are currently 
responsible for gathering data from upstream suppliers, but to fully embrace circularity, a dedicated 
collaboration with suppliers should be established, including those in the chemical se ctor.  This 
collaboration should also cover material design and end -of-use considerations, aiming to enable vehicle 
parts recycling. This approach boosts efficiency, reduces impurities, and minimises waste. 
  
Article 16 – Extended producer responsibility – scope to cover spare parts 
Beyond vehicles, also spare parts marketed separately that are  assembled during the life -cycle of the 
vehicle should be in the scope of extended producer responsibility , as these also require collection and 
treatment at the end-of-life of the vehicle. 
 
Article 18 – Producer Responsibility Organisation – larger Member State flexibility should be ensured 
We believe that some articles require larger Member State flexibility based on the specific, national 
circumstances. In accordance with the subsidiarity principle, a right balance has to be found between 
the EU harmonisation and effective Member State specific legislation. This principle has a specific legal 
basis deriving Article 15 of the Directive 2008/98 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 18 
November 2008 on waste (in the following: the Waste Framework Directive, or WFD). Article 15 (1) has 
already been interpreted by the CJEU, and in its case-law the Court clarified that Member States enjoyed 
broad discretion in organising their waste management systems. According to Article 15 (1) the Member 
State has a right to decide for the optimal waste management system including its main features. 
According to Article 15 (1) of the Waste Framework Directive Member States shall have the legislative 
room to choose the way how they foresee the guarantees for such accessible and non-discriminatory 
system and also to decide in which (governing or administrative) bodies to involve producers and 
recyclers. 
 
Article 20 – Financial responsibility of producers – the regulation should not go beyond Article 8a of the 
Waste Framework Directive in regulating EPR schemes and should leave flexibility for Member States 
While the harmonisation of extended producer responsibility schemes is welcome to a certain extent, the 
proposal is too restrictive and should leave more flexibility for Member States who already have made 
significant achievements in setting up such systems based on their national or regional specificities.  
The Commission proposal in Article 20 would determine not only which costs need to be covered by 
producers, but also the related revenues of waste management operators linked to the sales of used spare 
parts and used spare components, of depolluted end -of-life vehicles, or of secondary raw materials 
recycled from end-of-life vehicles. Firstly, such restriction is not serving the purpose of this regulation  
and does not make sense economically in the context of stimulating investments for development of

collection systems, recycling and treatment technologies, as well as their safe, long-term sustainable and 
responsible operation. Additionally, based on past experiences, the average costs of collection, recycling 
and treatment operations and the revenues of waste management operators can n ever be calculated, 
relate to sensible internal company data which shall remain confidential in line with competition law and 
data protection. 
The proposal is also contrary to the principle of subsidiarity and Article 15 of the Waste Framework 
Directive, under which it is the responsibility and right of the Member States to establish their waste 
management systems and to determine (within certain limits) the waste management responsibilities and 
tasks of producers. As already mentioned, Article 15(1) has  been interpreted by the CJEU, and in its case-
law the Court clarified that Member States enjoyed broad discretion in organising their waste 
management systems. The provision for producers to pay solely the net costs of waste management is 
also not justified based on the WFD’s Article 8a paragraph (4) points (a-c), as those points outline that net 
costs are to be used to define the minimum amount of contributions (point a), but they allow a range up 
until a maximum amount that covers the f ull costs of waste management without netting revenues, as 
long as it is carried out in a cost -effective way (point c). Such a restriction proposed by the Commission 
can seriously jeopardise the viability of already established Member State waste management systems. 
Finally, the proposal is contrary to the principle of proportionality  because, in order to improve the 
functioning of the internal market, it severely restricts the right of establishment of potential PROs and 
the freedom of services. This jeopardises the environmental objectives of the legislation, since it deprives 
Member States of the possibility of applying a market regulation in which this activity is carried out on a 
profit-oriented basis by an undertaking independent of the producers, in o rder to encourage better 
environmental results. The proposal also fails to state reasons for why such market restriction is justified 
and proportionate. 
For the end -of-life vehicle waste stream, we call for less harmonisation, reliance on the Waste 
Framework Directive and taking into account Member State specificities. 
 
Article 21 – Fee modulation – prioritisation of circularity criteria needed 
In terms of the modulation of the financial contributions to be paid by producers,  we believe a 
prioritization should be set regarding the circularity criteria, where recyclability always comes first before 
recycled content. Otherwise, there is a risk of having lower EPR fees for end-of-life vehicles with recycled 
content, even if they are not recyclable, and therefore resulting in higher treatment costs. In addition, fee 
modulation on the percentage of recycled content should take into account the principle of proximity  in 
terms of the origin of that feedstock. 
Furthermore, we suggest the addition of  a definition for composites , and the proper differentiation 
between thermosets and thermoplastics,  as it might create confusion with the different materials  (e.g. 
compounds) overall. The clarification of these definitions is important because point (e) in paragraph 1 in 
Article 21 would discriminate against composite materials in the EPR fee modulation due to the assumed 
recycling difficulties.

Article 22 – Cost allocation mechanism for vehicles becoming end -of-life vehicles in another Member 
State – need to take into account actual cost of waste management in receiving state 
We support the development of  a cost allocation mechanism where producers or PROs within whose 
territory the vehicle was made available on the market for the first time shall ensure that the costs of 
waste management incurred by waste management operators in other Member States are covered. This 
issue is especially relevant for Central and Eastern European Member States, which are the recipients of 
used vehicles from Western Europe. 
Such mechanism should make sure that the transferred contributions do not correspond to the EPR fees 
where the vehicle was made available on the market for the first time, but rather cover the total costs of 
waste management in the receiving country. This is important as the average age of vehicles and therefore 
the related treatment costs differ greatly between regions. Due to the arguments listed in case of Article 
20, the transferred contributions should not necessarily mean net costs, but also involve certain profits to 
cover investments and improvements of the system. 
 
Article 34 – Reuse, recycling and recovery targets – further assessment needed on feasibility in line 
with other elements of the proposal 
Considering that the definition of recycling  in this regulation  was aligned with the Waste Framework 
Directive definition, it has to be further assessed if the reuse or recycling of 85% by mass target can be 
met within the suggested 3 years, as it will exclude some currently allowed technologies. 
The timing of the  plastic recycling target  is not feasible within 5 years f rom entry into force of this 
regulation, and should be delayed by at least additional 5 years, as its achievement will depend on other 
design related elements of this regulation, such as design for recyclability (Article 4); design to enable 
removal and replacement of certain parts and components in vehicl es (Article 7), which would also only 
be compulsory after 6 years. 
 
Article 35 – Ban on landfilling of non-inert waste - further assessment needed on feasibility in line 
with other elements of the proposal 
Similarly to previous points, the timing of the ban on landfilling non -inert waste might not be feasible 
within 3 years from entry into force of this regulation, and due to synergies, should be delayed until the 
closed loop recycled content targets come into force.

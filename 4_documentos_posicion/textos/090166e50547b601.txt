PROPOSAL FOR A REGULATION ON CIRCULARITY REQUIREMENT FOR VEHICLES DESIGN AND ON 
MANAGEMENT OF END-OF-LIFE VEHICLES (ELV REGULATION) 
This note intends to comment on the proposal for regulation on circularity requirement for vehicles design and on 
management of end-of-life vehicles (ELV regulation), submitted to public consultation by the European. 
Reference: 2023/0284 (COD)  
Date: 21 November 2023 
Auteurs: Saskia Walraedt  (swalraedt@essenscia.be – +32 496 593 619) 
 
 
essenscia position addresses 4 issues: 
1. Regulatory coherence and better regulation 
2. Substances of concern 
3. Ecomodulation of EPR fees 
4. Recycled content 
 
 
1 Introduction 
As the Belgian Federation for Chemicals, Life Sciences and Polymers, we have the unique distinction of covering both very 
demanding users as well as manufacturers of plastics. essenscia covers the entire value chain, from basic substances to 
recycling.  
The EU has implemented a dedicated action plan to reduce waste, including both regulatory and non -regulatory actions, 
as part of the Circular Economy Action Plan and the Green Deal.  essenscia strongly believes that a systemic approach to 
assess the objectives of this plan is paramount to avoid unexpected shift of environmental impact or undesired harm to 
the sustainable development goals.  
With this proposal for an ELV regulation, the European Commission proposes to increase the circularity of the automotive 
industry. The automotive industry is amongst the biggest application for plastics in Europe and with the electrification of 
mobility, the share of plastics in vehicles is only increasing. We therefore would like to provide comments on an number 
of aspects of the proposal. 
2 Regulatory coherence and better regulation 
• The scope of the ELV legislation has changed. The proposal not only passes from a Directive to a Regulation but 
also includes now articles related to the placing on the market of a car, such as recycled content requirements 
and EPR provisions. Therefore, the proposed regulation remains a waste legislation but adds products norm 
requirements such as recycled content obligations, becomes a mix of both waste and product legislation.  
• The proposal contains various provisions to develop  under implementing acts and  delegated acts to further 
clarify or develop technical aspects of the legislation. This raises concerns about the consultation of the relevant 
stakeholders in the development of these delegated acts. 
• Definitions:  
o The definition of plastics in the ELV proposal is not adequate as it covers only thermosets and not the 
wide variety of plastics used in automotive applications. For regulatory coherence and completeness, 
we advise to refer to the definition as defined under the FCM regulation EU10/2011. 
o There is no definition for recycling nor high quality recycling included.  
In alignment with the Waste Framework Directive, we call for  including or refering to : ‘recycling’ means any recovery 
operation by which waste materials are reprocessed into products, materials, or substances whether for the original 
or other purposes.

3 Substances of concern 
We regret the tendency in recent legislative proposals to regulated substances through a growing number of separate 
legislations: REACH, ESPR, ELVR etc… ELVR is not the appropriate legislative tool to regulate substances of concern or 
chemical safety. By adding provisions on hazardous substances in ELV, an additional layer of legislation is created while 
the REACH regulation perfectly allows to address the concerns of the legislator to restrict the use of certain substances.  
We call for using the REACH regulation to deal with any concern on chemical safety. 
4 Eco-modulation of EPR fees 
We would like to express our concern in the fact that the proposal foresees to ecomodulate  Extended Producer 
Responsibility schemes (EPR) fee based on – amongst others – recycled content.  
The EPR scheme aims at valuing waste  by avoiding its destruction in accordance with the basic principles set out in the 
Waste Framework Directive. The EPR scheme is defined under the WFD and therefore requires the acquisition of waste 
status.  
In this respect, it should be recalled that the Lansink's ladder ranks ways to avoid destruction of waste, among which are 
avoiding discarding an item as waste (=prevention), reusing a wasted item (reuse before becoming waste is not part of the 
WFD), recycling, etc. 
The EPR system is foreseen under Article 8 of the Waste Framework Directive as a proportional instrument involving 
producers, public authorities, and end users including consumers. This instrument is driven by the reduction of the waste 
environmental impacts, which allows it to not strictly follow the Lansink's ladder.  
Although the charge for EPR is levied at the time of placing on the market (e.g. car), it is only linked to the end of the 
product's life and it is not a characteristic assigned on the product itself. It is however based on its nature or composition.  
The proposed eco modulation is based on a number of aspects of products such as recyclability and recycled content.  
• the term recyclability is not yet defined, as art 4.3 requires the Commission to develop in an Implementing Act a 
methodology to verify the recyclability.  
• recycled content: There is no known advantage for waste management if a recycled content is present or not: it 
does neither improve collection, nor sorting nor recycling.  
The EPR instrument cannot be used to set up eco -modulation schemes based on recycled content, which is applicable to 
the placing on the market of an item, before any consideration of its end-of-life.  
These considerations are important as the EC is re -defining the EPR principles in several Green Deal related proposals 
such as ESPR, ELV, and PPWR, while still referring to the WFD, with which the new principles are not consistent. 
We call for a re-evaluation of the new parameters of the EPR scheme and the exclusion of product standards (recycled 
content) from its establishment . Potential eco -modulation of EPR fees should be based on clear, predictable, and 
harmonised criteria, supporting the valuation of the discard item. 
5 Recycled content 
The ELVR proposal sets ambitions targets on recycled content of 25% for plastic in a vehicle, all of which has to come from 
post-consumer waste and of which a quarter is to come from closed loop recycling.  
5.1 Safeguard clause 
In case of the lack of the availability of sufficient quantities  of recycled material such that the industry is not able to 
comply with this market access requirement on recycled content, the legislator should be able to postpone, put on hold or 
adapt the targets.  
We call for the Commission to draft a safeguard clause to adapt recycled content requirements.

5.2 Recycling options 
The wide variety of plastic streams require the development of various new presorting and recycling technologies . 
Current collection and pre -sorting and shredding facilities prioritize the recovery of metals and leave the non -metal 
components (i.e. plastics) largely in a multi-material shredded mixture that becomes extremely challenging for separation 
and recycling. To meet the proposed recycled content targets, including the use of post -consumer ELV recycled plastics, 
a revision of the setup of the existing dismantling facilities would be required. This is demanding significant investments 
and is unlikely that this is achievable in the proposed time frames. Chemical recycling via pyrolysis would allow a pathway 
to close the material loop by handling this heterogenous waste and providing feedstock for producing virgin plastics.  
In addition, for reinforced plastics little to no recycling facilities are existing today or only at low TRL level of development. 
The proposed targets on the use of post -consumer recycled plastics from ELV ignore the contamination of the plastics 
streams with non-intentionally added substances which impedes largely the mechanical recycling routes or requires 
additional purification at scale , for example in the case of plastics used in fuel containers, breaking  fluid containers, 
bumpers… 
Therefore the recognition of other recycling routes such as physical and chemical recycling in the new ELV regulation is 
of utmost importance. Those innovative recycling technologies  should be considered to reach the ambitious recycled 
content targets. The likelihood of investment decisions largely depends on the acceptance of these technologies and on 
the established of harmonized rules for the calculation and verification of recycled or circular content. With regards to the 
calculation of recycled content via chemical recycling we call for using mass balance allocation methodology to support 
the transition to a circular economy for materials.  
5.3 Closed loop recycling 
Imposing closed loop recycling , for products with a long life time such as vehicles , the use of the material only for the 
same purpose than the previous one, raises the concern that a part of the waste streams deriving from end-of-life vehicles 
is likely to contain legacy substances  that were fully compliant with the legislation in force at the moment to car was 
placed on the market for the first time but that have been banned by the time the vehicle reaches its end of life.  
Therefore, we call for open loop  recycling ensuring a steadier supply of secondary materials  and the reduction of 
potential risks associated with legacy substances.

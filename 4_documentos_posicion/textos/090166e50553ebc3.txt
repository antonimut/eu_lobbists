▪ DERICHEBOURG, a European player in automotive recycling 
 
DERICHEBOURG wishes to react in the context  of the public consultation launched by the European 
Commission until 4 December 2023, concerning the draft regulation published on 13 July 2023, on circularity 
requirements for the design of vehicles and the management of end-of-life vehicles. 
 
DERICHEBOURG is a family -owned group, created in 1956 by entrepreneurs who were aware of the recovery 
potential of metal waste. Particularly present in France and Europe, with a dense territorial network, the 
group produced 6 million tons of recycled metals in 2022. DERICHEBOURG is a long -standing member of 
FEDEREC, the French federation of recycling companies, as well as EuRIC, the European confederation of the 
recycling industry. 
 
DERICHEBOURG is a major player in end-of-life vehicle (ELV) recycling in France and Europe with: 
- 200 authorized treatment facilites (ATFs), including 
25 shredders in Europe (Belgium, Germany, Spain, 
France, Italy, Romania). 
- More than 500,000  ELVs recycled in 2022 in 
Europe. 
- Recycling and recovery rates of 87 % (85 % target) 
and 97 % (95 % target). 
 
▪ A new framework for vehicle design and recycling 
 
DERICHEBOURG welcomes the European Commission's decision to revise the legislation applicable to vehicle 
design and recycling. The Commission's approach, which encompasses the entire life cycle of the vehicle, is 
an excellent approach for the circular economy. Since the early 2000s, the political, economic, and geopolitical 
context in Europe has undergone profound changes, which make evolution essential for the automotive industry.  
 
The transformation of the vehicle fleet on a European scale, the challenges of carbon neutrality and 
industrial sovereignty, are all issues that are the subject of strong framework measures, such as the European 
regulation on batteries, the draft European regulation on critical raw materials (CRM Act) and the draft regulation 
on zero-emission industry (NZI Act). 
 
While DERICHEBOURG supports the European Commission's desire to considerably strengthen the circularity and 
eco-design requirements of new vehicles,  the timing of the draft regulation must be consistent. The 
deadlines for the new end-of-life requirements must be consistent with the time limit set for the new production 
criteria and take into account the average age of end-of-life vehicles of almost 20 years. A time shift between 
design and recycling requirements is essential for overall text coherence.  
 
In addition, some requirements seem to be uncorrelated from the reality and reflect either a lack of 
pragmatism or the absence of a prior technical and economic feasibility study , especially t he re quirement 
regarding the removal of parts and components before shredding (article 30). Finally, the leading role given to 
car manufacturers through the principle of Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) must be accompanied 
by safeguards to avoid an unbalanced balance of power, to the detriment of the recycling value chain.  
 
DERICHEBOURG makes comments and proposals, which are intended to be constructive, to actively contribute 
to the European legislative process.

CHAPTER 1 – GENERAL PROVISIONS 
 
 
ARTICLE 2 – SCOPE 
DERICHEBOURG supports the extension of the scope provided for in the draft regulation. Indeed, the 
extension of the scope to vehicles of categories M2, M3, N2, N3 and O and categories L3e, L4e, L5e, L6e and 
L7e, is a positive development.  
  
DERICHEBOURG welcomes the application period set at 5 years after the entry into force of the regulation. 
This period will be necessary for companies to organize themselves and invest in the necessary resources to 
manage motorcycles, trucks and buses at the end of their life. Indeed, the pollution control tools, such as 
pollution control stations or movable bridges, as well as the dismantling tools, are not the same as for managing 
the vehicles covered by Directive 2000/53/EC. 
 
ARTICLE 3 – DEFINITIONS 
| Treatment (16)  
DERICHEBOURG suggests the elimination of compacting, which is not a treatment  method, but rather a 
preparation for a transport operation, as well as the elimination of shearing, which is totally unsuitable to achieve 
the recycling and recovery targets set by the regulation. The various materials can only be satisfactorily sorted 
by shredding in order to obtain high-quality secondary raw materials (definition 25). 
 
| Shredding (17) 
DERICHEBOURG proposes to revise the definition to add the notion of sorting, which is the primary purpose of 
a shredder. The definition adopted is reductive , insufficiently precise  and unfit to  meet the best available 
techniques for waste treatment, referenced in the BREF Waste Treatment Industries1. DERICHEBOURG proposes 
to remove the possibility of "tearing into pieces", which is not the role of a shredder, but rather of a shear, a tool 
that does not guarantee the achievement of the targets of the regulation. In addition, DERICHEBOURG proposes 
to add the notion of "recycling processes", which is included in the definition of "secondary raw materials". 
 
| Waste Management Operators (21)  
DERICHEBOURG proposes to revise the wording to ensure consistency with Article 24 on the handing over of 
ELVs to approved treatment facilities (in the sense of installations authorized by the administration). It is 
imperative to limit the activities of waste management operators to collection operations only . Only 
approved treatment facilities are authorised  to carry out treatment operations. Otherwise, there is a risk of the 
development of parallel sectors that are not subject to the same requirements for environmental preservation.  
 
PROPOSAL 1 :  
 
► Amend the definition of treatment (16) as follows: "Any activity after the end-of-life vehicle has been handed 
over to a facility for the depollution, dismantling, compacting, shearing, shredding, recovery or preparation for 
disposal of the shredded waste, and any other operation carried out for the recovery or disposal of the end -of-
life vehicle and its parts, components and materials. »  
 
► Amend the definition of shredding (17) to read: "Any operationrecycling process used for tearing into 
pieces or fragmenting and sort end-of-life vehicles, in accordance with the best available techniques of 
the BREF of the waste treatment industries". 
 
► Amend the definition of waste management operators (21) to read: "Any natural or legal person who dealing 
on a professional basis with collection or treatment of end-of-life vehicles".

CHAPTER 2 – CIRCULARITY REQUIREMENTS 
 
 
ARTICLE 4 – REUSABILITY, RECYCLABILITY AND RECOVERABILITY 
The application period of 6 years after the entry into force of the regulation seems disproportionate, since the 
targets of 85% and 95% of recyclabili ty and recoverability have already been in force since 2005 2. The longer 
the application deadlines last, the more limited the possibilities for reuse, recycling and recovery of vehicles sold 
between now and that date will be limited, making it impossible to achieve the targets set out in Article 34.  
 
PROPOSAL 2:  
► Strengthen the ambition of the regulation in terms of the recyclability and recoverability of new vehicles, with 
application as soon as the regulation enters into force, i.e. 12 months after its publication.  
 
ARTICLE 5 – SUBSTANCES 
Exemptions for the use of certain substances of concern (lead, mercury, cadmium or hexavalent chromium) 
should not have the effect of restricting the possibility , of secondary raw materials produced from end -of-life 
vehicles, being reintroduced into new products. An assessment must be integrated into manufacturers' 
circularity strategies (Article 9). 
 
PROPOSAL 3:  
► Assess exemptions for the use of certain substances of concern, to ensure that their use does not come at 
the expense of recycling and recovery of end-of-life vehicles. 
 
ARTICLE 6 – MINIMUM RECYCLED CONTENT 
DERICHEBOURG supports the incorporation rate of 25% recycled (post -consumer) plastics in new vehicles, with 
the possibility of setting rates for steel, aluminum, magnesium and critical metals. While only 12% of the raw 
materials consumed by European industry come from recycling (EUROSTAT), this measure is a big step forward 
for the circular economy. On the other hand, to ensure the effectiveness of the target, independent certification 
of the minimum recycled content is necessary. 
 
PROPOSAL 4: 
► Have the minimum recycled content assessment certified by an independent and accredited third-party body.  
 
ARTICLE 7 – DESIGN FOR EXTRACTION AND REPLACEMENT  
DERICHEBOURG welcomes the obligation for each vehicle to be designed in such a way as not to hinder the 
extraction of the parts and components listed in Annex VII, Part C, from the end-of-life vehicle. 
 
The period of application (6 years after the entry into force of the regulation) of the design and recycling 
requirements must be reflected at the end of life, to consider the average age of end -of-life vehicles (between 
18 and 20 years in Europe). The new ecodesign obligations for vehicles placed on the market will not have an 
immediate effect on improving the recyclability of end -of-life vehicles.  Considering the average age of 
vehicles, a transitional period will have to be put in place for the application of the new end -of-life 
targets, the achievability of which will be strongly linked to a circular design of vehicles.  
 
PROPOSAL 5:  
► Reassess the deadlines for the implementation of the new ELV management obligations (Chapter IV), to 
better consider the average age of end-of-life vehicles.

CHAPTER 3 – OBLIGATIONS OF MANUFACTURERS 
 
 
ARTICLE 9 – CIRCULARITY STRATEGY  
For each vehicle type, the manufacturer shall develop a circularity strategy which contains the elements set out 
in Annex IV, Part A.  
 
The annex in question contains confidential information related to the innovation policy developed by 
recycling companies (e.g. information on assumptions regarding the end-of-life vehicle treatment technologies 
in place, relevant technological developments in the treatment of end -of-life vehicles and investments in 
capacities related to these technologies).  
 
The draft regulation should not lead car manufacturers to take the place of recycling companies (vertical 
integration which shows trough ), for the performance of research and development activities, as well as 
associated investments. There is no provision for preserving trade secrets during data collection, even 
though Extended Producer Responsibility could place recycling companies in an unbalanced power 
relation towards manufacturers. 
 
PROPOSAL 6:  
► Require that the collection of data necessary for the development of the manufacturers' circularity strategy 
be carried out by an independent and accredited third party. 
 
ARTICLE 11 – INFORMATION ON THE EXTRACTION AND REPLACEMENT OF PARTS, COMPONENTS 
AND MATERIALS & ARTICLE 13 – CIRCULARITY PASSPORT 
Manufacturers must provide unlimited, standardized, and non-discriminatory access to the information listed in 
Annex V. The circularity passport (Article 13) shall contain all information on the dismantling and replacement 
of the parts referred to in Article 11.  
 
While this information can be useful for reuse activities, which are still manual and poorly automated, this data 
will be of little use at the treatment and recycling stage, which are largely industrialized operations . A 
real step forward would be an incentive for manufacturers to harmonize materials and their location, at least on 
critical products, to ease their extraction on an industrial scale. 
 
PROPOSAL 7:  
► Integrate accessibility of critical and strategic products into the criteria for the modulation of fees (Article 21). 
 
CHAPTER 4 – ELV MANAGEMENT 
 
 
ARTICLE 18 – PRODUCER RESPONSIBILITY ORGANISATION  
DERICHEBOURG welcomes the establishment of a " fair representation of producers and waste management 
operators within [the] governing bodies " of the organisations responsible for producer responsibility. On the 
other hand, this governance obligation must also apply to producers who fulfil their obligations individually.  
For example, this EPR model will be the predominant model chosen by car manufacturers in France, in the 
context of the recent evolution of the automotive sector3. Balanced governance, regardless of the EPR model 
chosen by producers, is essential.  
 
PROPOSAL 8:  
► Extend the governance obligation to individual system producers and integrate approved treatment facilities 
into the stakeholders to be associated.

their obligations individual ly shall ensure a fair representation of producers and authorized treatment 
facilities and waste management operators in their governing bodies. »

ARTICLE 20 – FINANCIAL RESPONSIBILITY OF PRODUCERS  
To ensure that the costs are fairly distributed among all interested operators, the competent authority will have 
to monitor (i) the average cost of collection, recycling and treatment operations as well as the revenues of waste 
management operators and (ii) the level of financial contributions to be paid by producers to eco-organisations.  
 
This involves the collection of confidential and strategic data related to the business model of recycling 
companies. It is imperative that this monitoring be carried out independently and not be used to drive down 
the costs of recycling, at the risk of moving towards a  low-cost circular economy policy , which would be 
environmentally and socially detrimental. 
 
DERICHEBOURG proposes that supervision should be based exclusively on an independent and accredited 
third-party body, applying the rules of statutory auditing. Data collection must be subject to confidentiality 
agreements. This is also the method used by France in 2015 4. 
 
To ensure balanced cost-sharing, financial contributions made by producers must reflect the full costs (minus 
revenues) and consider economic conditions. To this end, indexation is a relevant tool to ensure adequate 
coverage of ELV management costs . Indexation is widely used in contracts between recyclers and their 
partners (manufacturers, administrations, local authorities) and would make it possible to consider, over time, 
changes in labor, transport, energy and raw material costs5.  
 
PROPOSAL 10:  
► Rely on an independent and accredited third -party body, applying the rules of statutory audit, as part of the 
cost monitoring mechanism, and study the implementation of indexation on a regular basis, to reflect economic 
conditions.

The financial contributions paid by producers to collective schemes are modulated according to several criteria. 
In point 1 -e) of Article 21, DERICHEBOURG wishes to  complete the list of materials and substances that 
prevent a high-quality recycling process. Alongside adhesives, composite plastics or carbon fiber reinforced 
materials, we ask for the addition of filled plastics, as additives such as talcum powder or fiberglass are a barrier 
to recycling, as is the inclusion of electronic components in bumpers (reversing cameras and sensors ), which 
cannot be easily recovered.

ARTICLE 23 – COLLECTION OF END-OF-LIFE VEHICLES  
The draft regulation allows Member States to permit waste management operators other than authorized 
treatment facilities to set up collection points for ELVs.  
 
The possibility of having collection points, which are not operated by authorized treatment facilities, should be 
an exception for situations where the territorial coverage is insufficient. If this condition is not met, it will 
pave the way for illegal dumping and plants that will not have the same level of environmental requirements.  
 
In addition, the handing over of ELVs to authorized treatment facilities must be done in accordance with the 
principle of proximity to avoid additional costs related to logistics and the carbon impact of transport 
operations. 
 
PROPOSAL 12:  
► Limit the possibility of having collection points, outside of authorized treatment facilities, to geographical 
areas where the territorial coverage is insufficient.  
Amend point 4 as follows: "In the event of insufficient territorial coverage of  authorized treatment 
facilities, Member States may authorize waste management operators other than authorized treatment facilities 
to set up collection points for end-of-life vehicles." 
 
SECTION 24 – DELIVERY OF END-OF-LIFE VEHICLES TO AUTHORIZED TREATMENT FACILITIES 
The proposed regulation provides for the free take-back of an ELV by a authorized treatment facility, unless the 
ELV does not contain any of the essential parts and components, except the electric vehicle battery, or contains 
waste that has been added.  
 
Essential parts and components should be listed to avoid misinterpretation, and include at least the battery, 
catalytic converter, powertrain, chassis and wheels. This list needs to be updated in line with technological 
developments. Safely removing the battery from an electric vehicle requires technical knowledge and training, 
which only authorized treatment facilities can have.  More globally, the SLI lead battery is a hazardous waste 
and has to be removed and stored by authorized treatment facilities. 
In addition, the current wording implies that the obligation to take back free of charge does not apply to vehicles 
with "none" of the essential parts and components. However, the absence of at least one essential part or 
component must be sufficient for free take-back not to be compulsory. 
PROPOSAL 13: 
► Amend point 2 to read: "  The handing over of an end-of-life vehicle to an approved treatment facility shall be 
free of charge for the last owner of the vehicle, unless the end -of-life vehicle does not contain any   of the 
essential parts or   components of  the vehicle, with the exception of the electric vehicle battery, or contains 
waste that has been added to the end -of-life vehicle. The essential parts and components are: the battery, 
the catalytic converter, the powertrain, the chassis and the wheels. This list must be updated according 
to technological developments in the types of vehicles placed on the market.  » 
 
ARTICLE 26 – OBLIGATIONS OF THE VEHICLE OWNER  
The proposed regulation requires the owner to return the ELV to an authorized treatment facility or collection 
point, after being informed that the vehicle meets one of the criteria for unrepairability . The obligation to verify 
the repairability criteria (Annex 1, point A) considerably complicates the handing over of the ELV to an authorized 
installation. The more complex the procedure, the more the illegal sector will develop to the detriment of 
the official recycling plants . The regulation also does not specify who is responsible for determining the 
technical or economic irreparability of a vehicle.

ARTICLE 27 – OBLIGATIONS RELATING TO APPROVED TREATMENT FACILITIES  
The obligation in point 3(c) to remove the parts and components listed in Part C of Annex VII is not suitable  for 
the management of end-of-life vehicles. Indeed, some parts and components will be removed to be recycled, 
not just for their potential for reuse, remanufacturing or refurbishment. This is implied by the title of Article 30 
"Mandatory removal of parts and components for reuse and recycling prior to shredding ". However, the 
recycling sector is completely missing from Article 27. 
 
In addition, if certain parts or components are removed for recycling, the obligation of  systematic " non-
destructive" extraction does not make sense. This obligation is unrealistic, since it presupposes knowledge 
of all vehicle models, whereas there is no harmonization between manufacturers, or even between  the models 
of the same manufacturer. In addition, the current market for reused parts is mainly located for export to third 
countries (the case of engines and gearboxes, the main parts concerned by export ). 
 
DERICHEBOURG is not in favour of the compulsory and systematic withdrawal of parts and components. 
The regulation must ensure that the incentives put in place by the Member States (Article 33) will create 
a market for the part s, before imposing removal that will lead to unmanageable stocks or massive 
exports outside the European Union. The financial responsibility of producers must be mobilised when 
the environmental added value is certain, under justified technical and economic conditions. Does the 
European Union want to give priority to the large -scale export of parts in order to extend the life of 
polluting vehicles and lose  industrial sovereignty, or does the EU  want to preserve European 
independence in the supply of strategic materials? 
 
PROPOSAL 15: 
► Amend point 3(c) to read: " remove, where technically and economically justified, the parts and 
components listed in Part C of Annex VII from the end -of-life vehicle, prior to shredding or compacting, by 
means of manual dismantling or (semi-)automated disassembly, in a non-destructive manner, for components 
with a reuse, remanufacturing,  refurbishment or recycling potential ; »

ARTICLE 30 – MANDATORY REMOVAL OF PARTS AND COMPONENTS FOR REUSE AND RECYCLING 
PRIOR TO SHREDDING  
The draft regulation provides that authorized treatment  facilities shall remove in a non -destructive manner 
(Article 27), certain parts and components, referenced in Annex VII, Part C, prior to shredding. An exception is 
provided for parts and components 13 to 19, if it is demonstrated (Annex VII, Part G) that post -shredder 
technologies provide to be as efficient as manual dismantling or semi-automated disassembly processes. 
 
The provisions of this article are in line with the objectives that DERICHEBOURG supports: better sorting 
performance and a higher incorporation of recycled materials. However, companies must remain free to use the 
means to achieve them, especially since recyclers have developed sophisticated, automated and digitized 
technologies. We therefore call on the European Commission not to impose obligations of means on the 
recycling industry. The Commission must rather  set up performance obligations, while supporting a 
technology-neutral approach.   
 
• DERICHEBOURG  is surprised by the distribution of  the parts, because some of the parts and 
components involved are impossible to extract , such as the electrically driven  headlights  and 
motors, because their access is very complex and different depending on the vehicle. Shredding allows 
the motors to be sorted separately, and to route the fractions to specific recycling units specialized on 
copper sorting.  
• For other parts and components, we do not understand the interest of extraction before 
shredding:  the dashboards cannot be reused, and due to their very heterogeneous composition, they 
must be shredded to ensure the separation of the materials that compose them. It is also the case for 
motors, which are recycled through the shredder ; while their removal before shredding only intends in 
exporting ou t of the EU, second-hand motors. Finally, the glass must be extracted before crushing 
because this allows for better recovery. However, while glass can be removed intelligently, it cannot be 
removed non-destructively. 
• On the other hand, some parts or components are negatively affected by the removal exemption, even 
though they are easy to remove and better recoverable if they are removed before shredding, 
such as bumpers and tanks. 
 
DERICHEBOURG calls for the deletion of the removal obligation for all parts and components for which 
there is insufficient or non -existent demand (no reuse market), or whose extraction upstream of 
shredding will not allow an improvement in recycling (no added value for high -quality recycling).  
Generally speaking, a systematic withdrawal makes no sense in the absence of a market, and would be 
counterproductive. Authorized treat ment facilities must be able to decide on the appropriateness of 
removal before shredding, depending on the models and demand on the second-hand market.

ARTICLE 31 – REQUIREMENTS FOR EXTRACTED PARTS AND COMPONENTS  
The issues of industrial sovereignty and the draft European regulation on critical materials (CRM Act) are 
legitimate if Europe wishes to reduce its dependence on third countries that own resources. On the other hand, 
the requirements set out in Part F of Annex VII for the removal of permanent magnets or parts containing 
permanent magnets do not seem to consider the operational and economic constraints of the recycling 
industry.  
 
The concept of "excessive cost" needs to be clarified . The storage and labelling of components, in the 
absence of technical progress to recycle them, seems totally inappropriate to industrial reality. The 
responsibility for building up such stockpiles, in the absence of recycling technology and a market, 
should not lie upon treatment facilities.  
 
PROPOSAL 19: 
► Carry out a techno -economic study, 12 months after the entry into force of the regulation, to validate the 
content of Annex VII, Part F, to take into account the feasibility of the obligations.

ARTICLE 34 – REUSE, RECYCLING AND RECOVERY OBJECTIVES  
Seemingly unchanged from the reuse, recycling and recovery objectives of Directive 2000/53/EC currently in 
force, the exclusion of batteries represents a significant evolution, with a change in the scope that is not 
without consequences. 
 
A battery is 100% recycled, and therefore makes a positive contribution to the recycling rate today. If the battery 
is removed from the final mass, the mass of the other subfractions, including those that are difficult to recycle, 
will not change. The ratio would be unfavourable to the achievement of the objectives, and the lighter the car, 
the more it will impact the recycling rate. 
 
Indeed, while for internal combustion vehicles, the lead -acid starter battery represents  1.4% of the vehicle's 
mass, the magnitude is much higher when it comes to the traction battery of electric vehicles, with 
nearly 20% of the mass concerned . The recycling target will therefore be much more difficult to achieve 
without the battery, even if the battery is removed from the mass under consideration. 
 
In order to adapt these objectives to electric vehicles, the composition of which may differ from that of internal 
combustion vehicles, a study on the recyclability of electric vehicles should be carried out, in conjunction with 
authorized treatment facilities. 
 
PROPOSAL 20: 
► Conduct a study on the recyclability of electric vehicles to adapt recycling and recovery objectives to the 
transformation of the vehicle fleet. 
 
CHAPTER 6 – INSPECTIONS 
 
SECTION 46 - INSPECTIONS 
The draft regulation stipulates that inspections will have to cover at least 10% of operators, each calendar year, 
and will also cover the export of used vehicles. 
 
The European Union's  ambition to combat the illegal sector must be stronger. Inspections must concern in 
priority unapproved or unauthorized facilities that are deemed to handle end -of-life vehicles without 
authorization.  
 
The identification of illegal sites could be done through artificial intelligence and aerial photos, which could 
easily target sites where several vehicles are stored, without this being a declared activity. Since 2021, the tax 
administration has been tracking down undeclared swimming pools in France thanks to dedicated artificial 
intelligence, and aerial photos, which make it possible to scrutinize each plot.  
 
An official register of inspections carried out  should be kept by the competent authority of each Member 
State, together with a list of the sites inspected. The list of inspected operators must change every year, in order 
to avoid the same companies being targeted and eventually all installations havin g been inspected.  
 
PROPOSAL 21: 
► Carry out an annual publication of the inspections and rely on artificial intelligence to identify illegal sites.

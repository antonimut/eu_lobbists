EU Commission proposal on the “Circularity requirements for vehicle design 
and on management of end-of-life vehicles” Regulation 
Volkswagen Group Position 
As a vehicle manufacturer (OEM), we fully support the advancing of the European Union 
into a modern, resource-efficient and competitive economy. Therefore, we welcome the 
revision of the end-of-life (ELV) vehicle regulation - the Commission’s current proposal will 
lead to a transformation towards improved sustainability, circularity and innovation. The car 
industry is ready to design their products to achieve further advanced circularity. 
The ELV Regulation is currently and should remain the central piece of legislation dealing 
with circularity requirements for design, production and end-of-life treatment of vehicles 
and their components. Consequently, there is no need to regulate the automotive sector in 
other legislative acts, e.g., the Eco-design for Sustainable Products Regulation or the 
Directive on common rules promoting the repair of goods (right to repair). 
In addition, the choice of the legal instrument (Regulation to replace Directive) will secure 
the implementation of a harmonised framework for vehicles in each Member State, 
reducing operating costs for all economic operators while delivering solid environmental 
benefits. The co-legislators should therefore refrain from providing unnecessary, costly and 
burdensome flexibility to Member States, especially for the extended producer 
responsibility obligations. 
The priority challenges arising from the draft proposal are: 
I. The recycled content target for plastic (Art. 6) does not reflect the expected availability 
of supply or the limitations from technical feasibility. In addition, the definition of plastic 
in Art. 3 includes types of plastic, which cannot be recycled. 
 Therefore, the target for plastics should be reduced to a technically achievable value 
and the definition refined. 
 
II. The design for dismantling and removability obligations (Art. 7) include, in Annex VII C, 
components that are very complex and highly integrated into the vehicle. At the same 
time, highly efficient recycling processes lead to high recycling rates and lower CO2 
footprints. 
 Therefore removal obligations for components must always be technically feasible and 
should be reasonable and proportionate.  
 
III. The extended producer responsibilities of the vehicle producer have significantly 
expanded (Art. 16 ff), including the obligation to monitor ELV treatment facilities and 
waste management operators and to compensate financial gaps. 
 Therefore, obligations for the producer should be limited to those waste management 
operators or treatment facilities, which are included in the producer’s organisation or 
where a contractual basis between producer and waste management operator or 
treatment facility exists.

Detailed Analyses 
In general, based on an initial assessment of the draft regulation, we have identified a 
number of ambiguities, uncertainties and challenges. These include obligations and 
responsibilities, lack of methodologies, definitions and information requirements. They can 
be found in the following Articles: 
 
1. Article 3 – Definitions 
Art. 3 sets out a number of definitions. 
Analysis:  
• The definition of “plastic” includes elastomers & process materials (paints, adhesives, 
sealant agents), in addition to thermoplastics and PU-foam whereas the JRC-study 
excluded elastomers which are not mechanically recyclable.  
• The definition of “Substance of Concern” is linked to a piece of legislation (the Draft 
ESPR) for which vehicles are not included in the scope. There is therefore a mismatch in 
terms of applicability.  
• In a number of the translations of the draft Regulation (e.g. German, Czech and Swedish) 
the text is not aligned with the originally published English. Examples include the 
definitions of “Producer” (Art 3.1(22)) and “Manufacturer” (Art.3.2(b)). This impacts the 
applicability of the Regulation to affected parties.  
VW Proposal: 
• The definition of plastic should only apply to polymers, which were in the focus of the 
JRC study during impact assessment. Process materials should not be in the scope at all, 
because these materials cannot be removed as a separate waste stream and 
subsequently recycled. 
• The definition of “Substance of Concern” should be amended so that a list of 
“substances of concern” are provided, including CAS-numbers for all substances. For all 
substances, standardised analytical methods must exist.  
• The translations of the Regulation must be accurate. In the German translation 
definitions of both “Producer” (“Hersteller”) and “Manufacturer” (also translated as 
“Hersteller” instead of “Erzeuger”) must be provided and used accordingly. 
 
2. Article 4 - Reusability, recyclability and recoverability of vehicles 
Art. 4 sets out the key goals for manufacturers of “(a) reusability or recyclability to a 
minimum of 85% by mass and (b) reusability or recoverability to a minimum of 95% by 
mass”. At the same time, Art. 34 requires waste management operators to meet similar 85% 
and 95% targets but without including EV-batteries in the calculation methodology. The 
achievement of the latter targets is to be ensured both by producers (Art. 16) and by 
Member States (Art. 34). Furthermore, waste management operators shall recycle 30% of 
the plastic. 
Analysis: There are some major concerns about these provisions: 
• Most importantly, there is unclarity in the wording of Art.4.1.a and 4.1.b, “reusable or 
recyclable to a minimum of”.  
• There is inconsistency as to whether or not batteries are included in the calculation of 
the reuse, recycling and recovery targets between Art. 4 and Art. 34. 
• There is a duplication of the responsibility for achieving the targets between producers 
and waste management operators. 
• Manufacturers and producers do not have effective tools nor the legal basis to enforce 
compliance by waste management operators, especially as such operators do not 
recycle on an OEM specific basis.  
VW Proposal: 
• The wording of Art.4.1.a and 4.1.b should be amended so that the sentence reads: 
“reusability and/or recyclability” respective “reusability and/or recoverability”. 
• Calculations under Art. 34 should be aligned with those for Art. 4, i.e., for both type-
approval and reporting, the battery of a vehicle should be included in the calculation to 
fulfil these goals. 
• Although achieving quotas requires action from all actors, waste management operators 
should be the only actor that report to the Member States on the achievement of the 
targets (Art. 16 and Art. 34). 
• The role of manufacturers should be limited to the requirement set out in Art. 4 to 
design and construct reusable, recyclable and recoverable vehicles. 
 
3. Article 5 – Substances in vehicles 
Art. 5 requires that the presence of substances of concern in vehicles and in their parts and 
components shall be minimised as far as possible. For type-approval, manufacturers need to 
prove that they comply with the restriction for lead, mercury, cadmium or hexavalent 
chromium, as well as other material restrictions, such as SoCs, EU REACH, EU POPs and EU 
Battery Regulation. 
Analysis: 
• The definition of SoCs is linked to a piece of legislation (the Draft ESPR) for which vehicles 
are not included in the scope and includes differing criteria, e.g. the REACH SVHC list. It 
also links to the CLP, which is a dynamic piece of legislation and relates Art. 5.1 to 
thousands of substances, adding additional burden. 
• The sentence “minimised as far as possible” is ambiguous. It is not clear as to what is 
reasonably achievable and proportionate. 
• For the change to the process for renewal of Annex III exemptions, relying only on a 
report from ECHA removes the stakeholder engagement process that currently takes 
place.  
VW Proposal: 
• The evidence to be submitted for type-approval to prove compliance with the restriction 
for lead, mercury, cadmium or hexavalent chromium, as well as other material 
restrictions, such as SoCs, EU REACH, EU POPs and EU Battery Regulation, must be 
clarified.  
• “minimised as far as possible” must be further clarified. In particular, as to what is 
reasonably achievable and proportional, e.g. a definition of required documentation or 
processes to prove that this requirement has been met.  
• For the change to the process for renewal of Annex III exemptions, the stakeholder 
engagement process that currently takes place should be continued.  
• We expect that the exemption for lead in batteries (Annex III, Exemption 5bi) remains 
and continues to be linked to the vehicle type approval date. 
 
4. Article 6 - Minimum recycled content in vehicles 
Art. 6 lays down a minimum recycled content target for plastics of 25% (post-consumer), 
where at least 25% of the 25% (= 6.25%) target shall be achieved by including plastics 
recycled from end-of-life vehicles. 
Analysis: 
• The recycling content target has been defined, before a methodology has been 
introduced. This leads to uncertainty regarding how to account for the material stream 
and allowed technologies. 
• No supply and demand study for recyclates has been conducted and it is therefore 
unclear whether there will be enough material of automotive quality available on the 
market, particularly due to legally enforced competition from other sectors (see other 
newly adopted legislation or draft legislation).  
• In addition to the supply, the technical feasibility is also rated as critical, because of 
recyclates from mechanical recycling do not have the same quality as primary material, 
which is needed, for example, for safety-relevant components. 
• Pre-consumer (post-industrial) recycled content, workshop waste and material obtained 
from chemical recycling are not explicitly counted towards the calculation. If pre-
consumer recyclate is not accounted for in the target, it risks not being used and 
becoming waste and moreover is needed to fulfil any target. 
• Regarding the closed-loop requirement, it must be pointed out that the challenge of 
legacy substances, due to vehicle longevity (15 - 18 years) and dynamic material 
legislations, e.g. EU REACH, EU POPs etc., has not been solved yet and it would 
contradict the EU’s Chemical Strategy for Sustainability if only mechanical recycling can 
be used. 
• See again our comment above related to the definition of plastic (Art. 3). 
VW Proposal: 
•  A detailed calculation methodology and certification process must be defined suitably 
early to give manufacturers certainty in the implementation. The proposed transition 
period of 72 months for implementation of the targets should start after publication of 
the methodology. 
• The overarching target should be reduced, in line with supply and technical feasibility. 
• With the aim of reducing primary raw materials and CO2, all sustainable materials 
should be considered, such as pre-consumer (post-industrial) recyclates, material from 
chemical recycling and bio-based materials. This target could then be reviewed and 
tightened over time, depending on the availability of the recycled plastics on the market.  
• We strongly recommend opening up the closed-loop requirement to include all waste 
streams, e.g. workshop waste. This leads to more flexibility to fulfil quotes, without 
reducing the ecological impact.  
• A solution must be found for the problem of legacy substances, especially with regard to 
closed-loop requirements. To achieve the recycled material quota, specific thresholds 
for allowable levels of hazardous substances in recycled materials or a general 
exemption for hazardous substances in materials already placed on the market or 
mechanically recycled materials (principle: recycling material as produced material) are 
required.  
• Similar to the Battery Regulation (EU 2023/1542 Art. 8) and the draft Packaging 
Regulation (Proposal of Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation, COM (2022)0677, 
Art. 7.9 and 7.10) an exception and a review clause should be introduced which enables 
the Commission to revise and amend the target in case of non-availability of recycled 
material. 
 
5. Article 7 - Design to enable removal and replacement of certain parts and components 
in vehicles 
Art. 7, in connection with Annex VII Part C, lays down certain design requirements for 
removal and replacement. 
Analysis:  
• The meaning of “does not hinder” in Art.7 1. is not sufficiently clearly defined.  
• For some components in the list, like the (whole) wire harness, dashboards, etc., there is 
no possibility for easy dismantling at the design stage.  
• For many components, the use of modern, economically efficient and industrially 
applicable sorting technologies after shredding leads to comparable separation results 
and unmixed material streams for further recycling. 
• The timeline of requirements for components listed in Part C of Annex VII is inconsistent: 
o After 12 months: ATFs to remove parts and assess if all removed parts are fit for 
reuse, remanufacturing, recycling (Art. 27). 
o After 36 months: OEMs to provide detailed dismantling info (Art. 11). 
o After 36 months: ATFs to ensure that components are removed, with certain 
exemptions if they demonstrate that PST can separate materials efficiently (Art. 30). 
o After 72 months: OEMs to design components in a way that does not hinder removal 
(Art. 7). 
• A dismantling of parts intended for reuse without demand for such parts would not only 
be unreasonable but even contradictory to the overarching goal to improve the 
ecological footprint.  
VW Proposal: 
• The subclause in Art. 7 1. “does not hinder” should be rephrased so as to be clearer and 
reasonably practicable.   
• Removal obligations for components must always be technically feasible and should be 
reasonable and proportionate. A specific use must also exist at all. Removal in a non-
destructive manner should not apply for removal for recycling and only apply for removal 
for re-use, refurbishment or re-manufacture.  
• A requirement for mandatory manual dismantling (Art. 30) is often counterproductive 
and should only be specified if the desired goals cannot be achieved otherwise. In 
principle, the best available technology should be used for each recycling process.  
• The timeline of requirements for components listed in Part C of Annex VII must be 
aligned. 
• Volkswagen welcomes the prohibition of reuse of airbags. However, current tools for 
neutralising airbags inside the vehicles are commonly used and should remain an option. 
Annex VII Part B No. 2 (a) should be complemented by adding neutralisation of airbags 
inside the vehicle as possible treatment step.   
 
6. Article 8 – General obligations 
Art. 8 requires that the requirements of Art. 4 - 7 & 9 - 11 are fulfilled, including 
documentation, in order to gain type approval. Art. 8 will be applicable 12 months after 
entry into force. 
Analysis: 
• Manufacturers and type approval authorities need clear clarification, tools and 
processes of how to assess these new requirements when issuing new type approvals. 
• Additionally, the new type approval demands are not in line with the globally 
harmonised UN R133 regulation. This will lead to divergence from non-EU markets, 
leading to a loss of synergies and worldwide-harmonised standards. Manufacturers and 
type approval authorities will need more time and resources to set up processes. 
VW Proposal 
• Clear clarification, tools and processes of how to assess these new requirements when 
issuing new type approvals must be issued.  
• Alignment with the globally harmonised standards should be maintained. 
 
 
7. Article 9 – Circularity strategy 
Art. 9, in connection with Annex IV, requires that a circularity strategy must be drawn up for 
newly type-approved vehicles. 
Analysis:  
• The benefit for submitting a circularity strategy for each newly type-approved model is 
unclear.  
• A format for how to set up this strategy and assessment process by the enforcement 
and type approval authorities is missing.  
• The expectations of Commission, enforcement, and type approval authorities is unclear. 
Different authorities may have different expectations, which could lead to 
unharmonised type approval processes in each EU Member State. 
• The requirements in the Annexes partly apply to the vehicle itself and partly to the 
strategy of the OEM. Annex IV Part A and B contain several requirements which cannot 
be controlled by OEMs or which cannot be “measured” or “assessed” by OEMs.  
For example, regarding part A, 5. C “monitoring on how parts, components and 
materials contained in vehicles belonging to the vehicle type are reused, recycled and 
recovered in practice”, ELVs are treated by waste management operators 15 - 18 years 
after type approval is granted. Therefore, it cannot be monitored at the time of type 
approval. Additionally, there is no known process for how to track all parts and materials 
globally by waste management operators. Waste streams will be mixed in/after the 
shredder and cannot be tracked.  
• Part B, 3a: The term “high quality recycling” is not defined. 
VW Proposal: 
• The obligation to submit the circularity strategy should apply for manufacturers at 
company level and not for each newly type-approved model, similarly to the current 
company recycling strategy under the ELV-Directive.  
• Clarification on the format or method for the strategy and process and expectations of 
enforcement and type approval authorities should be provided. Lack of harmonisation 
across EU Member States should be avoided. 
• Waste management operators (recyclers) should be targeted to improve and 
implement the recycling technologies (Annex IV, Point 6), since the expertise and 
responsibilities lie with them and not the producer of the vehicle.  
• Part A 5c, 6. and 7. should be deleted. 
• Part B, 2. and 3. should be deleted. 
 
  
8. Article 11 - Information on removal and replacement of parts, components and 
materials present in vehicles 
Art. 11 obliges manufacturers to provide waste management operators and repair and 
maintenance operators with information on removal and replacement of certain parts and 
components free of charge. 
VW Analysis: 
• Authorized treatment facilities are already today informed free of charge on removal of 
parts and components via the International Dismantling Information System (IDIS).  
• Authorized treatment facilities are informed via IDIS how to gain access to training and 
repair and maintenance information etc. https://www.idis2.com/part_reuse.php. 
• Waste management operators do not require the information on replacement and 
providing this information would increase the risk on non-qualified persons dealing with 
dangerous components like high-voltage batteries, gas tanks or pyrotechnical devices. 
• The obligation to provide repair and maintenance operators free charge with 
information on removal and replacement is contradictory to Art. 63 (1) of Regulation 
(EU) 2018/858 which allows the manufacturer to charge reasonable and proportionate 
fees for access to vehicle repair and maintenance information. It would be against 
competition law to provide information free of charge to other independent operators. 
VW proposals:  
• Art. 11 should oblige manufacturers to provide waste management operators only with 
information on removal free of charge via IDIS. In addition, the term free of charge 
needs to be removed for provision of information to waste management and repair and 
maintenance operators. 
 
9. Article 13 – Circularity Vehicle Passport 
Art. 13 describes the obligation for manufacturers to provide a circularity vehicle passport 
for each vehicle placed to the market. The manufacturer shall ensure that the information in 
the circular vehicle passport is accurate, complete and up to date. 
Analysis: 
• Today, the openly available IT system International Dismantling Information System (IDIS) 
already provides detailed information, e.g., on parts for recycling and dismantling of 
components, to all interested waste management operators, per vehicle type. 
• Additionally, the tracking of materials and substances in automotive products is ensured 
by the global International Materials Data System (IMDS), which connects the entire 
supply chain. This is constantly improved, e.g., IMDS was updated to enable tracking of 
recycled and bio-based content in materials and components. 
• The EU COM has already acknowledged that information per vehicle type is acceptable 
in the SCIP database, required under the EU Waste Framework Directive, to avoid an 
unnecessary burden and provision of overwhelming data volumes. 
• It has to be noted that a guarantee of completeness and up-to-date-ness for the vehicle 
passport can only be ensured by the manufacturer at the time the vehicle is produced 
and not later in its life because of maintenance, customisation and repair (especially in 
case of non-original parts) during its lifetime. 
VW Proposal  
• To avoid duplication and unnecessary burden, the circularity vehicle passport (Art. 13) 
should require information (e.g., for reuse, dismantling and recycling) only per vehicle 
type, but not for each individual vehicle. 
• Furthermore, duplication and unnecessary burden from differing product passports 
required under differing legislative regimes must be avoided. 
• The established system IDIS should be used and developed rather than developing new 
systems. 
• A guarantee of completeness by the manufacturer should be limited to the time of 
placing the vehicle on the market. 
• The circularity vehicle passport, as with all product passports, should not allow direct 
access to a vehicle‘s data, due to e.g. cyber security issues. The information can be 
shared via OEM backends. 
• Art. 13.5, and the cessation of a circularity vehicle passport, should also apply when a 
vehicle is legally exported from the EU, in addition to when a vehicle becomes an end-
of-life vehicle. 
 
10. Chapter IV - Management of end-of-life vehicles (Art. 14 – 36) 
Chapter IV (Art. 14 - 36) lays down requirements for the management of end-of-life 
vehicles:  
• Art. 16 implements extended producer responsibilities (EPR) according to Art. 8 and 8a 
of Directive 2008/98/EC.  
• Art. 20 defines the financial responsibility of the producer. 
• Art. 23 describes the collection requirements for end-of-life vehicles. 
• Art. 24 obliges authorised treatment facilities to accept ELVs free of charge, even in the 
case of vehicles missing the electric vehicle battery. 
• Art. 26 describes that the last owner may give the end-of-life vehicle to any authorised 
treatment facility without having to pay for the treatment, unless essential parts or 
components are missing (Art. 24). 
Analysis: 
General concept of extended producer responsibility:  
• The references of Art. 16 and 20 to Art. 23 suggest that all obligations of a producer/ 
producer responsibility organisation (PRO) refer only to their own collection system. The 
legislator does not clarify how to deal with today’s reality: that the treatment of end-of 
life vehicles is such an attractive business that there are tens-of-thousands of active 
treatment operators in Europe without a contract with the respective producer. The 
current draft does not oblige operators to be part of the producer’s/PRO collection 
system, therefore the legislator appears to accept treatment activities outside the scope 
of extended producer responsibility take-back systems. However, the producer can only 
fulfil extended producer responsibility properly if he as the right of precedence to 
organise his waste in order to steer it in the best performing channels.  
• Art. 16 (a) and (b) require producers to ensure that authorised treatment facilities 
belonging to their collection network treat all end-of-life vehicles in an environmentally 
sound manner and reach the treatment targets. However, the producers do not have the 
necessary tools to enforce compliance by contracted authorised treatment facilities.  
• Art. 18 (4) obliges Producer Responsibility Organisations (PRO) to ensure fair 
representation of producers and waste management operators in their governing bodies. 
A definition of “fair” is missing and it must be ensured that such actions required by this 
regulation comply with competition law. There is a possible conflict of interest here if 
contract partners of a PRO can be represented in the governing body. 
• Art. 20 (1) obliges the producer to finance the costs of their collection and treatment 
system which are not covered by revenues from sales of used spare parts, depolluted 
ELVs or secondary raw materials. An end-of-life vehicle is, in contrast to other end-of-life 
consumer products (e.g., toasters, furniture), a valuable good. If a waste management 
operator claims insufficient profits, this claim cannot be fully verified by a producer. No 
contractual partner can be forced to disclose fully their financial information. 
• Art. 20 (2) (a) obliges the competent authorities to monitor costs and revenues of the 
waste management operators. According to recital (38), the aim of this monitoring is to 
“ensure a fair allocation of costs between all interested operators”. Volkswagen rejects 
any approach contradicting free-market principles. Furthermore, both costs and 
revenues should be fairly addressed between all operators. 
• Art. 23 (1) mentions the possibility for producers to include collection points in the take 
back system, which is an important alternative for offering an extensive customer 
friendly collection system. However, Art. 23 (4) restricts this possibility to set up 
collection points only to waste management operators and Art. 24 only obliges 
authorised treatment facilities to take back an ELV at zero cost for the last owner. 
• Art. 23 (2c) in combination with Art. 20 (1a) obliges the producer to ensure and finance 
workshop disposal. It is unclear how this is supposed to work for independent workshops 
without a contract / legal basis with the producer. In addition, it should not be expected 
that producers finance the disposal of parts that they have not themselves put on the 
market. 
• The high-voltage traction battery (up to 800V) is an integral part of an electric vehicle 
and handling requires trained personnel. An unauthorised separation of vehicle and 
traction battery before handing the vehicle over to a collection point or ATF by 
unqualified persons not acting on a professional basis and therefore without any 
obligation according to the Battery Regulation (or acting illegally) represents a 
considerable risk for people and the environment. An authorised removal e.g. by 
producer-controlled operators will enable circular economy approaches such as 
remanufacturing or securing of recyclates.  
VW Proposal: 
• Compliance with these aspects of the regulation should be the sole responsibility of the 
waste management operator or the authorised treatment facility and be monitored by 
local competent authorities.  
• Art. 16 / Art. 27 should clarify that only waste management operators contracted by the 
producer will be authorised to take back/treat the end-of-life vehicles of that producer. 
• It should be clarified that the compensation under Art. 20 (1) is part of contractual 
negotiation. In addition, it should be clarified that it is not the responsibility of the 
producers to subsidise an unlimited number of inefficient operators and that only 
contracted waste management operators can claim a deficit compensation. 
• Volkswagen request further clarification of Art. 20 (2) (a), regarding what is intended and 
how this shall be implemented uniformly in all Member States.  
• To complement Art 23 (1), Art. 23 (4) should be amended so that producers/PROs are 
also able to set up collection points. In addition, it should not be a Member State 
decision to approve the possibility of collection points but rather an EU-wide given, that 
waste management operators and producers/PROs have this possibility. 
• The sentence Art. 23 (2c) should be deleted. 
• Batteries missing from ELVs delivered to ATFs, as with any other missing parts must be 
regarded as parts for re-use in the calculations for the recycling quota.  
• It must be specified that battery removal from ELVs can only be conducted by 
professionals and not end users. 
 
12. Art. 21 - Fee modulation 
Art. 21 lays down rules for the fee modulation of a producer responsibility organisations 
(PRO) that will be completed with a delegated act. For example, the weight and time 
needed for dismantling shall influence the fee. 
VW Analysis: 
• The Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 on batteries and waste batteries already covers the 
electric vehicle battery.  
• In addition, the Commission should consider that a higher weight of a vehicle might also 
reflect higher material value and should not necessarily lead to a higher fee. 
• We understand that the Commission would like to use time needed for dismantling as a 
factor for the fee because it might have an impact of the costs of a PRO. However, we 
would like to emphasize that it should not be the goal to subsidize inefficient treatment 
operations.   
VW Proposal: 
• Double financing of EPRs for electric vehicle batteries (e.g. obligations Art. 61 (3) 
Regulation 2023/1542/EU) must be avoided.  
• The electric vehicle battery should not be considered in the weight of the vehicle 
regarding the fee modulation.  
• The criteria should be limited to recycled content and RRR rates. 
 
13. Article 25 - Certificate of destruction 
Analysis:  
The certificate of destruction is intended to serve as the basis for the final deregistration of 
the vehicle. In order to give this important article a real impact, it should be clarified that 
the enforcement is vital.  
VW Proposal:  
In case of temporary de-registrations, it should be only possible for a limited period, e.g., 
one year, and the vehicle needs to be presented for prolongation of a temporary de-
registration. 
There needs to be a consequence for ongoing registration, e.g., continuation of 
tax/insurance payments. 
 
14. Article 2 – Scope extension 
We welcome the European Commission’s gradual approach to extending the legislation’s 
scope to heavy-duty vehicles and trailers. The decision to grant a partial exemption from the 
Regulation’s requirements to M2, M3, N2, N3 and O vehicle categories coupled with the 
obligation to provide information on the removal and replacement of parts, components 
and materials contained in vehicles will allow filling of knowledge gaps regarding the end-of-
life of heavy-duty vehicles and inform the development of future rules. However, we note 
that the scope extension generates a series of specific ambiguities and uncertainties related 
to the newly included vehicle categories:  
• Art. 2, 1. (b) expands the application scope to vehicles and end-of-life vehicles of 
categories M2, M3, N2, N3 and O. It can be interpreted as applying the requirement on 
provision of dismantling information to any vehicle of the named categories already on 
the EU market. It is extremely challenging to ensure compliance with this requirement for 
old vehicle types that become an ELV after EiF + 60 months. The scope in this case should 
be limited to vehicles that have been type approved after EiF + 60 months. The most 
certain way to do so is to make providing dismantling information part of the type 
approval . 
• Art. 2, 2. (b) explicitly puts bodywork of multi-stage vehicles out of the Regulation scope 
(keeping only the base vehicle in). Yet no rule is foreseen for 
dismantling/depolluting/treating end-of-life bodywork of multistage vehicles, which 
would normally arrive at ATFs together with the base vehicle. This might lead to 
uncertainty in the application of producers’ responsibility and on how to deal with the 
bodywork treatment process and associated costs, since the liability for vehicle collection 
and depollution lies with the base vehicle producer. To exclude such undesired effects, 
responsibilities for decoupling bodywork and further managing it (depollution, storage, 
etc.) should be clearly assigned in the Regulation. 
• Article 2, 5. (a) and 6. regulate the application of requirements on special purpose 
vehicles (SPV) and specify the requirements on extended producer responsibility for 
categories M2, M3, N2, N3, O. Though it follows from the rest of the text that the 
intention of the regulator is not to impose Art. 5 requirements (substances of concern) 
on heavy duty SPVs and not to shorten the transition period for the named vehicle 
categories from 60 to 36 months after the Regulations’ entry into force, the legal 
technicalities of the text may suggest an opposite interpretation which would warrant a 
clarification. 
VW Proposal: 
• Special purpose heavy-duty vehicles should be ruled the same way for substances of 
concern as heavy-duty vehicles overall. 
•  Dismantling information should become part of type approval for heavy commercial 
vehicles and requirement to provide it should apply to vehicles type approved after EiF + 
60 months.. For this  Art. 2, 3., (e) should be amended to include requirements of Art. 8, 
point 5 into the scope for categories M2, M3, N2, N3. 
• Bodywork of multi-stage vehicles (referred to in Art. 2 2. (b) should be in scope for 
Section 2 on extended producer responsibility and in particular for Article 20 on the 
financial responsibility of the producers. 
• Uncertainty around requirements imposed on heavy-duty special purpose vehicles 
should be addressed. Namely, 
• The first paragraph of Art. 2, par. 5 should read: 
“Notwithstanding paragraph 2, point (a), and without prejudice to paragraph 3, the 
following provisions shall apply to special purpose vehicles”: 
• The first paragraph of Art. 2, par. 6 should read: 
“Without prejudice to paragraph 1, points (b) and (c), Articles 16, 19, 20, 27 and 46 to 49 
shall apply to vehicles and end-of-life vehicles of categories L3, L4, L5, L6 L7, M2, M3, N2, N3 
and O with the following modifications”. 
 
15. General remarks 
Generally, the draft Regulation is to enter into force on the twentieth day following that of 
its publication in the Official Journal of the EU and is to be applicable 12 months after the 
date of its entry into force. No further transition period is given, including for Member 
States to implement measures. 
VW Proposal: 
• Suitable time should be given for the industry to implement the measures. 
• Additionally, the use of secondary legislation should be minimised. Where necessary, it 
should be utilised only with clearly outline timeframes.

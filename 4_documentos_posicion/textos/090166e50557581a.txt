 
28.11.2023 
 
Written feedback of PowerShift on the European Commission’s Proposal for a 
Regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council on circularity 
requirements for vehicle design and on management of end-of-life vehicles, 
amending Regulations (EU) 2018/858 and 2019/1020 and repealing Directives 
2000/53/EC and 2005/64/EC 
 
 
General remarks 
 
PowerShift welcomes the Commission's plan to replace Directive 2000/53/EC with a Regulation on 
circularity requirements for vehicle design and on management of end-of-life vehicles (ELV), as the 
Directive has not been successfully implemented across the EU. So we support a stronger legal 
framework and ambitious circular economy in the mobility sector. 
 
We take positive note of the fact that the European Commission's current draft of the Regulation 
already fulfils various demands from civil society and takes measures to improve the circularity of 
ELV. Nevertheless, we would like to take this opportunity to point out shortcomings in the current 
draft and opportunities for improvement. We therefore set out below our recommendations to 
make the management of ELV more sustainable and increase the potentials for recycling and reuse. 
 
1. Implement the Waste Management Hierarchy 
 
In general, we would like to highlight the fact that in its Waste Framework Directive the EU 
recognises the Waste Management Hierarchy, the first priority of which is waste prevention, 
followed by reuse. Accordingly, these two aspects must also be considered in the ELV Regulation.  
The current draft mainly refers to recycling. We are therefore calling for the Regulation to also set 
binding minimum quotas for the reuse of vehicle components. Also, the exchange of best practices 
should be promoted and manufacturers should be obliged to facilitate the disassembly of all reusable 
vehicle parts (design for disassembly). This position also corresponds to that of other environmental 
organisations, which had called in the previous 2020 and 2021 consultation rounds for specific 
targets for reuse to be implemented in addition to recycling targets. For example, this was 
demanded in more detail in the supporting paper for the Open Public Consultation of the European 
Environmental Bureau (EEB).  
 
2. Avoid human rights violations, set ambitious recycling targets 
 
In 2008 a survey of UN Special Representative John Ruggie found out nearly one third (28 percent) of 
all corporate-related human rights violations are connected to the extractives sector.1 Civil society 
organizations regularly reveal links between mining and human rights violations. For example, the 
international organisation Global Witness showed in their recent report “Standing Firm” that mining 
is one of the sectors linked to the highest number of killings of land and environmental defenders in 
in the period between 2012 and 2022.2

There is a huge risk that the pressure on Indigenous People, local communities and human rights, 
land and environment defenders will increase. But also the environmental and climate change 
aspects of the metal consumption are a risk for humankind. The OECD Global Material Resource 
Outlook to 2060 reports that seven metals including copper, iron and aluminium are responsible for 
minimum seven percent of global greenhouse gas emissions. Copper and nickel cause the greatest 
per-kilo environmental impacts of the metals investigated, while iron has the highest absolute 
environmental impacts due to the large volumes used. The total environmental impacts of using 
these metals are projected to double or even quadruple by 2060.3 
 
The Commission's draft of the new ELV Regulation states in its Whereas: “The environmental 
footprint linked to the manufacturing of new vehicles could increase with the ongoing electrification 
of the fleet, as well as due to more widespread use of electronics in future models, which both require 
a considerable amount of critical and strategic raw materials and precious metals, such as copper 
and rare earth elements. The result of these changes is that the production phase could have a larger 
environmental footprint than the use phase of vehicles.” 
 
In order to reduce the extraction of primary raw materials despite this predicted increase in demand 
for raw materials, a strong ELV Regulation is important. The automotive industry in particular, with 
its high demand for metal, represents an important lever for achieving the EU's recycling targets. 
According to the coming Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA), the Unions consumption of strategic raw 
materials like rare earth elements or platin group metals should be 25 percent covered by secondary 
raw materials. Especially for permanent magnets, sensors and board electronic equipment in 
automobiles, there is a need for ambitious targets for recycling.4 
 
3. Utilising the recycling potentials, extend the scope (Art. 2) 
 
In the current draft of the Regulation, Article 2 states that many of the Regulation’s Articles do not 
apply to vehicle categories M2, M3, N2, N3, O, L3e, L4e, L5e, L6e and L7e, but only to M1 and N1, i.e. 
motor vehicles with a maximum of eight seats and a maximum total mass of 3.5 tonnes.  
 
However, it remains unclear why the recycling requirements of the Regulation do not also apply to 
larger vehicles, motorbikes, quads and trailers. This is particularly surprising given that the impact 
assessment, which was published together with the Commission's proposal for the Regulation, 
clearly emphasises that one of the problem areas to tackle at EU level is the unexploited circularity 
potential of vehicles currently outside the scope of the ELV Directive. 
 
During the previous consultation rounds in 2020 and 2021, various stakeholders had also called for 
the scope of the Regulation to be extended and applied to other types of vehicles, such as trucks and 
motorbikes. As both the Commission itself and environmental NGOs recognise considerable potential 
in extending the scope of the Regulation to include further vehicle categories, this article should be 
adapted accordingly.  
 
4. Increase reusability, recyclability and recoverability of vehicles (Art. 4) 
 
Article 4 of the current draft proposes that each vehicle shall be constructed so that it is reusable or 
recyclable to a minimum of 85 percent by mass and reusable or recoverable to a minimum of 95 
percent by mass. This corresponds to the quotas already announced in the 2000 Directive, becoming 
applicable in 2015.

At the same time, one of the Commission's declared aims with the new regulation is to reduce its 
dependency on primary raw materials and energy.5 It is therefore difficult to understand why the 
decision was taken not to increase the recycling quotas for ELV. Maintaining the quotas that were 
already announced in 2000 is outdated and does not do sufficient justice to today's crises and 
technological possibilities. Accordingly, the recyclability quotas set out in Article 4 should be 
increased. Especially the recycling of parts containing strategic and critical raw materials, such as 
permanent magnets, sensors and board electronics needs to be improved. 
 
5. Set targets for minimum recycled content in vehicles (Art. 6) 
 
We welcome the fact that the Commission plans to set a minimum share of steel recycled from post-
consumer steel waste to be present and incorporated into vehicle types in its proposal. 
Further, the feasibility of establishing a requirement on the minimum share of aluminium, 
magnesium and their alloys is to be review. Since the automotive industry is one of the largest users 
of primary aluminium and steel, we favour the setting of minimum recyclate quotas for these two 
and other raw materials. But since other critical and strategic raw materials are used in high amounts 
in automobiles as well, the EU should also set targets to use recycled copper, rare earth elements 
and other raw materials being perceived as of strategic importance for the EU.  
 
6. Improve the design to enable removal and replacement of certain parts 
and components in vehicles (Art. 7) 
 
In Article 7, the Commission’s proposal contains the following wording: “Each vehicle belonging to a 
vehicle type that is type-approved […] shall be designed in a way which does not hinder the removal 
by authorised treatment facilities of the parts and components listed in Part C of Annex VII from the 
concerned vehicle during the waste phase of the vehicle.” Since the phrase "does not hinder" is 
comparatively weak, it should instead be replaced by "so as to enable". 
 
In the impact assessment, which was published together with the proposal of the Regulation, lack of 
integration of circularity in vehicle design and production leading to high dependencies for primary 
raw materials was identified as a problem area to be tackled by the EU. In the preceding consultation 
process, environmental organisations, including the EEB, had also called for improvements in the 
design of vehicles at production stage. Industry representatives have repeatedly emphasised to 
PowerShift that the current bonding of parts, for example, makes it difficult if not impossible to 
recycle metals. 
 
We are therefore calling for greater inclusion of eco-design in the ELV Regulation. Design for X should 
be embedded as an objective and vehicle manufacturers should be required to produce their 
products in such a way that they are as repairable, reusable and recyclable as possible. Reduction of 
raw materials should also be included. In order to fulfil the EU's goal of reducing the demand for 
primary raw materials, reduction potentials must also be taken into account when designing vehicles. 
Where there are conflicting objectives, prioritisation should be based on social and ecological 
criteria. 
 
7. Increase the targets for reuse, recycling and recovery (Art. 34) 
 
According to Article 34 of the Commission’s proposal for the ELV Regulation, Member States shall 
ensure that the following targets are met by the waste management operators:

“(a) the reuse and recovery, as calculated together, shall be a minimum of 95 %, by average weight 
per vehicle, excluding batteries, and year; 
(b) the reuse and recycling, as calculated together, shall be a minimum of 85 %, by average weight 
per vehicle, excluding batteries, and year.” 
 
According to the Directive of 2000, the reuse and recovery of all ELV should be increased to at least 
95 percent of the average vehicle weight per year and the reuse and recycling to at least 85 percent 
by 1 January 2015 at the latest (Art. 7, Directive 2000/53/EC). Here, too, the new Regulation does not 
set any new increased targets, but instead relies on outdated requirements.  
 
However, in order to fulfil its stated objectives of improving access to resources for the EU economy 
and making the European vehicle industry more sustainable6, the EU Commission must also improve 
its proposal in this regard. 
 
8. Ensure an absolute reduction of metallic primary raw materials  
 
In order to ensure human rights and environmental protection in the mining and processing of 
metallic raw materials, the EU must guarantee the absolute reduction of metallic primary raw 
materials consumption. The ELV Regulation plays a crucial role here. It must set out significantly 
stronger measures for the recovery of raw materials and the reuse of components of ELV. If the EU is 
to succeed in reducing the demand for primary raw materials, it needs to incorporate measurable 
and ambitious targets promoting recycling while prioritising reuse into the ELV Regulation.

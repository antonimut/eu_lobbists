FIM’s feedback on the End-of-life vehicles proposal – December 2023 
 
Introduction 
The Fédération Internationale de Motocyclisme  (FIM), as the foremost global advocate and 
authoritative voice for the motorcycling community, plays a central role in championing the 
interests and safety of riders on a worldwide scale. We appreciate the opportunity to provide 
insights on the European C ommission's proposed revision of the End -of-Life Vehicle (ELV) 
Directive, a crucial piece of legislation that impacts not only the motorcycle industry but all 
road users. While we endorse the Commission's aim to improve environmental standards and 
road saf ety, we urge a careful assessment of the potential implications, particularly 
concerning the supply of spare parts for motorcycles and the economic factors involved. The 
FIM underscores the need for a balanced approach that ensures the continued availability of 
affordable spare parts while aligning with environmental and safety objectives. 
 
1. Impact on the market for spare parts 
 
Overview 
The proposal under consideration, relating to End -of-Life Vehicles (ELV), presents significant 
implications for the motorcycle industry. At present, most second-hand spare parts in the 
market are supplied through the dismantling of end -of-life motorcycles, primarily by 
specialised companies. This practice is essential in ensuring a sufficient supply of spare parts 
for well -maintained motorcycles. However, the current ELV proposal threatens this 
mechanism and raises concerns regarding the supply of affordable spare parts for 
motorcycles. This not only has implications for motorcycle riders, but for riders across many 
of our sporting events, which also provide a huge contribution to local economies : in excess 
of €2.1billion economic, cultural and social contribution of motorcycle sp ort and related 
activities to wider society. 
 
Challenges Posed by the Proposal 
The proposed changes to ELV regulations could lead to a loss of financial incentive for 
motorcycle owners to direct their vehicles to speciali sed dismantling facilities.  Article 24 
states that delivering an ELV to an Authorised Treatment Facility (ATF) is only free of charge 
if it does not “lack any of the essential vehicle parts or components.” Instead, to enable them 
to sell on spare parts in the large spare parts market for motorcycles, owners may opt for 
Authorised Treatment Facilities (ATFs) that don’t specialise in motorcycles. This could lead to 
the direct shredding of motorcycles rather than dismantling. The consequence of this shift is 
the disruption of the mechanism that directs motorcycles to companies speciali sing in their 
treatment, thus jeopardising the used spare parts market's supply. 
 
Not only this, Article 30 requires the mandatory removal of parts and components for reuse 
and recycling before shredding, while Article 31 obliges ATFs to assess the reusability of these 
parts, and, if reusable, defines these parts as non-waste. This effectively will lead the ATFs to 
trade the used parts, which will result in better availability of spare parts for M and N category 
vehicles. However, these Articles do not apply to motorcycles. Consequently, there is no 
incentive for ATFs to disassemble end-of-life motorcycles but shred them directly.

As mentioned, this not only has implications for individual riders, but also racers who compete 
in the FIM’s sporting events, whose motorcycles are specialised and are reliant on a strong 
used spare parts market. 
 
Recommended Approach 
Based on our analysis, either advocating an obligation for ATFs to dismantle motorcycles, as 
is required for cars, with the option to sell ELVs to specialised motorcycle ATFs, or preferably 
creating specialist ATFs specifically for motorcycles under the provision of the legislation 
appear to be the most balanced solutions. This approach ensures that motorcycles end up in 
specialised ATFs, promoting proper treatment while maintaining a sufficient supply of  
affordable spare parts. 
 
2. Addressing the Issue of Stolen Motorcycles 
 
The proposal tackles the issue of stolen motorcycles by increasing documentation 
requirements. The problem of crashed motorcycles being dismantled to obtain the registered 
frames and then fitted with spare parts taken from stolen motorcycles would most likely be 
addressed by the proposal. With the obligations on vehicle owners to check and declare that 
the vehicle they sell is not an ELV, to indicate any change of ownership of the vehi cle, to 
deliver all ELVs to ATFs, and to present the Certificate of Destruction to the authorities, this 
issue should be effectively tackled by the proposal. 
 
3. Impact on historic vehicles 
 
While it is important to note that historic vehicles are not affected by the Regulation, a 
possible consequence of economic irreparability could be that certain motorcycles would no 
longer achieve historic vehicle status: if they need repair just before tu rning into a historic 
vehicle, and if this repair is more expensive than the value of the vehicle at that time, the 
proposal seems to suggest that the vehicle would turn into an ELV due to economic 
irreparability. 
 
Conclusions 
In conclusion, the FIM would like to express its gratitude to the Commission for its efforts in 
valuing the activities of motorcycle enthusiasts. However, the ELV proposal, while well -
intentioned, poses challenges to the motorcycle industry's supply chain for affordable used 
spare parts. Careful consideration of the options, alongside a re-evaluation of background 
assumptions, is essential to ensure that the industry continues to operate effectively and 
responsibly. The recommended approach es in this paper strike a balanc e between 
motorcycle owner incentives, industry needs, and environmental responsibility.

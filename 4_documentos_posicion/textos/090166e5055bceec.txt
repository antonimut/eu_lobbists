CLEPA Key Messages to the End-of-Life Vehicles Regulation Proposal  
Input to the Have Your Say Consultation  
 
1. Remanufacturing and all authorised economic operators 
The important role of remanufacturing and all authorised economic operators should be 
acknowledged and be subject to the same rules e.g. in relation to handling removed parts, for 
evaluation and access to software to enable upgrades or software updates during 
remanufacturing.       
We appreciate the Commission’s intention to extend the scope of the ELVR proposal beyond its 
previous scope focused on waste, acknowledging the important role of lifetime maximisation 
strategies in automotive circularity, such as repair, remanufacturing and refurbishment. 
The current draft of the regulation infers waste management operators as the only ones to be included 
in the dialogue with vehicle manufacturers and that all the material operations will be done within 
waste management. The consequence would be that remanufacturers and other economic operators 
such as dismantlers, could be expected to obtain a waste management license and to follow waste 
management procedures instead of remanufacturing commercial activity.  
CLEPA sees the need to add clear mention to remanufacturing authorised economic operators in the 
text in relation to handling of removed or dismantled parts from a vehicle in its use phase of end of 
life, for evaluation and access to software to enable upg rades and software updates to a product 
during remanufacturing.  The concern is that the recovery of parts and components is done by other 
economic operators such as dismantlers who are not permitted as treatment facilities.

2. Waste vs non-waste status for used parts and components removed from 
vehicles 
 It is crucial to differentiate used parts and components from waste across the whole text. 
We are very supportive of the clear mention in the text (Article 31(1)) that “The parts and components 
that are fit for reuse, remanufacturing or refurbishment shall not be considered waste.”  It is crucial to 
differentiate      used parts and components used in the remanufacturing process from waste, across 
the whole text. For instance, the current definitions of end-of-life vehicle (art 3(2)) and post-consumer 
waste (Art 3(11)) could lead to the misunderstanding that any part, component, or material removed 
from a vehicle placed on the market is waste. These parts, components or materials would then require 
'treatment' by an 'authorised treatment facility'.  This is also a clear contradiction of the proposal’s 
intention stated in preamble 58: "It should therefore be clarified that parts and components removed 
from an end -of-life vehicle, which are suitable for reuse, remanufacturing or refurbishment, should 
not be considered waste." The regulatory text should clearly portray the remanufacturing cycle 
(reverse logistics) which handles used parts and components, not waste or scrap.      
Furthermore, the ELV centre or Repair and Maintenance operators do not necessarily have all the 
skillset and know -how to estimate if the parts or components could "fit for" Reman (e.g. testing 
equipment for electronic parts). This assessment could be done, in some case, only by the treatment 
centre (for Reuse, Remanufacturing, Repair or Refurbishment). Hence it is  very important that the 
parts or components in transit to a treatment centre are not considered as a waste      . The parts 
considered in Article 31 refers to Article 30 which could limit the recovery of the parts and components 
fit for reuse remanufacturing or refurbishment.  Our proposal is to only exclude the parts and

components which are not to be reused (as listed in Annex VII Part E). Nevertheless, we believe the list

3. Sector specific concepts and processes 
Sector specific concepts and processes that are well established in our industry should be specified 
in ELVR, taking precedence over those in horizontal files. 
The current definition of plastics (Article 3(9)) which follows the one used in REACH fails to include 
several important polymer types commonly used in vehicles. CLEPA acknowledges the importance of 
extending the scope of the plastics definition deeming it a viable and commendable approach in 
particular for the labelling requirements.   
The      proposed      definition of remanufacturing (Article 3(28)) follows the one proposed in the ESPR 
(COM/2022/142) which is a horizontal piece of legislation. It however does not accurately reflect the 
situation in the automotive sector for the following reasons: 
●      In this proposed definition there is no clear commitment that the remanufactured product 
will be at the same level of performance as the original one. 
 
4 
 
● The proposed definition suggests a link with safety which could lead to misunderstanding on 
the scope of remanufacturing. Remanufacturing applies equally to safety and non -safety 
products and allows restoring them back to the original level of performance. If the product at 
the origin is a safe product, after remanufacturing it will come back to this level of original 
safety.  
● The wording "Change" may give the impression that the remanufactured product will be 
modified leading to the misinterpretation that for instance re -homologation should be 
requested, which is often technically and economically unfeasible. This is a contradiction with 
the concept of restoring the product's performance to original ones. 
● The use of the word ‘type’ creates unnecessary confusion on whether it means identity or type 
approval. 
Similarly to the Batteries Regulation we call for a sector specific definition of remanufacturing for 
automotive products as proposed below  following the understanding agreed by several key 
automotive industry stakeholders (link).       
Regarding provisions for permanent magnets, deviations between the ELVR text and CRMA should be 
avoided but more importantly, we see it as crucial that ELVR takes precedence over CRMA (COM(2023) 
160) including e.g. labelling requirements, access to product information. 
We are also concerned with the duplication of regulations for some automotive relevant parts and 
materials, due to the first draft list of priority products proposed for the ESPR (notably textiles, ceramic 
products, lubricants, paints and varnishes, as well as intermediate products iron and steel, non-ferrous 
metals, aluminium, plastic and polymers and glass). The reason is that automotive products are 
addressed under the End-of-Life Vehicle Directive, now Regulation proposal. The ongoing revision of 
the ELVD should avoid any duplication with the ESPR actions.

4. Substances’ restrictions in ELVR 
We support the Commission intention to maintain the heavy metal restriction rules under ELVR 
scope (except for battery substances) and further applicable restrictions in REACH Regulation.  
The current proposal foresees (Article 5(2)) that in addition to the restrictions set out in Annex XVII to 
REACH Regulation, in the Batteries Regulation and in the POP Regulation, any vehicle type belonging 
to the category M1 and N1 shall not contain lead,  mercury, cadmium or hexavalent chromium. The 
current wording can lead to a misinterpretation that compliance with every other  substance 
restrictions is part of the type -approval process. This would be a clear contradiction to preamble 15 
and its statement that this regulation should only restrict lead, cadmium, mercury, and hexavalent

chromium. Following that logic, it would also imply (according to Article 29 Paragraph 3) the removal 
of all parts, components and materials containing substances referred to in Article 5 paragraph 2.  
The restrictions of battery substances are to be removed entirely from the scope of the proposed 
Regulation on circularity requirements for vehicle design and on management of end -of-life vehicles 
as  this issue is already addressed through Article 6 of the new Battery Regulation. The next scheduled 
lead battery exemption review scheduled for 2025 under the ELV Directive annex II.5b(i) (or future 
Regulation on circularity requirements for vehicle  design and on management of end-of-life vehicles 
annex III.5(b)(i)) should transition and be incorporated into the comprehensive report required under 
Article 6(5) of the Batteries Regulation (EU 2023/1542) being undertaken by the Commission and 
European Chemicals Age ncy (ECHA) on substances of concern present in batteries or used in their 
manufacture. 
 
According to the proposal, the Commission may request ECHA to assess the technical and socio -
economic feasibility of reviewing the exemption list of Annex III. The concern is whether ECHA can 
assess the possibility of substituting substances based on the performance required for automotive 
components. Technical feasibility is also to be evaluated according to the proposed requirements.  But 
it may be necessary to clarify definition of the technical feasibility.  
 
The issue of accumulated legacy substances must be addressed, in ELVR and any other automotive 
relevant substance restriction.  
As the industry advances in the intention to close the loop of resources , CLEPA acknowledges the 
challenge of legacy substances being that in relation to repaired/remanufactured products or recycled 
materials. After a product is first placed on the market, without knowledge about the presence of 
substance restricted since the placing on the market date  it is also impossible to remove those 
substances from it. For example, most vehicle components coming in for remanufacturing or recycling 
in 2030 will contai n PFAS, mainly fluoropolymers. A lot of PFAS occurrences will be unknown since 
there was no obligation to report these substances at the time of manufacturing (up to 20 years 
before). Nevertheless, for the current REACH Regulation (EC) 1907/2006, as well a s POPs legal text, 
both the first and all subsequent supplies of a substance, mixture or article are considered to be placed 
on the market. This means any requirements as defined in the regular amended in Annex XIV (List of 
substances subject to authorisation) and Annex XVII (List of restricted substances) apply equally to all 
products placed on the market.

5. Plastic recycled content requirements  
 Technological neutral recycled content targets are important to improve automotive circularity, if 
they are achievable and aligned with the EU decarbonisation goals. 
For the moment, it is very difficult for the automotive supply chain to verify if the proposed mandatory 
recycled content targets in Article 6 are achievable, given the (lack of) of recycled plastic content with 
the necessary quality. It is well known that mechanical recycling cannot reprocess all types of plastics, 
nor consistently provide the quality that is needed for safety and performance. Furthermore, we fear 
aggravated competition for secondary material from increased closed loop targets in different sectors.  
CLEPA is concerned about the restrictive approach to the feedstock and how it links with the currently 
unclear definition of plastics in Art 3(9).  
● The focus on post -consumer plastic would prevent many other waste types that can be 
used as feedstock for new plastics via mass balance accounting and chemical recycling (e.g. 
agriculture waste, waste oils (including from ELVs) or tyres). This concern is e qually 
applicable for the closed -loop requirement for recycled content as different ELV wastes 
can be recycled to make plastics. 
● The closed-loop target also fails to consider that parts and components removed from 
vehicles during their lifetime will eventually need recycling too. In the current drafting, 
only materials recovered from ELVs count towards the closed-loop target. 
● Instead, plastic types for which recycling is currently technologically and economically 
unfeasible should not be considered in the context of Article 6 regardless of the plastics 
definition of Article 3(9). CLEPA therefore supports the inclusion of thermoplastics and 
polyurethane foams in the scope of plastic recycled content as suggested in the JRC report. 
This targeted adjustment not only aligns with practical recycling considerations but also 
reinforces ou r commitment to advancing sustainable practices within the automotive 
industry. 
● Furthermore, we would highlight that the current phrasing - “[…] material from end-of-life 
vehicles in the vehicle type concerned […]” - could lead to the interpretation that only 
recycled material from the exact vehicle type could be used to fulfil the requirement (e.g. 
recycled material from motorcycles could not be used in a car and vice versa). This would 
further restrict the range of feedstock available for the fulfilment of recycled content 
target. The concern is that this may come along with establishing separate recycling lines 
for every single vehicle type, which would make recycling (and respective documentation) 
even more complex and complicated. 
In consideration of all these aspects, we propose a revision of the availability of recycled materials 6 
months after the date of entry into force of implementing act laid down in Article 6 paragraph 2 to 
ensure production of newly type -approved vehicles. The revision shall decide upon a potential delay 
by 5 years of the targets in case supply can not be guaranteed. The revision shall assess the need to 
adjust the scope of plastic types to be considered in accordance with advancements of recycling

technology. Where appropriate, the European Commission shall consider existing certification 
schemes for recycled content in plastics when doing so. 
6. Information requirements 
We need a streamlined approach to information requirements, with clear reporting thresholds as a 
minimum, and calculation/reporting methodologies when needed. 
We appreciate the importance of efficient information sharing in the automotive supply chain as a key 
need to increase circularity. The automotive industry has many best practices in implementing data 
sharing mechanisms that are functioning across the supply chain being IMDS one such tool, for 
declaration of material related information and soon product carbon footprint data.        The text      
proposes a declaration on recycled content (Article 10). It however fails to mention any calculation 
methodologies for the recycled content proposed and no provision for adopting implementing acts on 
it (Article 10(1). As a result, we fear information on recycled contents may be requested by OEMs with 
their own different methodologies. At the very least a minimum threshold should be defined for the 
declaration requirements.      
Related to the proposed minimum information requirements for the labelling of the parts and 
components for reuse, remanufacturing and refurbishment (Annex VII Part D 2): 
● In general, for mechanical parts, the VIN number is not mandatory for Reuse, Remanufacturing 
or refurbishment and after for refitment on the cars. Nevertheless, for the electronics parts 
with embedded software, this information could be relevant to know th e right version of 
hardware and software embedded in some products or components. CLEPA suggests making 
the VIN only mandatory for Electronics parts with embedded software. 
● Individually identifying all dismantled parts through labelling is not necessary and in line with 
the industrial processes in place. Certain parts such as starters, alternators, clutches, etc… are 
collected in bulk and subsequently sorted during the Remanufacturing pro cess. CLEPA 
suggests that the information requested in Annex VII Part D (2c) may be indicated on the part 
label or on the packaging for parts sold in bulk. 
Finally on the labelling requirements for e -drive motors containing permanent magnet materials 
(Article 12 (2) and Annex VI Part point 4) CLEPA has some reservations on such an obligation for the 
purpose of circularity. Removability of permanent magnets is an energy inte nsive process with a 
negative impact on the life -cycle of those materials particularly considering the current design 
practices and the low weight (they are potted/glued and normally weight about 15 grams). At least a 
transitional period is needed before implementation of such obligation.

7. Removal requirements 
Mandatory removal is not the most efficient strategy to increase re-use/remanufacturing, improve 
recycling or reduce the environmental footprint for several parts listed in Annex VII Part C. 
A design that does not hinder removal is required (Article 7(1)). However, there is no provision for 
specific criteria in ELVR proposal and no provision for the development of implementing act. During 
vehicle certification, it may be expected that certification authorities used by OEMs provide different 
criteria to determining compliance with this requirement. As a result, there is a concern that the 
standard of judgment differs from each OEM, which may hinder the standardization of design. 
Concerning the list of parts which must be removal from ELVs, it is unclear what is meant with “directly 
accessible” (Annex VII Part C(11). For instance, it is not clear if speakers in headrest or door panels are 
to be considered accessible, and whether seats should be removed to access to other parts in car. 
The proposal foresees dedicated removal requirements for batteries (Article 29 (4)) without specifying 
the relevant battery categories.

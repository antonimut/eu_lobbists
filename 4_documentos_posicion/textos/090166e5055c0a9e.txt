End-of-life vehicles 
JLR feedback to the revision of EU rules 
 
Introduction - JLR 
Jaguar Land Rover Automotive PLC (JLR) is a British company with global capabilities. JLR has 
manufacturing facilities in the UK, China, Brazil and Slovakia, a contract manufacturing site in 
Austria and a vehicle assembly site in India. 
JLR welcomes the proposal from the Commission to significantly revisit the end-of-life vehicle 
rules within the EU as the Commission’s ambition aligns with the JLR Reimagine strategy to 
become net-zero carbon by 2039. 
Please note that some elements from this position paper  might differ  from the industry ’s 
position(s). While JLR’s own targets in some cases go beyond the specific targets proposed by 
trade associations , JLR  nevertheless supports industry-wide targets  as they reflect  the 
consensus among other industry stakeholders (which is essential for bringing about industry-
wide change). 
As a preliminary comment, it is important that the Commission keeps in mind the different 
loops underpinned by the Circular Economy to eventually deliver more circularity within the 
automotive industry. Reuse, remanufacture  and refurbish are valuable approaches saving 
resources and products and should be valued even more than recycling , as per the waste 
hierarchy established in the EU Directive 2008/98/EC. The current draft Regulation seems to 
put more emphasis on recycling w hile vehicle longevity should play a key role in enhancing 
circularity within the EU. Targeting longevity can be achieved through  durability, reuse, 
remanufacturing and refurbishment among others. 
In addition, JLR would propose that only new vehicles going through type-approval should be 
subject to this Regulation as opposed to vehicles being re-certified through type-approval (e.g. 
type-approval extensions, mid-cycle fresh). 
General provisions 
Article 4 – global RRR rules for type-approval 
The Commission should ensure alignment between the revised EU End -of-Life Vehicle 
Regulation and globally agreed and implemented accounting methods like the UN -ECE GTR 
133 and the ISO  22628. Any misalignment would put an unnecessary burden on JLR as a 
global manufacturer. In addition, consensus  should be sought by the Commission with its 
global partners to implement a single calculation and declaration method globally. 
Article 5 – Requirements for substances in vehicles 
Requirements on substances of concern (SOCs) put a huge additional burden on collecting 
information for SOCs in articles, as well as putting unbalanced requirements on the 
automotive industry to eliminate these SOCs.  While JLR acknowledges the importance of 
regulating the SOCs including the substances that inhibit recycling, it is impractical to manage

Article 6 – Recycled content 
JLR proactively set an internal  recycled polymer target  several years ago. However, 
JLR has historically struggled to achieve this target due to lack of quality and volume. 
Many of the polymers chosen for their engineering properties are not currently 
available in recycled form, and not economically recoverable from end-of-life vehicles. 
Experience has also shown us that implementing very high post -consumer recycled 
polymer content targets can come at the expense of overall material consumption 
and vehicle mass as more (virgin) material can be added to the recycled material to 
compensate its reduced performance . Such an approach has  a knock -on effect on 
other environmental impacts such as an increase in whole vehicle lifecycle CO2e 
impact. Allowing the use of post-industrial (pre-consumer) recycled polymer to reach 
the target could therefore be beneficial as it may enable a greater avoidance of virgin 
material in the blend. 
Based on these elements, JLR would recommend keeping the proposed target for 
recycled polymers at 25%, as per the JRC definition (thermoplastics and PUR foams) 
IF post-industrial recycled polymers AND chemically recycled post -consumer 
polymer content can count towards this target.  One of the conditions to  fulfil this 
ambition is to  recognise c hemical recycling as a suitable recycling technology 
provided that the output material effectively delivers equal or lower carbon emissions 
per kilogram of material produced over the whole product life cycle compared to its 
virgin equivalent  (or the equivalent mechanically recycled polymer grade when 
available). For what concerns the 25% closed-loop sub-target, JLR would recommend 
mandating a declaration rather than a strong target. It is our belief that the increasing 
use of post-consumer recycled polymer material will inevitably drive the sourcing of 
closed-loop recycled polymer as it will be in JLR’s own interest to keep hold of its own 
materials. In addition, it is likely that the additional traceability accounting related to 
this sub-target will add further cost to the already -constrained volumes of available 
post-consumer recycled polymer material. 
In order to provide business certainty, t he Commission should clarify as soon as possible its 
ambitions, the methodology proposed to declare recycled polymer content , which vehicle 
types the target affects and how it intends the manufacturers to certify it. JLR would propose 
to keep  and further develop  the IMDS (International Material Data System) as the main 
reference to track the recycled polymer content and its origin for each vehicle type. 
It is important to understand that  materials can be defined through purchasing agreements 
as early as 5 years before the vehicle is placed on the market. This locks in the material for at 
least another 7 years in vehicle production, no t even taking into account the actual vehicle 
use time.

Article 7 – design to enable removal and replacement of certain parts and components in 
vehicles 
Any target should be based on a detailed impact assessment taking into account the specifics 
of each part and the actual environmental and circularity benefits delivered if the target can 
be reached for each of them . This is especially important for large assemblies for which the 
dismantling benefits are not clear (e.g. the dashboard). The Commission should  therefore 
clarify: 
- On which scientific basis the list of components to be removed in Annex 7 – Part C 
was established; 
- The methodology proposed to: 
o Declare and report removability; 
o Assess whether a part is as recoverable using post-shredding technology or 
dismantling and by whom (ATFs, OEMs, other?); 
- Which vehicle types the target affects; 
- How it intends the manufacturers to certify the achievement of the target; 
- How this article aligns with the EU Critical Raw Material Act 2023/0079; 
- How the different parts and assemblies are defined  in order to clarify system 
boundaries. 
JLR suggests defining minimum targets for the removal of key components in such a way that 
actual dismantling can be certified  and used as an internal standard during the product 
development phase. As a matter of information, any component can be dismantled, provided 
that there is a market for it to be sold and driven by sufficient demand. However, JLR would 
suggest avoiding regulating the dismantling of complex assemblies such as a dashboard. In 
this example, the dashboard assembly performance would suffer  from Article 7 with e.g. 
squeak and rattle noises during vehicle lifespan thereby resulting in significant quality issues 
which are not aligned with our customer expec tations. Alternatively, rather than prescribing 
specific parts or assemblies for removal, the Commission could consider establishing a target 
percentage of polymer that is easily removed from the vehicle (as per current RRR calculation 
but with a minimum target). These parts could then be reserved for re-use or the material sent 
to dedicated recycling, thereby improving the quality and quantity of recovery post-consumer 
polymer.  
JLR also proposes to keep IDIS (International Dismantling Information System) as the main 
platform to provide downstream stakeholders such as ATFs with the relevant information.

Obligations of manufacturers 
Article 9 – Circularity strategy 
Article 9 refers to Annex IV – Part A which itself refers to Annex 7 – Part C as being “measures 
to facilitate removal” whereas Article 7 refers to “measures that do not hinder removal”. The 
Commission should ensure that the wording remains consistent throughout the document  
and provide a definition for what the settled wording effectively means. 
In Annex IV – Part A, the Commission requires “measures to monitor how parts, components 
and materials contained in vehicles are reused, recycled and recor ded”. In reality, it will be 
impossible to provide evidence of every vehicle being treated in line with the way it was 
originally designed for end-of-life and circularity as there is no existing way to track vehicles 
until they reach the ELV status.  In addition, vehicles can end up anywhere within the EU or 
outside the EU and will be treated and recycled with the available technologies in the 
particular local facilities where they end up. This is something JLR has no control over. 
In terms of timeline, it is expected that the Circularity Strategy will be reported 36 months 
after entry into force while many elements of it will only require reporting much later . This 
needs to be taken into account for the enforcement of Article 9. 
Article 10 – Declaration on recycled content present in vehicles 
The Commission should clarify if recycled content contained in elements regulated by the EU 
Battery Regulation 2023/1542 should be reported in line with Article 10  and if a specific 
weight threshold will be applied for the reporting of the materials referred to in this article . 
Definitions and methods of calculating should also be consistent and made available ahead of 
the start of the development of the affected vehicle types. This will ensure that key materials 
and components can be targeted throughout the vehicle development cycle . As a 
consequence, reporting will further improve if it is submitted via IMDS consistently. 
Article 11 - Information on removal and replacement of parts, components and materials 
present in vehicles 
JLR proposes to keep IDIS (International Dismantling Information System) as the main 
platform to provide downstream stakeholders such as ATFs with the relevant information. This 
requirement will likely require a membership fee increase on JLR and other OEMs to produce 
a more comprehensive data source. The Commission should take these costs into account 
when discussing the elements of the Regulation with the Parliament and Member States. 
Article 13 - Circularity Vehicle Passport 
The Commission should clarify as soon as possible which solutions (commercial or not) will be 
certified for reporting within its Implementing Act in order to give business certainty.

Management of end-of-life vehicles 
Article 22 – Cost allocation mechanism for vehicles becoming ELV in another Member State 
JLR would like to emphasise that this measure will not prevent vehicles from being exported 
outside of the EU and eventually dismantled after their usage outside of the EU boundaries. It 
will therefore make this requirement difficult to implement and to enforce in practice. 
Article 27 – Obligations for ATFs 
It will be difficult for JLR to provide information to ATFs about PCBs larger than 10 cm². First, 
ATFs have little knowledge about these PCBs and their location in the vehicle. Second, even if 
accurate information is provided by JLR, authorities will need to enforce this requirement by 
policing ATFs to ensure the removal of PCBs actually happens. 
Significant design changes will be required to deliver on this requirement . JLR therefore 
suggests that the Commission establishes a detailed impact assessment taking into account 
the specifics of the sector and the actual environmental and circularity benefits delivered if 
the target can be reached. 
Article 30 – Mandatory removal of parts and components for reuse and recycling prior to 
shredding 
Some parts listed in Annex VII – Part C will need to be removed prior to depollution as it will 
guarantee the parts’ integrity and value for reuse, refurbishment or remanufacture  but this 
requirement suggests that some of the targeted parts will need to be removed post -
depollution. To  better reflect the circularity ambition of this requirement, JLR therefore 
recommends that the Commission reviews its wording. 
Article 31 – Requirements concerning the removed parts and components. 
In Annex VII – Part E, it is stated that Electric Control Units cannot be reused. In light of Article 
30, it is unclear whether parts of the components which are forbidden from being reused can 
effectively be reused either as part or subcomponents. In addition, ECUs, as a hardware, have 
a significant value and could be reused and refurbished which could deliver significant 
circularity benefits. 
For aftertreatment catalyst s, JLR would recommend allowing the usage of second -hand 
catalysts on a same type approved vehicle as it could deliver significant circularity impact on 
a complex part which was designed for entire vehicle lifetime. 
Article 33 – Reuse, remanufacturing and refurbishment of parts and components 
It is unclear whether point (a) refers to all components or if one component type can fulfil this 
requirement.  
Article 34 – Reuse, recycling and recovery targets 
A similar wording to Article 4 should be used when referring to the vehicle as the average 
weight per vehicle (Article 34) is not the same as the reference vehicle type mass.

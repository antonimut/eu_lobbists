On 13 July 2023, the European Commission presented its proposal for a new End-of-Life Vehicles Regulation (ELV-Regulation), which will replace the current End-of-Life Vehicles Directive (Directive 2000/53/EC) and Directive 2005/64/EC on the type-approval of motor vehicles with regard to their reusability, recyclability and recoverability, and which is intended to comprehensively change the current system of end-of-life vehicle treatment.   The BDE welcomes the EU Commission's proposal for a new Regulation on end-of-life vehicles and its explicit aim of improving the recycling of end-of-life vehicles and strengthening the EU internal market. Facilitating the recycling of end-of-life vehicles and the associated recovery of the critical raw materials those vehicles contain is urgently needed, not least with regard to the goal of the EU’s long-term independence from imports of critical raw materials from third countries, as laid down in the proposal for a Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA). It is to be supported that the European Commission presents a proposal for a Regulation, as this implies harmonised rules for promoting the recycling of end-of-life vehicles throughout the EU.  The BDE Federation of the German Waste, Water and Circular Economy Management Industry was founded in 1961 and is the Industrial and Employers' Association of the circular and resource economy. The member companies of the BDE represent 75 percent of the turnover generated by the private sector in the fields of “wastewater disposal”, “collection, treatment, disposal and recycling of waste”, as well as “elimination of environmental pollution and other waste management” in Germany. The approximately 750 members of the BDE represent the entire value chain of the circular economy and resource management sector. This ranges from the collection, sorting, and recycling of waste to the use of the raw materials and products obtained from the recycling process.  With regard to the Commission proposal, we comment as follows:  I. Scope of application of the ELV-Regulation and definition of end-of-life vehicles  First, it is to be welcomed that the scope of the ELV-Regulation is to be gradually extended to new types of vehicles such as motorbikes, lorries, and buses in order to ensure a more comprehensive coverage as well as a level playing field. With regard to vehicle types not covered yet, there is a regulatory gap under the current system, which has led to legal uncertainty in this respect.   The BDE also welcomes that the definition of the term "end-of-life vehicle" has been revised, specifying precisely when an end-of-life vehicle is to be regarded as irreparable (Article 3(1), No. 2, in conjunction with Annex I, Part A, points 1 and 2). The distinction between technical and economic irreparability is important. An exhaustive list of criteria for the assessment of technical irreparability is imperatively needed, as only such a list provides the necessary legal certainty. Consequently, these criteria should not be unilaterally amended by the Commission by means of delegated acts (see point VIII).

II. Design for recycling of new vehicles and minimum recycled content  From the BDE's point of view, the requirements regarding the design for recycling of new vehicles (Art. 4 (1)) and the use of recyclates in the production of new vehicles (Art. 6 (1), subpara. 1 and (3), subpara. 1, as well as Art. 10), as well as the quotas for the recovery and the recycling of end-of-life vehicles (Art. 34 (1)) are particularly supported.   This concerns especially the requirement that every vehicle type should contain at least 25% recycled plastic. The mandatory use of secondary raw materials is urgently needed to create more incentives for the recycling of raw materials.   The planned closed-loop approach – 6.25% minimum recycled content from plastics that were previously used in vehicles – would initially create a market for recycled plastics from end-of-life vehicles. Such a mandatory quota would also have a positive impact on the design for recycling of new vehicles concerning their plastic content. However, it is questionable whether a closed material cycle could impair the free market and free competition for recyclates and thus the economic attractiveness of recycling. In any case, the concretely proposed quota –  with two decimal places – should be rejected.  It is to be welcomed that the recyclates to be used must be post-consumer recyclates and that production waste cannot be used as recyclates and counted towards the quotas. Otherwise, a sufficient contribution to environmental protection would not be guaranteed, as waste originating from production (post-industrial waste or pre-consumer waste) is significantly more pure regarding an overall assessment than waste generated by consumers (post-consumer waste).   In contrast to post-consumer waste, post-industrial waste refers to material streams originating directly from production and thus from brand-new and unmixed material that can, in most cases, be recycled without significant effort. Post-consumer waste, on the other hand, refers to material streams that originate directly from consumer use and are often mixed with other materials. As a result, the recycling of such waste is generally associated with significantly higher costs compared to the recycling of post-industrial waste, simply due to the quality of the material to be recycled. Overall – in addition to the aforementioned aspect of environmental protection – only a limitation of the minimum recycled content to post-consumer material can reasonably be maintained, as otherwise a quota for minimum recycled content could be achieved very easily by using post-industrial material. Only the mandatory use of post-consumer recyclates will create the necessary incentives for increased recycling.   Much will also depend on the method for calculating and reviewing the quotas regarding design for recycling (Article 4(1)), which is to be determined by the Commission in separate implementing acts (Article 4(3)). Due to the decisive importance of the calculation method, it would have been necessary for it to be part of the Regulation itself and not to be adopted by means of an implementing act (see also point VIII).

As far as the requirements for producers to design vehicles for recycling are concerned, specific parameters for assessing the recyclability of vehicles should also be included in the Regulation. Specifications for the use or avoidance of composite materials or alloys that are difficult to recycle could be considered. Such rules would also have to be part of the Regulation itself due to their importance for the design for recycling, and should not be adopted by means of delegated acts.  III. Requirements for substances in end-of-life vehicles  Article 5 on the requirements for substances in end-of-life vehicles is to be welcomed, especially its explicit aim of, as far as possible, minimising the presence of substances of concern in vehicles and their parts and components. However, the list of substances to be exempted in future should urgently be extended to include carbon fibres, insofar as these are used in vehicle parts in which abrasion occurs, such as in brake discs. Such brake discs, which are reinforced with carbon fibres, generate highly hazardous carcinogenic fine dust through abrasion during the operating phase.   IV. Delineation of the obligations of vehicle manufacturers and waste management operators The clear distinction between the general obligations of vehicle manufacturers (Chapter III, Art. 8) and those of authorised waste management operators (Chapter IV, Section 4, Art. 27) is also to be supported. Concerning vehicle manufacturers, however, these should also be obliged to publish the cumulative energy consumption and the specific CO2 footprint in addition to the declaration on the recycled content (Art. 10(1)), and to include this information in their circularity strategy (Art. 9). This would enable vehicle manufacturers going beyond the minimum requirements of the Regulation to make associated benefits to the environment and the climate transparent to customers.  Regarding the obligations of waste management operators and Section 4 of Chapter 4 as a whole, the ban on the landfilling of non-inert waste under Article 35 should be emphasised as particularly positive. This rule establishes a very much needed level playing field concerning the landfilling of non-inert waste, which is highly problematic from an environmental point of view.  V. Revision of the rules on extended producer responsibility and introduction of a circularity vehicle passport  Another important aspect of the proposed Regulation, which the BDE very much welcomes, is the extension of producer responsibility for vehicle manufacturers (Art. 16 ff.).   In future, all vehicle manufacturers will be subject to extended producer responsibility for vehicles that they place on the market for the first time in an EU Member State. Manufacturers will be able to choose whether to fulfil the resulting obligations individually or via an extended producer responsibility organisation recognised by the respective Member State. Producer responsibility includes specific obligations for the collection and treatment of end-of-life vehicles. In particular, the mandatory establishment of collection systems is envisaged. These collection systems must

cover the entire territory of a Member State and enable all end-of-life vehicles being collected. The manufacturers are responsible for bearing the resulting costs.  In addition, manufacturers will in future be subject to certain labelling and information obligations, e.g. for the safe removal of components and materials (Art. 11). This information must be accessible free of charge to waste management operators and repair and maintenance operators by means of a digital "circularity vehicle passport" (Art. 13).  The BDE particularly welcomes the adaptation of the obligation to bear costs with the recyclability of vehicles and the introduction of a digital "circularity vehicle passport". By aligning the obligation to bear costs with the recyclability of vehicles in accordance with Art. 21 (eco-modulation), it is ensured that the contribution to be paid by vehicle manufacturers to an organisation for extended producer responsibility is based, for example, on the recyclability of a vehicle or on the use of recycled materials. This effectively implements the polluter-pays principle in accordance with Art. 191(2) TFEU. This rule obliges those causing damage to the environment to ensure that this damage is remedied and to bear the resulting costs. At the same time, this provision creates financial incentives for design for recycling and for the use of secondary raw materials. Furthermore, the introduction of a digital "circularity vehicle passport" effectively contributes to the reduction of bureaucratic hurdles through digitalisation and to the improved monitoring of end-of-life vehicles.  However, the provision of Art. 18(4), according to which extended producer responsibility organisations should ensure that producers and waste management operators are adequately represented in their governing bodies, should be rejected. It is imperative that the rights and obligations of waste management operators are adequately taken into account by extended producer responsibility organisations. However, mandatory representation in the management bodies would be too great an encroachment on the rights of extended producer responsibility organisations. The specific design of their governance structures should be left to these organisations themselves. The wording of the provision is also not sufficiently clear. It remains unclear, what a "fair representation” is, which would lead to additional legal uncertainty.  VI. Certificate of destruction in electronic format  The foreseen rules on the management of end-of-life vehicles exclusively by authorised treatment facilities make a decisive contribution to strengthening the recycling of end-of-life vehicles and preserving valuable raw materials.   In this context, the provisions on the certificate of destruction in electronic format and the linking of the deregistration of an end-of-life vehicle with the submission of such a certificate of destruction (Article 25) are among the most important innovations that the BDE expressly supports. The lack of such a certificate of destruction is one of the key deficits in the current system. As a result of Article 25(1) of the Commission proposal, according to which only recognised treatment facilities can issue such a certificate of destruction, which is necessary for the deregistration of a vehicle, the illegal recycling of end-of-life vehicles and the crucial problem of losing precious raw materials can

be combated effectively. The recognition of a Member State's certificate of destruction in all other EU Member States is necessary to strengthen the EU internal market for end-of-life vehicles, and is therefore also to be welcomed.  VII. Export ban for vehicles that are not roadworthy  The BDE also supports a ban on the export to third countries of vehicles that are not classified as roadworthy (Art. 38). The necessary and clear distinction between used vehicles and end-of-life vehicles (Article 37), and the associated provision of Article 38(2), according to which only roadworthy used vehicles may be exported, prevents end-of-life vehicles from the EU from being illegally sold in third countries as roadworthy vehicles. This equally keeps valuable raw materials within the EU while simultaneously protecting the environment, road safety and human health.  In addition, an examination whether a vehicle is an end-of-life vehicle or a used vehicle should also be carried out for intra-European shipments of used vehicles. This is necessary in order to assess which legislative framework is applicable. The Waste Shipment Regulation (EC 1013/2006) applies to intra-EU and extra-EU shipments of end-of-life vehicles – which are considered hazardous waste. Moreover, such an examination also serves to protect road safety, as it can prevent the outflow of end-of-life vehicles to those EU Member States with the lowest roadworthiness certification requirements.   VIII. Excessive number of delegated acts  The BDE is fundamentally critical of the large number of provisions empowering the Commission to adopt delegated acts. These twelve provisions are listed in Article 50(2).   The delegation of power under Art. 290(1) TFEU represents an exception to the ordinary legislative procedure that is to be applied very restrictively. Under this provision, the power to adopt delegated acts to supplement or amend certain non-essential elements of a legislative act may only be delegated to the Commission in exceptional cases. A high number of delegated acts adopted by the Commission, as is to be made possible here, is highly problematic in terms of democratic legitimacy, as essential decisions are to be taken within the ordinary legislative procedure provided for this purpose, and not by means of delegated acts.  The large number of provisions in which the Commission is empowered to adopt delegated acts clearly shows that this delegation is of exceptional nature. One example is the specification of a minimum recycled content for materials other than plastics (Article 6(3) and (4)). One of the declared aims of the proposal is to promote the recycling of end-of-life vehicles. Against this background, the setting of quotas for minimum recycled content for other materials, such as steel or aluminium, in new vehicles, which is also to be delegated to the Commission, is of crucial importance, as it is precisely such provisions that promote the recycling of these materials. Consequently, these provisions are essential elements of a legislative act, for which a delegation of power to the Commission pursuant to Art. 290 (1) TFEU is excluded.

 Nevertheless, should delegation of power to the Commission occur in some cases, it is necessary to ensure that the economic operators concerned are involved in the elaboration of the related delegated acts. Therefore, Articles 50 and 51 should provide for the mandatory involvement of representatives of vehicle manufacturers and waste management companies in the elaboration of delegated acts.  IX. Mandatory data exchange between the competent authorities  From the BDE's point of view, the provisions on cooperation between the competent authorities at national level and between the Member States (Article 44) are again very welcome. In this context, the proposed provisions on enforcement cooperation at national and Member State level are also to be viewed very positively (Article 47). A functioning exchange of data between the competent authorities is one of the most important aspects that is currently lacking, but which is essential to prevent the illegal shipment of end-of-life vehicles outside the EU, as well as its improper management, which results in the diversion of many valuable raw materials from the cycle. Effective, simple, digital, and fast data exchange between all authorities involved – such as environmental or customs authorities –  is a basic prerequisite for achieving the core objective of the proposal, namely strengthening the circular economy for end-of-life vehicles.  X. Excessive reporting and documentation requirements and rules on environmental management systems  Contrastingly, the provisions on reporting to the Commission in accordance with Article 49 are excessive. It is important and necessary to submit reports to the European Commission to enable it to assess the Regulation’s implementation (Art. 49 (4)). However, the proposed provisions are clearly too far-reaching and would create a considerable bureaucratic burden for companies and authorities involved. In particular, the reporting obligations under Article 49(1)(g) regarding the total number and weight of all parts, components and materials removed from end-of-life vehicles are excessive. It is therefore imperative that the reporting obligations under Article 49 be limited, as otherwise not only the companies but also the Member State authorities, which have to collect the data and forward it to the Commission, would be overburdened.  The proposal also contains excessive bureaucratic hurdles with regard to other aspects. According to Article 29 in conjunction with Annex VII, Part B, No. 3(a), the exact time at which the pollutants were removed must also be stated in the context of the depollution of end-of-life vehicles, which constitutes an unnecessary documentation requirement. The date of the treatment must be sufficient; it makes no difference at what exact time it takes place.  Furthermore, the obligation under Annex IX to include information on the nationality of the holder or owner in the certificate of destruction should be deleted. This information does not contribute to achieving the objective of improving the overview of end-of-life vehicles on the market, but only creates unnecessary additional bureaucracy.

 The rule in Article 27 (5), according to which treatment facilities are "encouraged" to introduce EMAS as an environmental management system, should also be rejected. In the interest of free competition, the new legal framework should be open to all recognised and established environmental management systems and thus also cover ISO 14001, for example.  XI. Removal obligations under Art. 30(1) in conjunction with Annex VII, Part C  The removal obligations prior to shredding under Article 30(1) in conjunction with Annex VII, Part C, with the aim of enabling reuse or recycling and preventing explosion risks in shredder facilities, are to be generally welcomed. However, with regard to engines and gearboxes (Annex VII, Part C, No. 4 and 6), treatment facilities should be able to assess on a case-by-case basis whether shredding would not be more appropriate than prior removal. Therefore, no mandatory dismantling should be provided for in this respect.  In contrast, however, gas tanks and fuel tanks must be included in the list of Annex VII, Part C. Inclusion in Annex VII, Part B, No. 2 is not sufficient, as the components listed there can remain in the vehicle if they can be adequately recycled, for example using post-shredder technologies. In this regard, the wording of Annex VII, Part B, No. 2 (a), does not provide for the mandatory removal of all potentially explosive parts and components, but only for their mandatory neutralisation. In the case of gas or fuel tanks, however, this does not solve the core problem of explosions in shredder facilities in the event of incomplete emptying. Such explosions in shredder facilities lead to very high damage and represent an acute danger. For these reasons, gas and fuel tanks must imperatively be included in the list of parts and components that must mandatorily be removed under Annex VII, Part C.  XII. Green public procurement  The proposal for a CRMA, which is closely linked to the proposal for an ELV-Regulation, provides in its Art. 25(1)(c) for the explicit obligation of Member States to promote the use of secondary raw materials in manufacturing, where appropriate by taking into account the recycled content in award criteria regarding public procurement. As one of the explicit objectives of the Commission's proposal for an ELV-Regulation is to stimulate the market for secondary raw materials (Recital 22), this Regulation should also contain a similar provision requiring Member States to consider the recycled content of vehicles, where relevant, when awarding public contracts.  The European Commission's Communication of 15 May 2023 on a revised monitoring framework for the circular economy (COM(2023) 306 final) stated that the EU spends around 14% of its GDP (around EUR 2 trillion per year) on the purchase of services and goods through public procurement.1 Not least in view of these figures, green public procurement must be strengthened across the EU.   1 European Commission: Communication on a revised monitoring framework for the circular economy, COM(2023) 306 final, p. 6.

XIII. Provisions on the enforcement of the Regulation  With regard to the provisions on the enforcement of the Regulation (Art. 46 et seq.), further tightening appears necessary, as not all obligations, in particular those of manufacturers – such as the provisions relating to documentation and information (Art. 11 et seq.) – are covered by the provisions on sanctions. However, specific rules for sanctioning violations of the aforementioned provisions are strictly necessary to ensure compliance, and to ensure that a circular economy for end-of-life vehicles is created.  XIV. Summary  All in all, the BDE welcomes the European Commission's proposal for an ELV-Regulation. A comprehensive new legal framework at EU level was urgently needed and overdue taking into account the shortcomings in the current system, for example with regard to data exchange and the certificate of destruction in electronic format. In this respect, the proposed Regulation contains several very positive new provisions. Nevertheless, there is a need for adjustment in particular regarding excessive bureaucratic hurdles – for example considering reporting under Article 49 – as well as regarding the extremely high number of delegations of power to the European Commission. In this respect, changes are needed.

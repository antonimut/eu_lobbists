POSITION OF THE POLISH CHAMBER OF INSURANCE ON PROPOSAL FOR A REGULATION OF THE 
EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL ON CIRCULARITY REQUIREMENTS FOR VEHICLE 
DESIGN AND ON MANAGEMENT OF END-OF-LIFE VEHICLES, AMENDING REGULATIONS (EU) 
2018/858 AND 2019/1020 AND REPEALING DIRECTIVES 2000/53/EC AND 2005/64/EC 
ABOUT POLISH CHAMBER OF INSURANCE 
The Polish Chamber of Insurance (PIU) is a statutory organization of industry self-government. PIU was 
established in 1990 and represents all insurance companies operating in Poland.  PIU promotes good 
market practices and supports insurers initiatives. PIU increases public awareness about insurance. PIU 
supports both national and EU legislators in shaping insurance law and conducts a multilateral dialogue 
for the development of the insurance sector. 
GENERAL COMMENTS  
PIU supports proposed solutions aimed at systematizing requirements related to  circular system of 
vehicle design stage and managing wrecks. We certainly endorse the goal of the proposal, which is to 
improve the quality of vehicles in the secondary market, encourage re cycling, and achieve the most 
effective management of wrecks. Regardless of the environmental benefits, the proposal will have 
significant economic consequences for Polish insurance companies and their clients, especially 
considering the specific characteristics of the Polish market (an aging fleet of vehicles and currently 
applicable procedures for claims handling of total damage and managing wrecks after total damage, 
as well as customers' habits of repairing vehicles after total damage). After reviewing the content of 
the proposal, we pay special attention to the following issues indicated below. 
LACK OF THE CLARITY OF THE PROPOSAL 
Lack of clarity in the pr oposal may undermine its goals leading to higher claims handling costs for 
insurance undertakings and clients. This could increase MTPL premium and affect the most vulnerable 
clients. Lack of clarity in the proposal could also result in disputes with the injured parties. They, in 
contesting the classification of the damage, will not only be fighting for the amount of compensation 
but also for the possibility of repairing and continuing to use a vehicle. Many of them, even today, 
despite the classification of the damage as total, repair their vehicle because of various reasons which 
may not be possible in future. As a result the number of complaints and legal disputes on this matter 
is likely to i ncrease, generating additional costs for the  insurance market and burdening  national 
judicial system s. Undoubtedly, the proposal will also bring changes to existing claims handling 
procedures, requiring the renegotiation of contracts and an increase of prices for existing services.  
Another aspect that raises PIU doubts is the lack of clarification in terms of authorities or individuals 
authorized to classify a vehicle as ELVs. Only individuals with recognized knowledge are able to assess 
the criteria for determining ELVs.

Moreover, t he proposal seems to overlook the situation of already being registered vehicles. It 
mandates a circular system for new vehicles but not address management of wrecks meeting 
environmental and technical standards of vehicles which are already in use. There are also no 
provisions obliging manufacturers to use wrecks of already being registered vehicles  on the market 
that meet environmental and technical requirements. Moreover, the proposal does not include a clear 
definition of "new vehicles". 
DEFINITION OF ELVs 
According to Article 3, paragraph 1, point 2 of the regulation, a "ELV" means a vehicle which is waste 
as defined in Article 3, point (1), of Directive 2008/98/EC, or vehicles that are irreparable according to 
criteria Part A, points 1 and 2 of Annex. Thus, in accordance with the content of Article 3, paragraph 1 
of Directive 2008/98/EC, waste is defined any substance or object which the holder discards or intends 
or is required to discard. 
The proposed definition of a ELVs in the project is very broad , leaving a significant margin for 
interpretation. This may lead to divergent interpretations of this legal standard, allowing for 
considerable flexibility in classifying a particular damage. This, in turn, is likely to result in an increase 
in disputes over the classification of total damage. It may also lead to the assessment of a repair as 
uneconomical being driven more by the claims of the injured parties, repair workshops, and their 
representatives, rather than by technological and technical justifications. Such a definition of ELVs will 
significantly increase the number of total damages and the necessity of paying compensation equal to 
the market value of the vehicle. This in turn will allow virtually any damaged vehicle to be classified as 
ELVs. It should be noted that this will also affect possibilities for disposing of wrecks.  
PERSPECTIVE OF INSURANCE UNDERTAKINGS NEEDS TO BE REFLECTED - CONSIDERING RECOVERED 
(USED/NOT NEW) SPARE PARTS IN CALCULATING THE COSTS OF VEHICLE REPAIRS BY THE 
INSURANCE COMPANIES 
It is necessary to introduce a provision allowing insurance companies to incorporate recovered 
(used/not new) spare parts into the calculation of vehicle repair costs. Without the mentioned 
provisions, individuals affected in accidents will have no motivation to choose recycled spare parts. 
Consequently, the market for such parts will be severely restricted. 
Referring to the mandatory free collection systems for ELVs to manufactures, we point out that it may 
indeed limit the possibilities of wrecks managing procedures. Furthermore, after classifying vehicle 
damage as a total, it will exclude the possibility of managing the  wreck (damaged vehicle), ultimately 
resulting in the wrecks having no measurable value as befor e. In the context of such a regulated 
circulated procedure, it is the manufacturer who gains benefits at the expense of insurers, as they can 
reuse the ELVs received at no cost from the injured party (unjust enrichment). Consequently, insurers 
would bear the economic burden of the decrease in the value of wrecks in claims handling procedures, 
resulting from claims by the injured parties for the payment of the full pre-damage value of the vehicle. 
Today in the case of total damages, insurance undertakings most often pays compensation, calculating 
it as the difference between the value of the undamaged vehicle and the value of the wreck . In the

UNCLEAR REPARABILITY CRITERIA IN ANNEX I 
PIU observes that certain reparability criteria outlined in Annex I, Part A(1) of the proposal lack clarity 
or relevance: 
• Subpoint (a) - It has been cut into pieces or stripped 
From this definition, neither the scope of such damage (cutting into pieces or stripped), nor the 
quantity of potential pieces, is evident. Would even the "cutting" of a vehicle fragment (e.g., fender, 
roof section) in accordance with this definition necessitate treating such  a car as ELV? Similar 
uncertainties cover the concept of "stripped" – what degree of "stripped" would result in classifying 
the vehicle as a total damage? Does a ELV ( we also underline that this phrasing is also criticized by 
experts and replaced, for example, with phrase "disassembled") imply an irreversible state? In the case 
of theft-related damages, individual components that can be reinstalled may be stripped. 
The notion of "technical impossibility of repair," through which the degree of cutting or stripping 
qualifying a vehicle as ELV would be assessed, is also very vague. "Technical impossibility" is a relative 
concept (it does not refer to any objective or established criteria), and only in extreme situations can 
it be considered indisputable. In most situations, a technically cut or stripped vehicle can be repaired 
(disregarding the economic sense of such an operation, but the criterion does not refer to the economy 
of the operation). 
One way to mitigate the ambiguity of the provision (as a whole, also in relation to the sub-point below) 
could be to refer to the repair standards of the vehicle manufacturer (whether they allow the technical 
feasibility of a given reconstruction) in conjunction with the criterion of the economic viability of such 
an operation. However, this still does not completely eliminate the ambiguity of this definition and the 
potential for disputes. 
• Subpoint (b) – It has been welded up or closed by insulating foam 
It is not clear what does a vehicle welded/closed by insulating foam mean . To what extent does 
welding/foaming determine that the vehicle is considered waste? The provision does not specify this. 
And if so, does this apply to door or cargo doors? If yes, does each of them have to be "closed" in this 
way? Is spot welding the majority/one door/trunk/hole sufficient? And why should welding, as a 
principle, exclude vehicles from the possibility of repair? 
Again, referring to the overarching criterion of "technical impossibility of repair," what does 
welding/closure imply for the impossibility of repair? Technically, practically every welded component 
can be separated, and the foam can be cleaned. Therefore,  the technical side does not in any way 
specify this criterion. According to what criteria, then, should the technical impossibility of "unwelding" 
be evaluated? 
• Subpoint (c) - It has been completely burnt to the point where the engine compartment or 
passenger compartment is destroyed

The term "completely" is imprecise. It raises doubts only in cases where all equipment is burned, and 
the entire body melts, taking on the shape of a "metal stain"—but such cases are extremely rare. More 
often, it involves the burning of equipment or specific areas of the vehicle—how should such damage 
be classified? The mention of the destruction of the engine compartment or the interior also provides 
little clarification—does, for example, the burning of the vehic le's dashboard already constitute the 
destruction of its interior? Does the destruction of the interior have to affect all interior elements, or 
is it sufficient to damage only some of them? 
The phrases "destroyed," or "completely burned" seem to suggest irreversibility  and irreparability. 
However, there are no criteria according to which the "completeness" of burning a vehicle or 
destroying the interior/engine compartment would be assessed. From a technical point of view, in 
most cases, repair of vehicle is possible.  
• Subpoint (d) - It has been submerged in water to a level above the dashboard 
The definition is also not sufficiently clear. Does every submersion above this particular level, even if 
brief (a few seconds), always result in considering the vehicle as waste? Does water entering through 
the ventilation grilles of the dashboard already indicate subm ersion above the dashboard line? How 
can this be verified? Does splashing water onto the hood of a vehicle moving in water already qualify 
as immersion above the indicated threshold? What about specialized/off -road/amphibious vehicles 
designed for deep wading? 
• Subpoint (e) – One or several of the following components of the vehicle cannot be repaired or 
replaced  
It is challenging to imagine a vehicle fulfilling its functions without, for example, wheels. Therefore, the 
likely impossibility of replacing or repairing the mentioned components constitutes the actual 
withdrawal of the vehicle from operation. There are no criteria for assessing the repairability of the 
mentioned systems. Can the (presumed) absence of parts/components determine the impossibility of 
such repair? 
It is also not clear t o what should the lack of repairability refer —should the reference point be all 
technologies and repair facilities available on the market? For example, if an authorized service centre 
(ASC) of a certain brand claims the impossibility of repair (because it does not have such technology or 
the manufacturer has not developed it), but a workshop specializing in similar repairs (e.g. vehicle 
frames) is ready to perform an effective repair, does that mean the damage is reparable or not? 
• Subpoint (f) - Its structural and safety components have technical defects that are irreversible 
and render them non-replaceable, such as metal aging, multiple breaks in primers, or excessive 
perforating corrosion 
This criterion is filled with non -specific terms such as "aging”, “multiple” or “excessive." This leaves 
ample room for arbitrary interpretation and evaluation of the vehicle's condition, and therefore, its 
repair possibilities. The provision does not spec ify what structural components are, and there is no 
binding definition in this regard. Should every described flaw in these elements, by definition, exclude

the vehicle from further use? And if it does not impact the safety of the vehicle in any way but only its 
aesthetics, should such a vehicle also be considered waste? 
• Subpoint (g) - Its repair requires the replacement of the engine, gearbox, shell, or chassis 
assembly, resulting in the loss of the vehicle's original identity. 
This criterion is also unreasonable. If the manufacturer (taking into account safety and other aspects) 
allows for the replacement of the engine, gearbox, body, or chassis assembly, the loss of the original 
vehicle identification should not exclude such a repair, even if identification data are applied to these 
elements. Such data can be reconstructed, reapplied, or replaced. Additionally, even the replacement 
of the vehicle’s body, in vehicles based on a frame, may not affect their utility or safety properties in 
any way. If the aim of the regulation is to ensure the safety of vehicle use and trade, considering a 
vehicle with, for example, a necessary gearbox replacement (on w hich identification is present) as 
waste is definitely excessive and unjustified. E.g. in Poland, only  vehicles’ body identification is 
required, and the engine and gearbox can be replaced with others. Entries in the registration certificate 
with identification numbers for the engine and gearbox are not required. 
• Annex I – Part B 
Point (d) - Used vehicle is an end -of-life vehicle if it is not appropriately protected against damage 
during storage, transportation, loading and unloading: The criterion is too general and it is not clear 
whether it applies only to new vehicles before they are registered in a member state. 
• Annex I, Part A(2) – vehicles economically irreparable 
Proposal refers to an economic irreparability criterion that may not always be aligned with economic 
realities. The provision in Annex I, Part A(2) envisages the necessity to withdraw a vehicle from use 
when the repair cost exceeds its market value. Considering the average age of registered vehicles and 
the high costs of spare parts and bodywork/painting works, a significant portion of older vehicles with 
minor damage, allowing for continued use without any repair (e.g., repainting a few elements), would 
be withdrawn from use. Simultaneously, a relatively new vehicle with much more extensive damage 
may not qualify as ELVs according to the economic criterion due to its high market value. The ratio of 
comprehensive repair costs that qualify a vehicle for withdrawal from use should be in the range of 
250-300% of its market value. 
Any vehicle deemed "not economically irreparable" can potentially be repaired, even if the repair costs 
exceed the vehicle's value. Owners may choose to repair vehicles for reasons such as historical or 
sentimental value, or simply to minimize costs using second -hand parts or personal connections. 
Imposing economical repairability criteria based on repair costs contradicts the owner's autonomy in 
deciding whether to repair the vehicle  or not. Vehicle owner should have the right to repair an 
economically irreparable vehicle also due to personal reasons. Imposing the requirement to return the 
vehicle to the manufacturer constitutes a limitation on the ability to dispose of one's property.

COMMENTS TO ADDITIONAL ANNEXES  
• Annex II 
Risk of process change for insurance companies regarding the valuation of the residual value of the 
vehicle: It is not clear whether insurance companies will be required to wait for the valuation of parts 
for recycling and recovery by dismantling stations and depend on the described indicators. 
• Annex VII – Part E 
Similarly to the arguments in Annex I – Part A, Insurance Europe is of the view that, in Annex VII – Part 
E, if the part is unharmed or reparable and deemed safe, it should not be automatically considered as 
waste. 
In addition, we have the following observations regarding specific points of Annex VII – Part E: 
• Point 5 - Seats in cases where they incorporate safety belt anchorages and/or airbags 
Seat airbags are replaceable components, and therefore, seats with airbags should not be excluded 
from reuse.  
• Point 6 - Steering lock assemblies acting on the steering column 
This is a replaceable component. Therefore, the criterion should be removed.  
• Point 7 - Immobilisers, including transponders and electronic control units 
We suggest removing the criterion as this is a replaceable component. These are not safety elements 
but anti-theft devices, so they are not critical. 
REPARABILITY CRITERIA AND BATTERIES 
PIU is of the opinion that it is reasonable to exclude batteries from the reparability criteria. The average 
lifespan of vehicles exceeds that of batteries. Additionally, batteries can be considered a "separate 
commodity" (an energy resource) and replaced independently in vehicles, with potential economic 
use, such as in leasing arrangements. 
However, to prevent abuses in legal transactions and insurance fraud, batteries (despite being a 
separate commodity in recycling turnover) should be subject to the following rules:  
• Each battery should have a unique identification number (similar to the vehicle's Vehicle 
Identification Number - VIN) and be assigned to a specific vehicle (parallel to the vehicle's VIN).  
• The correlation between VIN and battery number should be documented in vehicle records (which 
can be electronic), essentially designating the "assigned battery" to the vehicle.

• Changes (replacement) should be documented in vehicle records, and the information should be 
accessible similarly to the vehicle registration number and VIN. 
We also underline necessity of establishing standards for the repair of electric vehicle batteries at the 
component level to achieve the intended goals in the circular system and strive for environmental and

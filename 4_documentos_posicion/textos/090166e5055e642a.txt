ECOS, the Environmental Coalition on Standards, is an international NGO with a network of members and experts 
advocating for environmentally friendly technical standards, policies and laws. 
Our organisation has achieved consistent successes in shaping environmental-friendly standards and policies for 
the energy transition, a circular economy and combating climate change, among others. ECOS works towards a more 
inclusive, open and transparent standardisation system, internationally and at the European and national levels, 
ensuring that standards compliment and facilitate policy implementation. 
ECOS, the Environmental Coalition on Standards, is an international NGO with a network of members and experts 
advocating for environmentally friendly technical standards, policies and laws. 
Our organisation supports that the old End-of-Life Vehicles Directive, which has been in force since 2000 with only 
minor changes, is in urgent need of a fundamental update to meet today's requirements in terms of circular economy 
and net zero emission targets.   
ECOS supports and welcomes the European Commission's approach to present a regulation on end-of-life vehicles 
and the type -approval of vehicles regarding reuse, recyclability and recovery, and in particular : 
 
• the aim to expand the scope to other type approved vehicles   
• to strengthen the circularity in the design phase   
• to aim for high quality recycling  
• to align the Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) system with the provisions in the Waste Framework 
Directive and add sector specific clarifications  
While we welcome the EC proposal, the full potential is not always realised. 
Attached to this feedback you will find further aspects and often detailed proposals on how to address the 
mentioned aspects, which we can summarise with the following: 
  
1. The legal basis shall be twofold, i.e., Article 114 and 192 of the TFEU to enable a) more strict national 
waste management regulations and b) the introduction of ESPR aspects under ELV -R, necessary due to the 
exclusion of vehicles under ESPR.  
3 
 
2. The timing of detailed entry into force is not ambitious at all. We propose better to distinguish the 
application for new type approvals (24 months transition time should be sufficient) and by when it must apply to all 
vehicles PoM, type-approved before the entry into force of the new regulation.  
3. The law fails to address the need to downsize and to decrease the number vehicles placed on the market 
as well as the overall material use and footprint of the sector. As (most likely) excluded under ESPR, we urge the 
legislator to introduce provisions on the total environmental impact of the production and criteria to assess the 
durability and repairability, possibly as a standard, and to establish a minimum period for the availability of spare 
parts.   
4. Regards the aspect of “missing vehicles” the proposal of the EC refers to a revision of the Council Directive 
1999/37/EC on the registration documents for vehicles in a recital only. This is risky and can jeopardise the effort to 
address this important aspect. Instead, it is inevitable to address the registration procedures in the new regulation 
directly by an article, revising Council Directive 1999/37/EC directly, without further delay and without risky 
interventions of other stakeholders, not interested in circularity.  
5. While we support the scope extension to other vehicles, we do not see the necessity for exclusion of 
several Articles to apply for these new categories. We further do not see any reasoning why producers of multi -
stage type approved vehicles (e.g., like caravans and motorhomes, known as difficult to recycle) shall be completely 
excluded from the scope of the new ELV-R.  
6. Regards the minimum recycled content of plastics the proposal is not ambitious and needs clarification 
whether chemical or physical recycling are accounted for the target too. In fact, some of the plastic fractions from 
current ELVs are not a good feedstock for mechanical recycling of high quality.   
7. For the circularity strategy we propose to add the strategy how to increase the use of remanufactured parts 
for new vehicles / vehicle (EV -) platforms and to increase the scope of the circularity vehicle passport to several 
aspects.  
8. If producers fulfil the EPR by individual systems they, apparently, do not have to demonstrate the material 
balance, demonstrating the fulfilment of the reuse and recycling target. We assess this as a major loophole. 
These and further aspects are discussed in the following full statement. 
1. Principle aspects of high relevance 
1.1 The proposal has to address the need to decrease the number of vehicles on the market 
We understand that limiting the number of vehicles is not in the scope of the draft ELV -R. However, if the mobility 
sector is to achieve carbon neutrality, the total number of vehicles in use must be inevitably reduced. This is feasible 
as the current vehicle stock is typically in use for 1 to 2 hours a day . Solutions like car sharing can increase  use 
intensity with a less total of vehicles in combination with the improvement and increased use of public transport.   
4 
 
Therefore, we propose the introd uction of a  recital which addresses the need for the vehicle sector to become 
resource-efficient and focused on quality services  rather than increased sale s. T he Commission should deliver 
proposals on how this can be achieved.  
1.2 The legal basis must be extended 
 
EC Proposal: The legislative proposal for the ELV-R is based on Article 114 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the 
European Union (TFEU), which aims to ensure the functioning of the single market. 
 
Comment: 
 
a) Referring exclusively to Art . 114, does not allow stricter national regulations. However, M ember States 
might opt for stricter regulations – for example, on the management of shredder residues (ban of landfill & 
incineration) and/or the minimum requirements for primary treatment at ATFs or secondary treatment at 
shredders. 
 
b) According to the council position, vehicles shall be excluded from the ESPR with the argument that eco-
design aspects are covered by other EU legislation . However, many elements typically regulated under 
ESPR are not addressed in the draft proposal or elsewhere (see separate section below). To include  
environmental impacts from vehicle production  as well as  durability and repairability in the ELV-R is 
meaningful. 
 
Proposal by ECOS: For both reasons a) and b) the legislative proposal shall be based on both, Article 114 and Article 
192 of the TFEU 
1.3 The law fails to address the need to downsize vehicles and limit the resources used for their 
manufacturing. 
We understand that the limitation of the size of vehicles is not in the scope of the draft proposal. In the following 
section on eco-design, we discuss the need to address the aspect of resource consumption in more detail. 
1.4 Eco-design 
  
Council Position and EC Proposal: The Council position (22 May 2023) to the draft ESPR (30 March 2022) excludes 
motor vehicles from the groups of goods covered by this regulation, “since specific laws address the environmental 
impact of automobiles”. 
Comment: 
 
5 
 
Taking the above -mentioned exclusion of “motor vehicles” from the ESPR as a given, the major environmental 
impact of vehicles is not addressed in the draft ELV -R nor elsewhere : 
  
• Total environmental impacts from production (LCA or PCF for the production of vehicles) 
• Criteria for the durability 
• Criteria for repairability 
 
Proposal by ECOS: Amend chapter II on “Circular Requirements” to “Eco-design and Circular Requirements” and 
add articles assessing the following aspects: 
 
• It should be made mandatory for OEMS to provide for the type-approval an LCA and PCF for the production 
and end -of-life management of vehicles but  excluding the use phase. When calculating the carbon 
emissions / CF the option to consider certificates of origin for electric power consumption shall be excluded. 
 
• Establish the option to define maximum (carbon) emissions  per vehicle  production. 
 
• It should be made obligatory for  OEMS to publish the durability and repairability of vehicles / a detailed 
method of how to calculate and assess the durability and repairability forwarded to a delegated act. Such 
a delegated act should consider, among othe rs, the duration of typical repair work s (replacement of 
batteries, replacement of front and rear lights, replacement of mudguards  and other parts of the vehicle 
shell, clutch, brakes, shock absorbers, exhaust, exchange and calibration of sensors) 
 
• Establish a minimum duration for the provision of spare parts of 15 years after the end of production; The 
provision of used or remanufactured parts should fulfil the obligation as far as warranty obligation remains 
in force. The software should be considered as an integral part of the provision of spare parts and continued 
functionality and must be functional  and available as well with functional upgrades or fully compatible 
new softwar e. The p rovision of information and spare parts must be accessible to independent repair 
workshops.

2. Specific Aspects 
2.1 Scope extension 
 
The draft ELV -R proposes to extend the scope for almost all other road vehicles which need a type approval. 
However, many articles on circularity do not apply for the extended scope. We therefore propose either to delete 
Art 2(2) - Art 2(6), or at least introduce a new Art 2(7) with the obligation for the EC to assess the option to apply 
more articles of the ELV-R for the newly introduced vehicle categories. 
2.2 Scope Article 2(2a)  
 
Article 2(2a) excludes parts of a vehicle which have been type -approved under a multi -stage type -approval of 
categories N1, N2, N3, M2 or M3 other than the base vehicle. This also excludes caravans and motorhomes. In 2021, 
260,000 motorhomes and caravans were placed on the market, and it is known that these vehicles and trailers are 
very difficult to recycle due to the high proportion of composite plastics. We therefore propose not to exclude 
second-stage caravan and trailer manufacturers or, in case the impact cannot be assessed t oday, to introduce the 
obligation for the European Commission to assess by 2025 how to include caravan and trailer manufacturers in the 
scope. 
2.3 Article 4 (3) Reusability, recyclability and recoverability of vehicles  
 
As it is well known that ISO standard 22628:2002 is outdated and the methodology does not reflect the effective 
recycling and by far not the effective high-quality recycling, the timing for the replacement by an implementing act 
is not challenging by far too late. Instead, we propose to establish the new methodology  and the consideration of 
recycling technologies, in line with the policy options assessed in the impact assessment, referring to the technology 
readiness level (TRL). In addition, a Standardisation Request would be necessary to respond to standardisation gaps. 
8 
 
2.4 Article 6 (1): Minimum recycled content in vehicles for plastics  
 
Article 6 requires that each vehicle type contains at least 25% of plastic recycled from post-consumer plastic waste, 
and that 25% of such material (equivalent to a closed loop recycled content rate of 6.25% only) should come from 
recycled end-of-life vehicles. 
We are aware that today only a small proportion of the shredder light fraction can be used for high-quality recycling 
in a closed loop. And as vehicles are long -lasting products (the current typical lifespan is 18 years), the plastic mix 
will remain for at least the next decade. 
We further see the risk that the remaining 75% of non-closed loop recirculates will be collected from other sectors 
like packaging. In result the automotive sector might even seek to acquire packaging waste which would be able to 
be recycled well in the packaging sector even with food contact. 
However, we also see great potential for improvement if modern post-shredder treatment with advanced separation 
of the plastic fraction is introduced throughout the EU. But even then, there remains a (high-density) plastic fraction 
that is currently disposed of but could be a feedstock for chemical recycling (although chemical recycling also does 
not appreciate PVC with its corrosive chlorine). 
We therefore urge the EC to reconsider the proposed approach and aim for a more closed loop approach, timewise 
a bit more challenging, with the following elements: 
• Starting sooner (e.g. by 2026) with a 15% closed loop recycled content form post-consumer waste from 
ELVs. 50% of this target shall be allowed to be provided by chemical recycling  or dissolution (“Physical 
Recycling”), in minimum 50% shall be achieved by mechanical recycling.  
• Increasing the target until 2030 to 30% with a share of 50% / 50% for mechanical and chemical / 
dissolution recycling 
• Increasing the target until 2034 to 50% with a share of 50% / 50% for mechanical and chemical / 
dissolution recycling. 
2.5 Article 6 (3) and (4): Minimum recycled content in vehicles for other materials 
  
The Article 6(3) and (4) empowers the Commission to adopt delegated acts setting recycled content targets for 
steel, aluminium, magnesium and selected rare earth. However, the timing could be more challenging. We propose 
to adjust the due date for the steel study to 12 months after the date of entry into force of the ELV -R and the due 
date for the feasibility study for the other material to be 24 months after the date of entry into force. You will find a 
more general comment on the timing in section 3.  
2.6 Article 7(2): Design to enable removal and replacement of certain parts and components in vehicles  
 
9 
 
The EC ’s draft mentions electric vehicle batteries  and e-drive motors  to be designed that removal and non -
destructive replacement is enabled. We take not that this list might be extended by a delegated act.  
Instead of waiting for the first delegated act with this regard we recommend to add right no also starting, lighting, 
and ignition batteies and other batteries with a lifetime below the expected liefetime of the vehicle. 
2.7 Article 9: Circularity strategy 
 
The circularity strategy shall include the aspect of reuse and remanufacturing with higher priority. 
Therefore we propose that:  
The circularity strategy shall explicitly address how the producer intends to increase reuse (or use of 
remanufactured) parts or components:  
a) for repair and maintenace of used vehicles,  
and 
b) for the production of new vehicles  - this might even apply for an entire plattform s with drive trains and 
battery.  
The Commission should be empowered to establish, through a Delegated Act, targets for the share of reused or use 
of remanufactured parts or components for the production of new vehicles either by brand or by producers or by 
type. 
2.8 Article 13 Circularity Vehicle Passport 
 
Article 14 on the Vehicle Passport is designed to pursue the dismantling, reuse and recycling. The implementation 
date is extremely late with 7 years after the entry into force of the ELV -R. We assume that the 7 years are aligned 
with the typical sales -period of a vehicle type. While considering the sales period, we propose the following 
approach to facility implementation in due time.:  
“Producers are obliged to provide the circularity passport for each vehicle which got a type approval 24 month after 
the entry into force of the ELV -R. By the lates t, 84 month each vehicle placed on the market must be equipped 
without exemption.” 
Furthermore Article 13 it is not designed to address aspects of the supply chain as it is done for example for the 
Electric Vehicle Battery Passport. Therefore, we recommend expanding the scope for the vehicle passport for other 
aspects like:  
• Information performance / state of health / use-phase: fuel consumption, milage, confirmed services, entries 
/ history of the on-board diagnostics, link to the batter passport. 
10 
 
• Information on production conditions / effort: results of the LCA / PCF, results of the Corporate Due 
Diligence in Supply, and others.  
Not to delay the implementation of the ELV-R we propose to add two paragraph to Article 13:  
Paragraph (7) empowering the EC to adopt delegated acts in order to extend the scope of the circularity Vehicle 
Passport for the e.g. fuel consumption, milage, confirmed services, entries / history of the on-board diagnostics, link 
to the batter passport , results of the LCA / PCF, results of the Corporate Due Diligence in Supply  and adapt it to 
technical and scientific progress.  
Paragraph (8) to task the EC with the provision of a study by 2025 on what extensions of the scope of Article 13 
might be meaningful to harmonise and align information in one single vehicle passport.  
2.9 Article 16, Article 18 and Article 19: Extended producer responsibility 
 
Art. 16 stipulates inter alia, that producers are obliged to ensure that “(b) the waste management operators treating 
end-of-life vehicles referred to in point (a) meet the targets laid down in Article 34.  
Art 18 stipulates inter alia, that Producer Responsibility Organisations must report on the achievement of targets. 
In Art 18 it is not clarified that a producer fulfilling its EPR obligation individually, must report on the achievement 
of targets too. 
Art 19 even excludes the reporting on the achievement of the targets as it refers to Waste Framework Directive Art 
8a (3) points (a) to (d) but neglect point (e) which refers to the achievement of the targets.  
To our understanding, this is a major loophole designed for the producers fulfilling their EPR individually. 
Apparently, producers fulfilling their EPR individually are not obligated to report the achievement of the targets (Art 
18 and Art 19) but can simply refer to a contract with ATFs (Art 16) and forward the obligation to them as a kind of 
general statement. By this way no direct mass -flow statistics will be available, and no validation will be possible. 
Therefore, we propose to extend “Art 18 to Obligations for Producers” and extend in Art 18 Paragraph (3): In 
addition […] producer responsibility organisations [and producers fulfilling their obligations individually] shall publish 
[…] and plastics recycling [by the producers which entrusted the producer responsibility organisation] 
2.10 Art 20 Financial responsibility of producers 
 
The current draft ELV-R obliges producers to recover cost for the collection and treatment only if the total cost for 
all collection and treatment operations exceeds the total revenues generated from the ELVs. At the same time, it is 
obvious that some of the new dismantling requirements and the alignment of the definition of the term recycling 
with the Waste Framework Directive (and the further definition of the calculation point for recycling, respectively 
the standard loss rate) will make the waste management more challenging and several of the single operations are 
not recovered by revenues. We expect this to trigger illegal treatment (bypassing the not profitable obligation) 
11 
 
twofold: on the one hand completely illegal waste management will increase (not registered as ATFs) but also ATFs 
will seek in parts to bypass the extended waste management obligations. 
To our reading the Article needs redrafting  to clarify that the cost compensation by producers for the waste 
management applies for each specific waste management operation.  
2.11 Article 21: Fee modulation 
 
A fee modulation should not only consider the cost in waste management but also the total life cycle perspective.  
The fee modulation shall have measurable impacts on the design of vehicles. Therefore, aspects like results of LCA 
and PCF shall be considered for the fee modulations as well. 
We further recommend including durability, repairability of wearing parts and repairability after accident. The latter 
is becoming increasingly relevant in connection with (new) technologies for large aluminium castings for vehicle 
bodies, as this technology could jeopardise repairability after a (moderately serious) accident. For the defintions and 
methodology to measure durability and repairability delegated acts shall be enabled. 
2.12 Article 22 cost allocation mechanism for vehicles becoming end-of-life vehicles in another Member State 
 
We take note of the aim to have a fair shift of the EPR obligation to those countries which import a high share of 
used vehicles from other Member States.  
 
At the same time we miss an approach to continue the EPR even when a used vehicle is exported to a third country 
(meaning a non-EU country). We are aware that this is not easy to regulate and a counterpart is necessary in the 
countries receving the used vehciels from Europe. Therefore, we propose to explore the following aspects in a study 
commissioned by the EC:  
 
- Do the producers benefit if used vehcles are exported to third countries by the reason that their EPR exires? 
- What environmental harm is caused by the exported used vehicels in the receiving countries if no EPR is 
in place? 
- What effort / inventment would be needed to establish appropriate waste management of ELVs in 
receiving countries? 
- What means has the EU to support the receiving countries in establishing an appropriate waste 
management of ELVs in receiving countries? 
- What legal opportunities exist to get the producers involved in the EPR when placing vehicles first at the 
EU market, before they are exported as used vehcies? 
12 
 
2.13 Article 27 (3c) 
 
Unfortunately, it is not clear which components shall be dismantled prior to shredding for reuse and / or 
remanufacturing. Apparently only those components from the Annex VII, Part C shall be dismantled “ with a reuse, 
remanufacturing or refurbishment potential”. But it remains unclear how this potential is defined. We assess the 
reuse potential of a wire harness as low, but we assess the reuse potential of rear lights as high (but these are not 
mentioned in Annex VII Part C. To be clearer on what parts shall be assessed for their reuse potential we propose 
to establish separate lists for mandatory removal for reuse / remanufacturing (Part C1) and mandatory removal for 
better recycling (Part C2). We further urge the legislator to establish a procedure how the market for 
reuse/remanufacturing is to be assessed and we urge the legislator to enable amendments to Annex 7. 
2.14 Article 32: Trade of used, remanufactured or refurbished parts and components  
 
Article 32 obliges any person to provide (inter alia) information on the origin (VIN) of used components / parts. Today 
most used component parts is offered via internet trade platforms. As it is difficult or even impossible to authorities 
to identify non-compliant sellers, the platforms shall be obliged to ensure that all offers for spare parts, used or 
remanufactured, comply with the requirements of Article 32.  
2.15 Article 36 Shipments of end-of-life vehicles 
 
Comment: Article 36 needs adjustments to define the term “ in conditions that are broadly equivalent ”. As the 
compliance with broadly equivalent conditions is a prerequisite that these exports are accounted towards Article 34 
on Reuse, recycling and recovery targets, it is essential to have a solid definition. 
Proposal of ECOS: add paragraph (3) to Article 36 as follows: 
(3) The Commission is empowered to adopt delegated acts in accordance with Article 50 to supplement this 
Regulation by setting out the required evidence to provides documentary evidence approved by the 
competent authority of destination demonstrating that the treatment took place in conditions that are 
broadly equivalent to the requirements laid down in this Regulation and to human health and 
environmental protection requirements laid down in other Union legislation. 
3. Timing 
The timeframes for the End-of-Life Vehicles proposal are not ambitious at all. We understand that delays might be 
caused by considering the development and production cycles in vehicle construction.  
13 
 
However, given the urgent need for improvement in reducing the resource footprint we urge the legislator to 
reconsider certain timeframes. First, regarding the requirements for vehicles getting a new t ype approval. Here a 
time span of 24 months shall be sufficient to allow the Producers to comply with requirements for these vehicles 
which get their type approval 2 years after the entry into force. This might be distinguished from the application of 
regulations for all vehicle placed on the market, even those which got a type approval before the new ELV-R enters 
into force.

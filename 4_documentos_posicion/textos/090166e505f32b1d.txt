FEEDBACK TO ADR FRAMEWORK REVISION 
Ecommerce Europe feedback to public consultation 
on proposal to revise the Alternative Dispute 
Resolution Framework 
Introduction 
Ecommerce Europe welcomes the opportunity to provide additional input to the Commission on the revision 
of the Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) framework. As mentioned in our 2022 feedback, we overall 
consider that the Directive successfully created a standard for EU -wide out-of-court commercial dispute 
resolution mechanisms. EU public authorities have become much better equipped to tackle widespread 
infringements and additionally they benefitted from increased cooperation.   
We appreciate that the Commission has taken heed of our advice and preserved the voluntary nature of 
the ADR procedures. Ecommerce Europe underlines that participation should remain voluntary. We 
consider that mandatory ADR would not be appropriate and could even disrupt the trader’s internal 
processes by unintentionally encouraging consumers to bypass the trader’s existing customer services, 
resulting in a worse service (and outcome) for consumers. The sole objective of ADR bodies should be on 
solving disputes rather than general inquiries and complaints.  
However, Ecommerce Europe notes that there is room for improvement in the Commission proposal to 
alleviate unintended negative consequences (e.g. scope), improve trader participation in ADR schemes 
and ultimately facilitate better consumer protection. With this paper, we would like to share our views on 
the current proposal and how it could be improved to benefit all the stakeholders involved. 
Analysis 
• Article 2 – Scope 
On the extension of the scope, we support it to cover voluntary ADR actions against non-EU traders. Doing 
this might help consumers that purchase goods and services from outside the EU to find redress in cases 
where it is necessary. We do however foresee potential difficulties in the execution of  an ADR decision 
against the non-EU trader in the jurisdiction of these traders. There is a risk that this provision might create 
additional work for ADR bodies without the possibility of solving the issues raised.  
On the other hand, we find it problematic to extend the scope to non-contractual and pre-contractual 
obligations. In our experience, we increasingly see cases of fraud (e.g., return fraud) which might very 
well be resolved through ADR procedures. However, we should also be cautious about the confusion it 
could create with regards to existing enforcement of such non -contractual issues. After all, ADR is about 
mediation, whereas non-contractual and pre-contractual obligations are generally seen as an enforcement 
issue. It is difficult to understand how the role ADR bodies would fit in this system given that they cannot 
issue opinions. We consider it to be outside of the duties of ADR bodies to solve issues related to misleading 
advertising, unclear or misleading information, unfair terms or guarantee rights especially in cases where 
there is no contract. In cases where a consumer purcha sed a product then it is a question of misleading 
practices and there are already other safeguards/enforcement avenues.

Moreover, with a mandatory extension of the scope to cover pre-contractual disputes, there is a risk that 
industry associations will no longer be willing to bear the bulk of the (case) costs (and even stop participating 
in ADR). An extension of the scope to pre-contractual disputes will mean an increase in dispute s and thus 
an increase in these costs for the industry organisations and their members. Such a proposal represents a 
philosophical shift in the role of the mediator, who will become an online site control authority. Given that 
mediation is not compulsory in all Member States, there is a risk of placing too heavy of a burden on existing 
mediators, thus threatening mediation services that are working very well. 
Consumers often contribute minimally to dispute resolution costs, regardless of the outcome. Considering 
that consumer claims are not always successful, and in traditional legal procedures, consumers bear part 
of the costs, it is crucial to address these i ssues. Pre-contractual disputes are by nature more complex to 
solve and involve determining expectations before an agreement, making proceedings lengthy and legally 
intricate. Imposing these costs on industry associations and e -commerce players is unreason able, 
especially when consumer claims may not be certain, burdening entrepreneurs with lengthy and costly 
procedures, with low thresholds for consumers in terms of costs. 
We do consider it important that consumers benefit from access to justice as this is a critical aspect for the 
functioning of the rule of law and for consumer protection. With this in mind, we do not believe that burdens 
for ADR bodies should increase. We would prefer instead to see a reduction in costs and the costs being 
largely borne by the government given the social importance. 
• Article 4 – Definitions 
It can only be beneficial for the Commission to clarify concepts such as “domestic disputes” and “cross -
border” disputes. Such a clarification will increase the likelihood of finding quick solutions to disputes that 
may fall into either of these categories. Our m embers’ experience has shown that dealing with ADR 
schemes in cross-border situations can be quite complicated for national ADR bodies. 
• Article 5 – Access to ADR entities and procedures 
Currently, the provision in Art. 5(1) asks the Member States to ensure the existence of ADR entities to deal 
with disputes between consumers and traders. However, the proposed amendment establishes an 
obligation on the Member States to establish ADR entities.  
We are of the view that this change should had been better paired with a change in Article 5(1) to reduce 
the difficulties of ADRs. While the new definition introduced in Art. 4 may provide some clarity, it will still be 
difficult to solve ADR cases where ADR bodies are required to apply the law of the state where the 
consumer purchased from rather than where the consumer is established. For example, in case a German 
consumer would order a product from a French website (directing sales to the German market), the French 
ADR scheme would need to apply the German law (under Rome II Regulation).  
We would have preferred for this section to benefit from concrete clarifications that if in a cross -border 
setting, it would be allowed for the ADR body of the country where the e-commerce company is established 
is allowed to rule the case under its own law.  
Ecommerce Europe supports the Commission’s Art. 5(2) proposal to ensure that ADR procedures are 
digital, easily accessible and inclusive tools. Digitalisation could support all parties involved in an ADR 
procedure and drive a more efficient complaint handl ing framework in both domestic and cross -border 
settings.

Additionally, we support the approach for Article 5(4) establishing that ADR entities should not make it 
disproportionately more difficult for consumers to contact the trader before being able to proceed to ADR. 
We consider though that this provision should be balanced with the inclusion of a point clarifying that 
companies may be able to share confidential data with ADR bodies (e.g. in case the company knows the 
consumer is a fraudulent actor). 
Concerning Art. 5(8) we have some reservations. We would only oppose the necessity of traders to reply 
within 20 working days as long as it would set an obligation for traders to participate in ADR procedure. The 
national legislation or the EU sector -specific legislation should continue setting the boundaries of 
participating or not. If this continues the case, then we could agree with the provision to respond within 20 
days, provided there is a possibility to request an extension for complicated cases. This is particularly the 
case in the travel sector, or during excessively busy periods (e.g., Christmas).  
Additionally, we would like to see the introduction of safeguards for this provision that would ensure 
confidentiality of decisions. We do not think that it would be appropriate for this category of decisions to be 
made public as that may lead to reputational damage for organisations and would also contrad ict the 
voluntary nature of the proposal. 
• Articles 4 and 19 – Transparency/ Information to be notified to competent authorities by 
dispute resolution entities 
Ecommerce Europe can only support the objective of the Commission’s to reduce administrative burdens 
and reporting obligations.  
We consider that such measures will enable ADR entities to make better use of their resources and focus 
more on dispute resolution, rather meeting administrative requirements.  
• Article 13 – Consumer information by traders 
We consider it positive that the Commission proposed the removal of Article 13(3). However, it should be 
made clear, that the information obligation only occurs, if the trader participates in ADR.  In other words, 
businesses should no longer have to inform the consumer of which ADR body the consumer can turn to if 
no agreement is reached after the consumer has submitted a complaint to the business if it does not intend 
to participate in such a dispute resolution body. 
• Article 14 – Assistance for consumers 
Such a provision can only be helpful to boost ADR uptake in cross -border cases. ADR contact points will 
further streamline cooperation and strengthen ADR as a whole. We do however suggest that this provision 
be adjusted so that to prevent consumers from forum shopping , where they would go to different ADRs 
targeting the same trader.  
• Articles 20 and 24 – Role of the competent authorities and of the Commission / 
Communication 
The goals of these articles could perhaps be boosted by awareness campaigns. The objective must be to 
simplify the system so that consumers and sellers find it easy and valuable to use. It cannot replace 
enforcement or going to courts. The ADR system has to remain voluntary. 
• Proposal to repeal of the ODR platform and the Recommendation 
We support the Commission in its repeal of the Regulation to discontinue the ODR platform. However, that 
should not mean that nothing is set up in its place. Instead, there should be system acting as pan-European

Concluding Remarks 
Finally, we would like to point out the importance of first ensuring that the authorities' supervision works 
effectively and not letting alternative dispute resolution bodies become a substitute for insufficient 
supervisory efforts. For this reason, the pr oposal to extend the scope of the directive to stages before the 
agreement was concluded  is unsuitable . It is critical to understand that ADR bodies do not have the 
resources, expertise and enforcement powers to achieve the proposed objectives.  
Still with reference to the proposal to extend the scope of application, questions are justified to be asked 
regarding what the exact differences between ADR bodies, consumer protection and market surveillance 
authorities would be.  
Overall, the implementation of the proposals in their current form would mean that the well -functioning 
system for ADR procedures would have to be fundamentally changed, which cannot be considered 
justifiable. This would disproportionately complicate and m ake the dispute resolution process more 
complicated and expensive.

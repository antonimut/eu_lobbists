 TUI Group preliminary position on the Package Travel Directive revision (March 2024)   I. Summary of key concerns  Package holidays are already the safest form of travel. There is no other industry where customers benefit from comparable protection. We urge for moderation in the PTD revision to not jeopardise the competitiveness of the European package holiday.  There should be no limitation of pre-payments for package holidays:   - Pre-payments are essential for an attractive market offer of package holidays which can compete fairly with loose travel arrangements offered by platforms and individual providers, which remain free to ask pre-payments and do not protect consumers to nearly the same level.  - While the proposed limitation is billed “flexible”, it is still very restrictive. Even where tour operators may rightly ask an advance above 25%, they will face complexity in doing so and may not be able to cover their own operational expenses or even all their B2B pre-payment obligations. This will reduce liquidity and increase external financing costs of tour operators, which will in turn make package holidays less attractive to offer and more expensive for consumers. Also, the final payment at 28 days is too restrictive and will make late cancellations more expensive for tour operators and consumers.  - Unlike in other sectors, pre-payments for package holidays are already fully protected through insolvency cover and strong refund rights, so there is no good reason to regulate in this space. The pandemic is a very poor reference as exceptional situations require exceptional measures and should not deprive the organised travel sector from liquidity in normal times.  There should be a clear definition of package holiday:  - To its merit, the revision proposal seeks to address existing flaws in the definitions which are used by some market players to circumvent consumer protection obligations.  - However, besides some obvious flaws, including a new loophole, the revision proposal does not reduce complexity and the new provisions on package holidays bought within 3 or 24 hours are difficult to apply in practice.  - From our view it would be better to incorporate the remaining scenario of “linked travel arrangement” into the package and come to a clear rule on how package holidays are established in different stages, where there is a close link between the different travel services included.  Other key concerns:  - Tour operators’ responsibility should not be extended to calamities occurring at the traveller’s place of residence or departure.  - The existing insolvency protection systems are already very expensive. Rather than further increasing obligations, measures are needed to remove double and triple protection layers.

 II. Full preliminary position   Introduction  TUI is Europe’s leading integrated tourism group, bringing the entire tourism value chain under one roof.1 TUI has traditionally focused on providing pre-arranged package holidays, and this continues to be a core part of our offering. In recent years we have also expanded our offer of dynamic packages and single travel services. We are therefore well placed to comment on the PTD revision proposal.  TUI strongly believes in the great value of package holidays for consumers, as this type of travel arrangement offers the best protection available in the market. An important challenge for package holidays is that the included protection does come at an additional cost which is not borne by providers of single travel services or loose arrangements, which are also widely available, but offer little or no protection. This has become more pronounced as consumers are more price-sensitive than ever before.   In order to achieve the high level of consumer protection that the Package Travel Directive is aiming for, it is essential to keep package holidays attractive in the market. Tour operators should be able to offer package holidays under reasonable conditions, and consumers should be able to find offers at competitive prices. To this end, the PTD reform should crucially do two things: (1) avoid additional burden on package holidays which does not effectively and proportionally increase consumer protection and (2) make sure that all providers of travel services play by the same rules.   From this perspective, TUI is critically reviewing the Commission’s proposal and wishes to share its current insights on the most relevant elements below.   As an important note to policymakers, TUI stresses that changes to the existing rules should be carefully considered to ensure these are balanced, effective and practicable both for consumers and the travel sector. We look forward to continuing to engage with the European Institutions and are happy to share our expertise.   * * *  Limitation of pre-payments  - TUI strongly opposes any limitation of prepayments which will not increase consumer protection but will make package holidays less attractive.    1 TUI’s activities include: travel shops and tour operators in all major European source markets, five European airlines, three cruise lines, an international hotel business, tours, excursions and local transport in destinations, business-to-business and business-to-consumer platforms. The tour operating business is very seasonal as travel in Europe is concentrated in the summer months. o The organised travel sector is used to flexibly provide solutions to its customers when unforeseen situations occur. Even when large numbers of customers were affected in the past, for instance when holiday destinations were closed even mid-season due to political instability or other force majeure reasons, alternative holidays were arranged and where this could not be agreed, customers received a full refund.  o The exceptional situation which presented itself in the early stages of the Covid-19 pandemic was of a different nature and proved to be very challenging for the entire travel sector. Due to the complete stalling of global travel and great levels of uncertainty, there was no opportunity to redirect travel in time and place, and tour operators had to refund a great number of customers simultaneously while their fixed financial commitments continued and hardly any new revenue was coming in.   o While it cannot be ruled out that another pandemic or similar situation could again trigger mass cancellations in the future, such a situation is however likely to remain exceptional. From our view, exceptional situations require exceptional measures and should not be addressed by measures which hamper the competitiveness of tour operators during normal business times. With its loan to the travel industry, which allowed tour operators to make fast refunds to customers while upholding their own liquidity during the pandemic, Denmark has delivered a best practice example which demonstrates that ad hoc measures can work to full satisfaction of all stakeholders.   Definitions of package and LTA  - TUI has since long taken the view that consumers should have clarity on their rights when something goes wrong regarding their holiday. Consumers who buy the same, or similar, combinations of holiday products, ought to be entitled to the same levels of protection, irrespective of the selling model. This directly benefits the consumer and ensures a level competitive landscape.  - We appreciate that the revision proposal seeks to address the existing flaws in the definitions of package and “linked travel arrangement” (LTA) to better protect consumers. Nevertheless, further adjustments are needed, to ensure the updated definitions are clear, work in practice and are enforceable in all possible scenarios, both on- and off-line. The definitions should also be less complex and easier to understand.   - Our current considerations on the detailed revision proposals: o The principle of incorporating the former LTA type (a) “single point of sale” into the package definition is welcome as this is currently actively used by market participants to avoid being subject to the full consumer protection obligations. Further work needs to be done however to ensure that providers and consumers are in all scenarios aware that a package has been established when consumers buy an additional travel service following the purchase of a first travel service and are able to fulfil their obligations; o There also seems to be a new loophole in that a provider can invite for a second travel service after the first purchase by the consumer, which does not make a difference from the consumer perception but could lead to a completely unprotected travel arrangement;

o Similarly, the changes to the definition of click-through-packages are welcome, but more work is needed to ensure that any circumvention is ruled out; o From our view, any commercial model which results in a consumer being targeted with an additional offer for a second travel service, matching a first travel service already agreed, should be considered as a package. As currently formulated, there will be an even closer link between the two travel services than is the case for click-through-packages, as in this case (new proposal for art 3.5) the consumer will be invited by the same trader with whom the first travel service was purchased. In addition, the requirement that an LTA can only be formed by a trader which is a “party to a contract on the provision of a travel service”, seems to limit the application of this provision to transport or accommodation providers (excluding travel intermediaries). This may lead to cases with no protection at all, unless the definition of packages formed at a single point of sale is further perfected (see first bullet).   Termination and right of withdrawal  - TUI welcomes that the Commission has sought to clarify package traveller’s rights to withdraw from the contract and obtain a full refund. More work will nevertheless be needed to make the proposals more balanced and even more clear to avoid divergent implementation and interpretation. Our reflections: o The reference to official travel warnings is welcome.  However, it will be necessary to define a timeframe as to the point in time before departure a travel warning can trigger a traveller’s rights to withdraw from the contract, and for how long this provides a valid reason to terminate the contract. From our view, travellers should not be able to cancel their booking free of cost for a reason that can and probably will be solved by the time the traveller would depart, and official travel warnings are often issued without end date. o The expansion of the right to withdrawal into circumstances occurring at the place of traveller’s residence or departure should be removed. Tour operators already incur a very high responsibility, as they must guarantee the proper performance of all services included in the package holiday, even where this is impacted by force majeure situations at the destination or underway. This responsibility should not be expanded to calamities occurring in the home area of the traveller as there is in principle no connection to the performance of the holiday. Such expansion makes tour operators subject to incalculable liabilities that cannot be recovered from any party. As a very minimum, there must be a clearer definition of the domestic circumstances that are considered to affect the performance of the package and it should be clear that the right to withdrawal does not apply to personal circumstances; o The reference to impacts on the holiday which are “reasonably expected” is very much up for interpretation and should be removed. The assessment of whether there is an impact on the performance of the package travel contract should be based on objective facts, not subjective expectations.

 - In case it is no longer economical for a tour operator to operate holidays to a certain destination and for a given departure date, due to a substantial number of customers withdrawing from their contract, the tour operator should be entitled to offer the remaining customers the choice between a full refund and an alternative holiday, without compensation.  - In the event of a major exceptional crisis such as a pandemic or similar situation triggering mass cancellations, refunds should be made as soon as possible, not within 14 days as in the current legislation.   Insolvency protection  - The proposed changes will most likely lead to a further increase of the already high costs of insolvency protection, without substantial benefit to consumers. We specifically reject any potential backup fund arrangements wherein travel businesses would finance such reserves as it would further strain finances and increase costs that would ultimately result in a higher cost of package holidays, leaving consumers to seek out cheaper, non-protected, holidays.  - It should be noted that the existing insolvency protection already is very protective and unique as such a system does not exist in other sectors which rely on customer prepayments.  - Importantly, the Commission proposals do not address at all the important issue of costly double and triple protections which currently exist and are borne by providers of package holidays. One of the issues is that many insolvency protection arrangements currently require tour operators to provide substantial cash collaterals or other securities to back up the protection which they are providing. Credit card companies / payment acquirers often also require substantial guarantees for the eventuality that they would be faced with a chargeback procedure or withhold all payments until the travel service has been performed. These equally raise costs and freeze up tour operators’ working capital.   B2B refunds  - TUI continues to believe that business-to-business refund rights are best agreed between contractual parties and not in consumer legislation. Tour operators and suppliers should continue to be able to freely decide which business risks they are willing and able to take up, and to negotiate their commercial agreements accordingly. However, we do not vehemently oppose the Commission’s proposal as currently formulated, as it largely reflects current common business practice.  - We would like to point out that:  o Refund rights or obligations do not provide a solution for a (highly exceptional) general liquidity crisis in the travel sector, as we have experienced with the pandemic; o It will likely be very difficult to enforce the new provisions, particularly against suppliers located in third countries.

 - We would also like to stress that any further expansion of the refund obligation, for example by extending it to the situation where a supplier does not cancel or fail to provide the service, will have important negative impacts. Vouchers  - TUI considers that the use of vouchers proved very useful during the pandemic, as one of the tools to safeguard customer rights while retaining liquidity. Vouchers are not being used frequently during normal business times, but it is positive to retain the possibility to make use of them. From our perspective, it is however not necessary to regulate vouchers to the full detail to adequately protect consumers. Provision of insolvency protection should suffice and for the rest, tour operators and consumers should be able to freely agree on the contents of vouchers. From our experience, if vouchers are not sufficiently attractive, consumers will not accept them.  - We would nevertheless like to provide some comments on the detailed proposals: o The proposed transferability of vouchers to another person (art 12.a.8) makes it impossible for the tour operator to refund the amount in cash automatically with no request by the traveller (art 12.a.7), because the tour operator does not necessarily know who is in the possession of the voucher when its validity expires. This might even open up possibilities for fraud; o The proposal also states that the refund should be the amount specified in the voucher. In our opinion, the amount to be refunded should be limited to the amount which the traveller originally paid to the tour operator. Travellers should not be able to get a cash payment for any amounts which were commercially offered by the tour operator in excess of what was paid by the traveller (see art 12.a.3); o Furthermore, it should be clarified that the proposed provisions apply exclusively for refunding any payments made by travellers for a package that was not fulfilled. Additionally, any other business-to-consumer payments such as compensation or commercial gestures awarded, should remain outside the scope of vouchers to avoid further regulatory complexity.  “Rapid refund mechanisms”  - While it is highly unlikely that the travel sector will frequently face a liquidity crisis like the one we experienced with the pandemic, TUI does welcome the Commission’s recognition of the possibility of rapid refund mechanisms at national level (new art 12.4).  - In order to effectively assure rapid refunds in times of crisis, there should be an active requirement for Member States to take the necessary steps so that ad hoc mechanisms can indeed be set up quickly when a major crisis occurs.  - TUI does explicitly reject any solutions which require the travel sector to finance a fund or similar arrangements (on top of the already existing requirements regarding insolvency protection and

EU Commission Rue du Luxembourg 1000 Bruxelles, Belgium    13th March 2024   Statement by Aktionsbündnis Tourismusvielfalt (ATV) on the European Commission's proposal for a revision of the Package Travel Directive  Honourable Members of Parliament,  Aktionsbündnis Tourismusvielfalt (ATV) is a coalition of 27 tourism industry associations representing all areas of the diverse tourism industry. We have set ourselves the goal of preserving tourism as a whole and achieving fair promotion for every market participant in the industry in order to ensure that the network remains a unified entity in the future. We would like to comment below on the European Commission's proposal for a revision of the Package Travel Directive.   General  In principle, the Package Travel Directive in its current form has proven itself in day-to-day business. However, this explicitly does not apply to the coronavirus pandemic as an event of global market failure. The Package Travel Directive was never intended for such an exceptional case; accordingly, the application of the rules has resulted in numerous problems for providers and customers, e.g. due to the vague definition of "unforeseen and unavoidable circumstances" and the immediate repayment obligations for organisers, but not for service providers. We had expected a revision of the Package Travel Act to take appropriate account of such exceptional cases in future and had repeatedly expressed this demand in preparatory consultations and discussions. From this perspective, the current draft can only be seen as disappointing, because while this crucial point has not been taken into account, or not taken into account adequately, further bureaucratic burdens are to be introduced elsewhere that address "problems" that do not or hardly ever arise in practice. Instead of taking into account the most important demand of the tourism industry, further hurdles and complications are being introduced instead, which will only make the package holiday product more expensive and therefore less attractive. This is neither in the interests of the industry nor of consumers.

1) Insufficient consideration of extraordinary global events The biggest challenge for tour operators during and especially at the beginning of the pandemic proved to be the obligation to refund customer money without deductions within 14 days in the event of exceptional and unforeseen circumstances. This was mainly due to the fact that customer monies had generally already been paid to service providers such as hotels and airlines, but these were not obliged to refund them promptly or were unable to do so. In addition, the regulation does not take into account the expenses already incurred by the tour operator as a result of its services. As a result, tour operators and travel agencies found themselves in financial difficulties through no fault of their own.  In our view, it is essential that the revision of the Package Travel Directive contains a provision that provides for reasonable burden-sharing between businesses and consumers in the event of another complete market failure. Before the outbreak of COVID-19, such a scenario was hardly conceivable, but it has now become a reality and must be integrated into the directive. The proposal once again leaves the risk exclusively with the organisers and, in the worst-case scenario, leads not only to widespread insolvencies but also to a collapse of customer money protection schemes.  We do not believe that the proposed solutions to counter another crisis like coronavirus are an effective response.  a) Article 12a: Vouchers Voluntary vouchers are proposed here as an alternative to refunds in order to avoid insolvencies. During the pandemic, we already criticised the fact that such voluntary vouchers do not require any legal regulation, as they can already be agreed between organisers and customers. For companies, voluntary vouchers represent a high uncertainty factor, as they are entirely dependent on the goodwill and acceptance of travellers. The proposal to set the voucher amount higher than the refund amount in order to increase acceptance by travellers is also not a solution. Small and medium-sized tour operators would have to pay dearly for their economic survival through no fault of their own.  Proposed solution: In the event of major loss events, the tour operator must be granted the right to provide customers with obligatory vouchers.

b) Article 22: Remedies and claims for reimbursement by organisers During the pandemic, travel organisers had to bear the costs of reimbursing customers within the 14-day legal requirement under the PACKAGE TRAVEL DIRECTIVE even if they themselves were not reimbursed by other service providers – specifically, the reimbursement for flights was considerably delayed to consumers and travel organisers alike. This creates immense liquidity pressure for travel organisers. Although the proposal of a B2B refund right is a step in the right direction, this will unfortunately not be enough. A B2B refund right would only apply if the service provider cancels or does not provide the service. Service providers could delay their own cancellation date until the day on which the service is to be provided - even if the cancellation of the trip took place long before. There could be weeks or months between the traveller's cancellation, when the 14-day refund obligation begins, and the service provider's obligation to refund. This would not solve the organisers' problem. For example, airlines could easily avoid this obligation by only allowing organisers to bundle fully refundable flights into packages. As the cost difference between refundable and non-refundable flights is significant, this would make such packages unattractive to consumers.  Therefore, both the Package Travel Directive and the Air Passenger Rights Regulation need to be further amended and aligned to ensure that package travel remains an attractive option for consumers and to ensure refund rights in case of cancellation due to unavoidable and extraordinary circumstances.   Proposed solution: Article 22 should be reformulated to the effect that the time of cancellation by the tour operator vis-à-vis the customer is decisive for the calculation of the time limits. The timeframe for refund obligations must only apply when the package organiser has been refunded by the service provider. Both the Package Travel Directive and the Air Passenger Rights Regulation must ensure a B2B refund right in the case of customers' cancellations as a result of unavoida-ble and extraordinary circumstances.   c) Article 17.3 Crisis fund In order to ensure the effectiveness of insolvency protection, the draft bill provides for the possibility of introducing a second level of protection.  Assuming complete coverage, the German market would be expected to require around €5 billion more. This sum would also have to be raised by the industry. This would not only be reflected in higher travel prices, but companies would also be deprived of further liquidity.  Although another global market failure due to an unforeseen and unavoidable event such as a pandemic is unlikely but not impossible, national insurance systems would have to be significantly increased or supplemented to cover this eventuality. For example, at what point is a pandemic no longer exceptional, but part of the risk of life? When can it be assumed that a traveller is aware of the risks before booking and is knowingly taking them? There are no clear criteria for triggering "unavoidable and extraordinary" circumstances. Although travel warnings are taken into account as an indication, there is no standardised catalogue of criteria, which will lead to contradictory results. An EU-wide consolidated system may be necessary here. If residence, place of departure and place of travel were to be decisive in future, up to three, possibly contradictory, travel warnings would have to be taken into account. Instead, the possibility of free cancellation due to exceptional and unforeseen circumstances was extended to the place of departure and place of residence, thereby disproportionately extending the organiser's liability.  Furthermore, when a global event such as the pandemic has been continuing and Is foreseeable and has been Informed about the risks Involved, the travel organiser should not bear the full cancellation cost.   Proposed solution: The extension of organiser liability to the place of residence and place of departure should be deleted. For the assessment of whether unavoidable and extraordinary circumstances apply, only a travel warning at the place of travel may be relevant. Only unforeseen events should qualify for free cancellations.

3) Article 5a New rules for customer down payments Restrictions on prepayments for organisers only in the EU would impose serious liquidity pressure on package travel organisers. The tourism ecosystem depends heavily on working capital to meet costs in addition to the requirements to prepay suppliers. The limitation of prepayments will have financial repercussions for many businesses and, in turn, have a negative impact on the pricing of packages and will cause disruption in the European package travel market.   We understand that consumers want to be protected from unreasonably high advance payments, but this does not seem necessary in view of the already high level of protection of customer funds and the high level of consumer protection that a package holiday already offers today. Furthermore, Germany has clear case law on advance payments and we therefore see no need to include them in the Package Travel Directive.   Proposed solution: The advance payment modalities are not to be regulated by law.   4) Effects of the new rules on the sale of individual services The draft directive speaks of simplification, but in our opinion the opposite is the case. Instead, the difference between package holidays and individual services is being blurred. This contradicts the principle that individual services are never a package holiday and devalues the recently introduced linked travel services.   On the contrary, our prediction that there would be few legal disputes in practice over the distinction between package holidays, linked travel arrangements and individual travel arrangements has proven to be correct. It seems that consumers appreciate the freedom of choice and make very conscious use of it when selecting their preferred travel product.   If the Commission now claims that the new rule will lead to fewer legal disputes, the question arises as to whether the EU has an overview of the number of procedures to clarify the status of a trip that requires such an intervention? Corona was the acid test for the application of the Package Travel Directive and, with hindsight, it can be seen that it was precisely this point that did not lead to a large number of procedures.  As part of the national implementation of the Package Travel Directive in Germany, an additional arbitration centre for package holidays was introduced at the SÖP in cooperation with the German Ministry of Justice in order to resolve any disputes where consumers are confused about the status of their form of travel. Liability obligation without possibility of objection According to the European Commission's draft revision of the Package Travel Directive (PTD), travel providers can become package tour operators if consumers buy a second travel service at a single point of sale within three hours of purchasing a first travel service ("3-hour rule"). In practice, this leads to the problem for all travel providers that, for example, services in a travel booking chain (e.g. a non-functioning connecting flight booked to a hotel) that are completely unreasonable and selected by the consumer can become a liability case for the travel provider without the consumer having the option to object.

Massive increase in the technical inspection effort The introduction of the "3-hour rule" would also mean that travel providers would have to check the entire travel booking chain (e.g. individual hotel bookings, holiday flats, flights, etc.) on the basis of the principle of liability under the Package Travel Directive.  This would lead to a massive increase in the technical inspection effort for all travel providers, as the following example calculation illustrates: According to data from the Forschungsgemeinschaft Urlaub und Reisen (FUR), around 138.77 million trips with at least one overnight stay are made in Germany every year, of which 41.53 million or 29.9% are package holidays. If the "3-hour rule" is introduced, around 97.24 million journeys will have to be additionally checked to see whether: a) a service has already been sold before  b) the same customer has booked another service c) it is the same journey  d) the sale has taken place within a certain time frame e) the combination of services actually constitutes a package holiday (e.g. booking two hotels is not a package holiday).   Increased bureaucratic workload, as auditing is only possible in the back office This immense increase in technical verification effort for 97.24 million travel bookings is compounded by the fact that purchases can be made via different sales channels (e.g. one service is booked in a call centre and the other online). The final check can therefore only be carried out in the back office. Before the information required for the check reaches the back office, it must flow through at least three systems (front-end, mid-end and back-end) and the enquiry must be passed through twice.   Disruption of the booking process sequence If a package holiday is identified within the three hours after the check, considerable problems arise in the sequence of the booking process:  a) The original price of the individual services viewed by the consumer can no longer apply, as the provider must now add calculations for insolvency insurance and other costs for the services of a package holiday.  b) The T&Cs, which were previously purely mediation terms, must also be changed retroactively to the T&Cs of an organiser for the purchase of the first service.  c) The down payment according to the planned revision of the Package Travel Directive may only be 25%. The current practice of recognising unavoidable exceptional circumstances as a reason for 100% free cancellations places a disproportionate burden on the industry, especially small and medium-sized companies.  Travel providers already provide services for existing bookings and bear costs such as labour, IT costs and local payments. These upfront costs should be appropriately taken into account and incorporated into a regulation that both provides financial protection for travellers and enables companies to operate in a crisis-proof manner and ensure their financial stability.

Legislation should ensure that work performed is adequately compensated to promote fair wages and recognition for services. Therefore, fair cancellation compensation is necessary to cover work and costs incurred when unavoidable exceptional circumstances occur, ensuring a fair distribution of risk between provider and customer.  It would make sense to introduce a regulation not only in the event of a pandemic, but in general in the event of unavoidable exceptional circumstances, which provides for an appropriate distribution of costs in order to maintain the liquidity of tour operators. It is inappropriate to force companies to make refunds from their own capital in a global pandemic.  The EU Commission should take action in this matter in order to prevent EU member states from having to support the market again with bridging aid in the event of renewed crises. Consumer protection should not be prioritised over the fundamental rights of companies (entrepreneurial freedom and property rights in accordance with the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU). A balance must be found.   Balanced risk sharing also means that a special EU crisis fund is not required for prompt reimbursement in the event of major events and that tour operators do not have to be additionally burdened. Many contracts, including in international business transactions, provide for risk sharing in cases of force majeure. A fair quota for bearing the costs of travel cancellations due to unavoidable extraordinary circumstances is therefore appropriate  Proposed solution: A fair distribution of costs in the event of cancellation of the trip due to unavoidable extraordinary circumstances should be introduced.   6) Claims of the traveller - assertion According to the current regulation, the traveller has until the expiry of the limitation period (two years) to assert claims for a reduction in price against the tour operator. The timely assertion of recourse claims against its service providers is thwarted, as is the notification of its insurer, if necessary. The customer's right is also only apparently consumer-friendly. The preclusive period for asserting claims against the tour operator should be limited to six months.  7) Holiday properties In addition to the EU Commission's proposals, there is a debate in Germany as to whether individual travel services, and in particular the booking of holiday flats and holiday homes, should be regulated in the Package Travel Directive.  We have already explained in Chapter 4 why the inclusion of individual travel services in the regulatory framework of the Package Travel Directive is inappropriate or even harmful. During the legislative process for the national implementation of the Package Travel Directive in Germany in 2017, consumer advocates called for the rental of holiday properties as individual travel services to be subject to package travel law.   This would have significantly expanded the scope of the Package Travel Directive, to the detriment of a single form of accommodation, which would have been placed in a worse position than other accommodation providers such as hotels, guesthouses, campsites and other individual travel offers. At the time, consumer advocates feared that not including the individual travel service of arranging a holiday apartment/house in the package holiday law would lead to a deterioration in consumer rights. This fear has not been confirmed, as confirmed by the aforementioned report by Interval under the direction of Prof. Dr Ansgar Staudinger on behalf of the Federal Ministry of Justice and Consumer Protection in 2020.  Furthermore, there are no indications that such regulation would be necessary. There are neither a relevant number of complaints nor court proceedings about consumers not being aware at the time of booking that they had booked an individual travel service and not a package holiday.   Accordingly, a solution would also be sought here for a problem that does not occur in reality or only in individual cases that can be satisfactorily resolved by legal means. Instead, the inclusion of the booking of holiday accommodation in the Package Travel Directive would lead to discrimination against a selected individual travel service and to high, disproportionate burdens, especially for small and medium-sized intermediary portals and agencies, but also for tourist information centres that broker accommodation offers. Established tourism structures would be jeopardised: 82% of holiday accommodation in Germany is provided by private landlords, who in turn manage and market a large proportion of these properties themselves. Neither medium-sized agencies/platforms nor private landlords would be able to fulfil the obligations of a tour operator in terms of personnel and finances. A shift towards

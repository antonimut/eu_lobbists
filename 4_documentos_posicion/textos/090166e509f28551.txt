2023 Adoption package for the Proposal to Amend the Package Travel 
Directive – easyJet Group’s Response 
 
Introduction 
This response represents easyJet Group’s views. easyJet Group is comprised of easyJet 
holidays and easyJet (the separate airline entity which for the purposes of this paper, we will 
refer to as “easyJet airline”). 
easyJet holidays is the fastest growing UK tour operator, offering great -value beach, city 
and lakes package holidays to more than 100 destinations across  Europe, directly through 
its website and through over 3000 travel agent partners.  
In December 2023, easyJet holidays expanded on its successful UK holiday business with 
the launch of websites in France and Germany.  
easyJet holidays sells holidays at an inclusive price, with flights, hotels and transfers 
combined as part of a package.  Consumers can further choose to book optional 
excursions/tours separately through recommended partners. 
easyJet airline is one of Europe’s largest airlines with bases across  Europe and operating 
flights on a wide network of city and beach holiday routes across Europe and North Africa. 
easyJet airline sells packages – specifically flights plus car rental. easyJet airline is also an 
arranger of Linked Travel Arrangements, offer ing the option to purchase hotels, car rental 
or tours/excursions via preferred partners, within 24 hours of booking a flight. 
easyJet Group takes its obligations under the Package Travel Directive very seriously as our 
focus is always on the consumer and welcomes the opportunity to provide its view on the 
Commission’s proposals for amending the Package Travel Directive (2015/2302). easyJet 
Group recognises the importance and value of a properly regulated package holiday 
industry, with consumer protection a t the centre of this. easyJet Group considers that the 
current Directive generally works well for business and consumers. easyJet Group does 
however have concerns about some of the proposals which it does not identify as being 
beneficial to consumers or businesses. 
easyJet Group’s view is that:  
1. The definition of a package should not be amended or extended;  
2. Linked Travel Arrangements should not be re-defined as packages; And 
3. Linked Travel Arrangements should be removed from the Package Travel Directive entirely 
for the reasons outlined below.

Linked Travel Arrangements  
• easyJet Group advocates for  the removal of LTAs entirely from the PTD for the 
reasons set out in the section above . easyJet Group does not consider it is 
appropriate to extend the current definition of a package to incorporate what are 
currently treated as LTAs, although regulation should be clearer on what constitutes 
a package and what is not required to be protected, to ensure a uniform approach. 
• LTAs offer consumers limited and unnecessary  protection. However, being 
presented as an arrangement under the banner of the Package Travel Directive may 
mislead consumers about the extent of the protection they have.  
• Hundreds of thousands of consumers book LTAs through easyJet airline  and 
although we do not record actual numbers of claims specific to this issue, we are 
aware of a few instances where our consumers have believed th ey have more 
  
  
  
  
 
 
protection (when booking an LTA ) than they actually have. Anecdotally, we  also 
believe many consumers will not read the ‘information notice’ (set out in the Annexes 
of the PTD) and therefore are not clear on what an LTA is and will therefore make 
purchases without understanding what level of protection applies to them.  Given the 
established protections in place (via other consumer protection laws and refunds 
offered via payment card schemes), we believe LTAs merely confuse consumers 
about how they can effectively claim redress when in difficulty. 
• easyJet airline are not aware of any examples of failures of LTA arrangers where a 
consumer has made a claim/suffered a financial loss  –although the Commission 
wants to provide consumers with protection when booking these linked 
arrangements, in reality there does not appear to be evidence of any consumer 
detriment over the last 5 years from LTA arrangers collapsing.   
• LTAs (within 24 hours) are very difficult to track. Whilst easyJet airline does arrange 
insolvency insurance to financially protect consumer payments it receives for flights, 
it is particularly complex and challenging to match flight bookings to 
accommodation, car hire and tourist services booked within 24 hours of a flight.   
Exchanging data between third party businesses that involves both commercially 
sensitive information and personal data, is not straightforward from a contractual, 
GDPR and information security point of view. This creates a cost and administrative 
burden on easyJet airline  but provides almost no value for easyJet airline ’s 
consumers. 
• It would be a simpler message for consumers to know that if they book a package it 
is protected – if they do not book a package, they must go to the direct provider of 
the travel service in question to seek redress . This would also prevent any back and 
forth for consumers trying to establish whether a booking is a package or an LTA.   
Consumer payments to the first trader (i.e. the current Linked Travel arranger), would 
in most cases  be covered by the payment card schemes (as they are today), or 
consumers could choose to obtain their own travel insurance for additional 
protection. 
• Whilst easyJet airline strongly advocates LTAs being removed from the regulations, 
easyJet airline  does not support arrangements that are currently treated as  LTA s 
being re-classified as packages for the reasons we have given in the section above . 
Organisers have to carefully consider the extent of the  liability they assume from 
suppliers where arrangements form part of a package .  The extension of the scope 
of packages to include services that currently involve separate contracts and 
separate payments to third parties  would significantly increase package organisers’ 
liability and this would   come at a cost that would inevitably be passed onto 
consumers. (This is explained further in our comments about Packages in the 
previous section). 
• easyJet airline is also particularly concerned by the proposal to introduce onerous 
information requirements (as set out in the notices in Annex II  and also explained in 
Recital 9 ).  Specifically the proposed requirements having to communicate and 
implement a process for consumers to take screenshots to record linked bookings, 
inform the arranger by email or webpage and for the arranger to confirm protection 
back to the consumer.  This is a convoluted process and too prescribed for digital 
ways of working.  The notice  states that for consumers to benefit from protection 
  
  
  
  
 
 
they must comply with these requirements.  It is not clear what the impact of non -
compliance is - does this mean t hat consumers who fail to comply with these 
requirements lose  any protection rights?  This proposal again adds a further 
administrative cost to the arranger to implement (both from a technology and people 
cost to service these enquiries) – these costs inevitably would need to be passed on 
to consumers.  The Commission has not provided sufficient evidence to demonstrate 
why this is necessary. 
 
Payments 
In relation to the European Commission’s proposal on  payments (Article 5a), easyJet airline 
is concerned about the requirement to cap the deposit/advance payments to 25% of the 
total cost, particularly where advance payments are financially protected and covered by 
stricter refund obligations.  Receiving consumer money months in advance of travel, can be 
beneficial to businesses from a cash flow perspective enabling wider consumer choice in the 
market.  It help s generate further investment and growth and improves consumer service 
(for example by investing in more efficient systems/processes or consumer service tools 
that enhance consumers experience and drive down prices).  Limiting pre-payments would 
therefore stifle some types of innovation and development and this seems unnecessary 
when consumers’ money remains protected. 
If the cap is implemented, easyJet Group considers that passengers should not be restricted 
if they choose to pay more than the requested 25% down  payment limit at their own 
discretion. This flexibility should be explicitly mentioned in Article 5a.  
Additionally, it is cru cial to specify that airplane tickets and car hire services are excluded 
from the 25% down payment rule (whether the package is sold directly by an airline or via an 
intermediary/third party). These services are often subject to different pricing structures and 
payment terms and should not be bound by the same limitations.  
Overall, easyJet Group has concerns about the proposed prepayment measures but if the 
measures are  implemented, easyJet Group requests the inclusion of the mentioned 
clarifications and exclusions to better align with the needs of both the travellers and the 
industry.  It is noted, for example, that Article 5a, does not explicitly refer to some of the 
points made in the recit al ( paragraphs 1  and 13) , in that advance payments covering 
payments (i) between group companies, (ii) including commissions requested by retailers, 
or (iii) the calculation for groups of packages having similar characteristics.  These are 
important clarifications that should be included in Article 5a.   
Furthermore, it is essential that the drafting of the proposal,  more clearly clarifies that the 
25% cap can be averaged across all holidays sold by the organiser, so that, for example, a 
standard deposit of 75 EUR can be applied  across all holidays offered by the organiser, 
without necessitating individual calculations at a booking level  – even if that deposit might 
represent more than 25% of the cost depending on the price of certain holidays.   easyJet 
holidays currently offers a standard deposit of €75 per person – whether that is for a city 
break package costing €200 per person, or a beach holiday costing €2,000 per person.  It 
is a clear amount for consumers to understand and a positive message to promote our low 
  
  
  
  
 
 
deposits.  We would like to be able to continue offering that, even though this may represent 
more than 25% pre-payment for selected holidays.  It would be helpful if the drafting of the 
amendment to the Directive would allow for this approach to continue. 
There also ought to be an exception around pre -payments using vouchers/credit.  If for 
example a consumer has a voucher that is due to expire shortly, and wants to make a 
booking and apply all of the voucher to the booking, but that would represent more than 
25% of the cost – consumers should be allowed to do that rather than risk the voucher 
expiring. 
 
Vouchers 
The concept of Refund Credit Notes  (RCNs) was developed in the UK market during the 
Covid-19 Pandemic.  This enabled organisers to provide a credit note (voucher) in place of a 
cash refund.  If the RCN was not used within 12 months, consumers could receive their money 
back. 
This was a means for tour operators to manage cash flow during this extreme event. 
easyJet Group is concerned that this PTD proposal from the Commission goes further , 
requiring organisers to both offer a refund at the time of the significant change/termination, 
and if the organiser chooses to offer a voucher too, to refund that voucher in cash if not 
used within 12 months.  easyJet Group believes this provides a dispro portionate level of 
protection for consumers.  If consumers are given a genuine choice between accepting a 
refund (within 14 days) of a significantly changed or terminated holiday, or a voucher to the 
same or higher amount , and the consumer chooses a vouch er – there should be no  
obligation on the organiser to later refund the voucher if it is not used.   
easyJet Group would like a more explicit reference to the position regarding vouchers issued 
in other circumstances – particularly under any other Articles (except 10, 11 and 12).   
It should be clear that if the organiser chooses to issue vouchers under any other Article, 
such as cancellations by the consumer, or in response to a complaint – those vouchers do 
not benefit from the same protection or refund status as vouchers issues under Articles 10, 
11 and 12. 
easyJet Group is not in favour of the proposal to financially protect the voucher during its 
validity date under the proposal.  The company is not aware that equivalent vouchers issued 
by retailers in other sectors/industries are generally protected against a trader’s insolvency.  
If the Commission is set on introducing such protection, easyJet Group believes this should 
be based on a risk assessment of the organisers financial health/position. 
 
Right of Redress and Refund Rights of Organisers  
easyJet Group notes the intention to oblige Member States to ensure service providers 
refund organisers within 7 days, and has the following views on this: 
  
  
  
  
 
 
• As a service provider to package holiday organiser s, easyJet airline is primarily 
concerned about ensuring any refunds it makes reach the end consumer.  There 
needs to be a mechanism in place to avoid double payment (where the airline has to 
refund the organiser and the consumer also claims the same refund directly from the 
airline). easyJet airline also needs complete assurance that refunds it makes to 
organisers will indeed be passed onto consumers.  We note this is subject to further 
consultation under EC261 proposals, so we are responding more substantively under 
that separate consultation.   Any obligation should remain subject to any rights or 
remedies the airline / service provider may have in respect of the organiser’s failure 
to comply with the airline / service providers terms of business. 
• easyJet Group has explained the difficulties associated with tracking Linked Travel 
Arrangements today, and what it foresees to be similar difficulties with the proposals 
regarding the extension of packages (to cover 3hr and 24hr booking windows).   It is 
highly l ikely that many service providers (hotel booking sites, car hire providers, 
tour/excursion providers etc), may not even know their product has been purchased 
within the requisite window such that it creates a package. If the service provider 
doesn’t know if the sale to a consumer is part of a wider package, how can they be 
required to issue a refund within 7 days if the service is cancelled. The same is true 
for easyJet airline, selling flights via Online Travel Agencies (OTAs) – easyJet airline 
doesn’t know for certain whether the OTA will sell the flight as part of a package, or 
just as an individual travel service. 
• easyJet Group would question how effective this proposal will be if each Member 
State can only place requirements on service providers operating/established within 
EU countries.  It seems unlikely that Member States could compel service providers 
(e.g. hotels) situated outside the EU to comply with the 7 day refund requirement. 
 
Right to Terminate Due to Unavoidable and Extraordinary Circumstances  
easyJet Group is concerned about the proposal to extend consumers’ rights to cancel a 
package due to unavoidable and extraordinary circumstances (“UEC”). The proposal seeks 
to extend this right to situations where UECs occur not only at the destination but also at the 
consumer’s place of residence or departure. (Art. 12(2), (3a)) 
We feel it is important to distinguish between situations impacting a consumer individually, 
that would affect their ability to take the holiday (even though the organiser could still 
perform the services) – for example, train strikes  or illness – that prevent a consumer 
travelling as planned.  All of which would be situations which  travel insurance should cover, 
and these should not place a liability on the organiser 
Other natural events, such as bad weather at the place of departure, which do occasionally 
impact the performance of a package, (e.g. causing flight delays or cancellat ions), do 
however need to be qualified to ensure that rights to refunds only materialise if the organiser 
cannot provide suitable alternative arrangements and it should be the organiser (not the 
consumer) that determines whether the performance of the pack age will be significantly 
impacted. 
  
  
  
  
 
 
We are not clear on what other types of situations either could arise or have arisen at the 
consumer’s place of residence that would affect the ability of the package organiser to 
perform the package?  It would be helpful if the Commission could provide some examples 
here?   
easyJet Group is also concerned that this proposed change might also place the 
responsibility for a refund onto the organiser where, for example and as seen during Covid, 
there are travel restrictions in the consumer’s place of residence or a requirement to 
quarantine on return.  Whilst these situations may well impact the consumer, performance 
of the package should be for the organiser to determine. We do not agree that a consumers 
failure to be able perform the package should give rise to termination and a full refund.  It is 
not clear how organisers could mitigate their loss in this situation whereas customers could 
protect themselves by obtaining appropriate travel insurance. 
 
Article 13 and 14 (2) 
easyJet Group considers the following change to the Regulations is necessary. 
Currently consumers are entitled to a price reduction where the services can’t be provided 
under the package holiday contract.  This applies even if the reason for  non-
performance/lack of conformity, is due to unavoidable and extraordinary circumstances. 
easyJet Group does not believe that organisers should be liable to provide a price reduction 
in the latter case. This should be something consumers claim for under travel insurance. This 
is particularly the case where organisers cannot obtain any price reduction/refund  from 3rd 
party suppliers. 
During summer 2023, easyJet holidays consumers experienced significant disruption due to 
forest fires in Greece and other countries. Consumers had to be evacuated from hotels, 
relocated to other accommodation (in some case emerge ncy shelters), and flown home 
early.  These type s of events are clearly distressing and disappointing for consumers and 
organisers alike.  In the example of the Greece fires, easyJet holidays paid for hotel rooms, 
even where consumers were not using them. easyJet holidays had the expense of relocating 
consumers to other properties and flying consumers home (in some cases on special 
repatriation flights). Due to the Package Travel Regulations, easyJet holidays also provided 
price reductions for the parts of the holiday it could not provide, whilst still incurring costs 
from suppliers. easyJet holidays strongly believes that package organisers should not be 
legally responsible for price reductions when these force majeure events occur.  This was 
not the tour operators responsibility under the 1990 Directive. This should be a situation that 
consumer travel insurance responds to. 
That is not to say tour operators should not still provide return travel home for the consumer 
and may also choose to offer reductions from a consumer experience point of view as a 
gesture of goodwill. But that should be a decision tour operators have disc retion to take , 
taking into account individual travellers circumstances, rather than a legal obligation.

Feedback on the proposal for a directive of the European Parliament and of the Council amending 
Directive (EU) 2015/2302 to make the protection of travellers more effective and to simplify and 
clarify certain aspects of the Directive. 
General Observations 
• IATA strongly opposes introducing restrictions on the practice of pre-payments, as proposed at 
Article 5a. Customer payment at the time of booking is the global standard for travel and tourism 
services as well as a range of other sectors. It is a well -established practice and has operated 
well over many decades, delivering benefits for consumers and businesse s alike. 
Prepayment benefits consumers by enabling businesses, including both airlines and tour 
operators, to offer lower prices and wider choices in return for upfront payment. Th ese benefits 
of choice and value  would be at stake  if such proposals were to  proceed. The European 
Commission should not use the COVID -19 pandemic - an exceptional and un precedented 
situation - as a baseline for regulating how the travel and tourism industry should work in normal 
business times. 
• A number of the elements  in the Pro posed Directive are also addressed in the Proposed 
Regulation on Multimodal Journeys and the Proposed Regulation amending  Regulation 
261/2004.  Where this is the case, any opinions expressed with regard to those other proposals 
are also valid in the context of the present remarks and vice versa. 
The scenario arises in particular with regard to reimbursements and therefore IATA’s comments 
on reimbursements are reproduced below. 
 
IATA Remarks related to Reimbursements 
IATA welcomes the initiative to clarify a nd improve the situation regarding reimbursements where the 
booking was made through an intermediary. This scenario creates challenges and uncertainty for 
carriers and consumers alike and therefore the attempt to address the issue is timely.  
However, the mechanics involved with regard to payment systems and flows are complex and the devil 
is in the detail. IATA has a number of concerns about the practicalities of how the proposed Regulation 
would work in practice. 
• Identification of the buyer / payer:  
o The first point is that it is fundamental to differentiate in all instances the buyer/payer 
from the passenger:

▪ While the passenger confronted with a cancelation is the one provided with

o The matter of refund, which is owed to the buyer/payer, should not be confused with the 
matter of compensation or indemnity for an inconvenience (delay, lost luggage….), which 
is due to the passenger. 
• Refunding the same account: 
o A second point is that requiring a carr ier to step up to cover for the deficiency of an 
intermediary in forwarding the refund to a buyer/payer that they had no prior contact 
with presents issues with recommended anti money laundering practices: Payment 
services providers restrict the possibilit y of redeeming money in the same way they 
restrict the funding methods. For example, redemption of funds may be restricted to a 
transfer of funds into the same account/or held in the customer’s name at a credit or 
financial institution (FATF Report 2010, Money Laundering Using New Payment Methods 
o A carrier has no way to know if the buyer/payer truly owns the payment instrument that 
is designated to receive the refund.  
o As an illustration of this concern about good practices in the field of anti -money 
laundering, card schemes rules routinely stipulate that the refund can only be performed 
by the ‘merchant of record’ who performed the original purchase transaction 1. Hence, 
no merchant can refund on the same card that the client used to pay the intermediary, 
unless card scheme rules are changed first. 
• Refunding through the original payment instrument: 
o Another related issue is that a carrier may not be able to transact with a specific payment 
instrument. There are multiple forms of domestic, one -country-only payments, and the 
recent years have seen an explosion of such solutions, based often on mobil e payment: 
Bizum in Spain, Swish in Sweden, MobilePay in Denmark, BLIK in Poland, Bancomat Pay 
in Italy… A carrier may well not accept the payment method of a specific country, in which 
case it relies on its network of travel intermediaries to collect paym ent from customers 
desiring to use this specific payment instrument. Hence, the carrier will not be able to 
perform a refund on said payment instrument, even if the intermediary fails to pass on

• Determination of the refund amount: 
o A carrier stepping in for a travel intermediary can only reimburse the amount it collected 
for the sale. No carrier can guarantee to make the buyer ‘whole’. The ticket price may 
have been marked up by the intermedi ary, or a service fee may have been added to the 
bill by the intermediary. The carrier does not control such elements, is not even aware of 
them, and cannot be called to account for them. The draft interpretative guidelines of 
(EC) Regulation 261/2004 noted as much in their footnote 68, when the commission was 
set without the knowledge of the air carrier. 
• Level playing field: 
o Finally, making the carrier the sole responsible for ensuring that a refund reaches the 
buyer/payer presents a risk of dis -incentivising the travel intermediary from playing its 
necessary role in the matter, in the knowledge that the carrier always has the  regulatory 
obligation to step in.  
o The regulation must ensure a level playing field in that both sales intermediary and carrier 
fee equally concerned in the safe delivery of a refund to the client. As such, the legislation 
should define clear and enforcea ble consequences for travel intermediaries failing to 
comply with their obligations towards the customers. 
 
Specific comments on the proposed text 
• Article 3 – Definitions 
o Paragraphs 2, 5: The revised definitions are even more confusing than the text of the  
original Directive and are bound to lead to challenges with interpretation. The definition 
of “linked travel arrangement” is particularly convoluted and confusion of what does or 
does not constitute a “linked travel arrangement” is likely to deter airline s and other 
operators from offering click -through options with the consequent detriment to 
consumers from reduced choice, reduced competition and ease of booking.  
We encourage the Commission to remove the concept of “linked travel arrangement” 
from the scope of the Directive and to focus on genuine packages, comprising a series 
of services purchased under a single contract.  
• Article 5a – Payments 
o As already noted in our general observations, IATA strongly opposes introducing 
restrictions on the practice of pre-payments.  Prepayment benefits consumers by 
enabling businesses, including both airlines and tour operators, to offer lower prices and 
wider choices in return for upfront payment. Th ese benefits of choice and value  would

be at stake if such proposals were to proceed.   Customers have the option to pay more 
for flexible tickets which they can change without penalty and can cancel with full 
reimbursement. 
o We note that the proposed text of Article 5a includes some flexibility around the  25% 
cap on downpayments, where this is necessary to cover advance payments to service 
providers. We encourage the Commission to introduce specific reference to air fares in 
this provision. Airlines must cover a significant share of total costs well in advance of the 
time of travel. If restrictions to prepayments were to apply to airlines then it is likely that 
many carriers would withdraw from participating in the package travel market, which is 
already a small share of business for many major airlines. 
• Article 12a – Vouchers 
o Paragraph 7:  IATA does not support requiring automatic refunds of unredeemed 
vouchers at end of validity period. 
o Paragraph 8: IATA does not support vouchers being transferable, at least to the extent 
that any vouchers may be specific to flights. Non -transferability is a long -standing 
industry practice in the airline industry and is supported by the pricing freedoms 
provisions as enshrined in many air services agreements.  
• Article 22 – Right of redress and refund rights of organisers 
o Paragraph 1: This provision is similar to provisions contained in Regulation (EC) 261 / 
2004. The right of redress provisions in that Regulation have been ineffective, The 
Commission should ensure that any right of redress provisions included in the revised 
Directive are workable in practice and effective in ensuring that the entity responsible is 
held accountable. 
o Paragraph 2: We propose that the following text “The 7- day period shall start on the day 
following the cancellation of the service or the  day when the service was due to be 
performed, whichever is the earlier date” should be changed to “The 7- day period shall 
start on the day following the service provider receives the request of the organizer”.  
In the event of a delay or cancellation, an  airline’s priority is to get the passenger or 
package traveler to their intended destination as soon as possible. Therefore, r efunds 
should always be seen as secondary and be triggered by a request from the traveler or 
the organizer. There is no justifica tion for treating package travel differently from flight -
only tickets.

PETA Science Consortium International e.V. welcomes the opportunity to contribute to the 
consultation ‘making best use of EU agencies to streamline scientific assessments.’ We 
support the objective stated in the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability (CSS) to ‘review 
how to use better the EU’s agencies and scientific bodies to move towards a process of 
‘one substance – one assessment’ and to provide greater transparency when prioritising 
action to deal with chemicals’ which forms the basis of this proposal. Simplification of the 
legal framework can eliminate duplication of animal testing, and greater transparenc y can 
be a driver for uptake of innovative non-animal methods and better protection of humans, 
animals, and the environment. We wish to offer suggestions on aligning this proposal with 
the goals of the CSS and the EU roadmap for eliminating animal testing in chemical safety 
assessments. 
The One Substance One Assessment (OSOA) framework should not lead to an 
increase in animal testing  
OSOA should be a tool to leverage the objectives to use data more efficiently, enhance 
information on potentially harmful chemicals and increase the use of non-animal methods. 
Therefore, any mention of chemical assessment methods should explicitly prioriti se the use 
of "non-animal" methods. We also urge caution against the potential for the OSOA 
framework to be used as a checklist exercise for animal testing. Requests for additional 
information should demonstrate that there is an actual risk (i.e. not only theoretical), that 
there are real information needs to address that risk, and that the requested data will lead 
to improved risk management measures. With Next Generation Risk Assessment (NGRA) 
paving the way to modern risk assessment methodologies, emphasis should be on using 
existing hazard and risk data on substances and similar substances and promoting the 
development and use of non-animal methods. Implementing OSOA in a box-ticking manner 
that results in new animal testing would contradict the objective to reduce and ultimately 
eliminate such testing, as outlined in the CSS and the proposal for an EU roadmap for 
phasing out animal testing in chemical safety assessments.  
Assessing substances in groups should be a tool to reduce animal testing  
To ensure that grouping effectively minimizes animal testing, as emphasised by 
authorities,1,2 it is crucial that the single criterion of belonging to a group does not in itself 
trigger more animal tests on other or all members of that group. Groups of substances 
should be constructed using transparent and scientifically sound criteria, which are 
available and communicated to the public and registrants. Ensuring sufficient 
commonalities in the group is essential, as grouping should allow for representative 
substances only to be tested, with findings applied across the group, thus fulfilling the 
objective to reduce animal testing. Importantly, refining the groups should exclusively be 
done using non-animal methods, and the use of animal testing to dispute a substance's 
group membership should be prohibited. 
However, reaching consensus on group membership or results will always be challenging, 
largely because most assessments continue to rely on the results of animal tests. These 
tests face intrinsic challenges, including low reproducibility, a weak correlation with human 
applicability, and insufficient mechanistic data to accurately predict effects in humans or 
target species. These issues contribute to complexity in decision-making and fuel 
controversy due to the resulting uncertainty. Such shortcomings highlight the urgency for 
the Commission to accelerate the transition towards more reliable, species-relevant 
methods, enhancing both the ethical and scientific value of chemical safety assessments. 
By adhering to these guidelines, the grouping of substances by ECHA can effectively 
contribute to minimising further tests on animals.

Caution Against the Separation of Hazard Assessment, Risk assessment and Risk Management  
As ECHA is tasked with assessing the intrinsic hazardous properties of substances, and EFSA with assessing 
their risk, it is likely that both agencies are sometimes tasked with assessing the same substance. Coordinating 
the exchange and acceptance of information between agencies and expert groups would accelerate the 
assessment process, improve its quality, and avoid duplication of animal testing.  
However, as noted above, generating hazard data should not become a routine check -box exercise; it must 
always be relevant and necessary for risk assessment. Emphasizing exposure data can help avoid animal testing 
in both hazard and risk assessments, without compromising safety, especially in cases where no exposure risk 
exists. Furthermore, ensuring that animal testing is only used when it is deemed to contribute significantly to risk 
management decisions requires seamless integration of risk assessment and management. Initiatives like 
COMRISK highlight the need for training and efficient communication between risk assessors and risk managers, 
ensuring that testing methods used in hazard and risk assessments contribute to risk management. 4 
Ensuring Continuity of Non-Animal Testing Expertise in Legislative Changes 
The reattribution of technical and scientific work on chemicals performed under the relevant pieces of legislation 
to European agencies should ensure that valuable expertise gathered by existing entities is preserved. In 
particular, the Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety (SCCS) has developed an advanced skillset to evaluate 
the safety of cosmetic ingredients without requiring new animal testing. It is crucial that ECHA's involvement in 
cosmetics evaluation adheres to the existing ban on animal testing and preserves the non-animal expertise 
established by the SCCS. Highlighting the shared commitment to prioritize non -animal methods among all 
agencies is crucial for a positive impact. The proposal could enhance its effectiveness by explicitly stating this  
goal, as the OSOA approach presents opportunities to phase out animal testing and actively promote the use of 
non-animal methods. 
Furthermore, the ambiguity created by the interface between the cosmetics and REACH regulations, along with 
the potential for mandating animal testing for cosmetic ingredients to safeguard workers and the environment 
could mistakenly undermine confidence in non-animal testing methods. As was demonstrated by the European 
Citizens Initiative ‘Save Cruelty Free Cosmetics,’ and multiple Parliamentary resolutions, European citizens firmly 
reject the testing of cosmetic ingredients on animals. Any reconfiguration of the SCCS must therefore ensure the 
preservation of its abilities within the EU framework, as their loss would entail a significant step back in achieving 
the CSS goal to foster innovations which move away from animal testing and ‘improve the quality, e fficiency and 
speed of chemical hazard and risk assessments.’ 
Harmonisation of the assessment framework for all chemicals should incorporate and expand the knowledge 
already used by the SCCS (e.g. non-animal approaches, NGRA), making this framework the default ‘one 
assessment’ applicable to all chemicals. The SCCS’s substance tailored approach, which allows for generation of 
hazard data, has also been invaluable at making maximum use of non-animal approaches. However, until the 
obligation to use exclusively non-animal methods for risk assessment applies to all chemicals, it is difficult to 
envisage a common assessment framework and committee for cosmetics and the rest of the chemical universe. 
Therefore, cosmetics should continue to be treated differently to other chemicals to ensure the cosmetics animal 
testing ban is upheld. 
Concluding remarks 
The ‘One Substance, One Assessment’ objective should not be at the cost of an increased burden on animal 
testing. It is a goal worth supporting, provided that an overhaul of the regulatory framework and approach to 
chemicals management incorporates a fully modernised, fit-for-purpose, animal-free test regime. Agencies and 
institutions’ expertise and abilities should reflect the rapid scientific, technological, and regulatory advances in 
hazard identification and risk assessment and allow for the immediate use of new methods when these become 
available. Any organization responsible for assessing chemicals should prioritize swiftly enhancing the skills of its 
staff in using non-animal methods. This includes ongoing training and promotion of the latest non-animal 
approaches. 
We acknowledge the Commission's work to simplify scientific assessments, enhancing accessibility for all 
involved parties. We trust our input will be considered during decision-making, ensuring alignment with the EU's 
commitments in the Chemical Strategy for Sustainability and the plan to eliminate animal testing in chemical 
safety assessments. This approach will result in more scientifically sound and ethically responsible assessment 
methods.

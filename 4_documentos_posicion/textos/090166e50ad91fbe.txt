 Stockholm, 19 Mar. 24   Comments from the Confederation of Swedish Enterprise on the European Commission's proposal for a revised European Works Council Directive  Summary   The 2009 Directive is still relevant and effective in improving European Works Councils ("EWCs"). Therefore, a revision of the EWC Directive is not justified. The proposal reduces the competitiveness of companies. In the context of competitiveness, it is important to recognize that companies have competitors operating outside the EU who are not and may not be covered by EWCs and therefore competitiveness is also reduced globally. The proposal may lead to existing operations and headquarters in Europe being established in countries outside the EU and to future investors and companies choosing countries other than EU countries for their investments.   The proposal to extend the concept of transnational is far-reaching and risks interfering with national co-determination. It has major and unpredictable consequences for companies' obligations to inform or consult the EWC. This delays companies' decision-making processes and their ability to adapt quickly, and increases the need for staff, material and organizational resources for the EWC process.    A company's ability to keep information confidential is crucial to its competitiveness and must be protected. The Commission's proposal increases the risk of disclosure of sensitive information and will delay or complicate companies' decision-making processes. Leakage of confidential information can result in financial losses for companies, lost or impaired business opportunities and damages for example for breaches of market abuse rules. It must remain the exclusive prerogative of company management to decide what information is confidential, regardless of the level of damage if it were to be disclosed.   The extended obligations for companies in informing and consulting the EWC will also delay companies' decision-making processes and increase the administrative burden. The timing of consultations should not be defined by mandatory rules but within each EWC agreement.

Background  
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise, its member organisations and member companies 
are strong advocates of social dialogue and the value of well-functioning European Works 
Councils ("EWCs"). The current EWC Directive ensures that EWCs provide a vital platform 
for transnational discussions between employers and employee representatives from all 
Member States in which they operate. The 2009 Directive remains relevant and effective in 
improving EWCs. Therefore, a revision of the EWC Directive is not justified. 
 
The European Commission's proposal for a revised EWC Directive deepens the challenges 
faced by European companies and reduces their competitiveness. The European 
Commission has declared its explicit focus on the competitiveness of European companies 
and reducing the regulatory burden at EU level. It is important that the European 
Commission continues with this strategy also in the area of labour market policy and refrain 
from proposals that actually severely damage the competitiveness. Competitiveness must 
also be seen in relation to other global players. These are often not covered by the EWC. 
European companies are thus losing competitiveness in relation to them.  
 
EWCs are of particular importance to Swedish business as the number of EWCs in the EU 
governed by Swedish law is the third largest after Germany and France. Many large 
corporate groups operating in Europe have chosen to establish or maintain their 
headquarters in Sweden. The contribution of these groups to Swedish society and the 
Swedish economy is important. They generate tax revenues, create highly qualified jobs and 
contribute to an ecosystem of large corporate groups that makes it attractive to invest and 
establish companies in Sweden. 
 
In an era characterised by unforeseen economic and geopolitical challenges, Swedish and 
European companies are struggling with losses in competitiveness. At the same time, the 
business community is concerned about the EU's development in business and labour 
3 (9) 
market policy. In recent years, there have been many initiatives involving increased central 
control of the European economy, increased detailed regulation in EU legislation, 
disproportionate reporting requirements for companies and a political will to establish a 
European labour market model. This is not a development that favours an innovative and 
trade-dependent country like Sweden. 
 
Employee involvement through EWCs goes to the heart of entrepreneurship: the freedom of 
enterprise and free corporate governance. Company owners, through the general meeting, 
the board of directors and the managing director, control the company's business direction 
and day-to-day operations. This is a cornerstone of free enterprise and a prerequisite for a 
free market economy. When investors and companies choose where to invest their 
resources or establish their operations, the ability to control the business without hindrance 
in the decision-making process is a very important parameter in the decision-making 
process. Another important factor is the cost of the regulatory burden. The European 
Commission's proposal for a revised EWC Directive imposes such limitations on the ability to 
manage operations effectively and rationally and such increased costs that it creates 
incentives to move existing operations to countries outside the EU. It also creates incentives 
to choose to establish new operations outside the EU instead of within the EU. If important 
decisions about the activities of companies are made more difficult, this has negative 
consequences for the competitiveness of companies and their employees.   
 
If European legislators do proceed with a revision of the Directive, the approach must be 
balanced, proportionate and pragmatic. Measures should be proposed to reduce the 
administrative and financial burden on businesses. Given the political landscape at EU level, 
a potential revision could lead to a deterioration of the regulatory framework that 
fundamentally changes the conditions for businesses to operate in the EU.  
 
Extended scope prevents efficient decision-making and increases costs 
The changes proposed by the European Commission to the concept of transnational imply a 
substantial extension of the scope of the Directive (Article 1(4)). The concept of transnational 
constitutes the basis for the EWC's competence, i.e. in which situation a company should 
initiate information and consultation with the EWC. The Commission's proposal means that 
instead, as a minimum, it will only be required that an issue is reasonably expected to affect 
employees in one Member State and that this issue is also reasonably expected to affect 
employees in another Member State. 1 
 
On paper, the changes may not appear to be far-reaching, but in practice this means that 
issues which are essentially strictly national may be regarded as transnational. This is 
because most company decisions in international groups are likely to have some, usually 
only marginal, impact on workers in a country other than those more directly affected by the 
decision. Issues that in practice can only reasonably be expected to affect one Member

State may need to be dealt with in the EWC because the measure could reasonably have 
consequences in another country. Many issues that currently fall outside the directive may 
therefore risk being covered by its provisions. This is despite the fact that the stated purpose 
of the EWC is to deal with transnational issues. It will often not be possible for companies to

effectiveness and efficient decision-making. In practice, the Directive will no longer be limited 
to issues that are relevantly transnational in nature.  
 
This represents a significant extension of companies' obligations with unforeseeable 
consequences for companies' obligations to inform or consult the EWC. In practice, 
companies will have to review the group's decision-making processes, as not only decisions 
taken by management are likely to be subject to information and consultation of the EWC. It 
will require considerable effort to ensure that the entire structure of the group, both at lower 
level and in national management bodies, which in practice only decide on issues of a 
national nature, is organised in such a way that the EWC rules can be complied with. In turn, 
this will delay companies' decision-making processes and their ability to adapt quickly, 
including, as mentioned, on issues which, in relevant parts, only affect employees in one 
Member State. In addition to preventing effective decision-making, companies will face an 
increased administrative burden and cost increases in managing the EWC processes. The 
proposal is not balanced. 
 
Several of the Swedish companies with EWCs testify that the proposed extended definition 
will require increased human and material resources and also the establishment of new 
resource-intensive organisations for the EWC processes. This is despite the fact that many 
of the issues that will be covered by this extension are of extremely limited importance to the 
group as a whole and sometimes even of a purely national nature.  
 
The amendment also entails a significant risk that information and consultation with the EWC 
may overlap with the issues dealt with in the context of national co-determination and also an 
interference in national co-determination. National issues that are best dealt with by the 
company together with the employee representatives in the Member State in question are 
taken away from the national employee representatives even though they are best placed to 
deal with the issue. The proposed wording can therefore be seen as an interference with 
national co-determination and thus a regulation of the same, which would require unanimity 
by the Council under Article 153(1)(f) TFEU. Furthermore, it violates the principle of 
subsidiarity, as issues of employee involvement that are not of a transnational nature should 
not be regulated at EU level but by national provisions on information and consultation 
procedures.  
 
Confidential information must be protected  
The ability of companies to keep information confidential is crucial for competitiveness and 
must therefore be protected. The proposal reintroduces the possibility for Member States to 
impose interim or other judicial authorisation of non-disclosure of confidential information by 
undertakings (Article 8(a)). Such a procedure increases the risk of dissemination of sensitive 
information, delays the decision-making process of undertakings and thus impairs the 
competitiveness of undertakings. The provision should therefore be deleted. It must be an 
exclusive competence of the management to decide which information is confidential, 
regardless of the level of damage if it were to be disclosed. 
 
5 (9) 
In addition, an obligation to provide information is introduced for the company on the reasons 
why the company does not disclose sensitive information (according to Article 8(a)(2)). The 
fact that the company would have to justify a decision not to disclose certain information will 
in itself entail a risk that outsiders or unauthorised persons understand the nature of the 
sensitive issue at stake and the information involved, such as acquisitions, sales of business 
and outsourcing. This could have far-reaching consequences for the company's compliance 
with rules in many other areas, such as the regulation of market-sensitive information for 
listed companies along with other rules related to acquisitions, sales and outsourcing. 
Relaxing confidentiality rules will make it more difficult for companies to comply with these 
requirements, creating legal uncertainty and the risk of legal action and claims for damages 
for alleged breaches of such rules. However, this not only poses legal and financial risks, but 
can also mean a risk of lost business opportunities or investments, as negotiations with third 
parties usually require strict confidentiality. Companies must always be careful to avoid leaks 
for both legal and commercial reasons. Leakage of information can make negotiations more 
difficult, weaken the negotiating position and thus lead to a worse deal, or even kill the deal 
or lead to sanctions under market abuse rules. It is important to be aware that market abuse 
rules exist both in the EU and in other countries, which makes the handling of confidential 
information even more complex. Many companies with EWCs are listed on several markets 
and thus have to take into account regulations in several countries both within the EU and 
outside the EU. 
 
Extended information and consultation obligations slow down decision-
making processes 
The extended obligations for companies to inform and consult the EWC risk delaying 
companies' decision-making processes and increasing the administrative burden (new 
Article 9). The provisions mean that the EWC will now also be able to express its opinion 
before the measure is implemented. Also, before the implementation of the measure, a new 
requirement is introduced for the management to respond in writing to any opinions 
expressed by the EWC. The increased requirement for the content of the information also 
entails a risk of leakage of confidential information. 
 
It is unnecessarily administratively burdening to introduce formal written requirements for 
management feedback to the EWC's views on the planned measure. The rule will not lead to 
improved information and consultation between management and the EWC. It should 
continue to be up to the management and the EWC to determine the form of communication 
between them.  
 
In this context, it is worth pointing out that the Swedish Co-determination Act, which is often 
highlighted as a model for employee participation, provides considerable scope for the local 
parties to design the detailed forms of co-operation. The same starting point should also 
apply to information and consultation between company management and the EWC. 
 
The timing of consultation should not be defined by mandatory rules but within each EWC 
agreement, considering that the opinion of the company management should be delivered to 
the EWC within a reasonable time. The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise also 
emphasises the risk that the proposed change may lead to the EWC consultation having to 
be concluded before national consultations are completed. It should therefore be clarified 
that EWC consultations are carried out with full respect for the national information and 
consultation procedures according to national legislation and practice. If the provision is 
nevertheless introduced, it should at least be clarified that the management's written 
6 (9) 
response should reach the EWC before the decision is implemented in the organisation - not 
before the decision is taken. 
 
Extended cost obligation is not reasonable   
The proposal unreasonably extends the company's cost obligation (Article 5(6) and point 5 of 
the Annex). The company shall cover the reasonable costs of the negotiating team for 
experts, including legal assistance, to the extent necessary for the negotiations on the 
establishment of the EWC. Similarly, the company shall cover the reasonable costs of legal 
representation and participation in administrative or judicial proceedings. The costs need 
only be notified to the central management before they are incurred (as per Article 5(6)). A 
similar cost obligation is introduced for EWCs that are subject to the provisions on non-
agreement of EWCs (according to point 5 of the Annex). 
 
The proposed wording regarding experts and legal costs imposes disproportionate cost 
obligations on companies and needs to be clarified. The possibility of obtaining the 
assistance of an expert is already recognised in the existing Directive and therefore no 
changes are required. If additional experts should be available at the expense of the 
company, it should be up to the management to decide on the mandate of the expert and a 
reasonable level of costs. 
 
The obligation to pay for legal representation and participation in administrative or judicial 
proceedings is in any case unacceptable. Such a right could mean, for example, that the 
company must pay in advance the legal costs of the negotiating team in a potential dispute 
against the company. This further creates an incentive to litigate and an abuse of that right 
as a mean to oppose a planned measure. These costs should instead, as today, be 
allocated according to the national rules on the allocation of legal costs. If cost liability is 
introduced for legal costs other than litigation against the company, these should be subject 
to management approval. 
 
Moreover, it is clarified that the necessary training costs for both the negotiating team and 
the EWC shall be borne by the company (as per Article 10(4), paragraph 2). While we share 
the view that training may be necessary for both the negotiating delegation and EWC 
members, given that the costs of training may be significant for the company, a requirement 
for prior authorisation by the company should be introduced also for these costs. This should 
in particular take into account the scope and format of such training, as well as costs related 
to travel, accommodation, interpretation and translation. 
 
Important to enable virtual meetings and not to regulate the content of EWC 
agreements  
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprises welcomes the addition of "format" in Article 6 
when referring to meetings, as such inclusion allows for the use of virtual meetings when 
conducting information and consultation with the EWC. However, it should also be clarified 
that negotiation meetings with the employees' negotiating delegation can be conducted 
virtually in order to alleviate companies' costs for setting up the EWC. The same applies to 
EWC meetings established under the Subsidiary Requirements in the Annex to the EWC 
Directive such cost reductions should be considered when the proposal otherwise only 
contains extended obligations that increase companies' negotiation costs. In addition, many 
of the travel and meeting policies in groups of companies with establishments in different 
countries state that meetings with participants from several countries should take place 
7 (9) 
virtually. Often that is an important element in the sustainability strategy of the companies. It 
is reasonable for the regulatory framework to reflect this reality.  
 
In addition, a list of costs and resources to be covered by the EWC agreement is introduced, 
such as the use of experts and training (as per Article 6(2)(f)). This list is unnecessarily 
detailed and reduces the parties' ability to control the content of the EWC agreement. It also 
risks increasing the risk of renegotiating existing well-functioning EWC agreements and thus 
disrupting functioning information and consultation procedures. The provision should 
therefore be deleted. 
 
Removing the protection for existing EWC agreements damages existing well-
functioning solutions 
The current protection for existing EWC agreements (formerly Article 14) is removed, which 
may harm well-functioning existing information and consultation arrangements. There are 
currently around 350 agreements concluded within the two existing categories under Article 
14 of the revised European Works Council Directive (EWC) 2009/38/EC. These agreements, 
the vast majority of which have proved satisfactory to both parties for over 30 years, often 
use the core definitions of the existing Directive and are based on well-established social 
dialogue practices at company level.  
 
Enabling one side, in this case the labour representatives, to unilaterally challenge existing 
voluntary agreements is a biased drive that goes against existing practices. This risk 
damaging trust between the two sides and affecting the quality of consultations in the EWC. 
At the same time, the focus of the workers' representatives in the EWC shifts for a significant 
period from the consultation and information purpose to the long process of creating a new 
EWC agreement. 
 
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise therefore considers that the existing exemptions 
should be maintained. 
 
If the EU-legislators decide to remove the protection of exempted agreements, it should not 
be sufficient to have only a request from the negotiating delegation under Article 5, but it 
should also be required that the request is supported by a two-thirds majority of the existing 
information and consultation body. Renegotiation should preferably also be done with the 
existing information and consultation body and not a newly established negotiating 
delegation. 
 
Transitional rules  
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise supports the proposal that the Directive's new 
requirements, for example on the content of the EWC agreement (under Article 14(a)), 
should not automatically apply to existing EWC agreements concluded under the regulatory 
framework. However, the new Article 14(a) provides a possibility to renegotiate the EWC 
agreement at the request of 100 workers in two Member States. This applies to all EWC 
agreements concluded since the entry into force of the original Directive (94/45/EC). 
However, the rules on the validity or renegotiation of the existing agreement must be 
respected.  
 
However, the Confederation of Swedish Enterprise considers that a requirement should be 
added to the transitional provision that the request for renegotiation must be supported by a 
two-thirds majority of the existing EWC. Renegotiations should preferably also take place 
8 (9) 
with the existing information and consultation body and not a newly established negotiating 
delegation. 
 
Sanctions and legal proceedings  
The Commission proposal requires Member States to ensure that effective, dissuasive and 
proportionate sanctions (Article 11, paragraph 2 (b)) can be imposed for breaches of the 
rights and obligations set out in the Directive. The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise 
welcomes the fact that the Commission's proposal respects the power of Member States, in 
accordance with Article 153 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU), 
to decide on the exact level of sanctions to be applied. However, financial sanctions should 
be limited to cases where the abuse of the information and consultation process has been 
intentional. 
 
However, the Confederation of Swedish Enterprise opposes the Commission's proposal that 
alternative dispute resolution mechanisms may not prevent a matter from being referred to a 
court or tribunal. Many of the EWC agreements of the Confederation's members contain 
standard arbitration clauses which state that any disputes shall be settled by an arbitrator or 
an arbitration court. It is important that the outcome of an arbitration remains binding on the 
parties and that valid arbitration clauses constitute a bar to any action in the public courts. 
The provision could potentially also have consequences for the applicability of the social 
partners' collective bargaining arrangements, which in many cases are applied to EWC-
related disputes governed by Swedish law. In cases where an EWC-related dispute is 
covered by a bargaining order, the bargaining order must be complied with, i.e. local and 
central bargaining completed, before a general court action is brought. The parties' 
negotiated settlement of the dispute should continue to constitute a procedural bar to 
proceedings before the general court. Thus, the provision needs to be clarified to the effect 
that it does not prevent the application of the arbitration clauses of the EWC Agreement or 
collective bargaining arrangements. 
 
Gender equality in negotiating delegations and EWCs  
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise shares the ambition that both the negotiating 
delegation (new Article 5.2a) and the EWC should strive for a gender-equal composition. In 
the case of the EWC, this is concretised by stating that when negotiating EWC agreements, 
the necessary arrangements for gender equality must be established (Article 6(2a)). In both 
contexts, however, it should be clarified that it cannot be the company's obligation to ensure 
that gender equality is achieved, as it is the employees who appoint members to the 
negotiating delegation and the EWC. 
 
The report of the European Parliament with recommendations to the European 
Commission on the revision of the European Works Council Directive. 
The European Parliament adopted in February 2023 a report with recommendations to the 
European Commission on a revised EWC Directive (2019/2183(INL)). Given that this report 
is expected to influence the position of the European Parliament in the further legislative 
process on a revised EWC Directive, the Confederation of Swedish Enterprise wishes to 
make specific comments. 
 
The European Parliament's 2023 proposal includes significant extensions of the EWC's 
powers, which would effectively transform it into a co-determination body with potentially 
significant consequences for corporate governance and decision-making processes. Of 
particular concern is the proposal to allow the EWC to postpone or block company decisions 
9 (9) 
through legal proceedings in the event of non-compliance with the regulatory framework, a 
measure that poses a far-reaching threat to the competitiveness of European companies and 
their ability to make quick and effective decisions. It is important to be aware of the 
incentives thus created to stop a measure opposed by the EWC by invoking, without 
justification, non-compliance with the acquis.  
 
For the same reasons as mentioned above, the proposals to limit the company's ability to 
impose confidentiality obligations on employee representatives regarding sensitive 
information should be left without action. The same applies to the proposal that a company 
decision on professional secrecy should be subject to appeal by the EWC and employee 
representatives. The legislator should therefore avoid introducing provisions that increase 
the risk of disclosing confidential information or delay decision-making, for example by 
requiring judicial or administrative authorisation before withholding or sharing information 
with external parties. 
 
The European Parliament's report also included provisions for the EWC to submit comments 
before the company finalises the national consultation. This constitutes a serious 
interference and regulation of national co-determination, which also requires unanimity in the 
Council under Article 153(1)(f) TFEU. 
 
The proposal for GDPR-sized sanctions must also be rejected, as they would be manifestly 
disproportionate and incompatible with the underlying idea of information and consultation 
that needs to be based on trust. Moreover, the legal basis of the Directive, based on Article 
153 TFEU, does not give the EU competence to determine the exact level of sanctions to be 
applied. This should remain a matter for Member States to decide, in accordance with their 
national laws and practices.

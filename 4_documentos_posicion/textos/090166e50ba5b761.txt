 
 
 
 
Perspectives on Select Initiatives in the 
European Economic Security Strategy 
 
17 April 2024 
 
ITI - the Information Technology Industry Council welcomes the opportunity to provide its 
perspectives on the Economic Security Strategy package adopted by the European Commission on 
24 January 2024 . This paper  addresses the package’s initiatives on outbound investment, export 
controls, inbound investment, and dual-use research and development. 
 
ITI is the premier global advocate for technology, representing the world’s most innovative 
companies. We promote public policies and industry standards that advance competition and 
innovation worldwide. Most of ITI’s members service the global market via complex supply chains in 
which technology is developed, made, and assembled in multiple countries, and service customers 
across all levels of government and the full range of global industry sectors, such as financial services, 
healthcare, and energy. ITI’s observati ons and recommendations come from our vision for secure 
and resilient supply chains in this dynamic and global ecosystem. 
 
Observations on the recommendation to monitor outbound investments for further risk 
assessment 
 
As ITI’s members service the global market, we are acutely aware of the importance of securing global 
supply chains as not only a key imperative for companies and customers alike, but also as critical to 
achieving security objectives.  
 
ITI appreciates the methodical approach in the Commission’s White Paper regarding potential 
regulation or legislation related to outbound investment transactions. Recognizing this consultation 
is a first opportunity, ITI encourages the Commission to conduc t regular formal and informal 
stakeholder engagement during its consideration and potential development and implementation of 
policy. Such engagement will help ensure that any resulting policies meet the Commission’s 
objectives while minimizing the likelih ood of unintended consequences on EU competitiveness and 
resilience.  
 
Over the past few years, ITI has engaged extensively with outbound investment policy discussions. 
We put forward several principles and points that we have developed over the years to guide the 
Commission’s consideration of whether and how to further regul ate outbound transactions, 
investments, and transfers: 
 
• Identify specific national security risks and narrowly target any consideration of rules to 
address those specific risks. 
• Coordinate closely with trusted allies, including through standing forums such as the EU-U.S. 
Trade and Technology Council (TTC), to enhance the effectiveness of any resulting framework 
and ensure that EU companies are not placed in a worse position than companies in foreign 
markets and to ensure the effectiveness of any resulting framework.

Observations on the White Paper on Dual-Use Export Controls 
 
As the Commission explores how to modernize the EU’s export control framework, ITI emphasizes 
the importance of ensuring that any proposed measures are targeted and tailored to the security 
risks being addressed, agreed to at the multilateral level, and do not undermine the EU’s leadership 
on innovative technologies. Further, any proposed export controls must be constructed practically 
enough to be implemented by small, medium, and large businesses with varying levels of compliance 
infrastructure. ITI also urges the Commission to put a greater focus on enforcement and consider 
devoting more resources to the administration and enforcement of export controls. Incorporating 
enforcement early in policymaking and better resourcing enforcement post -implementation would 
enable the Commission and Member States to better target higher risk activities, facilitate 
transactions by rule -abiding entities, and increase the likelihood that export controls meet the 
intended objective. 
 
Multilateral controls are the most effective approach and should be the preferred avenue for 
advancing any new controls. ITI shares the Commission’s concerns about the potential for unilateral 
controls to undermine global value chains  and emphasizes that such controls are less effective and 
even harm the ability of companies to participate in the global marketplace. This risk is especially 
pronounced for technologies that may be widely commercially available, which is why consideration 
of new or amended regimes must involve a thorough, ongoing evaluation of foreign availability and 
capability. In cases where technology of comparable quality, quantity and cost is available outside of 
the EU, controls with limited buy -in will be ineffective in preventing end u sers of concern from 
acquiring the controlled technology and result in limiting companies’ global competitiveness and 
their ability to lead in the development of core technologies. That gap will then be filled by companies 
that do not have presence in the EU. To support the prioritization of multilateral controls, ITI 
encourages both the Commission and Member States to dedicate more resources to preparing, 
coordinating views, and engaging with multilateral export control regimes. 
 
In instances where the Wassenaar Agreement (WA) or other multilateral processes may not be 
progressing as quickly as the Commission and Member States would like, taking a plurilateral 
approach may be appropriate in terms of achieving interim outcomes that could then inform broader 
multilateral efforts. While multilateral controls remain the most effective approach, a plurilateral 
approach is more effective than a unilateral control, and we encourage the EU to include like-minded

allies and partners – such as Japan, Korea, Taiwan, the United Kingdom and the United States – in 
any such endeavours.  
 
Given that unilateral controls are less effective than multilateral or plurilateral controls, ITI would 
support the Commission’s consideration of developing a mechanism for better coordination of 
National Control Lists. There has been an increase in nation al controls across the EU, and better 
coordination during development and implementation of such controls would ease the compliance 
burden for companies and authorities. For example, several EU Member States are moving forward 
with implementing new export control regimes for quantum technologies. It will be critical to ensure 
coordination between Member States to align these regimes as much as possible, as well as with key 
international allies. The establishment of a common licensing regime for quantum betw een trusted 
allies would increase legal certainty and foster greater allied technology cooperation, supporting 
European innovation and competitiveness. 
 
ITI encourages the Commission to consider exploring with trusted partners a common approach to 
implementation of multilateral controls, both in terms of timing and substance. Even if governments 
are basing policy off the same multilaterally developed contr ols, inconsistent implementation can 
lead to compliance challenges for companies complying with rules across jurisdictions. A common 
approach should also extend to the decontrol of items to ensure a smooth and effective transition 
for companies and enforcement officials. For example, there should be alignment for interpretation 
and implementation of WA dual-use control list decontrols of categories 5 part 1 and part 2. Different 
interpretations of Cryptography note 3 create complexity and significant gaps i n classification, with 
the effect of putting certain exporters at a competitive disadvantage. In addition, while items eligible 
for Cryptography note 3 or 5A002.a note 2 are considered to be non -listed in the EU, they have 
dedicated control entries in the U.S. Export Administration Regulations (EAR) Commerce Control list 
under ECCNs 5x992 and other AT -only controls and remain a burden for many exporters and re -
exporters. 
 
ITI supports the White Paper’s proposal to set up a forum for political coordination on export controls 
and would also encourage providing for regular, open and structured engagement with industry as 
part of any forum. Efforts by the Commission and the U.S . Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) in 
TTC Working Group 7 continue to demonstrate the value in political coordination related to export 
controls, which can be useful among Member States as well as with trusted partners. 
 
Observations on the Proposal for a Regulation on the screening of foreign investments in the Union 
and repealing Regulation (EU) 2019/452 
 
The Proposal for a Regulation on the screening of foreign investments in the Union (Regulation) is a 
welcome effort towards improving investment screening procedures in the EU. At present, Member 
States’ investment screening regimes vary significantly on substantive, procedural and jurisdictional 
grounds, and this has created disproportionate costs and burdens for foreign investors and their EU 
partners. In particular, the inconsistent filing thresholds, review timelines and remedial actions 
across Member S tates have yielded a patchwork of regulatory obligations that undermines the 
coherence of the Single Market and creates uncertainties for investors. The inability for investors to 
submit their views to screening authorities or seek judicial recourse in cer tain Member States also 
raises serious due process concerns.

The proposed Regulation is an opportunity to harmonise key investment screening rules across the 
EU, establish robust due process safeguards for investors, and ensure screening procedures focus on 
high-risk transactions, thereby enhancing the EU’s attracti veness as a destination for foreign 
investment. ITI also welcomes the Regulation’s recognition of the “need to maintain an open and 
welcoming regime for such investment into the EU” and  reiterates the importance of addressing 
potential risks related to security and public order stemming from certain types of investments in a 
targeted and proportionate manner . However , we believe that some of the provisions in the 
Commission’s proposal would actually increase administrative burdens. 
 
For example, we are concerned that, by requiring foreign investors to seek a regulatory authorization 
before investing in an EU company that is “economically active” in one of the areas listed in Annex II, 
the Regulation would significantly expand the numb er of transactions that may be subject to 
screening. In particular, by extending this authorisation requirement to all investments targeting 42 
broad “critical technology areas” that have not been clearly defined – and which include several 
widely available technologies (e.g., Artificial Intelligence, robotics, cloud, etc.) – the Commission risks 
extending national screening regimes to low-risk transactions and exacerbating divergences between 
Member States. This would raise costs for investors and potentially delay investments that are crucial 
for the EU’s competitiveness.  
 
In order to avoid this outcome, the Commission should narrow Annex II to better focus on high -risk 
transactions that raise genuine security concerns. As currently drafted, the vaguely defined list of 
critical technology areas does not provide Member States  with a sufficiently clear and risk -based 
framework to develop effective investment screening rules. Therefore, ITI recommends the use of 
alternative criteria that are well understood and grounded in existing EU law. Alternatively, and in 
line with the inv estment screening regime in the United States, the Commission’s list of critical 
technology areas could also be bundled with the existing export control reference in Annex II (i.e., 
national authorities would only be required to screen investments targetin g critical technology 
providers subject to dual-use export controls).  
 
In addition, the Commission should also define the concept of “economically active” in Article 4(4)(b) 
to avoid impacting investments where the targets are merely users (rather than developers) of the 
listed technologies. Similarly, the term “foreign inves tments” should be clarified to avoid screening 
transactions where the investor would hold only a minority non -controlling position in the targeted 
company. 
 
ITI also recommends that the Commission encourages Member States to provide for an increase in 
resources that is commensurate with the increase in screening activity. This would help ensure that 
screenings procedures are timely and do not inadvertently deter investments in the Single Market. 
 
To create further certainty for investors, Member States should also be required to introduce robust 
due process safeguards, including providing investors with sufficient time to share their views with 
screening authorities. While the provisions in Article  4(3) and 14(1) of the Commission’s proposal 
would enhance investors’ procedural rights, they do not go far enough to encourage Member States 
to make meaningful improvements to their screening procedures.

Observations on the White Paper on enhancing support for research and development involving 
technologies with dual-use potential 
 
ITI commends the European Commission for its commitment to promoting research and 
development (R&D) opportunities for dual -use technologies and endeavoring to support successful 
commercialisation. As the Commission evaluates different options to enhance re levant R&D, ITI 
underscores the importance of ensuring that EU-funded opportunities are available to all companies 
invested in the EU. Such openness will ensure researchers can make the most of collaboration with 
colleagues from around the world, and benef it EU competitiveness and resilience. Relatedly, better 
alignment with trusted partners will also foster stronger trade, investment, and R&D flows that 
involve EU companies and operations.

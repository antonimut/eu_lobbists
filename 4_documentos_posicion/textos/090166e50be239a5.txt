Introduction 
In the past couple of years, the EU's foreign direct investment (FDI) screening framework has played a crucial 
role in upholding security and public order objectives while also maintaining a EU economy that is open  to 
foreign investment. 
The FDI screening regulation has become an even more  vital instrument to safeguard the EU’s economic 
security strategy vis-à-vis new challenges in a changing geopolitical and geoeconomic landscape. In particular, 
foreign investment in sensitive areas of the EU-wide economy is increasing in terms of volume and criticality. 
Energy infrastructure is one such area. 
The EU has ambitious goals  to massively expand renewables and strengthen Europe’s clean energy supply 
chains. The EU wants 35% of Europe’s electricity consumption to be produced from wind energy by 2030, up 
from 19% today. Meeting these goals requires building 33 GW of new wind farms every year. Currently, there 
are over 250 factories around Europe making turbines and components, and over 300,000 jobs across the 
wind supply chain. However, Europe needs to accelerate investments in new factories as part of the scaling of 
wind energy across Europe.  
It is thus important, that the revised FDI screening mechanism be fully aligned with the objectives of the EU 
Green Deal Industrial Plan to ensure a secure energy transformation .  Further, the revised regulation  must 
support the strengthening and expansion of the critical supply chains of the five key green and digital 
technologies outlined in the EU Net Zero Industry Act, including wind energy. FDI screening should apply to 
inbound investments related to critical infrastructure projects so as the European clean energy sector scales, 
Europe is powered by a grid that reflects its national security interests.   
The European wind energy industry plays a crucial role in advancing sustainable energy solutions and 
supporting the EU's climate objectives.  
In this regard, WindEurope wishes to comment on following main changes proposed by the Commission  to 
revise the existing Foreign Direct Investment screening mechanism framework: 
 
1. Requiring All Member States to Establish an FDI Screening Mechanism: 
We commend the European Commission's proposal to mandate all Member States to establish FDI screening 
mechanisms. This move will help address existing gaps in the screening of risky transactions and ensure a 
consistent approach to protecting security and public order across the EU. 
While recognizing the importance of national sovereignty in decision -making, we believe that a harmonized 
approach to FDI screening is essential to prevent regulatory arbitrage and promote a cohesive internal market. 
The requirement for Member States to establish FDI screening systems with an implementation deadline will 
contribute to greater transparency and legal certainty for investors, ultimately fostering a more secure and 
competitive investment environment.

2. Minimum Sectoral Scope and Net-Zero Technologies Inclusion: 
WindEurope supports the definition of  a minimum sectoral scope that mandates screening by all Member 
States, while also granting Member States the flexibility to expand beyond this minimum scope based on their 
individual national security priorities.  
The inclusion of critical sectors that require heightened attention and scrutiny  in a minimum sectoral scope 
will serve as a foundational framework for consistent screening in strategic sectors more vulnerable to foreign 
control. Currently, some Member States have adopted FDI regulations that carve out projects below a 
minimum [output] threshold.  However, all renewable energy projects connected to a power grid can carry 
risk regardless of output.  Thus, by explicitly addressing net -zero technologies in point 3h) of Annex II., the 
regulation emphasizes the need to safeguard these critical assets from potential threats that could undermine 
the Union's climate, energy security and national security objectives.   
 
3. Harmonizing Key Elements of National FDI Screening Mechanisms: 
We support the Commission's efforts to harmonize key elements of national FDI screening mechanisms, 
including the scope of transactions subject to screening, procedural features, and assessment criteria , 
including a general exemption for failure of the investment to be in the national security interest of the EU . 
By aligning these aspects, the EU can enhance the effectiveness and efficiency of the screening process, 
reducing disparities among Member States and promoting a more coherent regulatory framework. 
The inclusion of specific criteria for identifying critical infrastructure and technologies in Annexes I and II will 
provide clarity and guidance to national screening authorities. This will help streamline decision -making 
processes and ensure a consistent approach to assessing potential risks to security and public order.  
 
4. Improving the Cooperation Mechanism: 
Enhancing cooperation between Member States and the Commission is essential for addressing cross-border 
risks associated with foreign investments. The Commission’s proposal would  strengthen the cooperation 
mechanism on cases presenting the highest risks to public order and national security, ensuring a more 
targeted and effective response to potential threats. 
The common minimum scope of transactions for notification, as outlined by the Commission, will promote 
greater transparency and information sharing among Member States, facilitating coordinated risk 
assessments and decision-making. By increasing accountability and dialogue among stakeholders, the revised 
cooperation mechanism will enhance the EU's ability to address emerging challenges and safeguard strategic 
assets and technologies. 
 
5. Ensuring investors with “control” over critical infrastructure are screened 
With respect to critical infrastructure, the entity subject to screening pursuant to the current draft regulation 
does not necessarily correlate to whether the investment agreement and delivery may pose a security risk to 
the EU . Under the pro posed text, the developer of a critical infrastructure project  would be subject to 
screening if they were foreign, however, a foreign supplier of the main components (such as a finished wind

turbine) would not.  The scope of s creening should be expanded to include delivery of main components  
where the main components of a critical infrastructure project can give a foreign entity direct access to 
influence security of supply (e.g. via remote management of supply assets via supplier software).  One Member 
State has already adopted this approach by including “executive agreements” into the scope of screening.  
“Executive agreements”, such as supply agreements, should be in scope, when they concern supply of critical 
components that may impact the security of a critical infrastructure project. Including "executive agreements” 
for main components of critical infrastructure  that shift control or significant influence of the energy source 
to the supplier under the scope of the Regulation will enable the screening process to meet the purpose of  
the regulation – ensure security and public order across the EU.   
 
6. Incorporating Greenfield Foreign Investments 
The proposed text extends the scope of the mechanism to greenfield foreign investments. The latter, which 
involve the establishment of new facilities or undertakings by foreign investors in the EU, can have significant 
implications for national security and public order. By subjecting such investments to FDI screening, Member 
States can proactively assess and mitigate potential risks associated with these projects. This aligns with the 
emerging trend in some Member States, such as Germany, to scrutinize greenfield investments more closely. 
Ensuring that greenfield investments are covered under the Regulation will contribute to a more 
comprehensive and effective FDI screening framework within the EU. 
 
6. Enhancing Coordination for Multi-Country Transactions 
Given the interconnected nature of the EU internal market, multi -country transactions present unique 
challenges that require a coordinated and efficient approach to FDI screening. The proposal to establish special 
rules for such transactions, including requiring parties to file notifications in all Member States concerned on 
the same day, is a step in the right direction. Enhancing coordination among Member States through a more 
stringent and synchronized system for exchanging information will facilitate mo re effective assessment of 
multi-jurisdictional transactions. This will help prevent regulatory arbitrage and ensure that FDI clearance 
decisions are made in a timely and informed manner. 
In conclusion, we believe that the  above-mentioned proposed revisions to the  existing EU FDI screening 
mechanism are critical for promoting a secure and competitive investment environment for the European 
wind energy industry players. By requiring all Member States to establish screening mechanisms, harmonizing 
key elements of national frameworks, and improving cooperation mechanisms, the EU can strengthen its 
resilience against external threats and safeguard critical assets and technologies. 
WindEurope calls on the European Commission to support the co -legislators towards embracing these 
improvements to the FDI screening mechanism framework.

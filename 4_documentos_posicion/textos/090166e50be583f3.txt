2024-04-29  Proposal for a Regulation on the screening of foreign direct investments in the European Union  The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise is the main business association in Sweden bringing together 60,000 companies and 48 industry and employer organisations. The Confederation generally welcomes the European Commission’s proposal for a Regulation on the screening of foreign investments in the Union, published on 24 January 2024, and concurs with the comments and views expressed by BusinessEurope to this proposal. We would in particular like to underline that “the review of the Regulation should aim to reduce national notification cases that are non-essential, reduce the number of unnecessary reviews by the European Commission and other Member States under the cooperation mechanism, and limit increased reporting for EU companies and foreign investors, while protecting confidential information.” In addition, we would like to make the following comments.  Inward foreign direct investment (FDI) screening in areas vital for national security is important and has been, or is being, put in place in most Member States. At the same time, FDI is essential for a strong economy which in turn is fundamental for our long-term security. In the continuing race for global investment, it is important that the European Union comes out ahead and upholds and develop an attractive investment climate. Consequently, the scope of the screening must not go further than what is strictly necessary for reasons of national security and its implementation should aim to minimize the costs to affected businesses and authorities.  In Sweden a national FDI screening entered into force on 1 December 2023. It sets a wide scope, i.a. by including a “catch all” category of activities vital to the functioning of society (samhällsviktig). Furthermore, notifications are obligatory for all investments falling within the scope regardless of nationality, the thresholds for control are low (the lowest starts at 10 %) and there are in principle no thresholds for value. Although it is too early for a comprehensive assessment, a few observations with relevance to the proposed EU regulation can be made.  Keeping the scope limited to what can reasonably be considered a serious security threat along with clear definitions of activities concerned is key to avoid excessive notifications of unproblematic investments with resulting unnecessary costs for affected businesses and regulatory authorities alike. Consequently, in particular Annex 2 of the proposed regulation should be limited to areas where foreign control generally would cause serious damage and these areas should be defined so as to avoid the possibility of wide interpretations.

 From a security perspective, there is a distinct difference between pure financial 
investments and investments to gain control. This should be reflected when defining 
the areas requiring prior authorizations and setting thresholds for notifications. 
 Time is often critical for an investment and financing models in many industries are 
such that delays of a few weeks or days (in some cases even hours) to get prior 
authorization may hinder a desirable investment. Apart from limiting the 
scope/introducing relevant thresholds mentioned above, this underlines the 
importance of safeguarding the speedy handling of notifications.   
 With more Member States adopting national FDI screening mechanisms, the 
importance of streamlining procedures to save costs and avoid delays as regards 
multi-national notifications is growing rapidly. This needs to be a part of the 
cooperation mechanism and there is an important role for the Commission to give 
guidance and spread best practice. 
 Requiring prior authorization of intra-EU investments when foreign owned 
subsidiaries are involved is likely to lead to many unproblematic notifications raising 
costs and pose risks for the proper functioning of the Single Market. To instead 
make the notification voluntary coupled with the possibility for Member States or 
the Commission to launch own initiative procedures when pre-defined criteria are 
met may be an alternative way of meeting legitimate security concerns without these 
negative side effects. 
 Article 6(2)(a)) which requires investors to file their authorisation requests in all 
relevant Member States on the same day will lead to unnecessary operational 
complexities and we would hence ask for a reasonable window for investors to 
notify multi-country investments and for the co-legislators to review the types of 
cases which may be subject to the Cooperation Mechanism to ensure that truly low-
risk transactions may continue to benefit from streamlined national review 
procedures.

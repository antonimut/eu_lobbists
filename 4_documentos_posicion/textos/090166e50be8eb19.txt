Screening of foreign direct investments (FDI) – 
evaluation and revision of the EU framework – 
Commission Adoption 
The In -House Competition Lawyer s’ Association ( ICLA) is an informal 
association of in -house competition lawyers with more than 500 members 
across the globe. The Association does not represent companies but is made 
up of individuals who are in -house experts in competition law  and who 
regularly manage foreign investment screening  filings. This paper represents 
the position of ICLA and does not necessarily represent the views of all its 
individual members. 
1. Introduction 
1. This paper serves as a follow-up to ICLA’s submission on 17 July 20231, in which we had identified 
three core areas for improving the FDI screening regime in the EU: 
a. Alignment of Member State laws 
b. Transparency and collaboration 
c. Administrative efficiency and best practices 
2. We acknowledge the European Commission (Commission)’s efforts to address these areas in the 
recently released Proposal for a regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council on the 
screening of foreign investments in the Union and repealing Regulation (EU) 2019/452 of the 
European Parliament and of the Council 2019/452 of the European Parliament and of the Council 
(Proposed Regulation).   
3. However, after thorough review of the Proposed Regulation, we believe that the text should be 
further refined to better meet those objectives and not only serve the Commission’s interest of a 
more streamlined process for the coordination mechanism. We propose to consider the following 
adjustments: 
2. Same Day Notifications For Multi-Jurisdictional Filings (Article 6(2)) 
Proposal 
4. The Proposed Regulation foresees a requirement for parties to submit multi-jurisdictional filings 
on the same day. 
Concerns 
5. The proposal could hinder the ability of parties to prioritize filings that are ready for submission 
over those still requiring additional information. This inflexibility might lead to unnecessary delays 
in the reviews. Flexibility in managing the submission of numerous parallel filings is crucial, 
especially in large transaction projects, as it enables parties to effectively manage their workload.

Mandating simultaneous submissions across all jurisdictions could remove this flexibility, 
complicating and delaying the process.  
6. Also, the regulation does not foresee  a corresponding requirement for Member States to clear 
transactions within a specific timeframe. This means that outside of the EU's cooperation 
mechanism, Member States remain largely free to set the pace of their reviews, including slowing 
them down and postpone decisions to the detriment of the notifying parties.  
Proposed Solutions 
7. To address these concerns, we suggest making the following adjustments to the current proposal: 
a. The requirement for simultaneous filing  should be removed, allowing for more efficient 
handling of multi-jurisdictional filings based on readiness and priority. 
b. Upon the submission of the last filing in case of multiple filings , the Proposed Regulation 
should mandate (or at least strongly encourage, by requiring “utmost consideration” here 
too) that each notified Member State decide s on the transaction within a predetermined 
deadline, unless grounds exist for an in-depth investigation, or a decision is made to extend 
the review period after consulting the notifying parties . This approach ensures a strong 
incentive for timely clearance by Member States and encourages parties to submit filings in 
a timely manner. 
8. Additionally, the Proposed Regulation should foresee that Member States adopt statutory "waiting 
periods" for Phase 1 and Phase 2 reviews, similar to those in merger control under the EU Merger 
Regulation. These waiting periods would allow transactions to proceed if not explicitly denied by 
the end of the period, ensuring a ba lance between thorough review and efficiency.  Recognizing 
the occasional need for additional information, it is proposed that authorities retain the ability to 
"stop the clock." However, this power should be limited to formal orders subject to judicial review, 
and the overall duration of the investment review process should be capped to prevent indefinite 
delays. 
3. Proposal for Greenfield Investment Screening (Recital 17, Article 2(2)) 
Proposal 
9. The Proposed Regulation does not mandate the screening of greenfield investments by Member 
States but allows for such screening if a Member State deems such screening necessary. When a 
greenfield investment is considered reportable by a Member State, the Member State is required 
under the Proposed Regulation  to notify any greenfield investments to the EU screening 
cooperation mechanism that fulfil the requirements in Article 5. In other words, even if a Member 
State is inclined to approve a greenfield investment for its economic benefits, the Member State 
must first consider feedback from the C ommission and other EU Member States through the 
enhanced cooperation mechanism if the requirements in Article 5 are met. 
Concerns 
10. We agree that the screening of greenfield investments should not be made mandatory, recognizing 
the significant economic benefits and generally lower national security risks associated with such 
investments.  
11. However, we are concerned about the  requirement for Member States that choose to screen 
greenfield investments to also engage with the cooperation mechanism. This requirement could 
introduce legal uncertainty and delays in establishing greenfield projects, as the receiving Member 
State must give considerable weight to comments from the Commission and other Member States, 
even where the greenfield investment does not expand to any other Member State.

12. Also, the Proposed R egulation does not address several novel interpretation questions for 
greenfield investments that might arise in this context, such as the definition of "completion" of 
an investment in the context of  the suspension obligations. The time of “c ompletion” may be 
challenging to ascertain in the context of greenfield joint ventures, in particular where there is no 
change of control. 
Proposed Solutions 
13. We propose to reconsider the current stance on encouraging the screening of greenfield 
investments until outstanding legal questions are resolved.  
14. The requirement that such investments undergo the cooperation mechanism review process 
should be removed, at least for situations where the greenfield investment project is confined to 
a single Member State and there are no definitive plans for the project to extend across borders. 
4. Proposal for “Own Initiative” Procedure (Article 9) 
Proposal 
15. The Proposed Regulation grants the Commission or Member States the authority to retrospectively 
review investments that were not notified to the cooperation mechanism if such investments are 
believed to likely affect the security or public order of the involved Member States negatively. The 
Commission or a Member State would have a period of at least 15 months following the 
completion of the foreign investment to initiate this own-initiative procedure. 
Concerns 
16. We are concerned  that the proposal could significantly undermine transaction certainty  and 
discourage investment due to the risk of decisions being revisited and possibly overturned after 
completion. That risk is elevated where t ransactions have already been reviewed and approved 
under a national procedure in one Member State before another Member State decides to invoke 
the own -initiative procedure. While ex -post review mechanisms are already in place in several 
Member States for transactions not previously reviewed, the proposed change would necessitate 
creating new ex -post review powers or expanding exi sting ones to include transactions already 
reviewed nationally.  
Proposed Solution 
17. Given the significant concerns regarding transaction certainty and the procedural complexities 
introduced by the own -initiative procedure, we suggest that this mechanism be removed or 
significantly restricted to ensure it does not undermine the stability and predictability of the 
investment environment in the EU. Ex-post reviews should only be possible in cases where no EU 
Member State has already screened and approved a transaction. 
18. If a post-closing intervention right is deemed necessary, it may be beneficial to include a provision 
for a revocation right, similar to Article 6(3) of the EU Merger Regulation (EUMR). This right would 
allow for the revocation of a decision if it was based on incorrect information for which an 
undertaking is responsible, obtained by deceit, or if the undertakings involved breach ed an 
obligation attached to the decision.

5. Proposal for Mandatory List of Sensitive Sectors (Annex II) 
Proposal  
19. The Draft EU FDI Screening Regulation requires all EU Member States to mandatorily screen 
investments into EU-based companies that participate in Union interest programs or are active or 
intend to be active in sectors deemed crucial for the security or public order of the EU, as listed in 
Annex II. These sectors include technologies, assets, facilities, equipm ent, networks, systems, 
services, and economic activities of significant importance. 
Identified Concerns: 
20. The Proposed Regulation ’s broad definition of critical technology areas may lead to excessive 
screening, hindering investment into emerging EU technology sectors. 
21. The proposed list , while more prescriptive than its predecessor, is still vague enough to face 
potential for divergent interpretations across Member States, especially for broad sectors 
requiring further national-level definition (e.g., “data analytics technologies”, “computer vision”, 
“hydrogen and new fuels”, “exoskeletons”).  
22. Current Member States ’ regimes differ significantly, and reportedly none encompasses all 
activities outlined in the proposal. The freedom for Member States to review sectors not included 
in the EU list could maintain sectoral divergence in FDI review practices across the EU. The 
proposed list’s broad nature could also lead to a n even wider scope of mandatory FDI reviews, 
even in Member States who are adopting a rather interventionist approach already today.  
23. This variability could increase the number of notifications, legal uncertainty and the administrative 
load on transaction parties and Member State authorities.  
Proposed Solution: 
24. The Proposed Regulation should focus on higher-risk investments and allow quicker capital access 
for low-risk technologies.  
25. We propose to adjust Annex II by making the list of relevant activities more precise, incorporating 
specific qualitative or quantitative thresholds. This refinement could help narrow down the sectors 
that truly require screening due to their importance to security or public order, thus mitigating the 
risk of overly broad interpretation and application. To ensure the criteria are proportionate to each 
Member State’s context, the Proposed Regulation could introduce corridors or ranges that allow 
for adjustments reflecting the varying population and economic sizes of Member States. 
26. To reduce the potential for divergent investment screening practices, we propose limiting Member 
States’ ability to extend their review beyond the activities listed in Annex II. This would encourage 
a more uniform approach to FDI screening across the EU, enhancing clarity for investors and 
reducing the likelihood of unnecessary notifications. 
27. Alternatively, EU Member States could be obliged to jointly discuss, align  on, and update a joint 
list applicable in all Member States every two years. 
6. Enhanced Cooperation, Information Sharing, and Reporting (Articles 7-8) 
Proposal: 
28. The Proposed Regulation aims to implement more stringent information -sharing protocols and 
increase the Commission ’s role in the review of certain investments under the cooperation 
mechanism.

Concerns: 
29. The proposed enhancements in information sharing and Commission involvement could 
complicate the FDI screening process, demanding more resources from both notifying parties and 
Member States.  
30. We are particularly concerned that the expanded requirements for information submission could 
lead to an overly burdensome process for the notifying parties. Member States, in anticipation of 
or in response to requests to notify deals via the cooperation mechanism, might extend the 
comprehensive information requirements to all national notifications. This approach could result 
in the need for substantial data collection for every notification, regardless of whether it will be 
shared with the Commission or other Member States. 
31. The Commission’s plans to develop a template for information submission, expected to mirror the 
current template used under the existing EU FDI Screening Regulation , will help  standardize 
submissions. However, one standard  template unlikely will fully address  the complexity and 
resource challenges. 
Proposed Solutions 
32. We recommend standardizing and streamlining the data collection process. This could be achieved 
by making mandatory a single filing form applicable across the EU, designed to gather all common 
data fields relevant to the parties involved and the transaction itself. 
33. Member-state specific information, such as details about a company's operations within th at 
Member State or reputational data (for example, criminal records),  as well as enhanced 
information required as part of the cooperation mechanism  could be collected through modular 
sections of the same form. These sections could be left blank when not applicable, ensuring 
flexibility and relevance.  This standardised form should be optimized to only collect strictly 
necessary information , in line with the Commission’s commitment to minimize red tape for 
companies operating in the EU. 
34. As previously suggested, a dopting English as the common filing language, where permitted by 
Member State laws, could significantly reduce the complexity and cost associated with translating 
filing materials into various European languages. This approach would facilitate a more efficient 
review process by  ensuring that information is readily accessible and understandable across 
different jurisdictions. 
7. Minimum Requirements (Article 4) 
Proposal: 
35. To enhance coherence among the different national screening regimes, Article 4 sets out certain 
requirements for national screening mechanisms.  It covers  notification triggers, the difference 
between initial reviews and in-depth investigations, protection of sensitive information, judicial 
recourse against screening decisions and transparency requirements.  
36. However, the Proposed Regulation currently does not aim for harmonization but only introduces 
minimum standards. For example, the Proposed Regulation lacks specific requirements that define 
'adequate' review procedures in Article 4(2)(a).

Concerns: 
37. The European Court of Auditors identified significant variances in the Member States' screening 
systems, which threaten the coherence of the Single Market. 2 We share these concerns, as such 
divergences create obstacles for investors. For example, in some countries, transactions are 
deemed rejected if not decided within the statutory timelines, while in others explicit approval is 
required before implementation, regardless of expired timelines. These differences can compel 
investors to lower their investment amount or accept disproportionate remedial actions to secure 
timely authorization, to the detriment of the Single Market.  
Proposed Solutions: 
38. The co -legislators should amend the Propos ed Regulation  to include more specific common 
requirements for national screening mechanisms. These requirements should be guided by the 
principles of due process, proportionality, and transparency.  
39. For example, the requirements should be more detailed in the following areas: 
a. Clarify that certain low-risk transactions, such as  internal corporate restructurings 
without a change of ultimate control, are generally exempt from Member States' 
screening mechanisms. 
b. Stipulate that investments are generally deemed approved after expiry of the 
statutory review timelines. 
c. Define more precisely “economically active” in Article 4(4)(b) , by setting out the 
specific economic activities that are in-scope (e.g., manufacturing, developing, testing). 
d. As regards “adequate procedure”  (Article 4(2)(a), (b), (i)), empower the Commission 
to consolidate experiences from Member States and on this basis formulate best 
practices in the form of guidance on handling the formal and substantive elements of 
investment reviews, incl. remedies. 
e. As regards “judicial recourse” (Article 4(2) ) and as far as EU law permits, specify the 
extent to which courts should be entitled to review investment screening decisions, 
e.g., whether parties can challenge the merits of the Member State’s determination 
that the transaction at issue poses a national security threat.

Comments on the Proposal for a Regulation  
‘Establishing the European Defence Industry Programme and a framework of measures to 
ensure the timely availability and supply of defence products (‘EDIP’)’ 
 
Overall, the European Defence Industry Programme (EDIP) represents a welcome and needed 
addition to the tools deployed by the European Commission to stimulate the competitiveness and 
preparedness of the EU’s Defence Technological and Industrial Base in a momentous geopolitical 
conjuncture. Indeed, the proposal features several elements which can prove conducive to this 
objective, including:  
• Focus on j oint procurement  and, therefore, early end -users’ commitment, essential in 
providing long-term visibility to the industry as a whole and particularly to those sectors – such 
as the naval one – whose products require costly R&D and long industrialisation times.  
• Structure for European Armament Programmes (SEAPs), providing a flexible framework for 
joint procurement and relevant elements (such as bonuses and a VAT exemption) encouraging 
industrial and Member States’ engagement in ambitious common programmes.   
• European Defence Projects of Common Interest , which could direct joint procurement 
towards large flagships  in several domains, especially those mentioned in the Joint 
Communication on the European Defence Industrial Strategy ( EDIS, p.9) 1. In this respect, the 
explicit mention of maritime and underwater assets is particularly suited to current and future 
operational scenarios.  
1) Streamlining funding and leveraging on existing EU large programmes with real added 
value 
While the instrument appears to be geared towards off-the-shelf joint acquisition, ensuring a degree 
of continuity between selected long-standing, large development actions –funded through the EDF 
– and the EDIP would benefit the overall coherence of the EU’s defence industrial initiatives.  
➢ In this respect, some of the largest EDF projects due to reach adequate development maturity 
during the current EDF – such as the European Patrol Corvette – could be taken into 
consideration as initial testbed for the instrument , particularly to streamline funding and ensure 
maximum effectiveness.  
➢ Such coherence could also provide a blueprint for EDF and EDIP successor programmes  (i.e. 
beyond 2027), which should ideally encompass research, development, industrialisation, and 
joint procurement under a single instrument.  
2) A gradual move towards technological autonomy 
The European naval sector has already achieved a considerable degree of technological autonomy, 
due to its continuous investment in R&D and to  the excellence of its solutions. Several European 
actors have even managed to achieve important results in foreign markets, successfully bringing 
tried-and-tested European solutions to third country allies. However, it is also evident that the 
complexity of naval capabilities calls for a broader approach, encompassing the need s of key 
European system providers.

➢ Eligible Actions (Art. 18, with specific reference to Art. 11.8c): in its current form, the proposal 
would de facto limit EDIP funding to those projects/capabilities which do not contain components 
potentially subject to control or restrictions by third -country entities. While on the long term this 
approach will prove necessary to stimulate the development of sovereign EU solutions, the 
regulation should foresee a gradual implementation,  allowing EU industrial actors to research 
and develop effective substitutes over a reasonable timeline, without compromising their supply 
chain and the operational efficiency of the respective armed forces . To address this issue, the 
regulation might foresee feasibility studies  to be carried  out with the Union’s support  – for 
example through EDF projects - to analyse the effort, timeline and milestones for the research 
and development of new solutions.  
3) Fostering readiness while maintaining industrial competitiveness  
The activation of the two ‘supply crisis’ states (i.e. Supply Crisis and Security-related Supply Crisis), 
as well as the monitoring activities preceding it, might carry significant implications for the industry. 
This is particularly true when it comes to potential  disruptions in the production and delivery cycle 
caused by sudden priority-rated orders, but also in the frequent exchange of technology- or market-
sensitive information, whose disclosure might potentially compromise industrial edge.  
➢ Monitoring (Art. 41)  and information gathering (Art. 46) : a secure -by-design information 
sharing system remains a key precondition for the communication, collection and sharing of any 
sort of data between the industry, the Member States, and the Commission. In the absence of or 
prior to the full operational capacity of such system - essential to guarantee the integrity of 
market-sensitive data – information shall only be provided  on a voluntary basis , with 
confidentiality guarantees on the part of the respective Member States.  
➢ Priority Rated Orders (Art. 47) Priority Rated Requests (Art. 50): in this respect, the issuing 
of both Priority Rated Orders (Art .47) and Priority Rated Requests  (Art. 50) should be strictly 
subject to a prior assessment and an agreement between the industry and the respective 
Member State, ensuring that PROs and PRRs won’t affect the market position, credibility, and 
the competitiveness of the industrial entity concerned.  
4) Ensuring an adequate industrial involvement  
Due to the proposal’s focus on joint procurement and, therefore, the requirements and needs of the 
end-users, the steering bodies featured in the proposal – namely the Defence Industrial Readiness 
Board – do not foresee a clear, institutionalised format for industrial consultation, akin, for example, 
to NATO’s Industrial Advisory Group (NIAG). A structured involvement of the industries involved in 
the production of new capabilities and infrastructural ramp-up could provide strategic overview on 
the state-of-the-art and concrete advice on the technological feasibility and timeframe of potential 
programmes.  
➢ Governance, evaluation, and control (Art. 57, with specific reference to Art. 57.10) : since 
EDIS explicitly mentions the establishment of a ‘a high-level European Defence Industry Group’ 
as ‘privileged interlocutor of the Board’ 2, the regulation could foresee a more formal role for this 
body, which could be involved in a broad range of activities and on a more regular basis.

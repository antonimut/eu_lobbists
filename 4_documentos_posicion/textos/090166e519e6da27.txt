 
Contribution du Syndicat National des Fabricants de Sucre de France à la 
consultation publique relative à la révision ciblée du règlement européen 
portant organisation commune des marchés agricoles. 
10 mars 2025 
 
“If it ain’t broken, don’t fix it !” (Thomas Bertram Lance) 
 
Le SNFS partage l’objectif politique de la Commission de conforter la position des agriculteurs  
dans la chaîne de valeur. De ce même fait, il est indispensable de considérer  la diversité des 
situations par catégorie de filière , ainsi que le besoin des opér ateurs d’avoir un cadre 
réglementaire non seulement prévisible et stable, mais également adapté à leurs spécificités 
sectorielles objectives. 
Aucune proposition législative en matière de contractualisation ne devrait entraîner de 
conséquences dommageables ou comporter d’effets contre -productifs pour la partie amont 
de la chaîne de valeur. Il faut entendre par « partie amont de la chaîne de valeur » l’ensemble 
indissociable réunissant la production agricole et le premier maillon de transformation de 
cette production agricole  : ce couplage est particulièrement pertinent dans le secteur 
betterave-sucre en raison de l’interdépendance et de l’intégrati on étroites des productions 
betteravière, d’une part, et sucrière, d’autre part. Cette intégration trouve d’ailleurs une 
concrétisation dans le modèle coopératif (ex. en France, où la composante coopérative 
représente plus de 90% de la production sucrière). 
Aucune initiative visant une harmonisation de principe des règles de contractualisation ne 
devrait remettre en cause les régimes spécifiques lorsque les contractualisations sectorielles 
se montrent plus adaptées que la réglementation générale ou lorsqu’elles présentent, pour les 
maillons de production concernés, un avantage avéré en termes d’équilibre, d’efficacité et de 
robustesse économique. 
Le secteur du sucre est emblématique à cet égard. La réglementation actuelle reflète les 
équilibres acquis au long des dernières décennies et atteste que le secteur betterave-sucre est 
précurseur en matière de contractualisation amont. Sa longévité et sa stabilité témoignent de 
son adaptation aux particularités et spécificités du secteur (saisonnalité, cycle productif long, 
…). La réglementation actuelle consacre notamment un modèle contractuel écrit, des clauses 
obligatoires sur les livraisons, des barèmes  tenant compte de la qualité des betteraves, des 
livraisons précoces ou tardives, la répartition des frais de transport, un encadrement des 
livraisons et de l’achat des betteraves par des accords interprofessionnels, etc. Plus encore, 
une disposition relative à la répartition ex post de la valeur répond de manière appropriée aux

effets de la longueur du cycle de production-commercialisation : la valorisation de la betterave 
proposée en début de contrat est en effet ajustée en fonction de la valorisation du sucre sur 
le marché observée à la fin de la campagne de commercialisation  (mécanisme de répartition 
de la valeur). 
Le partage de la valeur est un dispositif assurément plus efficace, adapté, juste et sécurisant 
que la clause de renégociation ou de rupture du contrat proposée dans le projet de règlement 
de la Commission. Conjuguée à l’étroite interdépendance dont il est fait mention ci -avant, le 
mécanisme de partage de la valeur  rend par définition superflue l’introduction d’indicateurs 
relatifs aux coûts de production. 
La proposition de la Commission présente  le double inconvénient de faire de la clause de 
renégociation un dispositif unilatéral à la seule initiative des agriculteurs et d’introduire un 
facteur d’incertitude s’étendant à toute la chaîne de valeur : 
• Le contrat de livraison de betteraves ne relève pas de la catégorie des contrats 
unilatéraux que l’on peut rencontrer en droit privé. Introduire une clause de 
renégociation unilatérale dans un contrat par nature synallagmatique ne peut que 
heurter le bon sen s et les principes les plus évidents du droit contractuel  : les parties 
s’engagent habituellement dans une relation contractuelle lorsque cette dernière leur 
offre un équilibre de droits & devoirs et la sécurité juridique propice à la conduite de 
leurs affaires. 
• Une clause de renégociation unilatérale serait un facteur d’incertitude susceptible de 
contaminer toute la chaîne de valeur aval puisqu’à tout moment, elle menace  
d’interrompre l’approvisionnement physique des sucreries et, à son tour, celui des 
acheteurs de sucre en aval. Du simple fait qu’il soit inscrit dans le règlement, ce facteur 
pèserait négativement sur la valorisation du sucre sur le marché et donc, en cascade, 
sur celui de la betterave. Dans ces conditions, et afin de sécuriser leur production, les 
acheteurs de sucre auraient tout avantage à préférer les sucres importés ou ceux issus 
du raffinage du sucre de canne importé, au détriment du sucre de betterave produit 
localement. 
Il est douteux que cela serve bien les intérêts et la robustesse de l’amont agricole… 
On regrettera enfin que les spécificités liées au mode de fonctionnement et à la 
contractualisation des coopératives ne soient pas prises en compte dans les propositions de la 
Commission. 
En conclusion : le règlement OCM actuel a consacré une contractualisation spécifique dans le 
secteur du sucre une contractualisation spécifique, équilibrée, éprouvée et fonctionnelle, qu’il 
importe de maintenir inchangée , notamment pour ce qui regarde les disposition s à l’article 
125 et à l’annexe X de ce règlement.  
Le SNFS complète la présente contribution en annexant la position du CEFS, à laquelle il a 
contribué et à laquelle il souscrit pleinement. 
  
 
Contribution of the National Union of Sugar Manufacturers of France to the 
public consultation on the targeted revision of the European regulation on the 
common organisation of agricultural markets 
March 10, 2025 
 
“If it ain’t broken, don’t fix it !” (Thomas Bertram Lance) 
 
The SNFS shares the Commission’s political objective of strengthening the position of farmers 
within the value chain. For this reason, it is essential to take into account the diversity of 
situations across different sectors, as well as the operators’ need for a regulatory framework 
that is not only predictable and stable but also tailored to their specific sectoral characteristics. 
No legislative proposal on contractualisation should result in harmful consequences or have 
counterproductive effects on the upstream part of the value chain. The "upstream part of the 
value chain" should be understood as the inseparable whole comprising a gricultural 
production and the first stage of  industrial processing. This linkage is particularly relevant in 
the beet-sugar sector due to the close interdependence and integration of beet and sugar 
production. This integration is reflected in the cooperati ve model, as seen in France, where 
cooperatives account for more than 90% of sugar production. 
No initiative aimed at a general harmonisation of contractual rules should call into question 
specific regimes when sectoral contractual arrangements prove to be more suitable than 
general regulations or when they provide a clear advantage in terms of balance, efficiency, and 
economic resilience for the relevant production segments. 
The sugar sector is emblematic in this respect. The c urrent regulation reflect s the balance 
achieved over past decades and places the beet -sugar sector  as a pioneer in upstream 
contractualisation. Its longevity and stability highlight its ability to adapt to the specific 
characteristics of the sector (seasonality, long production cycles, etc.). The present regulatory 
framework establishes a written contractual model with mandatory clauses on deliveries, 
scales accounting for beet quality, early or late deli veries, transport cost allocation, and the 
regulation of beet deliveries and purchases through interprofessional agreements. 
Furthermore, a special provision on ex post beet price adjustment appropriately addresses the 
effects of the long production-marketing cycle: the initial beet valuation proposed at the start 
of the contract is adjusted according to the sugar market variation observed at the end of the 
marketing year (value-sharing mechanism). 

The value-sharing mechanism is undoubtedly more effective, appropriate, fair, and secure than 
the renegotiation or contract termination clause proposed in the Commission’s draft 
regulation. Given the close interdependence mentioned above, the value-sharing mechanism 
inherently renders the introduction of production cost indicators unnecessary. 
The Commission’s proposal presents the dual drawback of making the renegotiation clause a 
unilateral mechanism initiated solely by farmers and introducing an element of uncertainty 
throughout the entire value chain: 
• The beet delivery contract does not fall into the category of unilateral contracts 
typically found in private law. Introducing a unilateral renegotiation clause into a 
contract that is by nature synallagmatic contradicts common sense and the 
fundamental princ iples of contract law. Typically, parties enter into contractual 
relationships when they offer a balance of rights and obligations, along with legal 
certainty conducive to business operations. 
• A unilateral renegotiation clause introduces uncertainty that could disrupt the entire 
downstream value chain, as it threatens to interrupt the physical supply of sugar 
factories and, in turn, the supply of sugar buyers. The mere fact that this clause is 
enshrined in regulation would negatively impact sugar market valuation and, 
consequently, the value of beet. Under such conditions, and to secure their supply, 
sugar buyers would have a strong incentive to favour imported sugars or refined cane 
sugar over locally produced beet sugar. 
It is doubtful that this would serve the interests and resilience of the upstream agricultural 
sector. 
Lastly, it is regrettable that the Commission’s proposals fail to take into account the specificities 
of cooperatives’ operational and contractual models. 
Conclusion: The current CMO regulation has established a specific, balanced, tested, and 
functional contractualisation framework in the sugar sector, which must remain unchanged, 
particularly with regard to the provisions in Article 125 and Annex X of this regulation. 
The SNFS supplements this contribution by annexing the CEFS position paper, to which it has 
contributed and fully subscribes.

“TARGETED” REVISION OF SINGLE CMO REGULATION 
CEFS’ consistent position is to maintain unchanged the current and longstanding provisions on 
contractualisation in Art. 125 and Annex X of the Single CMO Regulation, which take into account 
the unique specificities of the sugar sector and its contractualisation practices in a balanced and 
effective way. 
SPECIFICITIES OF EU BEET SUGAR PRODUCTION 
Beet sugar production is characterised by a mutually-dependent relationship between sugar 
manufacturers and growers owing to the unique characteristics of the sugar industry. 
These unique characteristics include:  
- Different options: A major difference between the two parties is that sugar factories have no 
option other than to process sugar beets, while sugar beet growers can choose to cultivate 
alternative crops. Sugar factories must therefore pay a price that is sufficiently attractive for 
farmers to grow sugar beet instead of alternative crops such as wheat, maize or rapeseed, 
for example. 
- Seasonality: sugar beets cannot be stored for longer than a couple of weeks without 
degrading, meaning they must be processed as soon as possible. It is not possible to import 
nor export sugar beets, so European farmers are the sole raw material suppliers for EU-made 
beet sugar. Because of sugar beets’ perishability, sugar production follows the beet harvest 
and takes place during a campaign of 90-150 days, generally running from September to 
February of the following year.1 For these reasons, any shortfall in sugar beet supply is 
impossible to make good during the campaign.

- Logistical complexity: On average, a sugar factory has to process more than 1.2 million 
tonnes of beets per production campaign. This requires meticulous and rigorous logistical 
planning, months in advance of the campaign. These beets must be harvested, piled in the 
field, and finally carried away in a very short time, with sometimes heavy and unpredictable 
constraints in terms of weather, public infrastructure and availability of means of transport. 
This requires heavy and yet flexible logistical planning, ahead of the campaign. 
- Local sourcing: Beet perishability and processing seasonality mean that sugar factories must 
source their beets from within a limited geographical radius of 50-100km maximum.2 Sugar 
factories must establish and maintain robust and long-term relationships with local sugar 
beet growers in order to ensure a sufficient supply of high-quality sugar beets during the 
sugar production campaign.  
- Capital intensity: sugar production is a highly capital-intensive business. Even limited 
investments can run into the hundreds of millions of euros. Operations depend on consistent 
cashflow to cover operating costs (e.g. beet purchase, energy expenses, salaries). In order to 
guarantee farmers an outlet for their beets, substantial capital must have been invested to 
build a factory and sufficient cashflow must be made available to cover operating costs. On 
the flip side, sugar manufacturers are dependent on sugar beets to produce the sugar that 
enables them to pay down investments and cover their costs. Sugar factories cannot process 
anything other than sugar beets. 
- Long lead times: the time between the conclusion of the beet contracts and the delivery of 
the final product (sugar) can take up to 24 months. In most EU Member States3 beet 
contracts are concluded in the autumn of year 1 or beginning of year 2, the seeds are sown 
in the spring of year 2, beet deliveries start in the autumn of year 2, and the resulting sugar 
can be delivered from the autumn of year 2 right up until the summer of year 3. There is a 
whole supply chain that depends on the reliable delivery of sufficient volumes of sugar at the 
right time and at an acceptable price: the system of contracts regulating this process must be 
credible and robust. The provision on value sharing clauses, mentioned in more details 
below, addresses that specificity and preserves the interests of beet growers. 
- Volatility: the EU sugar market, just like the world sugar market, is extremely volatile. Sugar is 
well known as the most volatile soft commodity. Periods of elevated, remunerative prices 
may be followed by periods of pullback where sugar prices may not cover production costs. 
Neither sugar manufacturers nor sugar beet growers assume all of the risk of operating in a 
volatile market: the risk is shared between them (and in most cases falls more on the 
shoulders of the manufacturer).

- High proportion of cooperatives: because of the above factors, most sugar manufacturers

Put simply, sugar factories rely on local sugar beet production and farmers rely on local sugar 
factories to provide an outlet for their sugar beet. Without sugar beet a sugar factory must close 
down and, due to the high costs of rebuilding or even restarting, is unlikely to reopen, leading to the

manufacturers and growers is essential for the sector’s success. 
Collaboration between sugar manufacturers and growers has always been essential for the sector’s 
success and contractualisation in the sector has historically offered sugar beet growers more security 
than any other in the Single CMO Regulation.  
THE EU BEET SUGAR SECTOR: A FRONTRUNNER AND A MODEL OF SUPPLY 
CHAIN RELATIONS FOR DECADES 
The EU beet sugar sector has been regulated by a specific framework for contractualisation (including 
compulsory written delivery contracts concluded before sowing) since production quotas were 
established in 1968. Written contracts have long been compulsory within the EU beet sugar sector, in 
addition to “agreements within the trade” to govern such contracts (Art. 125 to the Single CMO 
Regulation). These written contracts and agreements within the trade must conform to a detailed set 
of beet purchase terms laid down in Annex X to the Single CMO Regulation. 
Annex X, Point XI, point 5 also says that:  
A sugar undertaking and the beet sellers concerned may agree on value sharing clauses, including 
market bonuses and losses, determining how any evolution of relevant market prices of sugar or other 
commodity markets is to be allocated between them. 
That provision, although remaining specific to our sector in its wording, served as an inspiration for 
the one established by the Omnibus regulation in 2018 for other agricultural sectors (Article 172a of 
the Single CMO Regulation).  
In this way, the EU sugar sector has long been a frontrunner and a model of supply chain relations. 
CEFS has consistently argued that the current system of contractualisation within the EU beet sugar 
sector reflects a careful balance between beet growers and sugar manufacturers reached over 
successive in-depth discussions and must not be modified.  
The proposed changes affecting Annex X could introduce significant disruptions to the established 
framework governing the sugar sector. We would like to highlight the following specific concerns.

1. MISGUIDED COPY/PASTE OF PROVISIONS FROM REVISED ART. 168 NEGLECTS BEET 
SUGAR SECTOR’S SPECIFICITIES 
Many provisions in the proposal appear to be simply copy/pasted from the amended Art. 168, 
contradicting the specific treatment of the sugar sector historically respected by the European 
Commission as well as recital 24 that commits the Commission to take account of the specificity of 
the sugar sector. Undermining the sector’s unique characteristics and established practices, this is an 
inappropriate imposition of provisions ill-suited to the sector. The Commission’s interpretation of 
“alignment” denies the specificities of our sector and is therefore unacceptable.” 
Not only do these unfortunate “copy/pastes” deny the sector's specific features, but they are 
sometimes more restrictive than those proposed for other sectors in Art. 168 (see e.g. point 3.). 
2. REVISION CLAUSE COULD DESTABILISE THE ROBUST AND LONGSTANDING SYSTEM 
OF CONTRACTUALISATION IN THE SUGAR SECTOR 
Reference: Annex X, Point I, point 2 
The proposal would empower farmers and Producer Organisations to request contract revisions and 
terminate contracts unilaterally in case no agreement is reached. This could destabilise the current 
system, which relies on written agreements concluded before the sowings. As mentioned in the 
introduction, the entire sugar supply chain depends on a credible and robust system of contracts. The 
introduction of the possibility for farmers to request a revision of, and ultimately terminate, beet 
contracts unilaterally at any time would be detrimental to European beet and beet sugar production 
as well as damage the trust of sugar users and potentially lead to a loss of market share to the sole 
benefit of white sugar imports or imports of raw sugar for refining.  
CEFS understands that such a clause may be necessary for other sectors with a history of abusive 
contracts, but this is not the case for sugar, which has long been characterised by a high level of 
contractual regulation, effectiveness, and mutual trust, as well as a high proportion of cooperatives 
own by farmers.  
CEFS request: the proposed revision clause should be struck from the proposed revised Annex X. 
The current wording in Annex X should remain unchanged. 
3. INDICATOR-BASED PRICING IS REDUNDANT AND RAISES COMPETITION LAW 
CONCERNS, AND IS MISALIGNED WITH REVISED ART. 168 
Reference: Annex X, point II, point 2

The proposed modification to Annex X, point II, point 2 that requires price formation in contracts to 
use “objective indicators, indices or methods of calculation of the final price” is yet another example of 
a simple copy/paste from revised Art. 168. It lacks added value and creates unnecessary complexity. 
The use of indices linked to production costs in price formation has been recognised as problematic 
by both the CJEU and national competition authorities. The CJEU has expressed itself on this matter 
on numerous occasions.4 Meanwhile, in 2020 the CNMC (Spanish National Commission on Markets 
and Competition) issued a Report on Competition and the Agri-Food Sector stating “the competitive 
assessment of the establishment of reference indices is analogous to price fixing, as it affects 
competition in a similar manner (...).”5 
In addition, the article goes beyond even the French Egalim law (from which the contracts between 
beet/cane growers and sugar manufacturers are explicitly excluded) by granting Member States the 
authority to define pricing indicators. Even if parties are not legally obliged to use such indicators in 
contracts, this provision could well erode the autonomy and effectiveness of the existing system of 
contractualisation and the freedom to contract.  
Finally, we consider that the proposed change to Annex X, point II, point 2 is discriminatory vis-à-vis 
other sectors and contradicts recital 24, since it does not provide the possibility of static pricing in 
sugar beet contracts. This contrasts with the revised Art. 168, which provides flexibility by allowing 
either static or indicator-based pricing. In this way, the proposed change creates a distortion that 
would reduce flexibility for market participants. 
CEFS request: indicator-based pricing should be struck from the proposed revised Annex X. The 
current wording in Annex X should remain unchanged.

4. BEET DELIVERY CONTRACTS ARE CONCLUDED WELL BEFORE DELIVERY, BEFORE 
EVEN THE SEEDS ARE SOWN 
Reference: Annex X, Point I, point 1 
The proposed modification to Annex X, Point I, point 1 would stipulate that delivery contracts should 
be made in advance of the delivery. In fact, beet delivery contracts are concluded prior to sowing (i.e. 
many months in advance of delivery) to give beet growers the necessary security. Furthermore, 
article 125 explicitly states that beet delivery contracts are concluded before the sowings (“pre-
sowing”). Sugar factories also need to secure their beet supply well in advance in order to guarantee 
the smooth running of operations and conclude long-term contracts with sugar users. 
CEFS request: The current wording of Annex X, Point I, point 1 must be left unchanged. 
5. UNRECOGNISED POS COULD RAISE COMPETITION LAW ISSUES 
Reference: Annex X, Point I, point 2, together with Art. 152 para. 1a 
The proposed modification to Annex X, Point I, point 2 refers to producer organisations (POs) and 
associations thereof (APOs) despite their irrelevance in sugar sector contract negotiations. Sugar 
manufacturers have no choice but to secure their beet supply, and already negotiate with the beet 
growers concerned. Therefore, producer organisations are not necessary for contractual negotiations 
in the EU beet sugar sector. This reference to producer organisations could lead to confusion and 
legal uncertainties when it comes to contract negotiations and beet deliveries and have a 
counterproductive effect on supply chain relations.  
Allowing unrecognised POs to negotiate prices raises potential competition law issues. Furthermore, 
the proposal fails to clarify the consequences if such POs fail to comply with agreements, creating 
legal uncertainty and potential liabilities. This reference to unrecognised producer organisations could 
lead to confusion and legal uncertainties when it comes to contract negotiations and beet deliveries 
and have a counterproductive effect on supply chain relations. 
CEFS request: reference to POs should be struck from the proposed revised Annex X.

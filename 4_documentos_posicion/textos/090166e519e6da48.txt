Position 
2024 targeted amendments to the CMO 
Regulation – EuroCommerce views 
EuroCommerce calls on the co-legislators to preserve the market orientation of 
the agri-food chain and avoid interferences with freedom of contract  
Key messages  
• The Commission’s Vision for the Future of Agriculture and Food of 2025 recognises that agri-
food supply chains are complex and one-size-fits-all policies will not work. This is particularly 
true for contractual relations between actors in the chain. 
• The economic situation of farmers varies according to production, size and region. The report 
on the Strategic Dialogue offer ed some suggestions on how to improve the position of 
smaller farmers in the chain, such as joining Producers Organisations (POs). 
• Competition throughout the chain drives all operators to become more efficient and leads to 
more resilience, more choice and better prices for consumers. Exemptions from competition 
rules must be avoided  – for example in relation to non -recognised POs or agreements on 
economic sustainability under art. 210a. 
• The market orientation of the EU agri -food chain must be maintained: interfering with the 
free formation of prices or overregulating commercial relations will create administrative 
burden for all actors (including farmers), reduce the competitiveness of the chain and not 
help those in need. 
• The proposal must be consistent with the simplification efforts of the Commission. Some of 
the amendments in the proposal (for instance excessive rigidity on written contracts or the 
establishment of contract registries) will increase administrative burden for the agri -food 
chain and should be reconsidered. 
Retail & Wholesale’s role in the agri-food system 
1. European agriculture is diverse, with farmers having varying sizes, sectors, and geographies. A 
small number of farmers are large, organised, export driven and successful. A very large number 
of farmers are small to very small. They bring diversity, support the landscape, soils and rural areas. 
2. The agri -food chain is similarly diverse. It is a multifaceted system involving numerous 
stakeholders, including farmers and their suppliers, the food processing industry, logistics and 
transport providers, international traders, hospitality businesses, and retail and wholesale

operators. Each actor plays a critical role in moving food from the farm to the consumer, ensuring 
quality and safety at every step.  
3. Only 13% of the EU’s agricultural production is food for human consumption. Most fresh produce 
does not end up in supermarkets. In the Netherlands, 2% of onions and onion products end up in 
supermarkets, with the percentages of tomatoes and tomato products being 9%, eggs and egg 
products being 19% and apples and apple products being 35%.1 
4. From this part of agricultural production, r etailers and wholesalers only have limited direct 
relationships with farmers. They mainly negotiate and purchase products from the food industry 
which have undergone at least some processing. This means they deal with a variety of suppliers, 
such as large multinational suppliers, suppliers of private labels and – for agricultural products – 
with larger cooperatives and producer organisations. 
5. The relationship between farmers and retailers  and wholesalers, often seen as one -sided, is still 
limited and more complex and symbiotic in today’s globalised and competitive market. 
6. Direct relations between retailers and farmers are growing, as distributors develop long -term 
partnerships with farmers to meet consumer demand for sustainable and local products. However, 
to meet the size of demand that retailers and wholesalers require to serve consumers across the 
EU, these represent a small part of the total supply.  
7. Every actor along the supply chain adds a certain value before a product reaches the retailers and 
wholesalers’ shelves. Prices are formed at each stage between the farmers and producers to the 
final price paid by consumers. The dynamics between different players influence pricing, as well as 
the costs incurred along the way.  
8. Retailers and wholesalers operate on small profit margins (typically between 1% and 3%), 
reflecting high costs for logistics, storage, food safety regulations, packaging, marketing, and 
unsold goods, which leads to reduced net profits. 
9. Therefore, production costs, market demand, availability of supply, transportation, compliance 
costs, labour and social security, the level of competition at different stages of the chain and the 
outcome of negotiations between suppliers and retailers and the trade-offs made all play a role in 
price formation.  
10. This is complicated by the fact that the actors that are buying from the farmers may have multiple 
markets or destinations for the farm produce they buy. Supply chains are not linear but are more 
like a web , with different market dynamics depending on the final use  – which could be human 
consumption in the EU (either through retail or hospitality), export or other uses (e.g. biofuel).  
The changes proposed to the Organisation of the Common Market (CMO) 
11. Driven by the protests of farmers struggling with the burden of regulation and the costs of the 
sustainability transformation in March 2024, and the subsequent conclusions of the report of the 
Strategic Dialogue on the future of EU agriculture2, suggestions were made on how to strengthen 
the position of farmers in the chain and consequently improve their income. These include better 
farmers’ organisation in cooperatives and producer organisations, support for farmers to digitise, 
adopt digital solutions and differentiate, better use of the Common Agricultural Policy and more 
cooperation along the chain, for instance for sustainability objectives. 
12. We welcome these initiatives, as farmers need more targeted support, particularly small and 
family-run farms, through financial tools, capacity building, and digitalisation. In particular, joining

13. Some of those suggestions  are reflected in the amendments proposed in December 2024 by the

14. Other proposed amendments feature definitions for commercial modalities, a general obligation 
for contract in writing and further exemptions from competition rules. In the absence of an impact 
assessment, there is no transparency on the evidence behind the se policy choices – for example, 
what is the causality between certain contractual changes (e.g. mandatory written contracts) and 
the improvement of the position of the farmer. Any measure relating to contractual relations must 
not have negative effects on the other operators, including consumers, and assure legal certainty 
for all operators in the food chain.  
15. We call on the co-legislators to:  
• Preserve f air and free competition in the Single Market  and limit exemptions from 
competition rules to what is strictly necessary. Competition among different stages of the 
chain (e.g. among farmers or among retailers) and throughout the chain drives all operators to 
become more efficient, differentiate their offering (with new products, innovation, more 
sustainability) and offer better prices for their customers and ultimately consumers.  It also 
improves the resilience of all food chain actors, from farmers to consumers. 
• Maintain the market orientation of the EU agri-food chain by avoiding interference with the 
free formation of prices. Overregulation of commercial relations will not achieve its intended 
aims, create administrative burdens and reduce the competitiveness of all chain actors. 
• Not increase administrative burdens for agri-food chain operators. Some of the amendments 
in the proposal (for instance excessive rigidity on written contracts or the establishment of 
contract registries) will increase administrative burden for the agri -food chain : such 
requirements should be removed. 
Our suggestions to ensure that competition and free price setting are preserved by the proposed 
amendments to the Organisation of the Common Market (CMO) are set out in the Annex.

Annex – detailed comments 
Article 88a – optional terms for commercial modalities 
Retailers and wholesalers use of certain terms in their marketing (e.g. on labelling and in-store) to better inform consumers of the value of the products they 
are purchasing. 
This supports retailers and wholesalers respond to consumer demand for more local products and provide information on their cooperation with farmers. For 
example, to raise awareness with consumers of the efforts farmers are making to differentiate and embr ace more sustainable production, for instance by 
cutting emissions, improving animal welfare or transitioning to organic production. 
This also supports retailers and wholesalers who can offer a very attractive route to market for local producers. This includes those who may not be equipped 
for direct selling to offer a range of local products to a larger group of consumers – including those who may not be able to travel to the nearest farm to 
purchase there directly.

Article 152 and 153 – Producer Organisations 
EuroCommerce supports the recommendations of the Strategic Dialogue encouraging farmers to join cooperatives and producers’ o rganisations (‘POs’) to 
reduce costs, increase efficiency and technological uptake, exchange best practices and peer-to-peer learning and improve access to finance from banks and 
financial institutions. 
Indeed, many of the contractual relationships of larger retailers and wholesalers are with large cooperatives and POs due to the volumes that such larger 
distributors need for their stores.

Non-recognised POs 
 
Streamlining procedures for farmers to join a PO 
is beneficial to improve the position of farmers.  
Such an approach is preferable to creating 
further exemptions from competition rules 
which will not achieve their intended aim and 
reduce the competitiveness and resilience of the 
agri-food chain.  
Competition among different stages of the chain 
(e.g. among farmers or among retailers) and 
throughout the chain drives all operators to 
become more efficient, differentiate their 
offering (with new products, innovation, more 
sustainability) and better pri ces for their 
customers and ultimately consumers. It also 
improves the resilience of all food chain actors, 
from farmers to consumers. 
The Commission should look into further streamlining procedures to 
join POs rather than creating further exemptions from competition 
rules for non-recognised POs. 
 
Buyers’ contacts with individual 
members of a PO 
Retailers and wholesalers may interact with 
individual members of a PO. For example, to 
learn more about their sustainability efforts or 
for reporting requirements (e.g. about emissions 
in their supply chain).  
Prohibitions on buyers contacting individual members of a PO should 
remain narrow and be limited to interactions which indeed 
jeopardise the objectives of the PO.

Associations of Producer 
Organisation (APO) 
Competition among different actors in the chain 
is a driver for resilience, choice, innovation and 
affordable consumer prices.  
Exemptions for APOs from Article 101 TFEU 
should be limited to what is strictly necessary. 
The volume cap of 33% of national production for APOs must not be 
increased. A higher volume cap would excessively limit competition 
among producers of the same products, eliminating incentives for 
them to compete, innovate, improve production and diversify. This 
would damage resilience, food security and increase consumer 
prices.

Mandatory written contracts 
and exceptions 
 
Retailers and wholesalers may be the first buyers 
of agricultural products listed in Annex I TFEU  
and t herefore be obliged to provide written 
contracts for purchases from farmers, POs and 
APOs. 
Written contracts are already mandatory in 
several Member States and their uptake has 
increased following the implementation of 
Directive (EU) 2019/633.7 
However, we noted that verbal agreements may 
be common in certain circumstances: in 
relationships between small retailers and 
suppliers, personal agreements are common 
practice. The flexibility of such arrangements is 
beneficial for both farmers and suppliers and 
allows for insta nce for timely sales of an 
unforeseeable surplus of goods, especially if 
perishable.   
For the remainder of the Member States without existing rules, the 
obligation for written contract should be limited to relations between 
farmers, their POs and APOs and the first purchaser.  
Exceptions, for instance in relation to on-the-spot sales, that seek to 
protect well -established customs and avoid excessive bureaucracy 
for farmers and smaller buyers should be preserved. 
The text should provide a definition of ‘ written contracts’ to reflect 
the Commission’s views (as expressed several times by Commissioner 
Hansen) that a simple email would qualify as a written contract.

Elements of the contract Articles 148 and 168 prescribe the elements that 
written contracts should feature, for example 
price, duration, quality and quantity and 
payment terms and procedures. 
The co-legislators should strike the balance 
between certainty of terms and conditions in a 
contract and an overly prescriptive approach to 
contracts.   
Excessively rigid structures on quantity or 
duration may not account for changes in supply 
and demand or supply constraints. Volumes 
purchases may often need to be adjusted 
periodically based on market conditions , 
seasonal variations or unforeseen 
circumstances. 
Such necessary flexibility is beneficial for both 
suppliers and buyers. 
The amendments should reflect the need for flexibility in relation to 
elements of the contract such as duration or quantities/volumes. 
The proposed amendments should also avoid redundancies : the 
definition of measures in cases of ‘force majeure’ is already regulated 
by Regulation (EU)2021/2116 and therefore does not require 
additional implementation in the contracts.

Possibility for member states to 
establish further rules at 
national level  
Articles 148(8)( and 168(8) allow Member States 
to establish further rules at national level, 
namely:  
(i) An obligation for the parties to agree 
on a relationship between the given 
quantity of agricultural products 
delivered and the price payable for 
that delivery; 
(ii) a minimum duration for contracts of 
at least six months (provided this 
does not impair the proper 
functioning of the Single Market); 
(iii) an obligation that a written offer 
must include a minimum duration 
for a contract. 
We fail to understand the justifications behind 
imposing obligations on parties a) to agree on a 
ratio between the quantity of agricultural 
products supplied and the price to be paid for 
this supply or b) a mandatory minimum term for 
a contract. 
A minimum duration for a contract should be 
established by the parties by assessing their 
individual circumstances. 
In many areas of agricultural trade, where only 
one-off deliveries are common, obligations such 
as minimum durations would not make sense. 
We call to eliminate the possibility for Member States to provide for 
such types of national rules.

Mediation mechanisms We a wider uptake of alternative dispute 
resolution mechanisms (such as mediation), but 
a legal obligation to set up such mechanism for 
disputes related to agricultural products risks 
creating administrative burden for supply chain 
actors as well as Member States. 
It is unclear what the remit of such a mechanism 
would be as well as the obligations for economic 
operators, particularly regarding financing. 
Economic operators should not be responsible 
for financing a state mediation mechanism.  
We call to eliminate the obligation for Member State to establish a 
mediation mechanism for disputes between parties.  This should 
remain voluntary and economic operators should not be responsible 
for financing a state mediation mechanism. 
Contract registries  
 
The proposed amendments provide for the 
possibility for Member States to set up contract 
registries for agricultural products. 
Such registries already exist in some Member 
States and create an enormous administrative 
burden for retailers and wholesalers, who must 
register hundreds of thousands of their contracts 
every year. 
There are also confidentiality concerns in the 
obligation of registering contracts between 
private businesses with a public authority. 
Member States should refrain from obligatory registration of 
contracts by the buyer to avoid unnecessary bureaucracy.

 DRV contribution to Commission public consultation on 
amendments to the current legal framework of the Regulation on 
a common organization of the markets in agricultural products 
(CMO) (1308/2013) 
The Deutscher Raiffeisenverband e.V. (DRV) is the political umbrella organisation of all cooperatives and cooperative-
oriented companies in the German agricultural and food industry. As an important link in the food value chain, the 
1,656 member companies in the production, trade and processing of plant and animal products with 114,000 
employees and 6,000 people in training achieve a turnover of 82.6 billion euros. Farmers, gardeners and winegrowers 
are the members and therefore owners of the co-operatives. 
In the following, we comment in part on important points that will have a massive impact on the functioning of 
competition within the European single market. The draft regulation presented contains provisions that represent a 
major encroachment on companies' contractual and statutory autonomy lead to a massive increase in bureaucracy. 
Therefore, it is incomprehensible that the EU Commission has neither carried out a public consultation nor an impact 
assessment before publication of the draft. The overriding aim of the amendments is to strengthen farmers in the food 
supply chain. The DRV expressly supports this goal. However, this must not be at the expense of the stability and 
negotiating position of cooperative-oriented companies and alliances in the agri-food industry, whose owners and 
members are farmers.  
I. Articles 148 and 168: Contractual relations 
Extension of the exemption for cooperatives and POs and retention of the option by the member states (148/168 para. 
1 and para. 5 (a)) 
The DRV firmly rejects the direct, general contractual obligation with form and content requirements that is now 
envisaged, as it is a profound encroachment on contractual and statutory autonomy that is neither necessary nor 
expedient. In our opinion, the proposed amendments are not suitable for achieving the aim of the regulation, which is 
to strengthen farmers. On the contrary, some proposals, such as the revision clause, will have an unwanted negative 
impact on producers and are to the detriment of cooperatives and POs.  
There are partial exemptions for cooperatives and producer organisations (POs) in Articles 148 and 168. 
Nevertheless, the proposed amendments have an impact on cooperatives and POs and may require amendments to 
their statutes, as the exception only applies if the substantive provisions are contained in the statutes, rules and 
decisions of that cooperative or PO. Cooperatives and POs are therefore fully affected in terms of the provisions. This 
represents a far-reaching encroachment on the autonomy of cooperatives contractual relations and the approved 
principle of self-administration. As part of the democratic decision-making process, the members have the opportunity 
to influence the decisions, so that further legal requirements are not necessary. In addition, DRV member companies 
with a cooperative orientation (including other legal forms) are also affected by the proposed changes. As a result, all 
sectors represented by the DRV (milk, fruit & vegetables, cereals/ oilseeds, wine, livestock) would be affected. 
Furthermore, the requirements directly applicable under the EU Regulation appear to conflict in part with German civil 
and cooperative law and raise numerous practical questions. Consistency between the legal systems is essential for 
legal certainty of all stakeholders involved. The DRV therefore advocates that the current option under paragraph 1 
(old version) should remain in place for the Member States. In any case, the exception for cooperatives (para. 5 lit. a) 
respectively) must remain in place or be fully extended in order to take appropriate account of the special features of 
cooperatives. Otherwise, this would mean a far-reaching encroachment on the statutory autonomy of cooperatives and 
would have a massive negative impact on the existing supply relationship models. 
If the European legislator nevertheless introduces the intended contractual obligation, we would like to point out the 
following aspects in particular:

Adaptation of the written form requirement to the UTP Directive (148/168 para. 1 and para. 4 (b)) 
While we generally recommend written contracts to our member companies, we reject a strict legal written form for 
contracts and offers for a contract. It is not clear whether the amendment is intended to ensure that contracts that were 
previously concluded verbally only become effective once they have been written down or whether it is solely a formal 
requirement. This may lead to a conflict with German civil law. In practice, a wide variety of ways of initiating and 
concluding contracts have emerged. In many sectors, it is common for contracts to first be concluded verbally and 
then confirmed, for example, by means of a commercial letter of confirmation when purchasing grain. Due to those 
sector-specific characteristics and established business practices, there should be no strict written form requirement. 
As in the UTP Directive, the contracting parties should have the right of having the terms of contract concluded orally, 
confirmed in writing. In addition, it should be clarified in the regulation that this also covers digital legal transactions 
(e.g. via e-mail). 
Conclusion of the contract at the time of transfer of ownership (148/168 para. 4 (a)) 
The Regulation stipulates that the contract must take place before delivery. This should be rejected and the word 
“delivery” should be replaced by “transfer of ownership”. In practice, for example, grain is delivered and stored without 
the decision of selling it. In the event of a subsequent sale, the requirement of a written contract prior to delivery could 
therefore no longer be met. The principle of separation, which is expressed in the separation of the legal transaction 
under the law of obligations and the legal transaction under property law, is, among other things, a special feature of 
German civil law. This once again highlights the need for the option to remain with the Member States in order to avoid 
unlawful interference in the national legal system. 
Adjustment of mediation mechanism (148/168 para. 3) 
The DRV takes a critical view of the establishment of a mediation mechanism. In addition to the expected bureaucratic 
effort, the DRV rejects a mediation procedure prior to the conclusion of a contract, since the decision to conclude a 
contract can only ever be negotiated voluntarily by all parties. A reasonable mediation mechanism can only be 
established for problems within an existing contractual relationship. It must be made absolutely clear that this 
mediation mechanism is completely voluntary for all parties. 
Deleting specifications for pricing (148/168 para. 4 (c)) 
The DRV rejects legal regulations on pricing by companies. In a free market economy, it must be entirely up to the 
companies to determine prices and their components and to negotiate those with their contractual partners. Strict 
regulations on pricing promote inefficiencies and hinder competition. While some sectors are based on exchange 
prices, others are subject to intensive international trade or world market prices. In addition, there are individual factors 
that are negotiated between companies. Furthermore, a legally imposed one-sided consideration of suppliers' 
production costs conflicts with private autonomy and a free, open market. Furthermore, in practice it is not even 
possible to determine uniform production costs for agricultural businesses. This fails because there is no legal 
definition of production costs and no uniform calculation method. In addition, production costs can differ considerably 
even between member farms and are very volatile. It is therefore not possible to standardise production costs. 
Furthermore, determining production costs would mean additional bureaucracy and would contradict basic principles 
of competition. 
Deletion of the revision clause (148/168 para. 4 (c) (iii)) 
The regulation stipulates that contracts with a minimum term of more than six months must contain a revision clause, 
which can be exercised by the farmer, a PO or an association of POs. First, it is necessary to clarify whether the 
“minimum term” of a contract is intended to cover continuing obligations or whether it should also include contracts 
concluded in advance for a fixed date in the future. It is not clear which requirements exist for a revision clause, which 
leads to legal uncertainty because court-proof price adjustment clauses in German law are very complex and often 
impossible to implement in practice. The necessity of such a clause is not apparent, as both parties can voluntarily

of price and quantity. A termination option for contracts with a longer term counteracts these advantages and, in the

Register of written contracts (148/168 para. 9) 
The need for a register of written contracts is not apparent. This is associated with considerable costs and constitutes 
a bureaucratic burden, which directly counteracts the goal of reducing bureaucracy. A public register would also lead 
to a conflict with European competition law due to its transparency.  
As a result, the DRV is therefore calling for the option to enact contractual obligations to remain with the 
Member States in particular due to conflicts with national law in Germany. 
II. Selected sector-specific contributions 
Cereals, oilseeds and protein crops: Article 168 
The entire supply chain - from the farmer to the processing stage - works with a functioning and reliable contract 
system in the German grain market (and also in the oilseed market). This contract system is characterised by the fact 
that it is possible for all stages in the process to individually buy, hedge and sell the raw material grain, from short-term 
daily contracts with daily prices to long-term contracts with various price models. This contract system functions on the 
basis of German legislation (in particular the German Civil Code and the German Commercial Code) as well as other 
market-specific agreements, e.g. the standard terms and conditions in German grain trading, so that the stages in the 
chain can ensure a constant supply of grain with established contract loyalty between the players. Any kind of revision 
clause with a unilateral termination option would turn this system on its head. It would certainly lead to significant risk 
premiums or, more likely, to longer-term contracts no longer being offered. As a result, all participants in the supply 
chain would suffer. 
Milk and dairy products: Article 148 
The European dairy industry is structured very heterogeneously depending on the Member State, both in terms of milk 
production structures and in terms of processing and marketing. The reality is differentiated and does not allow for 
blanket regulations. The extent to which milk supply relationships are regulated also varies. The vast majority of 
German milk producers already have regulated supply relationships, so that there is no need for additional legal 
regulation.  
Milk producers have organized themselves in dairy cooperatives to jointly exploit their raw milk on the markets in the 
best possible way, to take advantage of market opportunities and to bear the market risks in the best possible way. 
The members decide on the future direction of their companies in democratic decision-making processes. Each 
member has one vote, regardless of the size of the company. The democratic decision-making process gives 
members a wide range of opportunities to shape the cooperative's supply and ownership relationships in line with their 
needs and expectations. This also applies to the offer of long-term price hedging systems by dairies. 
A joint study by the ife Institute and Kiel University of Applied Sciences has already shown that the implementation of 
Article 148 would weaken the entire milk value chain and producers in particular. The scientists at Kiel University even 
conclude that producers would have to expect considerable financial losses. The proposal to oblige dairies to make 
binding contract offers in terms of volume, price and duration ignores the reality of a highly volatile milk market. III. Article 152 
 
The DRV demands that the term “producer” and “producer member” to be kept because these two terms (as well as 
“non producer member”) have an important relevance for the rules related to fruit and vegetable PO recognition and to 
their OP management. 
IV. Article 153 para. 2a 
The DRV rejects the possibility of direct contact between PO-member and buyer parallel to the PO, as this could have 
a negative impact on the functioning of the PO.

Executive Summary: The EU Steel Carbon Shield – A pragmatic 
solution for the post-Safeguard era 
On 30 June 2026, the current safeguard measures on certain steel products in the EU will expire. At the 
same time, the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) will be fully implemented on January 1, 
2026. This means that the EU is facing a decisive trade policy decision. 
If both instruments were applied in parallel, there would be a double burden on steel imports – with 
particularly serious consequences for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). Such a situation not 
only undermines the competitiveness of European SMEs but could also violate World Trade Organisation 
(WTO) rules. 
The challenge in a nutshell: 
• CBAM + Safeguard = double burden for importers 
• SMEs in particular are hit hard: high bureaucratic and financial effort 
• Market imbalance: 3% of importers account for over 57% of steel imports 
• Growing distortion of competition to the detriment of smaller market players 
• Risk of WTO violations and weakening of industrial supply chains 
Our proposal: The EU Steel Carbon Shield 
In order to provide targeted relief for SMEs and at the same time take into account the EU's climate 
policy goals, we propose the introduction of a Steel Carbon Budget: 
• 25,300 tonnes allowance per year and SME for imported (stainless) steel 
• Within this allowance: 
o No CBAM reporting obligation 
o Exemption from future successor tariffs to Safeguard 
• No exemption from existing anti-dumping or anti-subsidy duties 
• Exceedances lead to the regular CBAM obligation from 25,301 tonnes 
• Implementation possible via existing customs and CBAM infrastructures 
What the EU Steel Carbon Shield does: 
• WTO-compliant and easy to implement 
• Politically compatible, for example to the SME Relief Package and Art. 173 TFEU 
• Promotes fair competition and strengthens the resilience of European supply chains 
• Protects SMEs from systematic disadvantages compared to large corporations 
• Consistent with climate targets: major emitters remain subject to CBAM 
The EU Steel Carbon Shield is a simple, fair and realistic solution for the time after the end of the 
safeguard measures. We call on Parliament, the Commission and the Member States to take this 
approach into account in the further development of the CBAM strategy and European steel policy – in 
the interests of fairness, legal clarity and competitiveness for all companies in Europe.

The EU Steel Carbon Shield - A practical solution for a Safeguard 
successor 
With the introduction of CBAM from 1 January 2026 and the expiry of the EU Safeguard measure on 
certain steel products on 30 June 2026, the problem of a violation of WTO trade law was originally 
intended to be prevented. For some time now, however, the major EU steel manufacturers have been 
lobbying for a successor to the EU Safeguard measure. 
CBAM and Safeguard in combination would constitute double taxation, to the detriment of the European 
economy and the taxpayer. As is well known, customs duties and taxes do not make the seller from a 
non-EU country more expensive but must be passed on to the consumer in the EU. The overlap of CBAM 
and Safeguard in the first six months of 2026 is therefore already a violation of WTO law and is at the 
expense of the European consumer. 
A circumstance that should be widely known from the discussion about the new US tariffs. Tariffs and 
taxes on imported goods lead to inflation and a reduction in purchasing power, damage the 
competitiveness of small and medium-sized enterprises and reduce competition in economic areas 
overall. Precisely then, as in the case of steel, these economic areas are dominated by cartels and 
oligopolies. 
Unfortunately, there seems to be political will within the European Commission and among some 
member states to find a successor to the EU Safeguard measure on steel and to force it into a WTO-
compliant construct so that it can be used at the same time as CBAM. 
"In view of this exceptional situation, it is therefore necessary to introduce an appropriate and effective 
protective measures beyond 30 June 2026"  
- A European Steel and Metals Action Plan, 19 March 20251

EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL amending Regulation (EU) 2023/956 as regards 
simplifying and strengthening the carbon border adjustment mechanism2 to the EU Parliament.  
This is a concrete proposal on how CBAM and a possible Safeguard successor could be implemented 
simultaneously and as SME-friendly as possible.

EU Safeguard measure on certain steel products (EU Safeguard) 
The EU Safeguard measure is currently based on an import quota system that differentiates according to 
countries of origin. For each country of origin and product, there is currently a certain amount of 
different steel products that can be imported duty-free until the quota is filled. Thereafter, a 25% 
safeguard duty will be payable on each additional ton of imported steel from this source. If trade defence 
measures are already in place, they will be levied until the quota of one origin is full, after which 25% 
Safeguard tariffs will be levied. 
Safeguard is also justifiably criticized for being incalculable in many cases, as the quota can easily 
overflow with some origins and with certain non-specific country quotas and has repeatedly occurred.  As 
mentioned in the introduction, this does not disadvantage suppliers from third countries but represents a 
significant competitive disadvantage for European buyers and importers of steel. Large importers, such as 
EU steel mills, are suspected of deliberately allowing quotas to overflow, which puts smaller market 
players at a structural disadvantage.

Safeguard full of regulatory loopholes with the potential to distort 
competition 
In addition, the EU Safeguard measure has a regulatory loophole, as it does not consider primary 
material, so-called slabs. By importing several million tonnes of slabs per year, EU steel producers are 
undermining these and other market protection measures, thus damaging the competitiveness of small 
and medium-sized enterprises in particular. 
In addition, the import of cheap slabs also hides CO2 emissions, which mean that some manufacturers 
have to melt and cast less or not at all themselves, thus having to spend significantly less free CO2 
certificates on their own production in order to then sell them on the free market. In 2023, this has led, 
among other things, to the fact that the large corporations have not only saved approx. 7 billion euros in 
CO2 costs with surplus free CO2 certificates but have also been able to generate approx. 3 billion euros 
through the sale of the surplus certificates. A record figure. The role model of this buoyant trade in 
surplus EU ETS certificates: the EV manufacturer Tesla. 
Safeguard must end after 8 years at the latest 
The EU Safeguard measure on certain steel products ends on 30 June 2026. Then it will have reached its 
longest term of eight years provided for by WTO law. According to the WTO Agreement on Safeguards 
Article 7 Duration and Review of Safeguard Measures, a new Safeguard measure on the same products 
may7 only be introduced after a further 8 years. 
SMEs are disadvantaged by the EU compared to large companies 
SMEs are primarily disadvantaged by CBAM and Safeguard. 
According to a statistical analysis by EUROSTAT from 
October 2024, the largest share of imports is attributable 
to large importers. Just 3% of all EU importers are large 
importers, but together they account for 57.3% of the 
total value of all extra-EU imports. On average, a large 
company imports around €35 million per year, according 
to EUROSTAT.  
97% of all other importers are thus to be classified as 
SMEs, even though this includes a share of 2.1% of 
unknown company sizes and whose average imports are 
only marginally higher than those of micro-enterprises. 
SMEs are thus responsible for just 42.7% of the total 
value of all extra-EU imports. A medium-sized company imports only for a little more than 4.6 million 
euros, a small one for 1.1 million euros and a micro-company even for only 0.2 million euros per year. 
While it is well known that large companies also receive better purchasing conditions and prices through 
greater economies of scale, SMEs cannot benefit from such advantages. Large companies also replenish 
the Safeguard quotas with their imports much faster than SMEs could. Thus, SMEs are always at a 
disadvantage compared to large companies when it comes to imports. Delays due to Safeguard quotas 
being overflowed too quickly or costs for Safeguard tariffs can be absorbed by SMEs much more poorly

3% of importers are responsible for more than 57% of steel imports 
CBAM was originally intended to replace the EU Safeguard measure – also because both measures 
together would violate the rules of the World Trade Organization (WTO). Now a decision is to be made on 
a new trade defence measure as the successor to EU Safeguard. The victims of this double taxation 
through CBAM and market protection plus significantly higher bureaucratic costs are therefore clearly 
SMEs. 
The Commission stated in (SWD(2025) 58 final)9 that of the 200,000 CBAM importing relevant products, 
116,000 (58%) import steel and 40,000 (20%) steel and aluminium. Thus, approximately 156,000

Proposal: EU Steel Carbon Shield 
In order to provide SMEs with a level playing field in the long term and a compensation that has been 
lacking for decades, we would like to propose that an exemption for SMEs be created for all steel and 
stainless steel relevant products currently covered by the EU Safeguard measure (and its successor). 
Imports are also important to promote competition in the EU market, which leads to monopoly power in 
product markets without imports13. 
In doing so, the EU would also comply with Article 173 (1) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the 
European Union14 , which provides for the promotion of small and medium-sized enterprises and their 
further development in particular and the creation of a favourable environment for them. 
Steel Carbon Budget for SMEs 
Small and medium sized companies, regardless of size, can apply for a free Steel Carbon Budget of 
25,300 tonnes of material per year for all steel and stainless steel products for which there are neither 
CBAM reporting obligations (or costs) nor Safeguard (or its successor) duties. Anti-dumping and anti-
subsidy duties remain in full force and effect and are not reduced or otherwise taken into account by the 
Steel Carbon Budget. 
Based on the currently available CBAM default values for the transitional period15 and the data from the 
latest technical Safeguard Review16, this would correspond to a CO2 volume of approximately 50,000 
tonnes per year (average approx. 1.97 tCO2eq/t steel). This would also provide sufficient protection for 
SMEs, which primarily concentrate on trading in imported steel and stainless steel and thus represent an 
important pillar for the supply chains of small and medium-sized enterprises and would strengthen their 
competitiveness in the long term.  
With an average EU sales price of approx. 1,073 euros per tonne of steel, no SME with the given steel 
carbon budget of 25,300 tonnes material can skip its status as an SME (27.2 million euros turnover from 
imported steel products vs. 50 million euros turnover limit to large companies). If this nevertheless 
occurs, the company in question will be excluded from the Steel Carbon Budget for SMEs in the following 
year and will be fully subject to CBAM from the first tonne.

This makes the EU Steel Carbon Shield highly controllable and fair. The Commission already wanted to 
exempt 182,000 companies from the CBAM reporting obligations. An increase in steel imports is 
therefore not to be expected even with a steel carbon budget of 25,300 tons material for SMEs that are

Monitoring by national customs authorities 
As with the Commission's current proposal (50 tonnes limit), imports can be monitored by the national 
customs authorities. As soon as a company exceeds its steel carbon budget, it becomes mandatory from 
the 25,301 ton CBAM. A retroactive CBAM calculation for the previously imported quantities does not 
occur, as otherwise entrepreneurial security and calculability would no longer be guaranteed. 
The monitoring of tariff rate quotas has been working well for years with the EU Safeguard measure. The 
CBAM declaration portal has also already proven that EU companies can and must provide extensive 
information if they want to obtain CBAM declarant status. In order to ensure that the Steel Carbon 
Budget is not circumvented by the establishment of countless SMEs under one individual or company, 
this can be linked to the Ultimate Beneficial Owner (UBO) and their national tax numbers, for example. 
Large companies remain fully subject to CBAM 
Large corporations remain fully subject to CBAM in the Steel Carbon Shield, as they have been proven to 
have significantly lower costs due to bureaucracy and are often at an advantage when it comes to 
subsidies and financing options. In addition, large corporations are also the largest CO2 emitters in the EU 
and must therefore be made to reduce their emissions as a matter of priority. In addition, the major CO2 
emitters in the EU have benefited extensively from the allocation of free EU ETS certificates since 2005. 
This is not available to SMEs or only to a very limited extent. 
Compensation is already taking place here through the free and slowly decreasing allocation of EU ETS 
certificates. This creates an incentive function for large companies, making investments in the reduction 
of their own CO2 emissions more urgent and unavoidable. 
The flood of steel from non-EU countries is absent 
To prevent excessive imports of steel products previously covered by Safeguard, only marginal 
adjustments are necessary. Since a large proportion of steel products imported into the EU are already 
subject to trade defence measures, and as soon as an SME has exhausted its Steel Carbon Budget, CBAM 
would become fully compulsory, there is effective protection for domestic producers without having to 
introduce another major trade defence measure. Nor does a separate system have to be created for 
exports, which would have to compensate for CO2 emissions paid in the EU. A system that would not 
only generate double bureaucratic costs and further costs in the member states but would also have to 
be made easily accessible to SMEs.

Summarized 
The country-specific quota regulation from Safeguard is reversed, it is made into a company-specific 
quota, and the EU applies it equally as a single instrument for both CBAM and a Safeguard successor. 
This ensures transparency, efficiency, reduction of bureaucracy costs and a real level playing field, 
where SMEs are finally given fair consideration without having to make a great effort. Our proposals in 
this regard on the respective tonnage quota should support this when viewed objectively. 
Conclusion: Think Small First – More Fairness and a Real Level 
Playing Field for SMEs 
Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) are the backbone of European industry. Nevertheless, they 
are often overlooked in the EU's trade and climate policy. Complex requirements, excessive bureaucratic 
effort and a lack of consideration for their structural disadvantages compared to large companies have 
been causing systematic discrimination for years. 
With the simultaneous application of CBAM and a possible successor to the safeguard measures, this 
imbalance threatens to worsen further. For many SMEs, this means double the burden – without the 
resources, networks or economies of scale to counter it. 
This is where the EU Steel Carbon Shield comes in. It offers a fair and practicable solution to provide 
targeted relief for SMEs – without losing sight of the climate targets. The model brings balance to trade 
policy instruments, strengthens the resilience of European supply chains and ensures real competition on 
an equal footing. 
It is time to end the structural disadvantages of small companies. The Steel Carbon Shield is a concrete, 
feasible step towards a fairer industrial policy – and an important signal to hundreds of thousands of 
companies in Europe: The EU is serious about the principle of "Think Small First20".

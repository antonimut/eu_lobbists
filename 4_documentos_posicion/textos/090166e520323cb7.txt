INTRODUCTION
Recent years have shown a worrying intensification 
of droughts, floods and heatwaves  in the 
EU. 2024 is set to be the warmest year  on record. 
Global average sea level  has risen about 
21 cm since 1900, Europe’s biodiversity 
continues to decline , and, between 2030 and 
2050, climate change is expected to cause around 
250,000 additional deaths per year globally.
Meanwhile, the scientific community continues 
to warn of the risks of passing tipping points and 
the irreversible consequences of runaway climate 
change (including those mentioned above), and 
to underline the urgency of taking action. The 
last Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change 
(IPCC) report highlights that global greenhouse 
gas emissions have continued to increase, with 
historical and ongoing contributions arising from 
unsustainable energy use (fossil fuels), 
land use and land-use change, lifestyles and 
patterns of consumption and production.
According to the same report, global surface 
temperature reached 1.09°C above 1850-
1900 levels in 2011-2020 , leading to extreme 
weather and climate events, widespread adverse 
impacts and related loss and damage to nature 
and people, rising sea levels, and loss of (human) 
lives. Recently, July 2023 was the hottest month  
on record globally and broke several further 
records within that month. June 2024  was the 
thirteenth month in a row that is the warmest 
on record for the respective month of the year. 
According to Copernicus, the global mean 
temperature for the year-to-date (January - 
November 2024) is the highest on record, at 
more than 1.5°C above the pre-industrial average.
In Europe, the situation is even more alarming 
as European land temperatures between 
2013 and 2023 were already 2.12 to 2.19°C 
warmer than the pre-industrial level , 
according to the European Environment Agency . 
Additionally, the latest UNEP emissions gap 
report highlights that policies currently in 
place are projected to heat the planet by 3.1°C.
It is more than urgent to curb global warming by 
adopting and implementing 1.5°C consistent 
policies and measures . The Secretary-General 
of the United Nations (UN), António Guterres, has 
called on the EU  to take the necessary actions to 
limit climate change: “We’re close to the tipping 
point that will make 1.5 degrees impossible to 
achieve. We need an acceleration agenda and 
once again we count on the leadership of the 
European Union in this regard” . Thus, the EU 
should go faster, not just because of our 
responsibility for historical emissions, 
but also because every fraction of a degree 
matters to vulnerable communities, endangered 
species and ecosystems, and people’s livelihoods. 
According to the European Scientific Advisory 
Board on Climate Change’s (ESABCC) advice on 
the 2040 target , under some ethical principles, 
the EU has already exhausted its fair share 
of the global emissions budget. Finally, the 
last UNEP emissions gap report highlights that 
the world must now reduce greenhouse 
gas emissions by 42% between 2019 and 
2030 if we are to limit the world’s global 
temperature increase to 1.5°C within the next 
decade. For the EU this would represent a cut of 
56% in net greenhouse gas emissions by 2030 
compared to 1990, without taking into account 
fairness, equity, and the EU’s responsibility 
for historical emissions.  For this reason, 
the EU must also increase its 2030 target to 
achieve a more rapid path to carbon neutrality.
On the 6th of February 2024, the European 
Commission published a communication 
“Europe’s 2040 climate target and path to climate 
neutrality by 2050 building a sustainable, just 
and prosperous society”, proposing a 90% net 
GHG emission reduction compared to 1990 levels 
as the ESABCC recommended target for 2040. 
The EU should commit to the following below.
“I urge Governments to make it happen by hitting fast forward on their 
net zero deadlines so that developed countries commit to reaching net-
zero as close as possible to 2040”
António Guterres, Secretary-General of the United Nations
SUMMARY AND POLICY RECOMENDATIONS
The year 2024 is set to be the hottest on record, with 
global sea levels rising by 21 cm since 1900. From 
2011 to 2020, global temperatures were already 
1.09°C above pre-industrial levels, and Europe saw 
a rise of over 2°C from 2013 to 2022. Current policies 
could lead to a 3.1°C global temperature increase, 
and between 2030 and 2050, climate change is 
expected to cause around 250,000 additional 
deaths annually. These figures underscore the 
urgent need for stronger climate action. On 
6 February 2024, the European Commission 
published a communication proposing a 90% 
net greenhouse gas emissions (GHG) reduction 
for 2040. However, given the importance of the 
climate crisis, this figure is not ambitious enough.
The EU should commit to the following:
1. Reach economy-wide climate neutrality 
(zero net emissions) by 2040 , and 
negative net emissions thereafter. This 
should consist in gross emissions reductions 
in non-LULUCF sectors of at least 92%.
2. Set a separate and significantly higher 
LULUCF target consistent with meeting 
the 2040 climate neutrality target , 
to be met through the rapid expansion of 
nature protection and restoration on land 
and at sea, and sustainable agricultural and 
forestry practices. Nature-based removals 
must be prioritised and scaled-up with due 
consideration of their biodiversity potential. 
3. If an EU-wide separate target for permanent 
removals is set, it should only be established 
following a rigorous impact assessment of 
their sustainable scale-up, including potential 
impacts on ecosystems and people. Further, 
their contribution to the overall EU climate 
neutrality target should be limited to the level 
of residual emissions at the time, to avoid 
the risk of emission reduction deterrence. 
4. Increase the EU’s 2030 net emissions reduction 
target from at least 55% net to at least 
65% gross, with all sectors contributing, and 
require targets to be set for 2035 onwards in 
line with science. Most of the GHG emissions 
reduction after 2030 should happen in 
the first half of the decade, before 2035.
5. Prioritise and protect nature, implement 
and enforce existing environmental 
and biodiversity legislation, and adopt 
a new climate adaptation framework 
prioritising nature-based solutions.  
Nature is our best ally. It can help us reduce 
rising temperatures while decreasing GHG 
emissions and storing increased levels of 
carbon; protect Europeans against climate 
hazards such as more intense and frequent 
droughts, heatwaves, floods and fires; 
and build resilience in our ecosystems.
6. Halve EU energy consumption by 
2040, since the full decarbonisation of the 
European economy will not be possible at 
the current level of energy consumption.
7. End the era of fossil fuels, by phasing 
out coal by 2030, fossil gas by 2035 
and oil by 2040 , to reach a 100 % 
renewable energy supply by that date.
8. Provide a strong EU just transition 
framework to ensure a fair, well-managed 
and orderly transition and avoid increasing 
inequalities. This includes support to the 
most vulnerable households and workers 
plus targeted territorial assistance. 
9. Provide significantly increased additional 
support through international climate 
finance to enable mitigation outside the EU, 
in order to make up for the EU’s responsibility 
for historical emissions. The amount of 
this support should be calculated by the 
European Commission, based on the Civil 
Society Equity Review Report , so as climate 
finance is commensurate with the amount 
by which the EU’s fair share exceeds even 
the most ambitious domestic mitigation.

THE EU’S AMBITION FOR 2040: EMISSIONS 
REDUCTION AND REMOVALS
WWF welcomes the ESABCC’s advice 
recommending emission reductions of 90-95% by 
2040 relative to 1990, and affirming that pursuing 
the more ambitious end of this range improves the 
fairness of the EU’s contribution. The ESABCC 
also found out that going beyond the current 2030 
target would decrease EU’s cumulative greenhouse 
gas emissions and increase the fairness of the EU’s 
contribution to global mitigation. In light of the 
EU’s historical emissions, and under equity and fair 
share principles, WWF believes that the 95% upper 
end of the range in the ESABCC recommendation 
should be the absolute minimum and the EU 
should aim to reach climate neutrality 
by 2040.  This would ensure that the EU both 
protects its own citizens and fulfil its international 
responsibilities, contributing to efforts to limit the 
global temperature increase to 1.5°C.
Given how much we have already emitted, carbon 
dioxide removals (CDR) have a role to play in 
limiting global warming to 1.5°C; however, while 
the IPCC states clearly that removals will be needed, 
it also says they should only be additional to 
steep emission reductions.  Indeed, “CDR 
cannot substitute for immediate and 
deep emission reductions” . The potential 
for carbon removals will be limited compared to 
current emissions; removals c annot come at 
the expense of very rapid emission cuts  in 
all sectors. In other words, emission reductions 
and removals are not fungible, highlighting 
the need to have separate targets.
Moreover, it is important to note that nature-
based removals and permanent 1 carbon 
removals are inherently different. Nature-
based removals are potentially reversible,  
the length of the biogenic carbon cycle varies from a 
few years to millennia. They are subject to the risk 
of intentional and unintentional reversals  
through events such as fire, floods, disease outbreaks, 
etc., which are exacerbated by climate change.
For this reason, WWF advocates for the 
adoption and implementation of three 
different targets in addition to the 
EU’s climate-neutrality objective:
• an at least 92% cut in EU gross 
greenhouse gas emissions  in the 
EU by 2040 compared to 1990 levels;

• a significantly higher LULUCF target

restoration and sustainable agricultural 
and forestry practices  that are a win-win-

and it is estimated that further restoration of

• a separate permanent removals target, 
which only includes technologies 
removing carbon from the atmosphere 
(as opposed to fossil-based Carbon 
Capture and Storage (CCS) and Carbon 
Capture and Utilisation (CCU)). This 
target should be set following a rigorous 
impact assessment of these removal activities’ 
sustainable scale-up and their contribution 
to the overall EU climate neutrality target 
should be limited to the level of residual 
emissions at the time, to avoid the risk of 
emission reduction deterrence. This target 
should not involve undue reliance on CCS.
Marine ecosystem-based removals may also be an 
important carbon sink to take into consideration. 
However, the potentials of this sink, linked 
to its tipping point on ocean acidification, are 
still unknown at the EU level. The European 
Commission should therefore conduct an 
assessment to analyse the feasibility and the 
full potential of this carbon sink, as well as the 
framework in which such sinks should be designed. 
Regarding the 2030 target, the EU long-term 
strategy and the EU Climate Law have currently 
a linear trajectory of GHG emission reductions, 
which means that they introduce the same 
absolute reduction (in tonnes) every year. But 
this means the annual percentage rate of GHG 
emission reductions increases significantly over 
the years, from single digit figures in the 2020s to 
double digit figures in the 2030s and 40s. Delaying 
emissions reductions in this way will lead to 
impossibly difficult annual rates of reduction later 
and it is thus urgent to already prioritise cutting 
emissions faster now, when there are still relatively 
easy changes that can be made with mature 
technologies. Moreover, the ESABCC asserts that 
additional efforts to increase the ambition 
beyond 55% is possible and would increase 
the fairness of the EU’s contribution to 
global mitigation. The EU should therefore 
aim to achieve at least 65% gross emission 
reductions by 2030 compared to 1990 levels. 
Under the Glasgow Pact, the Parties of the Paris 
Agreement agreed to 5-year Common Time 
Frames for Nationally Determined Contributions 
(NDCs). Since all Parties - including the EU - are 
expected to present NDCs with 2035 emission 
reduction targets in 2025, the EU should 
establish an EU-wide 2035 climate target 
and align EU climate policy cycles with 
international NDC requirements. The 
2035 target should be aligned with science and 
socio-economic feasibility, and reflect the EU’s 
responsibility for historical emissions; this means 
it should reflect the highest ambition possible 
and not be a halfway point on a linear trajectory 
between the 2030 and the 2040 targets. For this 
reason, most greenhouse gas emission reduction 
should happen in the first half of the decade. 
AN ENERGY SYSTEM CONSISTENT WITH EU 
CLIMATE GOALS
In order to achieve increased climate ambition 
targets, strengthened energy policy measures are 
essential. The Energy Efficiency and Renewable 
Energy Directives (RED) should be revised to:
• halve final energy demand  by 2040  
by increasing energy savings in 
enabling people to adopt more 
sustainable lifestyles, and improving 
energy efficiency  across the economy;
• reach 100% renewable energy 
supply by 2040  with (1) coal phase-
out by 2030, (2) fossil gas phase-out 
by 2035 and (3) oil phase-out by 2040.
The much-needed massive deployment of 
renewables has to be based very largely on wind 
and solar ; for example, the EU needs to more 
than double its current installed wind capacity by 
2030 to meet the new RED target. The EU should 
avoid being side-tracked by false climate solutions 
such as unsustainable bioenergy , which would 
increase emissions, or new nuclear , which would 
divert effort away from real solutions that are 
cheaper and quicker. To overachieve this target, 
which is necessary to keep our climate objective 
alive, the EU needs holistic ecosystem-based 
planning: new rules to accelerate the deployment 
of renewables should be planned in a way that 
minimises negative impacts on nature and 
local communities, while benefiting all citizens.
Regarding hydrogen, only renewable 
hydrogen should be accepted , meaning 
hydrogen produced thanks to electrolysis 
generated by additional renewable electricity 
from wind and solar. WWF advocates for:
• giving priority to energy efficiency, 
energy saving and direct 
electrification over renewable hydrogen 
to decarbonise all sectors by 2040;
• giving priority to domestic EU 
production of renewable hydrogen  
over relying on importing renewable 
hydrogen from third countries;
• and defining priority sectors for 
the use of renewable hydrogen  to 
make targeted use of limited sources of 
renewable hydrogen in sectors which could 
not achieve decarbonisation otherwise.
The expected contribution of planned 
hydropower to the energy transition 
is minimal  and hydropower dams  can 
contribute to methane emissions. In addition, 
the building of new hydropower infrastructure 
in Europe, including small and run-of-the-
river plants, results in disruptions to rivers’ 
flow, hinders fish migration, leads to 
habitat loss and negatively impacts 
freshwater ecosystems.  WWF advocates for:
• stopping new hydropower 
projects in the EU, including public 
funding for those new projects;
• redirecting these resources towards 
the ecological refurbishing of existing 
plants and the removal of obsolete dams.

A SOCIALL Y JUST AND FAIR TRANSITION
Many European citizens experience difficult living 
conditions and cite the cost of living  as a key EU 
challenge. Decreasing social cohesion is increasingly 
recognised as a threat to democracies, and as climate-
related disasters increase, their disproportionate 
impact on the poorest members of society becomes 
dramatically apparent. The transition to a 
climate-neutral economy is only possible if 
planned in a way which ensures a socially 
fair and just transition for all.  The ESABCC 
advises: “The recommended 2040 target requires 
rapid, inclusive and well-managed transitions 
[ which ] account for local context, engages 
stakeholders, and ensures equity and justice.” 
WWF advocates for:
• a 2040 climate neutrality target supported 
by a strengthened EU just transition 
policy framework  including both 
territorial and whole-of-society measures.
• targeted funding to most carbon-
intense regions  to enable inclusive 
and urgent transition to clean economy;
• large-scale clean energy, buildings 
renovation and energy-saving 
programmes for citizens   especially for 
most vulnerable and low-income households. 
• provision of training and re-
skilling opportunities  to 
maximise green job opportunities
• increased social protection 
measures and affordable essential 
services including energy;
• adaptation of tax systems  to prevent 
regressive impacts, transformative 
approaches to ensure reduced material 
dependency, and economic models 
commensurate with planetary boundaries.
The principle of fairness also requires a 
high level of ambition  in keeping with the 
EU’s capacity to act, its historical contribution to 
emissions, and the application of intergenerational 
justice. The EU accounted for around 9% 
of GHG emissions from 2015-2021, but is 
responsible for 17% of global emissions over the 
period 1850-2021 according to the ESABCC. 
Considering the fairness of the EU’s contribution 
under different ethical principles the ESABCC

PROTECT NATURE TO PROTECT OURSELVES
Prioritising nature is necessary to ensure safety 
and resilience in the face of the accelerating climate 
crisis. It is fundamental to protect and restore 
existing ecosystems; implement and enforce 
existing environmental and biodiversity legislation 
(Birds and Habitats Directives, Water Framework 
Directive, Marine Strategy Framework Directive, 
Nature Restoration Law), and step-up the 
implementation of the EU Biodiversity Strategy, in 
line with the Global Biodiversity Framework. This 
will reduce rising temperatures while decreasing 
greenhouse gas emissions and storing increased 
levels of carbon, increase our resilience against 
the impacts of climate change and protect food 
security. We need a rapid expansion of nature 
protection and restoration on land and at sea and 
sustainable agricultural and forestry practices. 
The implementation of the Nature Restoration 
Law should be a priority for all EU Member States. 
It is important to develop environmental measures 
that support farmers, foresters, landowners, 
fishers and coastal communities in achieving 
a long-term sustainable and viable future for 
their sector. Instead of scrapping environmental 
measures, policy makers should address the real 
problems, such as - in the case of agriculture 
- fair prices for farmers, more public funding, 
a fairer distribution of Common Agricultural 
Policy funds, and requiring agricultural imports 
to adhere to the same sustainability, health and 
safety standards as those in place in the EU. It 
is important to support all the vulnerable actors 
in adopting environmentally-friendly practices. 
To  close the existing funding gap for nature3, we call for 
the establishment of a dedicated nature restoration 
fund in the next Multiannual Financial Framework, 
to also support farmers, fishers, foresters and land 
owners to implement nature restoration activities.4
In addition to financial measures, we call for 
the upcoming European Climate Adaptation 
Plan announced by Ursula von der Leyen  
to prioritise nature-based solutions 5, to protect 
Europeans against climate hazards such as 
heatwaves, floods, fires and droughts, over “grey” 6 
or engineering solutions. Protected, restored 
and well-managed nature is a strong tool  
which can reduce rising temperatures, improve 
water retention, and increase the resilience of crops 
and abundance of fish, while decreasing greenhouse 
gas emissions and storing increased levels of 
carbon. Because of those wide co-benefits, nature-
based solutions are most of the time more cost-
effective than “grey” solutions.7 Climate adaptation 
needs to be mainstreamed in sectoral policies and 
in the EU budget, in order to avoid maladaptation. 
A legally binding framework to guarantee a timely 
and socially fair phase-out of subsidies that harm 
biodiversity, and redirect them towards nature-
based solutions that could better protect European 
citizens from climate impacts is urgently needed. 
Finally, the climate adaptation plan should 
also ensure consistency between adaptation 
strategies and plans at EU and national levels.

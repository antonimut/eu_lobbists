 
 
 
Statement | 30 July 2025 
 
Public consultation on  
“Trade measure addressing the negative trade-related  
effects of global excess capacity on the EU steel sector”. 
 
 
 
 
 
  
Ref. Ares(2025)6260284 - 31/07/2025

Statement statement | EU Consultation   Side 2 
Abstract  
 
This statement  was submitted by the German Steel Association. The German Steel Association is the 
voice of the steel industry in Germany, which has set itself the goal of climate -neutral production by 
2045 – and thus saving a third of total industrial greenhouse gas emis sions. The association is com-
mitted to a political framework that will make it possible to continue to have a strong, low -emission 
steel location in the future. With 37.2 million tons in 2024, Germany has the largest steel production 
in Europe.  
 
The continuing deteriorating situation in international steel trade poses an existential challenge for 
the steel industry in Germany and the EU. The increasing overcapacities, especially in Asia, are put-
ting the steel industry in Germany and the EU under massive import pressure. Reinforced by US cus-
toms policy,  the previous EU trade defence measures have failed to protect the industry. The urgent 
goal is to create a new EU trade instrument that limits imports to a viable  level in order to create fair 
compet itive conditions and enable investments in climate -friendly transformation.  
 
 
Requirements for a new trade defence instrument ("post -safeguard")  
 
− Introduction of a tariff quota system (TQR)  to limit excessive imports while at the same time 
controlling market opening  
− Applies to all countries of origin and all steel products , including downstream products, with 
no exceptions for development or FTA partners  
− No quota liberalisation , no national sub -quotas  
− Setting clear import limits , e.g.:  
o Flat steel: maximum 15% market share  
o Long steel: maximum 5 % market share  
o Stainless products: maximum 15% each  
− Quarterly quota allocation , not transferable to subsequent periods  
− Additional duty on import volumes above the quota , based on comparable safeguard mech-
anisms (e.g. 50% in the USA), cumulative with AD/AS tariffs  
− "Melted & Poured" rule  to clearly determine the origin of products  
− Annual review and adjustment of quotas  in line with market developments  
− Crisis mechanism  to respond to external demand or supply shocks in the short term  
− Opening  the instrument to steel -intensive downstream industries  that are also affected by 
market distortions  
 
 
 
 
  
Statement statement | EU Consultation   Side 3 
Facts in detail  
 
➢ Global overcapacity threatens the market balance  
 
The global steel industry is characterized by a structural imbalance between massively growing pro-
duction capacity and real market demand. While demand is stagnating or declining in many regions, 
production capacities are expanding at an alarming pace, esp ecially in Asia. According to OECD calcu-
lations, the global oversupply of steel is likely to rise from the current level of around 600 million 
tonnes to around 720 million tonnes by 2027 – equivalent to five times the total EU steel production. 
This overca pacity leads to considerable market distortions.  
 
The roots of this development lie primarily in China, where, after years of strong growth, a drastic 
slump in demand led to enormous export pressure from 2015 onwards. What began as a regional 
problem is now a global challenge: in addition to China, India,  the ASEAN countries, North Africa and 
the Middle East have also become new sources of massive overcapacity. India's announced capacity 
expansion is particularly worrying: At least 300 million tons of new capacity are to be built there by 
2030, around 80 p ercent of which will be in coal -based, CO2 -intensive blast furnaces. This planned 
production volume significantly exceeds the projected domestic demand and will inevitably push into 
the global market once the growth of India's domestic market loses momentu m. 
 
The result is global export competition with a destructive effect. Governments try to divert their 
overproduction – often at prices below production costs – in order to utilise their plants and cover 
high fixed costs. This strategy creates unequal competit ion, in which economically subsidized exports 
flow into open markets such as the EU, where they put massive pressure on domestic industry.  
 
➢ The EU is becoming an import destination  
 
The European Union – traditionally a location with a high level of industrial vertical integration and 
an open trade policy – is now the main target of these distorted export flows. In recent years, the EU 
has gone from being the largest net exporter of st eel to the world's largest net importer of steel. 
While other countries such as the USA seal off their markets through trade -restrictive measures – 
such as punitive tariffs or import restrictions – the European market remains largely unprotected.  
 
This has profound implications for the market balance. The import share of the EU steel market has 
risen continuously since 2015 and has almost doubled since then. Even more serious, the traditional 
link between demand and import volumes has dissolved: Nor mally, imports rise during growth 
phases and fall when demand falls. Since 2023, however, the opposite phenomenon has been ob-
served: demand in the EU is weak, but imports continue to rise. As a result, European manufacturers 
have to bear the full pressure of the weak market situation, while imported goods continue to gain 
market share.  
 
At the same time, a structural trade deficit is becoming entrenched along the entire value chain – 
from raw materials to semi -finished products and finished products. Prices in the domestic market 
have fallen to a historically low level due to the oversupp ly of imports, which has had a dramatic im-
pact on the profitability of steel companies in Germany and the EU and makes investments in future 
technologies almost impossible. The consequences are serious: production capacity in Germany and 
the EU will be irr etrievably closed.

➢ EU protection instruments no longer apply  
 
The EU's existing trade policy instruments, such as anti -dumping duties or safeguard measures in the 
form of tariff quotas, which were established in 2018 in response to US steel tariffs to protect against 
diversion effects from other third countries, have  not yet been able to effectively counter these de-
structive developments. Although selective protective measures against certain countries or products 
have been in place for years, they take effect too late, too selectively and with too little depth. The 
structural weakness lies in the fragmented and reactive nature of these measures.  
 
The current safeguard measures in the steel sector, which were designed as temporary protection 
against import pressure, expire in mid -2026. It is already evident today that their effect was limited. 
The quotas were successively liberalised, so that import  pressure could continue to increase and the 
measures became ineffective – despite minor administrative tightening. A comprehensive, long -term 
strategy to secure the European steel market has so far been lacking.  
 
➢ Demand: Introduction of a new EU trade defence instrument ("Post -Safeguard")  
 
In view of the structural challenges and the foreseeable intensification of import pressure, a far -
reaching change in European trade defence policy is essential. The steel industry welcomed the pro-
posal to introduce a new trade defence instrument announced  in the European Commission's Steel 
and Metals Action Plan on 19 March 2025. However, this 'post -safeguard' tool must be designed ef-
fective ly. A robust, comprehensive and flexible system is needed to protect the European steel indus-
try from unfair competit ion. 
 
➢ The core of such an instrument must be an effective import protection based on an intelligent 
tariff rate quota system. Unlike blanket tariffs or quantity restrictions, which are often legally 
challengeable or lead to market distortions, a quota tariff model (TQF) allows a controlled 
market openin g with clear protective mechanisms.  
➢ Clear upper limits must be defined for the permissible market shares of imports – 15% for flat 
steel and 5% for long steel – and quarterly quotas must be allocated.  
➢ Import volumes exceeding  these quotas should be subject to an effective additional tariff 
based on comparable safeguard measures, such as the US steel tariffs of 50%.  
➢ Such an instrument must also be fully applicable to all products in the steel value chain – in-
cluding downstream products – and must not include exemptions for developing or free trade 
partner countries.  
➢ A clear determination of origin via a "melted & poured" regulation is just as indispensable as 
a crisis mechanism for sudden drops in demand.  
 
Only such a strategically thought -out and long -term protection system can create an environment in 
which European steel companies can produce competitively and have the necessary planning secu-
rity to invest in the decarbonisation of production.  
 
➢ Goal: Ensuring competitiveness and capacity utilisation  
 
The steel industry is a key sector of European industry, both economically and strategically. It is the 
basis  for multiple  value chains – from the automotive industry , the mechanical engineering to the 
construction industry – and is at the same time  one of the most capital -intensive and energy -inten-
sive industries. Their competitiveness depends crucially on the fact that economically viable produc-
tion capacity utilisation can be achieved and maintained. A profitable steel industry requires capacity 
utilizat ion of at least 80 to 85 percent. Currently, it is significantly lower in the EU – in many cases 
below 70 percent – which leads to an existential threat to companies and their employees.

At the same time, the European steel industry is facing the challenge of decarboni sation. In line with 
the national and European climate targets, it aims to become climate -neutral, implement climate -
friendly production technologies and remain internationally competitive. However, this restructuring 
can only succeed if the companies operate in a fair market environment in which investments make 
economic sens e and can be planned in the long term.  
 
This now requires a strong trade policy defence instrument that effectively protects the European 
steel market from the destructive effects of global overcapacity and at the same time provides a sta-
ble basis  for the climate -friendly restructuring of the industry.

Executive Summary  
1. The EU steel capacity crisis : The European Steel and Metals Action Plan confirms the gravity of the 
EU steel crisis: EU steel capacity is significantly underutilized; production fell to its lowest level ever. 
From 2008 to 2023, capacities have been reduced by around 26 million tonnes.  An additional 9 
million tonnes of capacity were closed in 2024. The industry has lost 25% of its workforce, with an 
additional 8,000 to 13,000 job cuts already announced. 
2. EU de-industrialisation is a reality. Without decisive political action to defend industrial interests, 
EU steel production will continue to decline and risks imploding in a persisting low demand 
situation in Europe and globally. 
3. Overcapacity is structural, global, and worsening : Excess global steel capacity is structural and 
truly global, no longer confined to one country (e.g., China). Excess capacity is expected to explode 
to more than 700 million tonnes by 2027, up from around 600 million in 2024.  
4. Trade spillovers harm the EU market : Global overcapacity has spilled into the EU market, creating 
a persistent trade deficit and increasing import market shares, despite existing trade defence 
measures, including the safeguard action. 
5. Need for a highly effective trade measure : The Steel Action Plan calls for a “highly effective” new 
trade measure to protect and promote EU industrial capacity, replacing the safeguard.  
6. Import market shares need to be reset : Imports are undermining the viability of EU steel capacities 
and their decarbonisation. The new measure needs to reset viable import market shares that allow 
for workable capacity utilization rates as a necessary condition for profitability and 
decarbonisation. 
7. Wider impact across the value chain : Not only basic steel, but also steel -intensive downstream 
products are shifting from net exports to net imports. EU steel-intensive sectors and products need 
to be included in a broader trade action securing the European steel value chain. 
8. EU steel industry’s decline is not inevitable : In contrast to the capacity, production and job losses 
in the EU, the U.S. steel industry is building up new and additional steel capacities (around 9 million 
tonnes) benefitting from lower energy costs, green subsidisation, Buy US steel policy and stron g 
trade protection recently re-activating the blanket Section 232 National Security Steel Import Tariff 
(now at 50%)

Introduction 
The European steel industry requires a new trade tool that resets the situation back to a viable 
import market share taking into account demand, and implement this tool fast. A “polished” 
safeguard is NOT the highly effective measure the EU steel industry needs. 
The Steel and Metals Action Plan commits to install a new trade measure that will be highly 
effective in protecting and promoting European steel capacities in light of the existential threat 
of global excess capacity.  
This trade policy commitment has no meaning if not implemented in a context of radical 
industrial policy objectives securing the green transformation and viability of the European steel 
industry.  
Without that, economic resilience and security, including military capability build-up, will remain 
hollow words as the European steel industry will not, cannot, do the investments and 
commitments leaving its fate in the hands of governments as is happening today in the UK. 
The current and forward -looking picture is highly problematic: demand has fallen dramatically 
after Covid with no improvement in sight. Massive volumes of low cost material are spreading 
around in the world, while European costs are higher. Import penetration has extensively grown 
in the last 10 years due to spillover effects of global excess capacity and in a context of too weak 
steel safeguard measure, pushing out EU domestic producers both in the EU domestic market 
and in the export market. As a result, the integrated EU steel industry is at existential risk.  
The current safeguard measure must be replaced because its objective is not the one of the Steel 
Action Plan, which is securing and promoting steel capacity and the industries capabilities ; by 
fixing tariff-rate quota at a level of absolute import volumes of a past reference period and then 
massively increasing these quotas while EU steel demand has decreased, the safeguard TRQ 
regime has become irreparable to pursue the protection and promotion of EU steel capacities.  
Therefore, a further 'polished' Safeguard would certainly fail to delive r. What the EU steel 
industry urgently requires is a highly effective new trade measure that resets the situation back 
to a viable import market share. The safeguard allowed imports to surge dramatically , pushing 
EU steel capacities in idling and eventually into closures. Import market shares need to be 
identified from a period before global excess capacity spilled over through trade (before the first 
China steel export explosion of 201 5/2016) when capacity utilization was still viable as a 
condition for profitability and sustainability. This measure must be adopted swiftly and 
implemented without delay.

Global Steel Excess Capacity 
Global steel excess capacity has evolved into an existential threat to the viability of the industry 
and its jobs. While the EU steel industry has been adjusting its capacities, many other major 
economies have been expanding their capacities often far beyond domestic demand, including 
the U.S. Global steel demand is projected to grow only modestly in the medium term. In contrast, 
global steel excess capacity is expected to continue rising, with 165 million tonnes of new capacity 
additions projected for 2025 -27. In addition to China’s massive steel excess capacity, other 
markets in the world are building capacities that are not supported by domestic demand.  
Global excess capacity is expected to increase to 721 million tonnes by 2027, from an estimated 
602 million tonnes in 20241 representing a volume five times EU steel production.

Trade impact 
Excess capacity has triggered trade dynamics that are fueling unprecedented , market-distorting 
export flows -targeting the EU in particular – and turning the EU into the world’s largest import 
market.  
 
 
These dynamics encompass not only  massive Chinese exports 2, but also their  indirect trade 
impact, forcing third country-producers to redirect steel from depressed domestic market s to 
other regions, notably the EU. 
The industry is experiencing a shift from the China -centered to a truly global excess capacity, 
leading to a trade disaster.

Need for a comprehensive steel trade measure 
In response to the initial U.S. Section 232 steel import tariff in 2018, the EU implemented a 
safeguard regime applying a tariff-rate quota (TRQ) on steel imports . This aimed  to protect the 
EU steel industry and market from deflected steel imports resulting from rising protectionism in 
third countries, amid a context of massive global excess capacity.  
WTO and EU rules allow for safeguard action on ly on  an exceptional and temporary basis , 
imposing a relaxation of the measure over its life-time. However, global steel excess capacity and 
protectionism have become the new “normal” – no longer exceptional - and are expected to  
intensify further for many years, potentially decades, making the current situation  that is 
anything but not temporary.  
 
 
 
Moreover, the global situation of the steel sector has further deteriorated amid rising  geo-
political tensions and the proliferation of protectionist measures worldwide. The recent 
imposition of a blanket 50% import tariff by the United States' administration on all steel imports 
is creating massive trade deflection pressure towards the EU market3. Other countries have also 
adopted protectionist measures to safeguard their domestic industr ies, including South Africa 
and, most recently, India, which introduced a Safeguard import tariff of 12% on carbon flat steel 
products. In this context , a new, comprehensive EU steel trade measure is  urgently needed to 
replace the current temporary safeguard measure.

The European Steel and Metals Action Plan 
The Plan outlines a comprehensive set of policy actions, each indispensable to securing both the 
green transformation and viability of the European steel industry.  
Critically, the EU’s trade policy pillar aims to promote and protect EU steel capacities: 
 
A healthy domestic steel industry is also vital for national security, including military capability. 
Steel is indispensable for the production of military equipment and for  defence-supporting 
infrastructure that ensures mobility and logistics4.  
While the volume of steel directed into defence production represents a relatively small share of 
integrated steel production, it remains strategically significant. 
However, no company could afford to construct and operate a modern steel mill solely to supply 
defence needs. To ensure the availability of steel for defence , steel mills must attract sufficient 
and profitable commercial -civil business. Revenue from commercial markets  is essential to 
support the construction, operation, and maintenance of production capacity , as well as the 
ongoing upgrades, research and development required to continue to supply both current and 
future defence needs. Besides direct military equipment needs, steel is also essential for critical 
industries and infrastructure sectors that require high volumes of steel such as chemical 
production, communications, dams, energy, food production, nuclear reactors, transportation 
systems, water, and waste water systems.  
In this context, the European Steel Action Plan commits to install ing a new steel trade measure 
to replace the current safeguard regime , ensuring a highly effective level of protection against 
negative trade-related effects caused by global overcapacities. 
Critically, the Plan links comprehensive trade action to the stated objective of “promoting and 
protecting European industrial capacity”. This represents a paradigm shift in EU trade policy 
putting industrial capacities and viability at its core to ensure the green transition, economic 
resilience and security.

b. Designing a highly effective steel trade measure  
1: Imports substituting EU production have significantly depressed domestic capacity utilization, 
leading to capacity idling, closures and massive job losses. There is a compelling need for a new, 
unilateral EU action to effectively contain trade spill -over of global excess capacity . While  
addressing root causes of global excess capacity  requires coordinated international action , it 
must be pursued in parallel with decisive unilateral action. 
 
2: Addressing the global trade fall-out of excess capacity at the EU border must not  be confused 
with the “Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism ( CBAM), wich entails carbon intensity 
tariffication”, nor  with  existing Trade Defence Instruments – such as anti-dumping and anti-
subsidy measures - which are designed as  punctual actions to address exceptional situations of 
unfair trade practices. In fact, a  comprehensive and effective trade measure would reduce the 
need for complex and resource-intensive trade defence cases, which should remain reserved for 
limited, exceptional situations of unfair trade practices. 
 
3: The new trade measure must be comprehensive:  
3.1 It should cover all countries, based solely on the existence of global excess capacity (capacity 
versus demand). No exemptions or limitations  should be made  based on “non -market” 
considerations, individual countries’ levels of excess capacity or self -declared “developing 
country” status. This is  because trade spill-over effects result from export pressure across many 
economies, irrespective of the market -orientation, level of capacity or development of the 
domestic economy;  
 
3.2 The measure must c over all steel products , as  global excess capacity impacts the entire 
products’ range. 
 
4: To take into account the interests of all operators in the steel value chain (producers, users, 
service centres and distribution), the new trade measure should adopt a tariff-rate quota (TRQ) 
structure to avoid direct costs on steel -users (blanket percentage tariff) or risk of shortage 
(quantitative restriction in the form of absolute quota)5, as with the current safeguard measure.  
Downstream steel -intensive processors and users are increasingly exposed to the destructive 
trade impact of the global excess capacity , which  is now shifting down the steel value chain, 
mainly because of China. Many of these downstream products, with the exception of tubes and 
wire, were excluded from the scope of the current safeguard measure. Therefore, EUROFER calls 
for the legislative framework of the new steel trade measure to include a scope definition that

c. Viable import market shares  
Applying the above-mentioned objectives and design principles for a highly effective steel trade 
measure, EUROFER has developed a simulation for each steel segment , including carbon flat, 
carbon long, stainless flat and stainless long. 
This simulation demonstrates how the proposed framework could be applied in practice, 
including the setting of viable import market shares that support domestic capacity utilization 
and industry sustainability. 
 
CARBON FLAT SEGMENT: need for a new TRQ set at 15% import market share  
For carbon flat, a TRQ can be highly effective under the condition that imports are capped at a 
maximum import market share of 15%. This level is necessary to ensure a viable capacity 
utilization of the EU industry, as necessary condition for profitability and sustainability.  
Capacity utilization for carbon flat steel has fallen  to unviable levels , as import market share 
doubled from 14% in 2012 to 27% in 2024. In both years steel demand was at similar,  low levels.

EU demand for carbon flat steel is not expected to  return to pre -covid levels . T he global

A return to a viable, maximum import market share of max. 15%  - reflecting levels prior to the 
Chinese export surge of 2015 - is necessary to restore a workable capacity utilization of at least 
80%, which is conservatively recognized as the minimum threshold for sustainable operations . 
To achieve this, it is necessary to bring roughly 10 million tonnes of carbon flat steel back to 
European producers, corresponding to the volume lost to imports over the last decade.  
The production of carbon flat steel follows a so -called “waterfall process” , progressing from  
crude steel               to hot rolled          to hot-dipped galvanized          to organic coated. This 
integrated production chain  necessitates a holistic approach to the carbon flat segment. Treating 
the sub-products in isolation risks triggering a “communicating vessel” effect, where pressure in 
one part of the chain distorts the balance across the others. 
The concern over potential “shortage” in products downstream of crude steel and hot -rolled 
production is a misconception . The European steel industry has consistently demonstrated its 
ability to invest in finishing capacities to meet any demand. This precisely reflects the meaning 
and objective of the Steel Action Plan to “promote” European industrial capacities. 
Note: The product Grain -Oriented Electrical Steel (GOES) was not included in the safeguard 
product scope. Given its strategic functions – including its dual use applications, energy efficiency, 
and as a core material for electrificiation - this critical product must be integrated in the new TRQ 
measure replacing the safeguard. Import market shares of electrical steels have already reached 
exceptionally high levels. Additional modalities or new rules might be necessary if the “default” 
TRQ system does not protect sufficiently. 
 
CARBON LONG: need for a new TRQ set at 5% import market share 
With a crude steel production capacity of 70 million tonnes (Mt) and actual production output of 
just 46 Mt (9 Mt below with 2012), capacity utilization for long products in Europe stands at a 
critically low 66%. This is a result of a combination of higher imports, loss of exports and declining 
demand in Europe.  While the demand declined by 0.1 Mt in EU, imports surged by 2 Mt , 
increasing their markert share from 7.6% in 2012 to 12% in 2024 . During the same period, steel 
production for carbon long products decreased by 8.9 Mt.  
To materially improve capacity utilization, there is an urgent need to bring back steel production 
to Europe by limiting imports to a 5% market share. This would enable EU producers to regain 
2.6 Mt of production capacity for carbon long products. 
Implementing this action will allow carbon long steel producers to achieve a capacity utilization 
rate of around 69% (+3 % vs current 66%). While still significantly below the 80% cap utilization 
generally recognized as the minimum for economically  sustainable operations, this action - in 
combination with other ongoing measures - would provide a path towards restoring viability.

STAINLESS FLATS & LONGS: need for a new TRQ set at 15% import market share 
Since 2007, the EU stainless steel market – both segments - has been under sustained pressure 
from growing excess overcapacity. Stainless steel flats and longs have been experiencing 
increasing import pressure from countries like India, Indonesia, China, and Taiwan.  
Moreover, cheap exports of stainless -steel inputs (slabs, billets, and hot -rolled coils) have 
increased over the last ten years, creating a spill -over effect of global excess capacity and 
enabling circumvention of already imposed TDI measures. 
Consistently, EU stainless steel production capacity utilisation has initiated its decline in 2007, 
since the first strong wave of Chinese exports impacted the EU market. After a recovery and 
stabilisation period, a second, stronger, and more prolonged wave of export pressure began in 
2012 and continues today to impact the EU stainless steel industry.  
Like for carbon steel, stainless steel in the EU has reached economically unviable conditions, 
exacerbated by demand depression after Covid . N o recovery or meaningful improvement is 
expected.  
To restore viable operations, imports for the stainless flat and long segments must be capped at 
15 % of import market share, down from current levels of 23% and 29% registered respectively 
in 2023-24. The proposed level of import market share is justified and based on an historical 
analysis of the EU Stainless steel market and industry. 
The 2008-2012 reference period was chosen as it fully represents market dynamics prior to the 
onset of Chinese excess overcapacity spillover, followed by Taiwanese, Indian, and Indonesian.  
Over that period, imports share s were at 15,9 and 16,7 % respectively for flats and longs . This  
was associated – over the same period - with an average capacity utilisation of 73% for both 
macro segments. Today, capacity utilisation is at a very low, unviable level of 60%.  
A key concern is the persistently high level of imports of stainless steel products (longs and flats), 
which are marketed at pricing levels well below EU domestic ones.  
Moreover, the increasing capacity in the whole Asian region, the spill-over of the excess capacity 
and related cheap exports, the raw materials market distortions in countries such as China and 
Indonesia, coupled with heavy subsidisation of the stainless -steel industry, are all  factors that 
have contributed to further depress the stainless-market. This has resulted in prices that do not 
allow companies to recover viable levels  of production. EU domestic producers are constantly 
confronted with irrationally cheaper imports. 
In this context – considering the current demand scenario in 2023-2024 - an import share of 15% 
is essential for the both macro segments -  stainless steel flats and longs  – to restore viability. 
This must be however supported by  full deployment of TDI tools, including a ‘melt and pour’ 
regime to efficiently track the origin and tackle circumvention . Together, these measures are 
essential to regain competitive conditions against unfair imports and put the EU stainless steel 
industry back on a path to long-term sustainability.

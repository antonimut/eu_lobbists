European Commission’s recent proposal to set a 90% net emissions reduction target by 2040 
is welcomed by cities. They believe that it is a bold and essential step toward achieving the 
legally binding objective of climate neutrality by 2050 as established in the EU Climate Law.  
European Mayors strongly support this level of ambition.  According to the 2025 Eurocities 
Pulse Survey, climate action is ranked as the top political priority by 63% of city leaders. Yet 
regulatory barriers, insufficient funds, and weak multilevel governance remain obstacles.  
To ensure effective delivery of the 2040 target, Europe needs to improve its climate 
governance structures . The current regulatory framework often treats cities as passive 
implementers, rather than strategic partners.  
We urge the European Commission to create permanent and structured dialogue platforms 
between EU institutions, national governments, and cities. These platforms should feed into 
national and local energy and climate plans, recovery strategies, and regulatory packages. 
Cities must be involved early and meaningfully in shaping policies that they are ultimately 
responsible for delivering and knows more about the on-ground scenarios. 
Cities are ready to deliver, but need an unambiguous, coherent, and supportive policy and 
financial framework that empowers them to scale up climate action rapidly and inclusively. 
Domestic emission reductions must be the core of EU climate policy  
The Commission’s proposal introduces the option for up to 3% of the 2040 target to be 
achieved via high -quality international carbon credits from 2036 onward,  which creates a 
serious concern among cities.  While the use of Article 6 of the Paris Agreement to foster 
global cooperation is understandable, relying on carbon offsets , particularly when their 
governance and quality standards remain vague, could risk undermining the integrity and 
credibility of the EU climate goals. We urge the EU to remain focused on ambitious domestic 
emissions reductions. 
A just and inclusive transition is non-negotiable  
Climate neutrality must be reached  in a socially fair  way. Low -income households and 
vulnerable workers risk being left behind if access to clean housing, energy, and mobility 
remains, or becomes, unaffordable. The EU should provide direct support for households and 
cities, simplify access to funds, and create a permanent instrument for fair local transition 
projects. Investments in education, skills, and training, especially for youth, women, refugees, 
and workers in declining industries, are critical. Cities are well placed to coordinate this,  but 
they require resources and mandates. 
Our Future depends on today’s decisions  
The EU’s 2040 climate target is a milestone in the pathway towards climate neutrality and 
resilience by 2050. It will provide direction for strengthening Europe’s long -term 
competitiveness, energy security and affordability, in a climate neutral trajectory. The

An ambitious 2040 emission reduction target for the EU 
Key messages 
· European cities support the introduction of a legally binding 2040 EU target of a least 90% net emissions 
reduction to anchor Europe in the ambitious trajectory set by the Climate Law.  
· To meet an ambitious 2040 target, the European Commission must strengthen direct collaboration with 
and support for cities . National governments together with the EU must provide the right financial and 
legislative enabling conditions.  
· The EU, member states, and cities must unite to swiftly implement the Fit for 55 package. 
· The European Commission’s impact assessment should evaluate the future policy choices and investments 
needed to achieve the 2040 targets, including in cities, and their distributional impacts. 
· The transition must benefit all societal groups, including the disadvantaged . Investments must thus 
support households to shift to  sustainable options and be made in education, skilling and reskilling the 
workforce. 
· Today, a big share of emissions is left unchecked. While a few cities are leading the way on consumption-
based emissions, the Commission should set targets to reduce these emissions  and provide all 
stakeholders with a calculation methodology, together with training on how to measure them. 
· To reach net zero by 2050, we need natural sinks and permanent carbon capture technologies. A distinct 
target should be set for these, ensuring they focus solely on hard-to-abate emissions. 
· The EU should make climate adaptation integral to all EU policies and establish binding and measurable 
targets for EU and member states’ adaptation policies, taking into account local actions. 
· Finance and funding need to be better tailored to local needs to support the transformation. Key elements 
would be a comprehensive EIB investment programme for cities based on local needs, increased resources 
in the next MFF, mechanisms to connect cities with private financiers and more capacities to increase the 
update of innovative financing schemes.  
· Governance structures should better match the actual role of cities in the transition. Integrated strategies 
and timeline alignment are crucial to reach EU climate objectives.

Introduction 
Ambition and delivery  
Cities are committed to the 1.5°C target of the Paris Agreement  and to a climate neutral Europe by 2050.  
Understanding the specific urban responsibility to lead the way and achieve the ambition, European cities have 
supported the European Green Deal since its launch.1 It remains essential to emphasise the need for a  solid 
framework at national and EU level  that provides the right financial and legislative enabling conditions , 
supporting cities to deliver on the European ambition.  
On that condition, cities support the introduction of an ambitious legally binding 2040 EU interim target of a 
least 90% net emissions reduction compared to 1990 levels. The 2040 target setting must go beyond a 
remodelling exercise that considers the effects of the pandemic and Russia’s aggression in Ukraine. It should 
lay the ground for a wave of new policies that will be necessary to achieve net zero in 2050. Delaying action 
will slow down emissions abatement, fossil fuel phase-out, planned investments and electrification of energy 
systems. It  puts at risk the  goal of  climate-neutrality by mid -century, which would disproportionately and 
negatively impact cities and their residents.  
Building on the experience with the Mission for 100 Climate Neutral and Smart Cities  by 2030 and other EU 
initiatives,2 the European Commission must strengthen direct collaboration with and support for cities . Cities 
have ambitious targets to reduce GHG emissions, decarbonise mobility, become more resilient, and alleviate 
energy poverty. A number of EU programmes are delivering essential support for capacity building in local 
administrations and sustainable urban development. There is a need to further reinforce the effective ways of 
working between EU and city leaders to fully tap into the potential of cities.3   
Eurocities Pulse,4 a survey conducted with 92 Mayors in 28 European countries in 2023, revealed that the 
overarching priority for local leaders is to tackle climate change and speed up the implementation of climate-
related projects such as decarboni sing mobility, replacing the use of fossil fuels in energy use, and creat ing 
more green spaces in cities. The top investment priorities they identified to fight the energy crisis are 
sustainable mobility, building renovation, energy efficiency and the development of renewable/green energy.  
To support local ambitions and achiev e the climate neutrality target by 2050 at the latest, we need a swift 
adoption of the remaining files and a timely implementation of the European Green Deal. Also, the European 
institutions must ensure a quick agreement on the 2040 targets to link them with the mid-term review of the 
multiannual financial framework and developments in the Green Deal Industrial Plan , and provide long-term 
certainty for investors. Having the targets in place will also lay the ground for a critical review of the main 
sectoral policies and what is needed to reach the target. 
This ambitious transition towards climate neutrality should not leave anyone behind. The shift towards a more 
sustainable economy can become an opportunity to mitigate pre -existing patterns of social disparities. An

Green transition and social justice 
The European Union should set up a permanent financing instrument as part of a broader just transition 
strategy to support socially fair green initiatives locally.  To make sure it can really benefit local action s and 
plans, the use of these funds should be  directly manged by cities, or  decided through mandatory dialogues 
with the local level.  The financial instrument linking the energy transition in the European Union with the 
European Pillar of Social Rights should boost investment in reduc tion of carbon emissions while promoting 
social inclusion. 
While decarbonising the European economy will create new opportunities and better living conditions, there 
is a risk of negative impacts on people, especially disadvantaged and vulnerable groups. People at risk of 
poverty face not just the brunt of climate crisis but also the greatest barriers to accessing clean energy solutions 
for housing and transport. Support should be provided to low - and medium- income households to ensure 
they can access green solutions such as retrofitting their houses, solar panels or electric vehi cles, which 
currently have higher costs than fossil fuel -based options.  As a part of the strategy to deliver a socially fair  
energy transition, European cities are undertaking strategic interventions to promote energy-saving practices 
and housing renovations 5. They are supporting vulnerable citizens through integrated home renovation 
services and energy advice, boosting energy-efficient buildings, ensuring residents’ participation in renovation 
projects and switching to more sustainable energy systems. While cities are the best placed to identify which 
projects to prioritise and support, the financial effort must be shared across all levels of government.  
Decarbonising our economy also implies a reshaping of the labour market to generate net employment gains 
and the creation of new business sectors. However, labour demand and supply are often mismatched. Workers 
in carbon -intensive industries, usually low -skilled or vulnerable individuals, face job losses without the 
necessary skills or resources to transition into green jobs. Moreover, skills gaps, social norms and systemic 
barriers also signific antly impact women’s participation in renewable energy sectors  or climate mitigation 
occupations, often dominated by men.  
To avoid the risk of long-term unemployment for those people, it is important to invest in education, upskilling 
and reskilling and to provide support for job transitions. Cities can be key actors in nurturing high-quality green 
jobs and pav ing the way towards a true economy of wellbeing. Active labour market policies could target 
workers in declining industries with tailored training and vocational programmes adapted to their needs. 
Educational initiatives could also encourage women in STEM (sc ience, technology, engineering, and 
mathematics) fields from an early age.    
Cities play an important role in the provision of Continued Education and Training (CET) and skills development. 
Local strategy for CET and skills development relies on their collaboration with local actors and their proximity 
to their residents. Partnerships with civil society organizations are instrumental for outreach efforts, especially 
to develop targeted programs for more vulnerable groups; while partnerships with private companies enable 
cities to co-develop employment opportunities for these groups.

The twin green and digital transition 
The transformation of cities into innovative and sustainable places is a long and complex process that differs 
from city to city. Sustainable digital technologies and solutions are strong enablers for reaching climate 
neutrality in cities. By using data -driven decision-making, local governments can more easily and efficiently  
design and implement emissions reduction, for example, reduce food waste, optimise route planning and 
increase the amount of renewable energy. On the other hand, the rising demand for cloud computing, AI, and 
energy-intensive devices by ever greater urban populations has a substantial environmental footprint. 
Therefore, the green and digital transitions must be tackled in an integrated way to reap their full potential for 
long-term syst emic socioeconomic changes in cities , without undermining a sustainable use of natural 
resources. 
The Living -in.eu6 movement and  the Intelligent Cities 7 challenge are helpful initiatives  supporting the 
development of more multilevel and integrated urban governance approaches driving digital transformation 
in Europe. The EU must further support  these initiatives as part of developing a comprehensive response to 
the interactions between climate and digital policies. This can be done through raising the ambition for design 
and reuse of digital devices and networks , investment in the access, sharing and use of data, while also 
financially supporting cities to implement for example open data spaces, especially with regards to the mobility 
and energy sectors. 
A swift and successful delivery of the Fit for 55 package  
The success of the EU Green Deal objectives and a transformative pathway towards a clear milestone in 2040 
will depend on the determination of the EU, member states, and cities to implement the Fit for 55 package.  
Buildings  
Cities’ long-term vision is that new buildings do not produce any carbon emissions throughout their lifecycle; 
they are constructed and refurbished in zero -emissions construction sites and include shares of renewable 
energies and resources at the building or district level, ultimately developing into positive energy buildings .8 
Many cities are stepping up their efforts to decarbonise their built environment through renovations, energy 
system interventions, and decarbonisation of heating. Additionally, circular construction principles are key to 
address the use of resources and embodied emissions of buildings.  
Currently, buildings are responsible for 40% of the total energy consumption and  36% of greenhouse gas 
emissions in Europe. The agreement9 on the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive needs to pave the way 
for decarbonised and resilient buildings, defining a clear trajectory with successive and predictable increases 
in the standards that will help local and regional governments, industry, and households to adjust.  
Most buildings in Europe are still heated through natural gas, with 65 million boilers installed. Climate 
neutrality by or before 2050 won’t be possible without a transformation in heating systems. We support tthe 
current European Parliament’s mandate for a phase -out of fossil fuel heating systems from all buildings, 
including existing ones, by 2040 at the latest. To reach the 2040 targets and follow the scientific advice to be 
on the trajectory of neutrality by 2050, the implementation of these measures should not be delayed.

Improving the energy efficiency of buildings and appliances should be accompanied by stepping up efforts to

benefits such as increasing energy security, alleviating energy poverty, improving air quality, etc. Actions to

A swift implementation of the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive will be pivotal to reaching the 2030

Transport  
Despite the boost to decarbonisation coming from the Fit for 55 package, transport  GHG emissions are still 
expected to increase and peak around 2025 in Europe. 10 The demand for transport has increased, 11 and by 
2040, most of the transport sector is unlikely to have achieved its total decarbonisation. Therefore, EU policies 
must support the speeding up of decarbonisation and support the potential of measures to limit transport 
demand to reduce the need for long distance travel daily, and ensure a modal shift towards more active and 
collective modes of local transport.   
The EU should complete missing railway connections and extend the rail network in Europe to provide medium 
and long -distance sustainable options for passengers. The EU should also use a wide range of measures 
including fuel taxation to limit the use of aviation transport demand, at least until zero-emission technologies 
are available and air and noise pollution can also be reduced . The EU should support public authorities to 
continue to prioritise active and collective modes of transport, limit investments in infrastructures for 
motorised transport, increase the convenience of land use for the other modes, and connect the different 
modes of transportation with physical or digital solutions. 
For freight transport, concluding the decarbonisation transition of the heavy-duty vehicles sector in 2040 will 
be necessary. The European Commission has proposed a -90% objective by 2040 for trucks as part of the 
regulation on new CO2 emission standards for heavy-duty vehicles. The EU mitigation targets for 2040 should 
consider this proposal and create the conditions for complete decarbonisation of freight transport, shifting 
from road to rail or other modes of transport. In parallel with decarbonisation, the share of rail freight and 
inland waterway freight transport should be further increased, following the ‘avoid-shift-improve’12 principles. 
Further investments in the relevant infrastructure (e.g., multimodal freight terminals) will also be necessary 
around cities and critical transport infrastructure to further decarbonise the sector and support decarbonised 
last-mile delivery. 
Nature 
Nature has a significant role in reaching climate neutrality by 2050 and a high decarbonisation target by 2040. 
Yet nature is in a worrying state of decline, with more than 80% of habitats in poor condition. Nature-based 
solutions (NBS), such as the rewetting of peatlands, have huge potential to store carbon and mitigate climate 
change, storing twice as much carbon as all the world’s forests combined.  When compared to typical ‘grey’ 
approaches to urban development, NBS are, on average, 50% more cost-effective and deliver 28% more added 
value.13

Looking ahead: cities’ recommendations for the future 
Assessing the impact of 2040 targets  
The European Commission ’s forthcoming impact assessment , considering the advisory board’s scientific 
advice, will be the necessary starting point to lay the ground for the 2040 legislative proposal based on a solid 
understanding of the policy changes needed in the medium term. The analysis of the possible transition 
pathways will need to take into account the following aspects: 
- the current gaps to achieving the targets, for example, the social and distributional aspects to inform 
the mitigation measures needed to ensure a just transition; 
- scenarios that are not only technology -oriented but assess the potential impacts of decarbonisation 
policies on all social groups and propose solutions to address them;  
- integration of climate impacts and resilience in the scenarios, both in terms of the impacts they will 
have on critical infrastructures and population and of the co -benefits of coordinated action tackling 
both adaptation and mitigation; 
- the investment gaps to achieve the 2040 target and the need for public funding/finance on one hand 
and for private investments on the other hand; and 
- an estimation of the environmental and climate impact of digital solutions and appliances in the 
different scenarios, also in terms of GHG emissions, environmental lifecycle of digital products, and 
benefits. 
The impact assessment should already point towards a critical assessment of the further policy choices needed 
to accelerate the transition to climate neutrality, including new policies and those directives and regulations 
under revision as part of the Fit for 55 package to make them fit for the 2040 targets. 
Scope 3 and consumption-based emissions  
To implement robust climate mitigation policies, cities monitor the emissions produced in their territory. Their 
climate mitigation plans include direct emissions  (scope 1 emissions ), which cover direct emissions from 
industries, cars, trucks, and public transport; direct emissions from fuel and gas consumption produced by city 
services, households, and companies; and direct emissions from waste treatment, taking place within the city’s 
boundaries. These plans also cover indirect emissions from the energy purchased by households or companies 
in the city (scope 2 emissions).  
Cities can reduce direct emissions (scope 1) and emissions from the energy (scope 2) produced within their 
territory. However, cities don’t have the competence to act on the emissions of the services and goods 
produced outside but consumed within the city’s boundaries. Those emissions (scope 3) cover a wide range of 
emission sectors, such as food production and consumption, waste treated outside cities, and consumer goods 
and services produced outside the city but used within the city. These emissions represent a large part of the

cities’ carbon footprint but are often left unmeasured and unchecked, and in most cases, cities don’t have the 
competencies required to reduce them.  
Still, delivering a climate-neutral Europe by 2050 will require dramatically reducing ‘scope 3’ emissions. Some 
European cities are setting strategies to tackle those. Paris aims to reduce consumption emissions by 40% by 
2030 and Stockholm by 50% by 2030 . Amsterdam’s circular strategy aims to reduce the economy's 
environmental impact through three consumption emission sectors: food consumption and organic waste, 
consumer goods and the built environment.14  
Urban consumption drives change within and outside city’s borders. Thanks to their purchasing power, cities 
can exercise pressure on the market and steer the transition, for example, towards bio-based materials in the 
built environment, lower-carbon cement and reuse of buildings; circular design of (reusable, repairable and 
recyclable) goods consumption; and behavioural change. They can also work with rural areas around the city 
through agreements on renewable energy or food production.  Achieving drastic em issions reduction will 
require action at all levels, from European and national to local and individual.  
A strong circular EU legislative framework is needed to steer the EU single market towards sustainable goods 
and services (e.g. construction products, textiles, electronics, and packaging). The European Commission 
should:  
- set up transparent and consistent targets to reduce ‘scope 3’ emissions in the EU;  
- develop a user-friendly calculation methodology  at EU level , together with member states, local 
governments, the private sector, including SMEs, as well as civil society; and 
- provide support and training to cities on how to use it and enable knowledge transfer between cities.  
This would help guide strategic planning and decision making, improve transparency and accountability of all 
and accelerate the climate transition. Cities could then use this framework as leverage to contribute to 
reducing the scope 3 emissions. 
Carbon removals 
While reductions of emissions should take precedence, both natural carbon sinks and technological removals 
with permanent storage will be required to meet the 1.5 °C target. Mitigation, adaptation and natural carbon 
sinks should remain the priority in Europe . However, natural carbon sinks like the ocean and forests  are not 
permanent. Climate change and human activities make these sinks even more vulnerable, sometimes even 
turning them into carbon emitters (e.g. peatlands). In parallel with natural sinks, carbon capture and removal 
technologies with permanent storage are needed to counteract hard-to-abate emissions and reach net zero.  
To allow the permanent removal industry to grow and become efficient in delivering on the volumes predicted 
by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), investments need to start now, while the pace and 
volume of deployment will be determined by cost in relation to the marginal abatement cost of emissions. As 
per the IPCC recommendations, a guiding principle should be ‘emissions reductions first’  to consider natural 
carbon sinks and carbon removals as complements, and not substitutes, of emissio ns reductions. A robust 
framework should be developed to encourage the wise deployment of carbon removal technologies.

Carbon capture technologies and projects should be applied with respect to the waste hierarchy. And circular 
economy should always be the way forward . According to Eurostat, 23% of household waste in Europe was 
landfilled in 2021. Waste incineration is necessary to take care of the waste that cannot be recycled and phase

waste hierarchy should be the priority , and carbon capture technologies should intervene when incineration 
is needed. 
• Energy 
If no other option is available, carbon capture and storage could be a mitigation option for process -related 
industrial emissions such as cement production. In electricity production, carbon capture and storage in fossil 
fuel-based electricity production may prolong the age of fossil fuels at the expense of moving to renewable 
energy and other zero-carbon technologies. Under all scenarios for meeting the climate goals of the Paris 
Agreement, phase-out of fossil fuels is necessary.  
• Biomass  
Sustainable bio energy-based carbon capture technologies (BECCS) are a cost-efficient way to handle hard-to-
abate emissions. In Europe, there is already a potential for 200 million tonnes of biogenic carbon removal per 
year based on existing sources. 15 RED III, the EU Deforestation Regulation, and the work on a biodiversity 
strategy for the EU are important developments to ensure that biomass play s a key role in a sustainable 
transition away from fossil fuels. However, bioenergy-based carbon capture technologies can lead to harmful 
practices in forestry and jeopardise biodiversity. Devoting large land areas to bioenergy crops could raise food 
prices and increase water demand. Biomass and biofuel combustion could lead to increase d air pollution. 
Norms, standards, strict forest management practices, and location-specific rules should lower the risk.  
• Financing carbon removals  
The costs of permanent removals are still high, and only first movers are committing significant funds to 
acquire such removals. Consequently, since the build-up of the industry needs to happen in the absence of a 
compliance market, government and other aid will be required in the coming years to kick -start the industry 
and lower the threshold for private sector purchases of removal credits. It is essential to develop a framework 
where public and private capital can co -fund the industry, based on the polluters pay principle , to minimise 
the costs for taxpayers. 
To ensure that permanent removals are not impacting the reduction trajectories of the Emission Trading 
System (ETS) and the Effort Sharing Regulation (ESR), a separate target should be set up for such removals, and 
their role as an alternative to emission allowance be carefully handled to ensure that they only target hard-to-
abate emissions. Offsetting shouldn’t become cheaper than mitigation and shouldn’t undermine long -term 
decarbonisation. 
In parallel, introducing certificates for carbon removals would foster a voluntary market to ensure that carbon 
removals are monitored, verified, and accounted for. This should help prevent greenwashing and false green 
claims.

• Industrial partnerships for carbon removals  
Partnerships between industry, cities and the EU to help build carbon capture and storage capacities  are 
necessary to reach the 2040 ambition . The European Commission and national authorities should support  
partnerships between cities and industries in CO2 hubs . These hubs could act as labs in cities, and projects 
would develop around them. 
A notable example is the ‘Ghent Carbon Hub’ pilot under the Connecting Europe Facility for Energy, managed 
by Fluxys Belgium, North Sea Port and ArcelorMittal Belgium. The EU financed a study on a CO 2 terminal in 
Ghent combined with a CO 2 pipeline network to Wallonia – an important step towards the investment 
decision. The ‘Ghent Carbon Hub’ will be able to process 6 million tons of CO2 each year, representing  about 
15% of Belgium’s industrial CO2 emissions.  
The EU should stimulate the investment in an efficient common infrastructure for the transportation of CO₂ 
from points of capture to sources of use as well as sites of storage , through European Projects of Common 
Interest and the EU Innovation Fund. 
Mainstreaming climate adaptation in all EU policies  
Weather and extreme climate events have caused economic losses of €560 billion in the EU between 1980 and 
2021 and nearly 195,000 fatalities.  
The European Commission must use the 2040 targets as an opportunity to further mainstream climate 
adaptation in EU policies. All EU member states have adopted an adaptation strategy, and the last EU 
adaptation strategy is a first step in the right direction. However, there is a need for more coordinated action 
at the EU level to ensure adaptation is mainstreamed in EU policies and that member states will do the same 
at their level.  
The World Meteorological Organisation of the United Nations declared the beginning of July 2023 the hottest 
week on record for the planet.16 Heatwaves are becoming more frequent and will continue to do so under all 
climate scenarios, as reported by the European Environment Agency. The increase in temperatures and 
extreme weather events such as severe flooding, droughts, and wildfires is putting European citizens and 
economies under huge pressure, and the effects of climate change will continue to be felt and increase for 
many years, even within the most optimistic trajectories under a 1.5-degree scenario.  
The direct impacts of climate change are felt locally. They cascade through urban systems , leading to a wide 
range of secondary impacts on the economy, human health, social wellbeing, ecosystems, physical 
infrastructure, and overall quality of life and functioning of a city.  Climate change impacts will also deepen 
existing divisions and social inequalities. The EU adaptation strategy acknowledges the importance of cities 
and the importance of just resilience, with particular attention to speeding up the implementation of local 
plans and actions.  
Many cities have already adopted local climate adaptation strategies, for example through the Covenant of 
Mayors initiative, to reduce the impact of climate change and make their territories more resilient. As cities 
progress towards mainstreaming adaptation across local policies, increased technical and financial support is 
needed to design, implement, and measure the impact of their plans. The EU Adaptation Strategy is a step in

Financing the transition 
Achieving the 2030 climate target at the EU level will require investments of around €1,040 billion per year,17 
with a strong reliance on private finance. However, for cities accessing private financing is challenging without 
public project development assistance, without seed money  and due to the scale of investment needed and 
the wide breadth of project s, both smaller and larger scale . In the 2040 horizon, there is a need for a much 
broader approach to financing the climate transition, which must stimulate reflections around tax policies in 
Europe more generally, to ensure that private and public funding can significantly increase in size and scale.   
Public funding, backed up with solutions to reduce the cost of capital , is an essential leverage for attracting 
private investments. It has the highest fiscal multiplier among key expenditure classes, crowding private 
resources. However, as fiscal space is being reduced more in municipalities compared to other government 
levels, the lack of public funding commitments means that private investment is harder to crowd in. Ensuring 
that cities have adequate investment capacity – be it through fiscal decentralisation, involvement in the 
development of the National Investment Plans as part of the EU’s economic governance, or direct funding – is 
paramount to the scale of infrastructure and economic transition needed.  
EU funding is and will continue to be essential for all cities and their capacity to deliver on the objectives of 
climate neutrality and tackling inequalities on the ground. The next multiannual financial framework will be  
essential to review if EU’s funding programmes are fit for delivering an accelerated transition and ensure 
revenue mechanisms which grow EU’s own resources. Increased resources should in turn support the green, 
just and digital transformation, including scaling up direct support for the absolutely essential transformation 
of urban areas. Post-2030, the Commission should consider extending and strengthening the Social Climate 
Fund to mitigate potentially adverse distributional effects and earmark  Emission Trading System  (ETS) 
revenues for a just climate transition locally.

A multi-level governance fit for the climate challenge 
Policy choices made at the EU level now and in the next political mandate regarding collaborating with, 
supporting and empowering cities – in partnership with regions and national governments – will be critical to 
achieving the necessary transformation at scale in Europe.  
When it comes to climate mitigation and the governance of the energy union, the current ways of working in 
most member states are inadequate in terms of ensuring that cities are included as partners for the 
development, implementation and management of national plans for energy and climate, for example for the 
National Energy and Climate Plans. The regulation on the Governance of the Energy Union mandates the setup 
of national dialogues with local governments . Still,  they have  often been  limited to ad -hoc a nd short 
consultations on draft plans.  
In the future, the governance structures need to better match the actual role of cities in the transition , with 
the establishment of dialogue platforms for a constructive, ongoing, and genuine multilevel dialogue on 
climate and energy at the national level. The dialogue platforms would deliver several co-benefits: continuous 
political support, feedback loops, shared responsibility , and better implementation of the necessary actions. 
This should also support a joined-up approach to other initiatives, such as the National Recovery Plans or the 
building renovation strategies, climate adaptation plans and different initiatives happening at the regional and 
local levels. 
Integrated strategies and alignment are crucial to reach EU objectives, and the EU 2040 target is an excellent 
opportunity to achieve this . Cities have a crucial role in implementing socially fair transition policies by 
providing essential services, measures to address energy poverty and other targeted support to protect 
vulnerable groups and low -income households adversely impacted by  the cost of living. Therefore, member 
states should also align  their national energy and climate plans  (NECPs) with Social Climate Plans, which will 
include targets and indicators for the groups targeted by the Social Climate Fund and involve cities in the 
preparation of the plans in accordance with the Social Climate Fund Regulation.  
Finally, consistency is necessary to efficiently implement the EU energy and climate targets. EU member states 
must develop several plans, including the NECPs, REPowerEU chapters, the social climate plans , and nature 
(restoration) strategies, which should all be coherent in their scope and actions . However, the timelines of 
these different planning processes do not  align, hindering their efficient implementation. More careful 
coordination in terms of timelines and processes will also be key to ensuring effective participation at the local 
level in the design and implementation of these plans. Cities often have limited resources and capacities to get 
engaged, but their contribution is crucial to successful implementation. The European Commission and 
member states should aim to reduce the administrative burden and the adverse impact of multiple planning 
and reporting processes through a streamlined approach to these plans.

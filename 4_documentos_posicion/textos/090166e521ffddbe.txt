Introduction....................
On July 2 nd 2025, after over one year of delays, the European Commission published its legislative 
proposal to amend the European Climate Law and establish a 2040 climate target. This proposal 
comes just before the European Union prepares to submit its updated Nationally Determined Contri-
bution (NDC) with a 2035 target to the UN by September 2025, ahead of COP30 in Brazil, in line with 
commitments under the Paris Agreement. 
The European Climate Law, agreed in 2020, is the framework law which establishes the overarching 
legal framework under which other climate policy must operate. It is primarily known for establishing 
the world’s first legally binding climate neutrality goal across multiple countries, for establishing the 
European Scientific Advisory Board on Climate Change (ESABCC), and for establishing the target to 
reduce emissions by net 55% by 2030 compared to 1990 levels.
But this crucial moment is about more than fulfilling procedural obligations. 
The climate targets for 2035 and 2040 will define Europe’s environmental, 
economic and geopolitical strategy, and will signal whether the EU remains 
committed to become the first climate-neutral continent of the world. 
From a strategic perspective, near-term action, and clear and ambitious targets are essential not only 
to meet climate neutrality by the half of this century, but also to enhance Europe’s strategic autono-
my, increase energy security and strengthen economic resilience. These priorities are central to the 
EU Clean Industrial Deal, which aims to turn decarbonisation into a driver of sustainable growth by 
lowering energy costs, boosting demand for clean products, securing critical raw materials, and mo-
bilising investment and innovation.
Until recently, the Commission had consistently supported a net emissions reduction target of 90% 
(compared to 1990 levels) without flexibilities through previous official communications and an im-
pact assessment. This is in line with the lower end of the ESABCC's recommendations .
While the reaffirmation of the 90% target for 2040 in the proposal is both welcome and 
necessary, the recently published proposed amendments introduce concerning possible 
loopholes which would dilute the target from inside: the use of international credits after 2036, 
sectoral flexibility and the integration of permanent removals in the EU Emissions Trading System 
(ETS). Such flexibilities carry significant risks to delay climate action, weaken domestic investment 
signals and undermine Europe’s credibility ahead of the international negotiations ahead of COP30. 
Major companies have voiced support for high and credible EU targets, including associations repre-
senting Nordic energy companies.
This brief explores the proposal in further detail, questioning the credibility of flexibilities, why they 
should not be pursued, and what safeguards are necessary to minimise damage from their potential 
inclusion. This document shows that they must be constrained to ensure the target remains a credi-
ble compass for investments and climate neutrality.

The need for a clear path to be 
fit for 90% 
The Commission’s proposal to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 90% by 2040 is both scientifically 
sound and strategically necessary. To deliver on this ambition, the target must be made credible and 
investable through separate and limited sub-targets for permanent carbon removals and the 
Land-Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) sector. 
Conclusions from the 2024 Commission’s 
Impact Assessment:
“Target Option 3 with a range of 90-95% is the preferred option . It provides the 
best balance between climate ambition and contribution to a fair share of the global 
carbon budget to meet the Paris Agreement temperature goals on the one hand, and 
feasibility on the other.”
“This target is also the most effective in ensuring the EU’s security of energy 
supply and strategic autonomy, with an energy import dependency ratio 3 percent -
age points lower than the “baseline” Option 2 and a reduction in fossil fuel imports of 
4%. It is also the option that best protects the EU against the negative socio-economic 
impacts of potential fossil fuel price shocks in future.”
“By supporting a higher development of low-carbon technologies, Option 3 
would thus increase the positioning of the EU in the global race to clean tech -
nologies and solutions . Finally, in addition, the increased energy independence is a 
strong advantage for the competitiveness of the EU industry, in reducing its exposure 
the international markets volatility.”
“This target is the most effective in terms of bringing the EU to climate neutrality by 
2050. With the lowest GHG budget, it also provides the strongest leadership 
from the EU and the most credible push to the EU’s partners worldwide  on the 
need and opportunities for accelerating climate action.”

Moreover, the 2024 Draghi report on the future of European competitiveness makes a strong case 
that Europe can become more competitive through decarbonisation. Reduced fossil fuel de-
pendency, green technologies and clean infrastructure are strategic growth opportunities 
if they are enabled by coherent and coordinated policies. 
A 90% domestic reduction target allows for a realistic path, leaving time for hard-to-abate sectors 
to scale up solutions while providing a strong and stable horizon for businesses. Academic analysis 
shows that insufficient near-term emission reductions are locking in long-term dependence on car -
bon removals, with high delivery and governance risks. This is also an opportunity to reduce import 
dependency, for instance by investing in clean energy.
Delivering climate neutrality does require significant upfront investment, but delaying action would 
multiply costs. Without greater ambition and immediate action, the world risks locking in carbon-in -
tensive infrastructure and pushing temperatures toward 2.6–3.1°C . This increases the likelihood of 
overshoot and irreversible climate impacts.
To provide the predictability that industry and investors need, the EU must adopt separate targets for 
permanent removals and the LULUCF sector. The European Parliament has already voiced support for 
this approach early in its mandate. For LULUCF, a cap is needed as already done in the 2030 frame-
work to avoid over-reliance on reversible carbon sinks, while maintaining target ambition at least at 
current levels. 
A clear framework will help de-risk investments and support planning across sectors. But targets 
alone are not enough, and the entire Fit-for-90 package must be designed to drive progress toward 
climate neutrality while strengthening European competitiveness, resilience and leadership. 
In July 2025, the International Court of Justice (ICJ) released an advisory opinion on the obligations 
of states regarding climate change. Drawing on international climate treaties, customary international 
law and international human rights law, the Court concluded that States have a legal duty to protect 
the climate system. This includes submitting NDCs that reflect their highest possible ambition, with 
each successive NDC showing progression. Developed countries (Annex I under the UNFCCC) have 
heightened responsibilities to lead in global emissions reductions. 
Ambition now lowers the risk of future legal consequences. The ICJ clearly states that failure to sub-
mit or implement adequate NDCs, or to effectively regulate greenhouse gas emissions, may 
breach international obligations. Such failures could trigger legal action, with courts potentially re-
quiring states to revise and strengthen inadequate NDCs. The Court also confirms that climate harm 
can be attributed to states. The ICJ advisory opinion represents a critical shift:  climate inaction is no 
longer just a policy failure but a potential breach of international law. The EU must treat ambition in 
climate targets not just as a political imperative, but as a legal and human rights obligation.

The loopholes on the table
In the proposed amendments by the Commission, several possible ‘flexibilities’ have been introduced:
1. A non-linear pathway towards 2040
2. The possible introduction of international credits
3. Further reliance on carbon removals
4. Further flexibility between economic sectors
A weaker trajectory towards 2040
The most questionable option that is reportedly being discussed is the establishment of a target for 
2035 which is not consistent with a trajectory towards a net 90% emission reductions by 2040. 
In the context of the Paris Agreement, the EU must submit a 2035 climate target by September 
2025 at the latest, postponed from an earlier, missed, deadline of February 2025. This applies to all 
Parties to the Paris Agreement. Since this requirement sits outside ordinary EU procedures, it only 
requires agreement amongst EU Member States, potentially sidelining the European Parliament and 
the ordinary co-decision process.
Since the European Climate Law required that the Commission propose a target for 2040 by June 
2024, the expectations until now has been that the EU would agree to a 2040 target which would 
then inform the target-setting for 2035. Given the substantial delays in the process, firstly attributed 
to the EU elections and subsequently for unclear political reasons, there is a real risk that the 2035 
target could be agreed and submitted to the UNFCCC before there is agreement on the 
2040 target. 
Therefore, the attention must now turn towards the EU Member States’ intentions with regards to the 
2035 target.
Broadly speaking, three types of trajectories are possible within the current context:
1- A slower trajectory with slower emissions cuts before 2035 and an acceleration of annual 
cuts by 2040, reaching climate neutrality by 2050
2- A linear trajectory with a consistent rate of emission cuts between 2030 and 2040, reaching 
climate neutrality by 2050
3- A faster trajectory with more emission cuts before 2035 and slower rate of reductions to -
wards 2040, reaching climate neutrality by 2050

Since greenhouse gases accumulate in the atmosphere, particularly CO2, it is not only the annual rate 
of emissions which matters but the total cumulative emissions over time. This is the key reason why 
the concept of a ‘greenhouse gas budget’ was introduced in the European Climate Law. Effectively, it 
means the shape of the trajectory is just as important as the target for any given year.
Figure 1 represents 3 highly illustrative examples of such trajectories and highlights that a slower rate 
of reductions in the shorter term means higher cumulative emissions even if the EU’s climate neutral-
ity target is met at the same time in all three pathways. 
The slower trajectory results in a relaxation of efforts in the 5-year period between 2030 and 2035, 
followed by an acceleration towards 2040, and another relaxation towards 2050. Overall, the EU’s 
cumulative emissions would amount to approximately 17.6 billion tonnes of CO 2e emissions for the 
period 2030 to 2050 (Figure 2).
The linear trajectory results in a constant annual reduction of emissions by 2040, followed by a relax-
ation towards 2050, totalling approximately 16.2 billion tonnes of CO2e emissions between 2030 and 
2050 (Figure 2).
The faster trajectory results in an acceleration of reducing between 2030-2035, followed by a relax-
ation towards 2040, and another relaxation towards 2050. This pathway generates 14.7 billion tonnes 
of CO2e between 2030 and 2050 (Figure 2).

Diluting domestic action with 
questionable international 
credits
One proposed flexibility which is at the forefront of the discussions is the potential inclusion of inter-
national credits into the EU’s 2040 climate target. While framed by some as a pragmatic solution, al-
lowing such credits into the EU climate target risks diluting domestic climate ambition and weakening 
its ability to build a resilient and competitive clean economy.
In its current form, the European Climate Law is domestic, meaning it only applies to activities which 
occur within the collective national boundaries of the EU. The inclusion of credits in the target con -
tradicts scientific recommendations and was not included in the Commission’s dedicated Impact As-
sessment, further raising concerns about whether the impact of their potential inclusion has been 
properly evaluated. 
In the Commission’s proposal, the use of international credits may only be permitted after 2036, and 
must meet high-quality criteria based on Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, although these have not 
been clearly spelled out. While these safeguards are necessary and welcome, allowing these credits 
to count toward the domestic target is a significant risk. In addition, introducing them before 2036 
would be premature as a strong dedicated EU legal framework would need to be put in place, and 
their inclusion could undermine the ambition of the 2035 NDC. 
International credits represent emissions reductions or carbon removals occurring outside of the EU, 
generated through activities such as forest conservation, renewable energy, energy efficiency, Car -
bon Dioxide Removals (CDR) or Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS). Several studies have raised con-
cerns about the integrity of these credits, highlighting shown some cases of poor and questionable 
quality.

Article 6 of the Paris Agreement
Article 6 of the Paris Agreement is the section that lays out how countries can voluntarily 
cooperate to achieve their NDCs.
•	 Article 6.2 – Cooperative approaches
Allows countries to trade Internationally Transferred Mitigation Outcomes (ITMOs) to -
wards their NDCs. These are decentralized transactions but must follow agreed ac -
counting rules to avoid double counting.
•	 Article 6.4 – UN-supervised crediting mechanism
Creates the new and centralised “Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism” which is de -
signed to generate tradeable credits from projects that contribute to sustainable devel-
opment and to the mitigation of greenhouse gas emissions. This mechanism is overseen 
by a UN Supervisory Body.
Bellona’s assessment
Almost a decade after the Paris Agreement, the Article 6 mechanisms have only just 
started to generate credits and exchange units in the past couple of years. While the 
bilateral exchanges facilitated under Article 6.2 could be tied to the EU’s own stringent 
approaches, the centralised exchanges under Article 6.4 are primarily subject to the 
methodologies which are still being developed. For example, debates are still ongoing as 
to how to assess the permanence of carbon removal projects, with deep implications of 
the credibility of credits generated from such projects. It is possible these requirements 
would substantially undercut the EU’s ongoing Carbon Removal Certification Frame -
work (CRCF) methodologies.
Therefore, it is difficult to ascertain the credibility and climate value of these credits. 
Initial assessments suggest that only 1 in 26 credits are the result of genuine emission 
reductions. While this is primarily the result of legacy Clean Development Mechanism 
credits being allowed, it does not bode well for the future of the new mechanism. 
Beyond the question of credibility, purchasing carbon credits from third countries clearly 
undermines the purpose of the Clean Industrial Deal and the findings of the Draghi Re-
port which highlight the need to further invest in domestic decarbonisation projects to 
jointly combat climate change and retain European industrial competitiveness.

Although the exact nature and role of the international credits remain vague, some concerns can 
already be raised in this context, namely that changing the scope of the law from domestic to 
international raises multiple questions as to the EU’s commitment to meeting climate neu -
trality on the European continent, and that it would set a risky global precedent , as well as 
the questionable credibility of the international credits to be included.

EU investments in emissions reductions projects that deliver clear benefits for local ecosystems and 
communities are needed. However, they should be additional to, not counted toward domestic cli -
mate targets. In other words, international credits should be a complement, not a substitute for do -
mestic action. This position is supported not only by Bellona Europa, but also 126 other civil society 
organisations, academia and businesses. 
While international credits are excluded from the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) - and it should 
continue to be the case -, any use of such credits toward the 2040 target should be strictly limited. 
They should meet high-quality standards and be backed by a robust Monitoring, Reporting and Veri-
fication (MRV) system. If this moves forward, a dedicated EU legal framework must be established for 
their use. There should be clear conditionalities, and the use of such credits should be transparent. 
The Öko-Institut recommended that:
•	 Carbon credits should only be used to go beyond what can be achieved domestically (i.e., be-
yond a 90% emissions reduction). The use of credits should decline after the third NDC period 
(2036-2040) to meet the 2050 climate neutrality target. 
•	 Any credits used to counterbalance fossil CO₂ emissions must come from carbon removals 
with no or negligible reversal risks, ensuring climate equivalence.
•	 Payments for carbon credits must not count as climate finance. Credit purchases should be 
accounted for separately to avoid undermining financial commitments to developing coun -
tries.
•	 The EU should not purchase carbon credits from countries that use the averaging approach 
which allows them to report their emissions reductions as an average over a multi-year period 
(e.g., 2026–2030), rather than reporting what they achieved year by year.

The role of carbon removals is acknowledged in the Commission’s proposal. However, the role of car-
bon dioxide removal (CDR) within the EU climate strategy should remain limited and carefully defined. 
Carbon removals must be generated and quantified the right way, guided by four essential principles 
which collectively define CDR: 
• Atmospheric CO₂ is physically removed. 
• The removed CO₂ is permanently stored.
• All greenhouse gas emissions from the removal and storage processes are comprehensively 
estimated and included. 
• A net atmospheric benefit is achieved: more CO₂ is permanently removed than the total emis-
sions generated across the removal process. 
However, CDR presents two categories of risks that must be clearly understood and managed. 
First, there are risks of overreliance : integrating high levels of CDR into climate targets without 
guaranteed delivery undermines near-term emissions cuts, increases the likelihood of temperature 
overshoot and shifts the burden to future generations. 
Second, there are inherent risks in CDR deployment itself, depending on the method used. These 
include non-permanent carbon storage, high energy and water use, land-use pressure, technological 
uncertainty and the need for extensive CO₂ transport and storage infrastructure. 
Scientific analysis of climate plans of 70 countries shows significant ambiguity regarding CDR depen-
dence, undermining the credibility of net-zero targets. Conversely, mitigation pathways that rely less 
on CDR generally carry lower climate, environmental and socioeconomic risks. The paper emphasises 
that updated NDCs and long-term strategies should prioritise pathways that minimise reli -
ance on CDR, clearly define its role and disclose the risks and uncertainties linked to poten-
tial non-delivery.
The NEGEM project analysis further indicates that the availability of CDR will be insufficient to fully 
counterbalance even one sector’s level of current emissions, despite rapid scale-up of available tech-
nologies.
The legislative proposal for 2040 only specifically mentions that domestic permanent removals like 
Direct Air Carbon Capture and Storage (DACCS) and Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage 
(BioCCS) may be used to compensate for “hard-to-abate” emissions, nevertheless the methodologies 
to quantify removals generated by these projects are still under development. It also mentions a pos-
sible inclusion of CDR into the EU ETS.

Before any inclusion of CDR with the EU ETS, a comprehensive impact assessment by the 
Commission must be conducted before any decisions are taken, also assessing other in -
teraction options than direct integration. Only permanent removals with strict Monitoring, 
Reporting and Verification (MRV) should be considered.  Bellona has provided recommenda-
tions on how CDR could interact with the EU ETS.
More broadly, the EU needs a clear and long-term governance framework for CDR that supports 
deployment without weakening emissions reduction goals. This includes a separate target for 
permanent removals: high enough to ensure progress, but realistic and aligned with actu-
al potential. It must not divert focus and funding away from emissions cuts which remain 
the top priority . This could be set by secondary EU legislation and based on the Commission’s 
impact assessment. A well-designed framework should also address the gap between supply and 
demand and lay out how CDR will be financed post-2030.
Ultimately, one tonne of CO₂ not emitted is always preferable to one that must be removed. 
Climate strategy should reflect this while ensuring removals are used carefully, only when truly 
needed and respect planetary boundaries.

Land sinks as a flexibility
The LULUCF sector must remain a separate and essential pillar of climate action, not a source of flex-
ibility for other sectors. It should be governed by its own dedicated target and integrity safeguards. 
Its performance must not affect the required pace of emissions reductions in other sectors. This is 
important as the climate effects of land sinks are not equivalent to fossil emission reductions. Land 
sinks do not only fulfil climate goals but act as vital safeguards for humanity by providing for a range of 
ecosystem services, like the provision of fresh water and air, landscape cooling and biodiversity. Thus, 
their role must at least serve a dual purpose of tackling the intertwined climate-biodiversity crisis. 
According to the European Environmental Agency, EU land sinks have shrunk by 30% between 2014 
and 2023 due to a combination of over-harvesting and climate change induced disturbances. Policy mea-
sures to maintain and even increase the sinks do exist – most notably changing biomass consumption 
and managing ecosystems for climate-resilience and more close-to-nature. Still, the uncertainty of 
how climate change will affect land sinks on top of land management efforts, makes them ill-suited to 
be used to counterbalance permanent emissions notably from fossil fuels.
Using LULUCF to compensate for underperformance in other sectors would be introducing unclear 
accountability and risking overall goal attainment. Conversely, asking other sectors to compensate for 
a potentially lower land sink performance would impose additional pressures. Much like overdepen -
dence on industrial CDR, overdependence on land sinks in NDCs can result in underestimating the 
extent of necessary emission cuts to meet global temperature targets. The Land Gap Report  high-
lights this issue, whereby countries are excessively relying on land sinks to meet their climate targets, 
assuming about 1.8 times more removals than is likely to be feasible, with over 40% involving land-use 
change (e.g. afforestation, BECCS).
In short, LULUCF should "do its own job" as it did under the 2030 framework, with a separate 
capped contribution for 2040. The ambition should be maintained, while updating it based 
on current data and potential of EU land sinks.  A report from Umweltbundesamt showed that 
the potential of the LULUCF sector is higher than the agreed upon goals, with high estimates ranging 
from –400 to –500Mt CO2e.

More sectoral flexibility
The proposal includes possible sectoral flexibility: overperformance in one sector could compen -
sate for underperformance in another. While it may offer short-term advantages, important concerns 
must be carefully considered. Ultimately, the EU’s climate neutrality goal is economy-wide, but clar-
ity on the specific contribution from each economic sector provides better regulatory certainty and 
a clearer signal to the market.
Such sectoral flexibility may leave an unconstructive ambiguity whereby each emitter may assume it 
can pursue a less ambitious reduction pathway on the basis that a flexibility mechanism will be made 
available to them at a later stage. However, if all sectors pursue the same logic, overperformance will 
not materialise and therefore the flexibility will merely result in a failure to meet the economy-wide 
climate targets.
Any sectoral flexibility should not become a disincentive for the necessary structural tran-
sitions such as sustainable agriculture or low-carbon technologies. Moreover,  sectoral overper-
formance should ideally serve to raise overall ambition rather than enable inaction in other 
sectors, especially with a view towards achieving a net-negative EU in the longer term.
Allowing for the possibility of such compensations makes long-term planning more diffi -
cult, weakens incentives for investment in green technologies and undermines the regulatory 
certainty that is crucial for guiding capital and innovation towards the transition.

 
   
 
 
Policy Recommendations Digital Omnibus 
The recent wave of EU digital legislation, such as the AI Act, Data Act, CRA, and NIS2, has, 
contrary to its intentions, led to increased regulatory fragmentation and complexity for companies 
in the (high-tech) manufacturing industry. This has major consequences for the sector, despite 
these companies playing a key role in realizing Europe’s green and digital transition.  
Rather than providing clarity and legal certainty, the rapid succession of EU digital legislation is 
creating an increasingly fragmented, burdensome and inconsistent regulatory environment, with 
high administrative costs. These challenges are particularly severe for SMEs and system suppliers 
who operate across multiple sectors and value chains. According to industry research, 70% of 
companies have already halted innovation projects due to regulatory concerns. This jeopardizes 
the ability of European industry to innovate, scale and be resilient in geopolitical times where this 
should be of the highest priority. 
FME therefore welcomes the European Commission’s ambition to simplify and harmonize digital 
legislation through the Digital Omnibus Package and reduce administrative burdens by 25% and 
35% for SMEs. This package offers a much-needed opportunity to move from fragmented 
rulemaking to a truly coherent implementation-oriented framework, mindful of SMEs, and that will 
strengthen Europe’s competitiveness. To ensure tangible simplification and enhance Europe’s 
strategic autonomy, we recommend the following five priorities: 
1. Clear definitions and terminology  
Key terms such as “product with digital elements”, “AI system”, or “technical documentation” are 
currently defined differently in separate laws, causing confusion and legal uncertainty. For 
example, a robotics manufacturer faces diverging definitions of “AI system” under the AI Act 
(2024/1689) and the Machinery Regulation (2023/1230). This results in two separate 
conformity processes for the same product, with different documentation formats, despite no 
change in functionality. The divergent definitions of "safety component" between the AI Act and 
Machinery Regulation illustrate this problem, while terms like "dark patterns" appear 
inconsistently across the Digital Markets Act (2022/1925), Digital Services Act (2022/2065) and 
Data Act (2023/2854). 
Furthermore, bottlenecks arise where different legislative frameworks overlap. The Data Act, for 
example, refers to ‘user’ and ‘data holder’, while the GDPR uses ‘data subject’ and ‘controller’. 
This leads to conflicts when the user of a smart device is not the same person as the data subject 
whose data is being processed, while the manufacturer is formally the data holder. 
➤ Clearly define roles in the GDPR and Data Act: who has which rights and obligations when the 
user, data subject, and data controller are not the same person? 
➤ Remove duplication and conflicting interpretations in parallel acts (for example, AI Act and CRA 
vs. Machinery Regulation).

➤ Establish a living dictionary or central glossary under the Omnibus, embedded in guidance and 
implementing acts to ensure legal consistency. 
2. Streamline reporting obligations 
Companies today must report the same security incident multiple times, to national CSIRTs under 
NIS2, to ENISA under the CRA, and sometimes to other authorities under sectoral law. This 
contradicts the Commission's stated goal of facilitating multi-purpose reporting and avoiding 
duplication in cybersecurity legislation. Moreover, this fragmentation starts even earlier in the 
process, with registration. For many companies, it is unclear which legal entity within a corporate 
group must be registered under NIS2, and in which country. Unlike the GDPR, NIS2 does not 
define a 'group company' concept that could guide the designation of a central entity. As a result, 
businesses face significant legal uncertainty. 
➤ Develop a single (harmonized) European registration structure (at least for NIS2) 
➤ Align and streamline cybersecurity reporting obligations across NIS2, CRA and sectoral 
legislation.  
➤ Develop a single European cyber incident reporting mechanism in close collaboration with 
ENISA and national CSIRTs, as in the CRA and open it to NIS2 reporting, to prevent unclarities and 
mistakes with, for example, complex business models. 
 
3. Harmonize Horizontal and Sectoral Requirements 
Horizontal legislation (AI Act, Data Act, CRA) often overlaps or conflicts with sector-specific product 
legislation, creating double conformity assessments and regulatory uncertainty. Companies do 
not know which rules take precedence or which assessment is sufficient. In addition, regulators 
may interpret the rules differently, which means that a product may be approved in one country 
but rejected in another. This can therefore lead not only to a lack of legal certainty but also to an 
uneven playing field. 
➤ Ensure that companies can demonstrate compliance of their product through a single process 
and that duplicate high-risk obligations are avoided. Integrate requirements into sectoral 
frameworks only when relevant and proportionate. 
➤ Align the Data Governance Act (DGA) Art. 12 with the Digital Product Passport (DPP) to avoid 
overlapping registration and governance requirements. Ensure that a DPP provider is not 
automatically also a ‘data intermediary’ unless this is explicitly relevant. 
➤ Clarify “lex specialis” relationships and prevent horizontal rules from duplicating or 
contradicting sector-specific frameworks. 
4. Realistic implementation and transition periods 
Many compliance obligations in the CRA and AI Act depend on harmonized technical standards, 
which are still under development. The introduction of new obligations requires considerable 
preparation time: for developing standards, adapting products and processes, training staff, and 
coordinating with suppliers. According to current plans, the most important standards will not be 
available before December 2026 due to the complexity of the consensus-building process. Not 
only the business community but also notified bodies and market surveillance authorities need 
sufficient preparation time. 
➤ Ensure feasible timelines that align with the availability of harmonized standards.  
➤ Where necessary, extend the transition periods for important laws such as the CRA and the AI 
Act: postpone the application of high-risk requirements until 12 months after the publication of 
harmonized standards. Give manufacturers at least 12 months to implement the vertical 
 
   
 
standards at the CRA after official publication in their development and production processes and 
allow Module A (internal production control) as a conformity assessment procedure for ‘important 
products’ (Annex III, Class I) until vertical standards are available. 
➤ Suspend sanctions until compliance is technically feasible and harmonized standards and 
guidelines have been established.   
 
5. Ensure Proportionality for B2B applications and SME manufacturers 
B2B industrial systems (for example, factory robots, sensors, power grid equipment) have very 
different risk profiles than consumer products. Long product lifecycles, complex integration with 
legacy systems, and contractual risk sharing are common in industrial environments. 
➤ Allow simplified technical documentation and support obligations for industrial components 
with long innovation cycles. 
➤ Make an exception in the CRA for simple products with digital elements that cannot reasonably 
and technically pose a cyber risk (benign products), as in the EMC (2014/30/EU). 
➤ Distinguish between digital lifetime and physical lifetime, so that updates do not remain 
mandatory for decades. 
➤ Extend the simplification provisions of Article 33 CRA (SME clause) to all low-risk products, 
regardless of the size of the manufacturer. 
 
From Policy to practice: the need for industrially grounded implementation 
FME strongly supports the European Commission’s shift in focus from legislative output to 
implementation outcomes. To succeed, the Omnibus must deliver a regulatory architecture that is 
coherent across laws, predictable and workable in the industrial reality of complex value chains. 
We urge the EU member states to work together and coordinate closely with relevant 
stakeholders in shaping national implementation to prevent national differences and maintain a 
level playing field. 
In a time of global uncertainty, regulatory clarity and coherence are strategic assets Europe can 
no longer afford to overlook. Only by simplifying, harmonizing, and supporting implementation can 
Europe unlock the full potential of its industrial digital transition.

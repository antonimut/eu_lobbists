Data Act: Respect contractual freedom – remove B2B cases  
 
Massive implementation effort that unnecessarily ties up resources: 
· The Data Act requires companies to document each individual data point to determine whether 
and to what extent 
à it currently falls within the scope of the regulation, and 
à it could potentially be made accessible.  
· Example: A modern commuter train from Siemens is a rolling computer with thousands of 
sensors. It measures passenger numbers, energy consumption, brake wear, door functions, and 
climate data. This intelligent data collection enables more punctual trains, predictive 
maintenance, and optimized energy consumption.  
à Under the Data Act, each of these data points would have to be individually classified and 
checked for trade secrets – for every train anew, as they are usually built to customer 
specifications. 
à The likely consequence: Manufacturers have no incentive to build 'smart' trains – with more 
sensors and more data. 
 
There is no market failure in B2B which would require regulation:  
· The market has been governing data exchange independently and without bureaucracy for years. 
· If two business partners agree, there’s no need for regulation. B2B contracts are negotiated on 
equal terms (unlike in B2C contracts). 
· The Data Act distorts a functioning market and hinders contractual freedom and thereby stifles 
innovation.  
· Example: A modern office building using DesigoCC knows when which rooms need to be heated, 
who opens which doors and when, and how, the lighting is optimally adjusted. 
à According to the Data Act, Siemens would have to share this data with any external 
maintenance company, which leads to a dilemma: the data also contains information about 
which employee uses which room and when – personal data protected by the General Data 
Protection Regulation (GDPR). 
à The technical effort to separate this data is enormous, especially for smaller companies. 
Providers of building management systems would rather forgo (additional) smart functions 
than be torn between the Data Act and data protection. 
à Result: This doesn't make our buildings smarter, but dumber.

Switching of “Data Processing services” severely affects cloud offerings: 
•  The Data Act stipulates that customers of data processing services can easily and quickly 
(within 2-4 months, depending on the individual case) switch providers and take their data 
with them.  
•  This chapter was originally intended only for the hyperscalers (Infrastructure as a Service, 
Platform as a Service). However, the term "data processing service" is very vaguely defined, 
potentially covering also SaaS offerings which were originally not “in scope”. This is mainly 
problematic in B2B offerings as they are often integrated into a larger / complex environment. 
For example: Switching of a cloud-based ERP system requires a huge transformational project 
beyond the pure switching, addressing processes, IT interdependencies, staff training, etc. 
Such projects usually require much more lead time than 2-4 months.    
•  From a customer perspective, the switch between services does not become easier, while the 
provider has a massive additional administrative and technical burden. Technical measures for 
the switch must be implemented by the provider and extensive contractual regulations must 
be agreed with customers in advance – the EU model clauses for this purpose comprise 
almost 100 pages (unproportionally to and even longer than a usual SaaS contract). 
•  The Data Act even provides a possibility for customers of data processing services to exit from 
contracts before the end of the agreed contractual term. This represents, at least in B2B 
environments, a significant intervention with contractual freedom, leads to revenue 
uncertainty and impairs investment recovery as providers of innovative offerings cannot be 
certain whether they can amortize high initial investments over the agreed contract period if 
the customer can switch prematurely.  
 
à All combined, this hinders innovation.  
 
 
AI Act: Exempt industrial AI from the AI Act  
 
AI in Manufacturing 
An industrial robot in one of our manufacturing facilities uses an AI-powered camera to detect 
that a person is entering its work area. The AI recognizes the danger and immediately stops the 
robot. The Machinery Regulation already ensures the safety of the autonomous robot for the 
environment.  
à The AI Act imposes new obligations on top of the Machinery Regulation to achieve the same 
goal – safety in machinery environments – which are often neither necessary nor effective: 
risk management, documentation, technical specifications. 
à The result: double bureaucracy instead of double safety. 
 
Medium Voltage Grid Example 
· Stability and resilience are paramount for Europe’s transmission grid. In Norway, Siemens tested 
an AI system that detects faults in transmission lines which conventional monitoring cannot see 
(for example, a tree falling on a line). The AI sends an alarm immediately, allowing the operator to 
act before a local fault would cascade into a large-scale blackout. 
· Operators asked whether the AI could also disconnect a line automatically in such emergencies. 
This would prevent delays caused by manual intervention and make the grid even safer. 
à However, the moment the AI is allowed to act autonomously in the grid, it is classified as a 
“high-risk” application under the AI Act. This imposes additional obligations, risk management 
systems, extensive documentation, and the application of technical standards that do not yet 
exist.

Example: Spare product challenge:  
A plant outage costs an average of EUR 10,000 per minute. To minimize the duration and costs of 
such outages, customers expect reliable product replacement solutions. This is usually handled 
through identical replacement products and contractual agreements that provide 1:1 product 
replacement for at least 10 years.  
à If suppliers are no longer permitted to replace a defective product because the identical

à For regulated plants, outage times can be even longer – lasting weeks or months - as operating 
licenses expire if no identical products are available.  
à It is not always possible to develop a compatible successor product. In these cases, suppliers

customer plants. Almost every critical infrastructure and manufacturing industry will be

Cyber Resilience Act (CRA): Call for moratorium & 
simplification 
 
Our key demands 
 
 
Why? (Reasoning behind) 
1. In the industrial B2B sector, entire and complex supply chains will need to implement time - 
consuming and costly measures to become “CRA-ready”. Securing the entire supply chain, both 
upstream and downstream, requires significant time and effort: 
o Upstream (Siemens as a customer) 
Many of products rely on components from suppliers, which may fall under the scope of the 
CRA. Thus, to ensure end to end security, it would  be valuable that  the suppliers ensure 
these components conform with the CRA first, and after this is ensured the conformity of 
the final product is assessed. 
o Downstream (Siemens as a supplier) 
Our products are used by various stakeholders, such as OEMs and integrators. We must 
ensure the conformity of our products with the CRA before these stakeholders can verify the 
CRA-conformity of their solutions that incorporate our products. 
Additionally, innovation cycles  in certain industries, such as critical infrastructure or industrial 
production lines, trains, rail infrastructure and electrical grids, are significantly longer compared to 
the rapid cycles in consumer electronics, often exceeding 10 years. 
Finally, most, if not all, of the harmonized standards (hENs) for the important and critical products 
with digital elements (PDEs) are likely not to be ready by the current CRA date of applicability. Hence, 
it is important that the date of applicability is adjusted to ensure the availability
9 
 
Siemens AG Status: 2025-07-07  I 
of cited harmonized standards, which are essential to raise the security bar and ensure an adequate 
level playing field in the EU market. 
 
2. Excluding “compatible system extensions”5 from the CRA’s scope  
Extensions to existing major infrastructure projects, such as rail or grid systems, should be feasible 
without the need of a complete replacement or upgrade of the current infrastructure. This is 
particularly relevant for installations in professional settings, industrial environments, or critical 
infrastructures, which typically have an expected lifespan of several decades. The compliance costs for 
system owners and operators would be disproportionate compared to the achievable security 
objectives, making such upgrades economically unviable. Referencing Article 13(4) sentence 3 of the 
CRA (non -applicability of certain requirements) and Recital of the 55 CRA (interoperability 
requirements), an additional exemption should be provided for compatible system extensions through 
an amendment to Article 2 (scope). 
Manufacturers shall address the associated risks through alternative means, such as limiting the 
intended purpose of the extension to trusted environments, minimizing the product’s exposure within 
the system, or implementing compensating countermeasures (e.g., threat detection, physical 
protection, etc.). Customers shall be adequately informed about these risks. Manufacturers of such 
large-scale infrastructure systems must ensure that no new security risks are introduced with the 
extension and that no new functionalities are added to the existing system. 
 
3. The current legislative framework presents overlaps between horizontal regulations, such as the 
CRA, and vertical legislations, including the EU Radio Equipment Directive (RED) and the EU 
Machinery Regulation (MR). It is essential that the CRA horizontally regulates the cybersecurity of 
all covered products. It should be implemented in a way that ensures existing, product -specific 
legislations do not take precedence according to the “lex specialis” principle. Similarly, it is critical 
that the RED Delegated Regulation is repealed by the date of applicability of the CRA to avoid 
unnecessary regulatory burden. 
 
Furthermore, the regulation regarding the exclusion of certain spare parts from the CRA’s scope (Art 
2(6) CRA), should be extended and aligned with industry common practices  to also include spare 
products in a complex system . For instance, when products are part of a complex system at the 
customer's site, the customer will need to replace the entire system if the original product breaks 
down and the supplier is not allowed to supply a replacement product. 
 
 
5 A ‘compatible system extension’ refers to new products that are integrated into and interact with an existing infrastructure system. 
Examples of ‘compatible system extensions’ could be the extension of a metro line with one new station or expanding an energy distribution 
grid to an additional city. These extensions are designed to be interoperable and compatible with the existing infrastructure (e.g., through 
external interfaces), ensuring the entire system functions as intended without the need for a complete system replacement, which would be 
economically unfeasible. Compatible system extensions do not introduce new functionalities or new security risks to the existing system.

3. Example for Spare Product topic:  
A plant outage costs an average of EUR 10,000.00 per minute. To reduce the duration and costs of a 
plant outage, customers expect product replacement solutions. This is usually handled with identical 
replacement products and contractual agreements that provide 1:1 product replacement for typically 
at least 10 years. 
If suppliers are no longer permitted to replace a defective product installed in a plant because the 
identical replacement product, developed before the application of the EU CRA but placed on the 
market afterwards, does not meet the requirements of the EU CRA, this can lead to a very long plant 
outage and significant costs for repair, reconfiguration, reengineering, recommissioning, and 
recertification. For regulated plants, outage times can be even longer (weeks and months) as operating 
licenses expire if no identical products are available. 
This critical situation can sometimes be mitigated by a compatible successor product. However, it is not 
always possible to develop a compatible successor product. In these cases, suppliers must be 
permitted to deliver a predecessor product as replacement to avoid long outages of customer plants, 
which often last days, weeks, or even months. 
Almost every critical infrastructure and every manufacturing sector will be affected by this, including 
thousands of plants in the areas of energy, water and wastewater, food and beverages,

o With the EU AI Act 
The AI Act contains cybersecurity -related requirements, such as accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity. For

o With the EU Radio Equipment Directive (RED) 
The RED Delegated Regulation (RED DR), which covers cybersecurity elements of internet- connected radio 
equipment (Articles 3.3(d,e,f) of the RED, must be repealed by December 11, 2027 to prevent unnecessary 
regulatory and administrative burdens. Compliance would otherwise need to be demonstrated for two 
overlapping pieces of legislation. The CRA encompasses more products than the RED DR and has more 
ambitious security objectives, making the repeal of the RED DR necessary.   
Moreover, the RED DR harmonized standards (EN 18031 series) have proven their appropriateness and provide 
legal effects since their citation in the Official Journal of the EU. The technical approach taken by the RED 
standards should be supported by the European Commission in the CRA standardization work (e.g., security 
mechanisms, assessment criteria). It is also recommended that the European Commission cites the CRA 
harmonized standards more swiftly, as the delay between the publication of the standards and their citation 
(six months in the case of the RED DR) creates market uncertainty, impacting investment in Europe.   
 
o With the EU NIS 2 Directive (NIS 2)  
The overlaps between NIS 2 and the CRA make their implementation challenging, particularly regarding the 
reporting obligations and cloud solutions (qualifying as remote data processing solutions).   
For reporting obligations, it is recommended to harmonize definitions (e.g., incident, “becoming aware”) and 
better align the reporting processes and interfaces so that a single incident reporting process can fulfil the 
requirements of both legislations.   
 
For certain cloud solutions, both NIS 2 and the CRA may be applicable. The NIS 2 and CRA requirements differ 
significantly in nature, making the implementation challenging. The lack of certainty regarding the interplay 
between these two major legislations undermines potential new developments and innovation in cloud -based 
services and operations due to the lack of visibility on the investments required for compliance. This lack of 
clarity also hampers the development of horizontal harmonized standards, as it is unclear how the essential 
requirements apply to remote data processing solutions. Remote data processing solutions should be excluded 
from the CRA’s scope, at least within its initial phase, to allow stakeholders to better understand the 
implications of including remote data processing solutions (e.g., what is covered by the product’s functions? 
Why are certain remote data processing solutions excluded while others are included, simply because they are 
accessible via a product with digital elements developed by the same manufacturer?). Alternatively, the 
European Commission should clearly specify the services within the scope of the CRA and which elements of 
these services must comply with the CRA requirements (i.e., the data processing/operations only, or also the 
software layer, the platform layer, the infrastructure layer?) to ensure the highest level of legal certainty.

these acts are useful for clarifying the requirements of the law, they should not introduce additional 
requirements or bureaucracy.

Observations from the implementation of the Data Act indicate that it will not

Data Act: Call for repeal  
 
 
 
Our key demands 
 
 
Why? (Reasoning behind) 
 
The Data Act (DA) aims to improve user access to data and compensate the imbalance between  
manufacturers and customers. This aim might apply for the B2C business, where end customers often 
need to accept extensive data usage rights defined by manufacturers. 
· Considering B2B business, there is already today a balance between manufacturers and 
customers when it comes to data. Customers are often specifying their own data usage rights 
in tenders which will be negotiated on an equal footing between the involved parties. In some 
industries, e.g. rail or energy industry, customers are even determining non -negotiable data 
usage rights within their tenders which need to be accepted by manufactures to be able to 
bid. 
· In industrial B2B business areas of application, the DA might  lead to devices being designed 
with less data to provide, due to high barriers for excluding trade secret relevant data and the 
unclear definition of processed data. Consequently, there will be fewer data- generating 
devices being used overall or less data being generated in devices. 
· In economic terms, the DA has the exact opposite effect to its original intention of promoting 
a European data economy. 
· It is thus proving to be a massive obstacle to the emergence of a European data economy in 
relevant industry segments and a heavy burden on competitiveness in international 
competition.

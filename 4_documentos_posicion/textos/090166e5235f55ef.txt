 SIMPLIFYING EU DIGITAL REGULATIONS IBM’S RESPONSE TO THE EUROPEAN COMMISSION’S CALL FOR EVIDENCE ON THE DIGITAL OMNIBUS PACKAGE – OCTOBER 2025  
    EXECUTIVE SUMMARY  Digital innovation will continue accelerating, creating new opportunities and disruption for policymakers to navigate. At a time when Europe is facing significant challenges to preserve its competitiveness, the choices the EU makes during the 2024-2029 political cycle related to how technology is developed, deployed, and governed will be critical to Europeans’ continued security, prosperity, and well-being.  There is an immediate need for governments in Europe to reduce the regulatory burden that companies operating in Europe are currently facing. Therefore, IBM fully supports the EU’s commitment to the so-called “Omnibus” approach to address such burden in several pieces of legislation. This is all the more important as Europe seeks to boost digital adoption.  IBM strongly believes some of the recently adopted digital EU legislations could be simplified and/or clarified, while preserving their overarching principles, and ultimately focusing on the intended policy objectives and outcomes.

For the Digital Omnibus Package, we would recommend the European Commission to prioritise simplifying some of the most burdensome provisions in the following laws:  Artificial Intelligence Act (EU) 2024/1689 and its related General purpose AI Code of Practice Network and Information Security (NIS2) Directive (EU) 2022/2555 Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 Proposed European Cloud Services Cybersecurity Certification Scheme (EUCS) in the context of the upcoming Cybersecurity Act Regulation (EU) 2019/881 review Data Act Regulation (EU) 2023/2854 and Data Governance Act Regulation (EU) 2022/868  Key priorities should include:  • Delaying the applicability of essential requirements of the EU AI Act by 12 months after the relevant standards are published. It is not realistic to ask businesses to put in place compliance processes when the necessary standards may not be available before the end of 2026. As a leading contributor to the ongoing AI Act standardisation work, IBM is best placed to witness the challenges that come with developing harmonised European standards in this new area. Currently several of the standards are available in some advanced draft status, yet some further work is required to make these standards implementable and workable. IBM remains strongly committed to continuing being actively engaged in this standardisation work and we are positive that, with some additional time granted, there will be high-quality standards available for the successful implementation of the AI Act.  • Aligning the concept of main establishment across the different cybersecurity pieces of legislation. For example, there are currently different definitions of this concept in NIS2 and the CRA, which creates significant compliance challenges for companies that are in scope of both acts. These entities should be able to use the same country of main establishment, as the goal should ultimately be about improving the reporting framework, not creating unnecessary administrative burden.   • Addressing the unnecessary overlaps and inconsistencies in incident reporting and audits requirements in cybersecurity legislation. For example, there should be a national single reporting entry point covering all relevant legislation (e.g. NIS2, CRA, DORA) to reduce fragmentation and simplify processes for companies. Liability protection clauses should also be included to allow the information to be shared in a more controlled manner. Additionally, we recommend enabling critical ICT third-party service providers designated under DORA that are also in scope of NIS2 to streamline compliance by avoiding duplicative audits. A mutual recognition framework should be established, ensuring that certifications and audit outcomes are accepted across market surveillance authorities and clients.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE  Targeted legislative acts: • AI Act (EU) 2024/1689, and its related General purpose AI Code of Practice  Issues:  The AI Act includes some overly prescriptive and burdensome requirements which do very little to improve the transparency and safety of AI systems, such as obligations to share source code or registration obligations. Importantly, compliance with the AI Act’s requirements for high-risk systems is dependent on the availability of harmonised standards. It is important that the date of application of the requirements for high-risks systems is based on a realistic assessment of the time and resources needed to develop, finalise and list harmonised standards in the Official Journal (OJEU), including a reasonable timeframe for industry to implement them. We encourage the European Commission to support a fast and successful continuation of the standardisation work, insisting on the development of workable and implementable standards.    Recommendations:  • Delaying the application of essential requirements for high-risk AI systems by 12 months: High-risk AI systems must comply with the EU AI Act starting August 2026. However, the development of harmonised standards needed to demonstrate compliance is delayed and may not be fully available until late 2026. This raises concerns about whether companies can realistically meet the requirements on time. Even after publication, businesses will need time to adapt systems and align with compliance processes. To address this, the simplification package should include a rule delaying the applicability of essential requirements by 12 months after the relevant standard is published.  • Removing the obligation to register AI systems: Art. 49 requires providers to register AI systems in an EU database, regardless of whether they are ultimately classified as high-risk. Since most systems will not qualify as high risk in practice, this obligation seems very burdensome and should be removed. Additionally, even if this obligation is applicable to high-risk systems only, storing this information centrally would create a serious security risk.  • Protecting source code: Art. 74(13) and Art. 92(3) which grant market surveillance authorities or the Commission the right to access the source code of AI systems should be deleted. Accessing source code would provide very little understanding for possible

concerns since it would require a very high level of technical understanding of the specific AI system. It would also set a dangerous precedent limiting intellectual property (IP) protection and increase the risk of data breaches and misuse.   CYBERSECURITY  Targeted legislative acts: • Network and Information Security (NIS2) Directive (EU) 2022/2555 • Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 • Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 • European Cloud Services Cybersecurity Certification Scheme (EUCS) in the context of the Cybersecurity Act Regulation (EU) 2019/881 review  1) Inconsistent cybersecurity reporting requirements:  Issues:  Incident reporting requires complex multi-disciplinary coordination within a company. This process involves several internal stakeholders (security operations, IT, legal, etc.) and external ones (affected customers, partners, authorities, etc.). When an incident occurs, it has been standard best practice within industry to first and foremost focus on mitigation and in containing the incident, while gathering the necessary complete information that can help competent authorities to understand the nature of the incident and conduct an effective triage so they can prioritise reports. Exposing information about an incident before a patch is applied or operations restored, makes operators and their customers vulnerable to increased hacker attacks.  Additionally, there could be existing confidentiality and contractual obligations (for example in the context of a standard cloud service agreement) where incidents should first be notified to the customer before the regulatory authorities. The lack of solid liability clauses in both the NIS2 and CRA will not incentivise entities to report incidents quicker and in providing more information. More importantly, the scope and criteria of reporting obligations are not streamlined across the body of EU cybersecurity legislations: for example, a cloud provider experiencing a security breach may need to file an initial incident report under NIS2, comply with CRA reporting requirements if the incident involves a product, and in some cases submit another report under DORA.  
 
5  
This situation adds significant complexity resulting in:  • drawing valuable company operational resources into administrative tasks, rather than mitigating the cybersecurity risk;  • governments lacking adequate situational awareness; and  • a lack of control over how sensitive information is shared by companies.   Recommendations:  • In order to avoid multiple reporting and dispersal, there should be single national notification entry points. For example, incidents under CRA, NIS2 and sector specific legislation should be reported to national CSIRTs, which shall inform sectoral regulators where appropriate.   • Both NIS2 and CRA are missing liability clauses which are necessary for allowing the information to be shared in a more controlled manner. While Art. 23(1) of NIS2 points that notification shall not subject the notifying entity to increased liability, the law should also clarify that they should not be liable for potential spill-over effects caused by the act of notification.  2) Inconsistent definition of ‘main establishment’:  Issues:  Art. 14(7) of the CRA provides a different definition of main establishment than Art. 26(2) of the NIS2 Directive. This creates a challenge for companies when selecting their main establishment based on where their most relevant cyber experts and operational teams qualified to deal with the legislative requirements are based. Indeed, the country with the largest number of employees may not always be the most appropriate choice if these relevant, qualified teams are not based in this country.  Moreover, member states seem to have adopted different interpretations of ‘main establishment’ when transposing NIS2. This divergence undermines the EU’s goal to reduce fragmentation.  Recommendations:  • A company’s main establishment recognised under NIS2 should automatically be recognised as main establishment under the CRA if the company is in scope of both pieces of legislation. Indeed, the one-stop-shop principle is particularly critical for incident reporting purposes. In addition, companies should use the same place of main 
 
6  
establishment as a point of contact under other legislative pieces related to cybersecurity.   • Entities in other critical sectors should be allowed to apply the principle of main establishment if they operate in two or more EU member states.  3) Overlap of audit and conformity assessment requirements:  Issues: Companies like IBM that are in scope of NIS2, DORA, GDPR, and CRA have to face multiple processes, conformity assessments and audits requirements to demonstrate compliance with each of these regulations. While each regulation has its specificity and scope, a lot of security requirements under one regulation could qualify as compliance with another, particularly among those that are applicable to critical infrastructure (i.e. NIS2 and related sector-specific laws such as DORA).  In practice, the lack of a coordinated EU-wide oversight framework has created a fragmented compliance environment. Financial institutions und public entities classified as critical infrastructure often impose overlapping audit and certification requirements on their third-party service providers to meet supervisory expectations. This results in repeated audits where providers must demonstrate the same risk controls to multiple clients throughout the year.  Additionally, the harmonisation goal of NIS2 is challenged by additional national rules and varying conformity assessment processes required nationally, which would risk further fragmentation. For example, NIS2 requires similar risk management measures across the EU, yet companies would have to undergo multiple conformity assessments in various forms (e.g. certification or third-party audits) in each of the countries they operate, even though this is the same requirement for all member states. All these processes are resource-intensive and costly, yet do not lead to meaningful improvements in risk management. These inefficiencies ultimately increase costs for end-users. Recommendations: • We encourage closer discussion between the member states on a common set of tools to demonstrate conformity with NIS2 and mutual recognition of national certifications and frameworks.  This would streamline supervision, avoid duplication, and enhance overall efficiency without weakening oversight.  • Sector-specific rules, such as DORA and the Network Code on Cybersecurity (NCCS), should be closely aligned with NIS2, as the horizontal framework for critical 
 
7  
infrastructure. For any upcoming sector-specific cybersecurity rules, we encourage member states to leverage Recital 22 of NIS2 Directive. Following the principle of this provision, NIS2 should be a baseline legal framework, and more specific sectoral rules should be introduced as implementing acts.  • We recommend that critical ICT third-party service providers designated under DORA that are also in scope of NIS2 be allowed to streamline their compliance process by reducing the need to repeatedly demonstrate compliance with the same controls across multiple audits. For example, once the European Supervisory Authorities (ESAs) make available the necessary documentation, it could be proposed to either 1) encourage financial entities conducting audits to align on a shared set of metrics and indicators, which would enable providers to prepare and report a single framework, reducing redundancy and ensuring consistency in compliance assessments or 2) recognising the ESA risk-tiering as a certification or benchmark so low-risk providers would be covered solely by the ESA audit, reducing duplicative client audits.  4) Discriminatory certification requirements:  Issues:  The initial proposed drafts for an EU Cybersecurity Certification Scheme (EUCS) included strict non-technical criteria (e.g. EU ownership and control, EU headquarter, immunity to non-EU laws) for cloud providers to be able to qualify for the highest assurance level under the scheme. As the EU prepares to review the Cybersecurity Act (CSA) to deal with certification frameworks, IBM remains concerned about the possible inclusion of such non-technical criteria into future schemes.  Indeed, requirements based on non-technical criteria could disproportionately hinder global vendors from accessing certain cloud market segments in the EU or potentially risk jeopardising existing strategic partnerships between such vendors and European companies.  The stakeholder group (SCCG) has not received the opportunity to voice its opinion on the draft scheme, while no public consultation was conducted on the revised EUCS drafts. The process envisaged in Recital 83 and Art. 49 (3) of the Cybersecurity Act (CSA) has not been fulfilled in practice with communication being conducted in a one-way manner.    
 
8  
Recommendations: • We recommend adopting the EUCS without the inclusion of a “High+” criterion, which would introduce legal requirements such as corporate ownership restrictions, “immunity to non-EU law” clauses, or strict data localisation mandates.   • EU certification framework should draw from international standards. This approach will be more time- and cost- efficient as well as it will help meet the certification demand in the new cybersecurity legislation.  • SCCG’s mandate should be revised to incorporate a more active role of industry in certification development processes (provide feedback and advice to ENISA, support market impact assessments) and regular dialogue with ECCG.   • CSA should remain a technical framework for a transparent, inclusive and evidence-based certification development process.  DATA REGULATION  Targeted legislative acts: • Data Act Regulation (EU) 2023/2854 • Data Governance Act Regulation (EU) 2022/868  Issues:  Data is the cornerstone of the digital economy and is crucial for the development and training of reliable AI models and applications. The current proliferation and complexity of EU data regulations (e.g. GDPR, Data Governance Act, Data Act, Free Flow of Non-Personal Data Regulation, Open Data Directive) impact businesses because it directly affects how they operate, innovate, and compete. Not only are there inconsistences and overlaps between these different pieces of legislation, but businesses also need to manage the overlap between data governance rules and other digital regulations such as the AI Act, the CRA and others, as well as divergent interpretations between EU member states. For example, it can be challenging to understand how the GDPR interplays with data sharing mandates in chapter II and III of the Data Act when it relates to personal data.  Additionally, it is important to ensure that removing barriers to data access is enabled within a framework that also protects innovation and ensures security: we still have concerns about existing data sharing provisions in the Data Act that may force manufacturers to share sensitive data, trade secrets and IP with competitors. Some of the data sharing provisions 
 
9  
could also raise cybersecurity issues as some data reflect vulnerabilities, and this data must be shared with third parties that may not present sufficient guarantees in terms of security.  Recommendations:  As a consequence, IBM believes that simplification should aim for a harmonised data governance framework that streamlines rules across sectors and data types, reducing legal uncertainty compliance costs, and administrative burdens, but also promotes data reuse and interoperability while enabling and not restricting cross-border data flows. More specifically, the Commission should:  • Preserve the free flow of data principle: such a harmonised framework should strive to further ease cross-border data flows rather than restrict or impede them. This means upholding the core principles of the 2018 Free Flow of Data Regulation and avoiding unintended consequences such as some EU member states unjustifiably imposing new data localisation mandates or restricting cross-border transfers of data, notably industrial and business data.  o We strongly welcome the European Commission’s clarification in the Data Act FAQs that Art. 31 of the Data Governance Act and Art. 32 of the Data Act are not intended to apply to intra- or inter-business data transfers. Instead, their scope is limited to situations involving unlawful access to or transfers of non-personal data by non-EU public authorities. This clarification is essential to ensure legal certainty for businesses operating in the EU’s data economy. We therefore urge policymakers to formally incorporate this interpretation into the legislative consolidation process, thereby granting it binding legal effect and safeguarding the integrity of cross-border business data flows.  o We wish to reiterate that any concerns about data transfers and foreign government access to data are best addressed through multilateral solutions that focus on common principles shared by like-minded countries, rather than by placing unilateral regulatory requirements on the IT sector.  • Harmonise enforcement: The enforcement of digital legislation should be anchored in a one-stop-shop mechanism, enabling businesses to rely on a single, consistent interpretation and point of contact across member states.   • Streamline the scope of data sharing mandates of chapters II and III of the Data act, in line with the objectives of the Data Strategy, to ensure that only the data which can be considered data generated by a connected product as set forth in the Data Act should be shared: for instance, in Art. 2, it should be made clear that only the software that is needed for the functioning of the product is a “related service”.

• Incentivise voluntary data sharing vs. mandated access: to ensure broader availability of quality data, we must also ensure we can maintain the necessary safeguards to protect IP, trade secrets and cybersecurity and protect innovation in general. Therefore, we strongly call for reinforcing the security and trade secrets handbrakes in the EU Data Act by (i) not limiting the trade secrets handbrake to a governance process but to a genuine protection; and (ii) expanding the security hand brake to include cybersecurity issues.  • Leverage existing international standards to enhance availability of quality data: standardisation is vital for data interoperability and should be guided by a global, consensus-based, transparent and industry-driven framework, leveraging the expertise of international bodies like ISO and the adoption of international standards by CEN/CENELEC to support effective and inclusive digital policy. Since companies operate also across EU borders, the EU should foster the use of: o existing relevant standards in data generation and sharing, such as standards that are available or under development in ISO/IEC JTC 1 SC27 and SC38, as well as the ones already provided by global standard setting organisations such as OASIS or W3C. The adoption of standards from global standards setting organisations should preferably happen via an adoption process in CEN-CENELEC and subject to the respective review of technical experts active in CEN-CENELEC JTC 25; o protocols developed in open-source technologies (e.g. through LF AI & Data) rather than through the typical standardisation process.  ***

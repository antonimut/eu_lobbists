1. Simplify e-Privacy rules to facilitate data uses that drive innovation  
The data protection and privacy regulatory framework is based on two separate legal 
instruments that both regulate the use of data from different perspectives: (1) the GDPR 
regulating the processing of personal data, directly applicable in national laws and (2) 
the e-Privacy Directive regulating the placing and reading of information (cookies) on a 
user’s device that is transposed in 27 different national laws.  
These laws often apply to similar activities: for instance, the use of cookies almost 
always involves the processing of personal data and must comply with both legal 
regimes cumulatively. In addition, national regulators have adopted their own 
interpretation of the GDPR and e-Privacy rules. These different regimes also trigger 
different enforcement models: one data protection authority (lead authority) under the 
one stop shop mechanism for the GDPR and 27 national enforcement authorities which 
can be data protection, telecoms of consumers authorities for the e-Privacy Directive.  
Unlike the GDPR which provides for five legal bases for processing that can be used 
depending on the context of the data use, the e-privacy directive subjects most 
interactions of users with the Internet, including the placing of cookies, to consent. This 
provision has narrow exceptions which have been interpreted very restrictively. This has 
led to user consent fatigue and has limited legitimate non-intrusive business activities.  
This complex framework is not only negatively impacting innovation as companies lack 
any form of legal certainty for their data and AI led projects, but also for individuals who 
have increasingly failed to provide consent meaningfully and lost trust in the use of their 
data on the Internet.  
The digital omnibus is the perfect vehicle to simplify this regulatory framework under a 
two-step approach aimed at unifying the rules both from a topic and a geographic 
standpoint:    
Step1: Streamline the e-Privacy Directive and the GDPR legal bases   
Article 5(3) of the e -Privacy Directive should be incorporated into Article 6 of the GDPR. 
This would allow organisations to rely on legal bases other than consent for the legitimate 
processing of data not intended for user tracking (fraud prevention, service improvement, 
quality control, implementing PETs …). This would also regroup all data processing 
activities, including the use of cookies, under the GDPR with only one data protection lead 
authority for organisations. Finally, it would reduce users’ consent fatigue  as consent 
would not be the only legal basis for the use of cookies.  
Step 2: Enable coherent GDPR interpretation and implementation 
National conflicting or divergent interpretation and implementation of the GDPR prevent 
any harmonisation and generate significant complexities and cost s for cross -border 
activities. Having one single set of uniform interpretation of the rules across the EU would 
bring more legal certainty and foster data driven innovation. To avoid national guidance 
proliferation, t he GDPR should specify that EDPB guidance takes precedence over

2. Align AI Act and GDPR requirements on DPIA to accelerate AI adoption  
Because AI is trained on vast amount of data, the data laws and in particular the GDPR 
also apply to the training of AI models/systems with personal data. However, there is a 
significant discrepancy between what constitutes a high-risk activity under the AI Act and 
how DPAs interpret the GDPR. The latter have developed a broad interpretation of 
activities that pose a high risk from a data protection perspect ive. For instance, any use 
of new technologies involving the processing of large amounts of personal data is high -
risk and therefore requires a data protection impact assessment (DPIA). This approach 
differs from the AI Act, which requires a fundamental rights impact assessment for a 
limited number of use cases (Article 27) that effectively pose a high risk to consumers and 
data subjects. The two approaches should be aligned to the extent that the processing 
activities concern the training, development or use of a  AI system or model. This would 
provide a maximum level of clarity for the concerned data cont rollers and reduce the 
burden of compliance for low-risk activities, thus accelerating AI adoption across sectors.

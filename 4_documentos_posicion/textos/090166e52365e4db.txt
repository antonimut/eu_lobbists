Feedback to Call for Evidence: 
Digital Omnibus (Digital Package on Simplification) 
(October 10, 2025) 
 
Zimmer Biomet, a global leader in medical technology, supports the European Union’s digital 
ambitions and shares the European Commission’s objective to foster innovation that benefits patients 
and health systems.  In healthcare, connected medical technologies enable personalized prevention, 
faster diagnosis, and patient -specific treatments. Data generated by medical devices  are part of the 
intended use under the Medical Devices Regulation (MDR ). The MDR  sets robust, comprehensive 
requirements for product design, patient safety, and security. When horizontal digital rules apply on 
top of MDR obligations, alignment is essential to safeguard patient access to high -quality, modern 
devices while avoiding unnecessary administrative burden. 
Today, medtech companies operate in a dense and fragmented framework of EU digital 
legislation. It includes the Artificial Intelligence (AI) Act, the Data Act, the European Health Data 
Space (EHDS), and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). While each instrument pursues 
legitimate and important policy goals, their combined effect can create overlapping, duplicative, and 
sometimes inconsistent obligations. Divergent implementation and enforcement across EU Member 
States further compound legal uncertainty, delay deployment, and divert resources from innovation and 
patient care. 
Zimmer Biomet welcomes the opportunity to contribute feedback to the Digital Package on 
Simplification. We support a bold, coherent, and innovation -friendly approach to digital regulation. 
The goal must be to streamline and clarify interactions between horizontal digital laws and sector -
specific requirements. Simplification is not about lowering standards ; it is about making compliance 
more efficient, predictable, and practicable. This will help ensure that safe, effective innovation reaches 
European patients and health systems faster. 
 
Broaden the Scope to Deliver Real Digital Simplification 
The Digital Package on Simplification presents a critical opportunity to streamline Europe’s 
complex digital regulatory landscape. However, in its current form, the package risks falling 
short of its purpose. By excluding major legislative pillars such as the Data Act and the EHDS, 
both central to Europe’s digital transition , it overlooks two frameworks that are creating 
significant overlap and uncertainty for actors in the healthsector.

The European Commission rightly identifies that cumulative regulatory burdens and 
inconsistencies across digital legislation hinder innovation and competitiveness. Yet, only by 
including the Data Act and EHDS in the scope of the Digital Package on Simplification, the 
effort will be successful. These two laws are among the most consequential for Europe’s digital 
health ecosystem, directly affecting data access, interoperability, and patient safety. Failing to 
align them with other horizontal instruments, such as the AI Act, the Cyber Resilience Act, and 
the GDPR, risks perpetuating the very fragmentation the package aims to resolve. 
Manufacturers of connected medical devices face conflicting obligations under the 
MDR, Data Act, and EHDS. For instance, a device that collects patient data for clinical 
monitoring must simultaneously comply with  MDR safety requirements, EHDS data-sharing 
rules, and  Data Act access obligations , alongside other cross -cutting product safety, 
cybersecurity, and privacy frameworks such as the  AI Act, NIS2 Directive, GDPR, and the  
Cyber Resilience Act. When these frameworks are not aligned, companies must apply multiple 
interpretations and reporting structures, creating unnecessary administrative burden, 
uncertainty for regulators, and delayed patient access to innovation. 
Recommendations: 
• Expand the scope of the Digital Package on Simplification to explicitly include the 
Data Act and the EHDS. 
• Ensure real simplification by addressing not only reporting obligations but also the 
structural interplay between digital laws , removing redundancies, and clarifying 
compliance pathways for innovation-driven sectors such as medical technologies. 
 
Align Digital and Sectoral Rules for Innovation and Safety 
Horizontal digital laws (e.g., the Data Act, GDPR, and the AI Act) were crafted for the wider 
economy, yet medical technologies already operate under comprehensive sectoral regimes 
(MDR and EHDS). When horizontal rules are applied without regard to sectoral realities, they 
generate overlap, legal uncertainty, and conflicting obligations. The absence of an explicit 
hierarchy between horizontal and sectoral law leaves manufacturers unsure which rul e 
prevails when duties clash, diverting resources from innovation to paperwork and slowing 
patient access. 
Examples: 
• Under the Data Act, manufacturers of connected medical devices may be required to 
share raw or uninterpreted data  outside the secure frameworks of the MDR and the 
EHDS. This risks loss of clinical context, misinterpretation, and security breaches, 
while discouraging investment in advanced technologies. For instance, a third party

requesting raw telemetry from an implant could receive data that, without MDR or 
EHDS safeguards, is easily misread and poses patient-safety risks. 
• Under the AI Act, unclear pathways for notified-body designation, unaligned definitions 
such as “substantial modification,” and duplicate conformity-assessment steps create 
bottlenecks and uncertainty. MDR  and AI Act -designated notified bodies face 
inconsistent requirements when assessing embedded AI, leading to delays and 
fragmented decisions across the EU. For instance, an AI -enabled medical device 
undergoing routine algorithm updates may face repeated conformity assessments due 
to differing interpretations of what constitutes a substantial modification  under the AI 
Act versus a substantial change under MDR , slowing innovation and patient access 
without improving safety. 
• Unclear boundaries between the Data Act and the GDPR create legal uncertainty in 
handling mixed personal and non-personal health data. Ambiguous role allocation and 
overlapping compliance duties increase administrative burden and litigation risk. This 
lack of coordination adds complexity, discourages investment, and delays the delivery 
of innovative medical technologies to patients. 
Recommendations: 
• Apply the lex specialis principle to ensure sector-specific rules take precedence by 
(1) establishing the EHDS as the primary framework  for health-data sharing and 
interoperability, and (2) issuing binding guidance confirming that, in case of conflict, 
sectoral frameworks prevail; specifically, the MDR over the AI Act and the EHDS over 
the Data Act. 
• Clarify how GDPR principles (data minimisation, proportionality, anonymisation, 
consent) interact with Data Act sharing obligations. 
• Align the AI Act’s “substantial modification” with MDR concepts of significant change 
and substantial change. 
 
Allow More Time to Make the EU’s Digital Agenda Work 
The European Union’s digital agenda is ambitious and forward -looking, yet its successful 
implementation demands greater time and predictability. In the medical technology sector, 
product lifecycles are long and complex , often spanning several years from design to 
deployment, due to the depth of research, internal and ext ernal clinical validation, and 
regulatory scrutiny required . For instance, the third -party conformity assessment required 
under the MDR alone typically takes 18 to 24 months to complete. As a result, manufacturers, 
notified bodies, and competent authorities are struggling to keep pace with the rapid and

overlapping timelines of new digital legislation. The absence of clear, enforceable deadlines 
and consistent interpretation across Member States further compounds uncertainty and delay, 
with some national authorities still establishing the necessary oversight structures. For medical 
technology manufacturers, this lack of predictability and preparation time increases 
compliance costs, disrupts innovation cycles, and risks delaying patient access to safe and 
effective technologies. 
Examples: 
• Under the current implementation plan of the AI Act, high -risk AI systems used in 
medical technologies are expected to comply by 2 August 2027. However, harmonised 
standards, critical for conformity assessment , are not expected before 2026, leaving 
manufacturers with only a narrow window to adapt compliance systems and to secure 
additional certifications. 
• Notified bodies are also still in the process of being designated to assess AI -enabled 
devices, and several procedural uncertainties remain unresolved. This compressed 
and uncertain timeline threatens to disrupt innovation pipelines and delay access to 
life-improving AI-enabled medical devices. 
Recommendations: 
• Align the application of the AI Act  for high-risk medical AI systems with sectoral 
frameworks by extending the application date to 2029, in line with the EHDS 
application date and the MDR/IVDR transitional periods. 
• Do not retroactively apply new digital requirements to products already placed on 
the market. 
• Harmonise implementation and decision -making across Member States through 
clear, EU -wide guidance and statutory duties on regulators ; standardise core 
definitions like data holder/controller, provider, user, significant/severe incident. 
• Publish guidances early, but at least 18-24 months before they come into effect and 
ensure meaningful consultation with stakeholders, such as manufacturers, notified 
bodies, and healthcare professionals during the drafting process. 
  
5/5 
The Digital Package on Simplification represents a timely and essential opportunity to address the 
growing complexity of the EU’s digital framework. Zimmer Biomet urges policymakers to take decisive 
action to broaden the package’s scope, align overlapping digital laws, and remove unnecessary 
administrative burdens. A more coordinated and proportionate approach will allow manufacturers to 
focus resource s on advancing safe, effective technologies rather than navigating regulatory 
uncertainty. 
Zimmer Biomet fully aligns with MedTech Europe’s position paper on the Simplification of EU 
Digital Legislation (available here) and shares its commitment to creating a coherent, proportionate, 
and future-ready regulatory environment that drives competitiveness and ensures faster access to safe 
and effective medical technologies across the European Union.

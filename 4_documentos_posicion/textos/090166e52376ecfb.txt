INTRODUCTION TIC Council, the international trade association representing the independent Testing, Inspection and Certification (TIC) sector, sees the Digital Omnibus and the broader Digital Package as an opportunity to promote efficient and proportionate legislation, without compromising the core policy objectives of protection, safety, and security. Simplification should ultimately enhance clarity and consistency to improve legal certainty for all -setter.  With this overarching principle in mind, TIC Council puts forward the following recommendations. CYBERSECURITY  TIC Council warns against the risks of postponing the implementation of the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA). The CRA is a landmark for consumer protection, in recognising that consumer products must also be cybersecure. Any delay would create legal uncertainty, weaken standards development, and put consumers and industries at risk by leaving personal data and critical systems exposed. o The TIC sector is prepared to support companies in complying with CRA requirements by acting as Notified Bodies1. To enable this, Member States must designate notifying authorities2 by the June 2026 deadline.  Through secondary legislation, recognise Cybersecurity Act (CSA) schemes such as EUCC as a pathway to compliance under the CRA for important and critical products. This would give industry flexible compliance options, allowing businesses to leverage CSA schemes as recognised pathways to meet CRA requirements.  Include the CSA  revision in the Digital Omnibus, for the sake of overall coherence of existing legal frameworks. Align review measures so stakeholders face a single, coordinated process rather than parallel legislative tracks. TIC Council recommendations for the CSA revision include: o Accelerate the accreditation of Conformity Assessment Bodies (CABs). o Develop new, targeted schemes that integrate multiple regulatory requirements (e.g., cybersecurity and data privacy).  Stress the need for all remaining Member States to fully transpose the NIS 2 Directive. Delays undermine the cybersecurity of critical infrastructure and create fragmented enforcement.  Uneven national implementation also complicates the accreditation and oversight of CABs.  1 A notified body is an organisation designated by an EU country to assess the conformity of certain products before being placed on the market.  Streamline reporting without weakening protections. Simplification of incident reporting must preserve necessary data for incident response and market surveillance. Harmonised templates, a single reporting portal or interoperable reporting mechanisms should be prioritised. AI ACT IMPLEMENTATION  Prioritise the designation of notifying authorities. The deadline for Member States to appoint notifying authorities under the AI Act was August 2025, yet most have not done so. The Digital Omnibus could include provisions to support Member Statesparticularly smaller onesin operationalising their national authorities for this task. o Without designated notifying authorities, TIC Council members cannot request notification as Notified Bodies3. This makes it harder for the TIC sector to support industry compliance, even when fully prepared both technically and procedurally to assess AI systems and products in line with the AI Act.  Activate the use of alternative compliance mechanisms4 envisioned in the AI Act (Art. 41) when standards are delayed.  TIC Council opposes a delay to the AI Act implementation. Postponement would lead to uncertainty, slow risks making the regulatory environment unpredictable, distorting competition, and leaving potential risks unaddressed, thereby setting a harmful precedent. o However, most Member States are still in the process of establishing their competent authorities, including notifying authorities. These delays could create practical challenges for the accreditation and notification of CABs. To mitigate these challenges, we call for fast-tracking and prioritising this accreditation process. o Further, the TIC industry could support a limited and clearly defined technical extension of up to six months, solely to ensure that CABs are notified in due time and, hence, are eligible to carry out conformity assessments for high-risk AI systems in line with the AI Act. Such an extension must, however, come hand in hand with national authorities prioritising the rapid accreditation and notification of CABs to enable timely and effective implementation.  Ensure identification meets high cybersecurity standards, using certification as a key tool to guarantee trust and reliability.  Consult relevant stakeholders, including the TIC sector, during the development of the Wallet and its security features.  Align the EU Business Wallet with the eIDAS Regulation. DATA ACQUIS   Call for consolidating the EU data acquis, which is currently fragmented across various legislations. Streamlining and aligning these frameworks would improve legal clarity and facilitate compliance for businesses.  TIC companies are well-positioned to act as trusted partners to industry (particularly SMEs and mid-caps) in operationalising the consolidated framework, supporting compliance through audits, training, and practical guidance. CONCLUSION standard-setter. The Digital Omnibus should be used as a practical tool to support the successful implementation of these laws, reducing administrative burdens without undermining safety, security, or policy objectives. By following the recommendations outlined in this paperstreamlining reporting, accelerating CRA and AI Act compliance, designating notifying authorities and supporting them, and ensuring secure and compliant digital identificationthe EU can simplify processes while maintaining high standards for safety, security, and trust in the digital economy. The independent TIC sector will remain a key ally in the process of simplification for the competitiveness of EU industries.

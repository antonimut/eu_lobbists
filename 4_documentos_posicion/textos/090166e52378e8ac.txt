Executive summary 
The EU is at a pivotal moment where excessive regulatory complexity risks 
undermining its competitiveness and the Commission’s ambition for a broader, 
simpler and faster Union. Simplification, embedded in the 2025 Competitiveness 
Compass and Work Programme , is strongly supported by the banking sector, 
which views it as essential for achieving Europe’s growth. Current overlapping and 
sometimes contradictory rules impose disproportionate obligations, unnecessary 
burdens, and misallocation of resources, limiti ng banks’ ability to compete and 
innovate and finance sustainability, and defence.  
The EBF welcomes the Commission’s 2025 focus on Simplification and 
acknowledges the progress made through Omnibus initiatives, while stressing the 
importance of getting this process right. At the same time, much more remains to 
be done, particularly in add ressing the overlapping of horizontal and sector -
specific rules, which is often the case as the financial sector is already heavily 
regulated. Some simplification measures, such as exempting digital channels 
distributing financial products and services fro m the Cyber Resilience Act or 
clarifying that logistic regression is not AI, are clear and quickly achievable 
milestones. Broader and more complex reforms, however, could be pursued in a 
second wave.  
In the pursuit for simplification, the EBF does not advocate deregulation. 
We call for a more effective and coherent regulatory framework that 
upholds key policy objectives such as financial stability, data protection 
and consumer protection, while fostering growth and competitiveness. To 
do so, we propose to: 
1. Streamline regulatory burdens and reporting in order to achieve regulatory 
coherence: aligning definitions, thresholds, and timelines across EU 
frameworks is crucial to prevent overreporting and duplication.  
2. Ensure that supervisory reviews focus on material risks rather than box -
ticking exercises: avoiding parallel obligations that dilute resources into 
compliance rather than strengthening defences, combined with clear 
feedback loops from authorities.

1. Data Acquis 
We believe it is essential to ensure consistency and legal coherence across data 
regulations. The EU has recently adopted numerous regulations related to data, 
both horizontal (e.g., Data Act, DGA, Open Data Directive, AI Act) and sectoral, 
which interact and sometimes overlap between them and with existing norms 
(e.g., GDPR). 
While we support the EU’s goal of promoting an integrated data economy in 
Europe, we find that in practice, the current framework leaves important gaps: 
1. The lack of harmonisation between data regulations and financial sector 
rules creates uncertainty for organisations managing cross -border data 
flows.  
2. Legal fragmentation among EU Member States and with third countries, 
coupled with diverging interpretations by supervisory authorities, further 
complicates compliance and increases operational burdens.  
3. A sector-specific approach to data sharing - as opposed to a horizontal, 
cross-sectoral framework - hinders the objective of creating a genuine 
Single Market for Data in Europe. 
At the financial sector level, the introduction of additional sector -specific 
frameworks - such as the Commission's proposal for a Regulation on Financial 
Data Access (FiDA) - should be carefully reconsidered in light of the Commission's 
simplification and competitiveness agenda. 
From a competitiveness standpoint, requiring financial institutions to develop 
data-sharing mechanisms (schemes) for a very wide range of (potentially 
sensitive) customer financial data, in absence of clear use cases and 
customer/market demand, risks imposing disproportionate burdens and diverting 
financial, IT and human resources from more concrete initiatives without clear 
returns on investments.  
From a simplification perspective, the interplay between FiDA and existing data 
regimes may lead to significant overlaps and regulatory uncertainties. For 
instance, the relationship between FiDA schemes and data spaces under the DGA 
remains unclear, with F iDA schemes potentially duplicating or adding to existing 
data spaces. Similar ambiguities also concern the data sharing modalities under 
FiDA and the legal restrictions under the Data Act, which may conflict or result in 
redundant obligations. Finally, the interplay with the GDPR remains unsettled, for 
example on the concepts of “permission” vs “consent”. 
Without greater legal and operational clarity, financial institution risk facing 
overlapping obligations across different regulations, increasing implementation 
and compliance costs, which would run contrary to the overall objective of creating 
a unified data economy in the EU.

Given these many uncertainties, the impact of FiDA should be reassessed through 
the lens of competitiveness and simplification, or withdrawn altogether, as the 
European Commission considered before presenting its work plan in 2025.   We 
also believe it is premature to establish FiDA, when the overarching European 
Strategy for Data is under review. 
To unlock the real benefits of data cross -fertilisation for the digital economy and 
ensure a level playing field, the EU's ambition should be for a voluntary, market-
driven, and cross -sectoral approach to data sharing  based on fair and 
reciprocal conditions and supported by incentives and clear governance 
frameworks.  
Such an approach could also help address data scarcity and poor data quality 
challenges in AI training, such as unlocking access to high-value datasets, reduce 
transaction costs, and facilitate the development of robust, data-driven AI models 
– an area oft en constrained by regulatory barriers or hindered by complex and 
resource-intensive contractual negotiations. 
We underline that any simplification measures in the field of data sharing must 
also be carried out without prejudice to Directive 96/9/EC on databases and 
Directive 2016/943 on trade secrets. To ensure legal certainty, the Omnibus 
should promote standard contractual models for data licensing, providing 
downstream controls and effective remedies under the IP Enforcement Directive. 
Moreover, the Omnibus should harmonise the text and data mining (TDM) opt-out 
under Article 4(3) DSM Directive with the disclosure obligations in Article 53 of the 
AI Act, guaranteeing that a machine-readable opt-out remains effective across the 
Single Market. In the financial sector, where sectoral frameworks such as FiDA 
may impose additional data-sharing obligations, we suggest the Commission carry 
out ex ante IP impact assessments to avoid undermining sui generis rights or 
revealing sensitive know-how.  
2. Rules on cookies 
We concur with the necessity of undertaking a thorough reflection on “cookies” 
and other tracking technologies regulated by the ePrivacy Directive, including an 
evaluation of whether current cookie management requirements have truly served 
the intended purpose (or whether they have contributed to “cookie fatigue”). 
Even though the GDPR and the current e -privacy are different rulesets, a 
necessary step is to ensure greater alignment between the ePrivacy Directive and 
the GDPR. We note that some requirements established by the Directive do not 
always coincide with thos e provided for by the GDPR, resulting in interpretative 
uncertainty, divergent enforcement practices among Member States and 
additional compliance costs for businesses. This is particularly evident with regard 
to the legal bases for processing: while the e Privacy Directive only recognises 
consent as a valid ground for processing, the GDPR allows for other bases, such

make them efficient and appealing. 
3. Simplification of cybersecurity regulation  
Financial institutions support the EU’s objective of strengthening cybersecurity and 
digital resilience. However, overlapping rules and inconsistencies between 
legislative frameworks risk creating unnecessary complexity, legal uncertainty, 
and, in some cases, conflicting obligations. A coherent regulatory environment is 
needed to ensure both security and efficiency. 
CRA – DORA interplay: The financial sector strongly supports the exemption of 
financial institutions regulated under the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) 
from the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA). As the sector has evidenced, the duplication 
of outcomes (link) and the duplication of scope between DORA and the CRA are 
extensive. As a result, while compliance with the CRA will require additional 
resource and create regulatory complexity for financial institutions, it will not 
enhance risk management in the sector.  
DORA is an all-encompassing cyber and ICT risk management regulation crafted 
for the financial sector. It includes a dedicated Regulatory Technical Standard on 
an ICT Risk Management Framework and is supplemented by further guidance 
from the European Central Bank and European Supervisory Authorities. In addition 
to DORA, the sector is subject to further ICT risk management rules through the 
supervisory review and evaluation process as well as numerous national 
requirements. DORA sets out in detail how cyber and ICT risk should be managed 
by all financial entities in the EU. As such, the requirements in DORA do not

delineate between systems and "products" - financial entities are expected to 
manage cyber and ICT risk regardless of where in their business that risk 
manifests. The CRA, in comparison, is the first horizontal product legislation to 
apply to financial services and by virtue of this more specific focus duplicates, but 
does not exceed, the scope of DORA.  
In the same way, supervision of financial institutions by EU financial regulators 
does not delineate between systems and "products". Supervisory exams and 
supervisory powers extend to all facets of the financial institutions business and 
operations. Supervisory powers are extensive, including up to the ability to revoke 
the financial institutions licences and therefore ability to operate in the EU. No EU 
financial supervisors would recognise a limitation on their authority to examine or 
make additional requ irements on a "product with digital elements" even if that 
concept is not one that explicitly used in the financial sector.   
In addition to scope and authorities, the objectives of DORA and the CRA directly 
overlap. The CRA introduces a cybersecurity framework throughout the lifecycle 
of a product with increased governance, transparency and reporting requirements. 
DORA, in comparison, lays out a comprehensive framework on cybersecurity and 
ICT risk management, with thorough governance, testing and incident reporting 
requirements. Thus, exempting the financial sector from the CRA would not reduce 
the security standards in-effect for financial services in the EU.  
Inclusion of the financial sector in the scope of the CRA would, however, add 
additional complexity and compliance processes. The CRA will introduce a further 
incident reporting regime, extensive technical documentation requirements and 
new conformity assessment procedures. As these would all need to be provided 
to authorities outside of the financial sector, they represent additional compliance 
burden on the sector. 
In conclusion, the exemption from the CRA will result in a significant simplification 
for the financial sector without any reduction in the security or risk management 
requirements applied to the sector. Reduced regulatory complexity will allow 
cybersecurity professionals to focus less on compliance tasks and more on 
managing the risks that the rapidly evolving cybersecurity threat landscape 
presents to the EU economy.   The financial sector should therefore be 
excluded from the scope of the CRA under the delegated act mechanisms 
in CRA Article 2(5). 
Cybersecurity-related incident reporting obligations:  Incident reporting 
obligations become burdensome when multiple frameworks impose duplicative or 
overlapping requirements, especially when operating across different jurisdictions. 
Under DORA, financial entities already face strict incident reporting and resilience 
requirements. The NIS2 Directive also introduces reporting and registration 
obligations. Without proper alignment, these parallel regimes risk inefficiencies 
and unnecessary administrative cost s. Harmonisation of reporting and clear

Moreover, the transposition of the NIS2 Directive into national law risks imposing

GDPR vs Cyber Resilience:  Potential conflicts exist between GDPR Article 15 
(Right of access) and operational resilience obligations under DORA. In order to 
comply with Article 15 GDPR, financial entities may be required to disclose detailed 
information about recipients of personal data, including ICT vendors, which could 
expose supply chains to targeted cyberattacks. While Recital 63 GDPR allows some 
limitation of access rights based on “rights or freedoms of others,” including trade 
secrets, this wording provides insufficient certainty in practice. Recent case law, 
such as C-154/21 Österreichische Post, has increased uncertainty around how far 
such exceptions can be applied. EU legislation should confirm that data 
access rights can be restricted on genuine security grounds, ensuring that 
privacy rights do not inadvertently compromise operational resilience. 
Supervisory guidance is recommended in this area. 
4. The smooth application of the AI Act rules 
The financial and insurance sectors are mature, highly regulated and early 
adopters of AI. To fully unlock its potential while ensuring its responsible use, we 
recommend:  
Maturity of AI Act:  The existing rules are not ready for implementation and 
legislators have yet to create multiple implementing acts. These specifications are 
essential; they will ensure that regulatory requirements can be implemented with 
legal certainty.   In particular, g iven the complex regulatory environment, 
implementation should be proportionate and be developed in partnership with 
sectoral regulators and industry. The Commission should also ensure there is a 
feasible timeframe between the publicatio n of guidelines and implementation 
deadlines.  
Sector-specific regulation: The requirements listed in the AI Act are already be 
covered in large part by existing requirements for banks. Therefore, discrepancies 
between the AI Act and the existing legal framework for banks must be avoided 
and the legislative body must clarify which rules shall take precedence.   
Definition: The definition of AI and the high -risk classification in the AI Act still 
begs several questions, which have to be addressed in order to ensure legal 
certainty. Sector-specific clarification or examples of commonly used banking 
models, including their classifications based on the AI Act, are deeply welcomed.

Conventional IT systems that do not use machine learning or self -optimisation in 
any form, should not be considered AI. This includes use cases that use basic 
statistics processes, such as linear and logistic regressions.  Misclassifying these 
as AI systems  under the AI Act would create unnecessary compliance costs 
without improving outcomes. Rather than excluding named techniques, we 
recommend EU guidance with examples distinguishing traditional deterministic 
software from systems performing inference within the meaning of art. 3 AI -Act. 
This avoids over-classification while preserving legal certainty. 
Roles: Uncertainties remain as to the separation between the role of the provider 
and that of other deployers, for example, where two institutions jointly deploy an 
AI application, for example under a joint venture in the event that a financial 
institution cooperates with another enterprise, or if an AI system is used within 
the corporate structure of several banks, where multi ple legal entities are all 
‘deployers’ of the same AI application. This hampers legal certainty and could, as 
a result, hinder progress. Therefore, the separation of these roles ha s to be 
clarified.  Considering that the PLD now covers AI systems and treats providers as 
manufacturers, we seek confirmation that deployers under the AI Act (who are 
not concurrently acting as providers/manufacturers) are not subject to the PLD's 
strict liability regime” 
Reporting serious incidents: For reporting serious incidents according to the AI 
Act, reporting methods and platforms that are already established for financial 
institutions should be used. This will keep reporting as streamlined and efficient 
as possible, so that the time, effort an d benefits remain proportional and, above 
all, practical.   
Supervisory architecture : assign responsibilities to sectoral supervisors. To 
ensure clear, consistent, and effective oversight of AI, these authorities should be 
promptly confirmed as AI market supervisors. Additionally, the AI Office should 
include financial and insurance supervisors when developing secondary legislation 
and guidelines, to promote supervisory convergence and prevent fragmented 
supervision and conflicting interpretations.  
Regulatory coherence across the AI Act, GDPR, DORA, and other financial and 
data protection rules is essential, since overlapping obligations and diverging 
interpretations create legal uncertainty and compliance costs/ burdens. Alignment 
and simplification are key to ensure c onsistent application of the rules, through 
clearly articulated and legally valid guidance jointly issued the by these regulators 
to provide the certainty and consistency needed by businesses. The focus should 
be on the ‘delta’ between the AI Act and the existing banking regulations, and the 
dedicated work done by legislators and supervisors to comprehensively 
understand and define this delta should be made public to support consistency 
and efficiency.

Bias testing: There is no harmonized definition of ‘bias testing’ or how ‘fairness’ 
should be formalized in AI -systems. Level 1 regulation does not clearly specify 
when and how bias should be examined and mitigated. A common understanding 
of what kind of processes or ac tivities should constitute bias testing and what 
specific outcomes it should produce is equally lacking. Therefore, we urge the 
convening of sector -specific round table discussions with banks and supervisory 
authorities with the aim of sharing EU-level good practices. 
Achieving the right balance: It is essential to promote a more balanced AI Act 
that protects consumers while supporting economic needs to foster European 
innovation and competitiveness. The strong emphasis on consumer protection in 
the AI Act, sometimes at the expense of European co mpetitiveness and 
sovereignty, risks being counterproductive and stifling innovation in Europe, thus 
widening the gap with American and Asian players and increasing technological 
dependency.  
5. European Digital Identity Framework 
Interplay between eIDAS  2.0 and PSD2 (and future PSR/PSD3): the 
financial sector supports the objective of establishing an EU trusted and secure 
digital identity framework that enhances user control, trust, and interoperability 
across Member States. 
Given the links between eIDAS 2.0 and the upcoming Payment Services 
Regulation (PSR), we believe that ensuring a correct interplay is a key priority. 
Additional clarity is needed on how the payments authentication process, 
regulated under PSD2, interacts w ith the EUDIW implementation. Since the 
financial sector operates within an increasingly complex regulatory environment, 
it is essential that legislative initiatives are conceived, read and implemented 
consistently also to be future proof. 
Definition of "online identification": the two terms “online” and “identification” 
have a specific meaning under PSD2 (confirmed in PSR/PSD3) and that they are 
used also in eIDAS 2.0 but without clarifying the specific meaning, we believe it is 
essential to first clarify the meaning of “online identification” within the scope of 
eIDAS 2.0 and then make sure that these terms are aligned under PSR/PSD3. This 
is of paramount importance to ensure the correct application of the rules regarding 
the PSPs’ acceptance of EUDIW, and to avoid any breaches or overlaps that may 
adversely affect PSPs, and ultimately their customers. 
The reading of SCA under PSD2 and SUA under eIDAS 2.0: Clarity is needed 
regarding the relationship between Strong Customer Authentication (SCA) under 
PSD2 and Strong User Authentication (SUA) under eIDAS 2.0. While the definitions 
of SCA and SUA are similar, they are not equivalent: SCA covers banking 
(specifically payments) services under a contractual relationship with a customer 
and with additional measures (e.g., Dynamic Linking) provided for under PSD2,

From a regulatory perspective, these differences bring considerably different

Furthermore, it is relevant to mention that PSD2 expressly requires that if PSPs 
decide to outsource part of the SCA application to a Third Party (e.g., wallet 
providers), they must have in place and comply with outsourcing agreements and 
determine the liability between the actors involved. 
These are priority areas where regulatory clarification is urgently required to 
prevent conflicting compliance obligations.   
EU Business Wallet: in relation to the Commission's plan to create a EU business 
wallet, at this stage we believe clarity is needed on the following issues: 
• The relationship between the proposed European Wallet for Businesses 
(EUBW) and the eIDAS2 digital identity wallet (EUDIW), as the latter is also 
aimed for legal persons; 
• The architecture and technical/operational requirements for the business 
wallet, which we expect to be predominantly deployed as a cloud -based 
solutions (while the EUDIW, based on the current ARF specifications, will be 
mobile device-bound).  
The alignment of eIDAS2 with the planned EU Business Wallet should address IP 
aspects explicitly. In particular, the use of trademarks and logos in credentials and 
trust services must be subject to clear licensing and swift takedown mechanisms 
against fraudulent or infringing use. Where interoperability relies on standardised 
technologies subject to SEPs, access should be guaranteed on FRAND terms to 
avoid market foreclosure.

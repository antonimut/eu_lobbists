Feedback: Digital Omnibus (Digital Package on Simplification) 
 
October 13, 2025 
 
Telekom Austria AG welcomes the European Commission’s initiative for a Digital Omnibus focused 
on simplification and harmonization of the digital regulatory framework. We appreciate the 
opportunity to provide feedback on this important endeavor. In our view, a broad and comprehensive 
approach is essential to achieve the desired outcomes. Therefore, we have deliberately raised issues 
relating not only to the legal acts specifically identified in the consultation, but also to other key 
instruments that form part of the EU’s di gital acquis. We believe that addressing these related 
frameworks together at this stage is necessary to effectively reduce administrative burdens, enhance 
consistency, and foster innovation and competitiveness within the European digital market. Based 
on our experience, we would like to highlight the following priority areas and recommendations: 
 
 
A1 Group´s Recommendations for the Digital Package on Simplification 
 
1. One-Stop Shop and Unified Regulatory Approach 
 
• Issue: Different regulatory authorities enforce frameworks at the national level, leading to 
inconsistent interpretations and legal uncertainty.  
• Recommendation: Establish unified contact points (One-Stop Shops), reducing the need to 
interact with multiple authorities.  Advocate for closer cooperation among existing authorities 
to consolidate overlapping functions into a single responsible body, enhancing consistency, 
reducing legal uncertainty, and lowering administrative burdens for businesses. 
 
2. Streamlined and Harmonized Reporting Requirements for Security Incidents 
 
• Issue: Current regulations, such as DORA, NIS 2, and the Cyber Resilience Act, impose similar 
requirements for reporting security incidents, leading to duplicated efforts and increased 
administrative burdens. Companies often have to submit multiple reports to different authorities, 
leading to unnecessary extra work. 
• Recommendation: Harmonizing reporting obligations, standardizing reporting mechanisms, 
and introducing a unified reporting form could significantly reduce administrative effort and 
improve efficiency for companies. The CRA should include a provision similar to that found in 
Article 8 of the Resilience of Critical Entities Directive (Directive (EU) 2022/2557) to avoid double 
burdens. For example, when operators of digital services —which are already subject to at least 
equivalent risk management measures and mandatory report ing—place products with digital 
elements on the market, these should be exempted from Articles 13 and 14, and instead the risk 
management measures and reporting requirements of the relevant legal framework (Directive 
(EU) 2022/2555) should be taken into account. 
 
3. Alignment of Overlapping Regulatory Requirements 
 
a) Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) and Radio Equipment Directive (RED) 
• Issue: Under the current legal framework, there are various overlaps between the requirements 
in the CRA and the RED. However, these are formulated differently and focus on different aspects 
of security. This leads to unnecessary complexity and increased effort for companies. 
Additionally, the delineation of authority for cybersecurity is not optimal: while Germany 
consolidates all cybersecurity matters under the BSI (Federal Office for Information Security), in 
our context responsibility is currently assigned to the Market Off ice (Marktamt), which is 
referenced both in the CRA and, partially, in the RED. This fragmentation of authority may limit 
effectiveness and creates further uncertainty, as the Market Office is not fully appropriate or 
equipped for cybersecurity oversight. 
• Recommendation: Harmonization of the requirements in the CRA and RED is necessary, 
particularly in the areas of privacy protection, protection against unauthorized access, security 
assessments and testing, CE marking and declaration of conformity, security updates and 
maintenance, documentation and technical files, risk management, user information and 
instructions, as well as interoperability and compatibility. This would reduce complexity and 
ensure that all relevant requirements are met without companies having to make double efforts. 
It is further recommended to bundle all cybersecurity competences and responsibilities within a 
single, specialized authority.

• Examples of overlapping requirements: 
• Protection of privacy and personal data: 
• RED: Includes requirements for protecting the privacy and personal data of users. 
• CRA: Sets specific cybersecurity requirements that also include the protection of 
personal data. 
• Protection against unauthorized access: 
• RED: Devices must be designed in such a way that they are protected against 
unauthorized access and secure communication is ensured. 
• CRA: Sets more detailed requirements for protection against unauthorized access 
and ensuring the integrity and confidentiality of data. 
• Security assessment and testing: 
• RED: Requires that radio equipment meets certain safety standards and undergoes 
appropriate testing. 
• CRA: Requires more comprehensive security assessments and testing, especially 
regarding cybersecurity risks. 
• CE marking and declaration of conformity: 
• RED: Products must bear the CE marking and have a declaration of conformity that 
certifies that they meet the requirements of the directive. 
• CRA: Products must also bear the CE marking, and the declaration of conformity 
must show that the requirements of the CRA, in particular with regard to 
cybersecurity, are met. 
• Security updates and maintenance: 
• RED: Manufacturers are implicitly expected to provide security updates to ensure 
the safety of the devices over their lifespan. 
• CRA: Explicitly requires the provision of security updates and maintenance for 
connected devices to address known vulnerabilities and continuously ensure 
cybersecurity. 
• Documentation and technical files: 
• RED: Manufacturers must provide technical documentation and a declaration of 
conformity that shows the device meets the requirements of the RED. 
• CRA: Also requires technical documentation and a declaration of conformity that 
demonstrates compliance with cybersecurity requirements. 
• Risk management: 
• RED: Manufacturers must apply basic risk management principles to ensure their 
devices are safe and compliant. 
• CRA: Goes further by requiring a comprehensive risk management system 
specifically targeting cybersecurity risks, including identification, assessment, and 
mitigation of risks. 
• User information and instructions: 
• RED: Devices must be supplied with user information and instructions to ensure 
safe use. 
• CRA: Sets more detailed requirements for the provision of information and 
instructions, especially regarding security features and protection against cyber 
threats. 
• Interoperability and compatibility: 
• RED: Devices must be interoperable and compatible with other devices and 
networks to ensure safe operation. 
• CRA: Sets similar requirements for interoperability and compatibility, but with a 
stronger focus on ensuring cybersecurity in connected environments. 
b) European Accessibility Act and European Electronic Communications Code 
(EECC)  
 
• Issue: There are duplicative provisions regarding accessibility (e.g., real -time text "RTT", total 
conversation services) in both the Accessibility Act and the EECC, unnecessarily complicating 
compliance and increasing barriers to market entry for new entrants.  
• Recommendation: We recommend ensuring that market-specific requirements are consistently 
addressed within the primary legislative acts for each sector. Cross -regulation and overlaps 
should be avoided to simplify compliance. The example of "EU -Alert" (Art. 100 EECC) for ci vil 
protection can serve as a positive model of streamlined regulation.

4. Simplification and Harmonization of GDPR Compliance  
 
• Issue: The implementation of data protection rights —particularly the rights of data subjects 
under GDPR (e.g., transparency, access, information)—often requires excessive time and effort 
from companies, without necessarily providing added benefit to individuals. Furthermore, the 
proliferation of guidance, national interpretations, and overlapping obligations (e.g., under e -
Privacy, AI Act, Data Act) creates significant complexity and legal uncertainty.  
• Recommendations: Advocate for the simplification and automation of GDPR compliance 
processes. Encourage regulatory bodies to provide clearer guidelines and support for businesses 
to streamline compliance efforts. It is imperative that the enforcement and interpretation of the 
rules be effectively harmonized to ensure a level playing field across Europe. In particular, 
aspects related to the rights of the data subject within the regulation should be reassessed or 
repealed in favor of a more balanced approach. This would significantly reduce the administrative 
burden and enable companies to concentrate on their core activities.  
• Examples 
• Promote the simplification and automation of GDPR compliance, especially regarding 
data subject rights. Clarify and possibly reassess the scope of these rights, ensuring 
they serve genuine data protection interests rather than other legal or tactical 
purposes (e.g., civil litigation). 
• Allow for the disclosure of broad categories of data recipients rather than the 
exhaustive listing of all processors and sub-processors. 
• For international data transfers, the conclusion of Standard Contractual Clauses 
(SCCs) with technical and organizational measures (TOMs) should suffice without the 
need for burdensome Transfer Impact Assessments (TIAs), or at least provide 
practical templates and support for TIAs. 
• Encourage a consultative, supportive approach by Data Protection Authorities, rather 
than a primarily punitive one. 
• Ensure effective collaboration between national and EU authorities for a consistent 
enforcement of GDPR. 
• The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) guidelines should be concise and 
focused, with an emphasis on practical case studies. 
 
5. Harmonization of Digital Legislation and Data Processing Rules (GDPR, AI Act, 
Data Act, DGA, etc.) 
 
• Issue: Divergent definitions, duplicative reporting obligations, and sometimes conflicting 
requirements across EU digital legislation (GDPR, Data Act, DGA, AI Act, ePrivacy, DSA, etc.) 
result in unnecessary complexity, duplicated efforts, and significant compli ance risks. For 
example, data minimization obligations under GDPR may conflict with extensive data sharing 
obligations for AI training under the Data Act. Redundant reporting requirements—such as those 
in the Digital Services Act for hosting providers or s maller platforms—also needlessly increase 
administrative burdens.  
• The application of EU legal acts is made more difficult when the terms used are not 
defined uniformly, but are instead defined differently in various legal acts. 
Duplication (same regulatory purpose but different forms) is currently commonplace, 
for example with the reporting of data breaches under the ePrivacy Directive and the 
GDPR (different deadlines for notification).  
• The data minimization principle of the GDPR conflicts with the extensive data 
requirements for AI training and the support of data sharing under the Data Act, 
creating significant challenges for companies due to complex and contradictory 
requirements for data processing. The complexity of the Data Act and its interaction 
with other data-focused regulations create considerable challenges for businesses.  
• Excessive reporting obligations under the Digital Services Act (DSA) not only affect 
large online platforms, but may also impact other providers of digital intermediary 
services, such as hosting services. Transparency reports must be prepared, and 
decisions regarding content moderation must be documented and reported. Article 
15 DSA contains the obligation to regularly publish transparency reports.  
• Recommendations: In general, it is necessary to reduce various reporting obligations to the 
absolute minimum required, such as under the Digital Services Act (DSA), which places excessive 
reporting burdens particularly on non-platform-based providers. Furthermore, there is a need to 
simplify and harmonize data regulations to reduce complexity and legal uncertainty. Clear 
guidelines should be established to balance the need for data sharing and protection, so that 
companies can comply with all relevant regulations without facing contradictory requirements.

With regard to the Data Act, unclear and circular definitions should be avoided. For e -Privacy, 
the current directive does not meet today’s requirements. To further streamline the legal 
framework and eliminate unnecessary regulatory overlap, the ePrivacy Directive should be 
completely repealed, with essential elements such as confidentiality of communications (e.g., 
Article 5) integrated into the GDPR, whil e outdated or redundant provisions (for example, 
provisions on security or the reporting of data breaches) —especially those disproportionately 
affecting telecommunications providers compared to OTT services —should be deleted without 
replacement. A risk-based approach for the processing of traffic data should be introduced.  
• Systematically simplify and harmonize digital legislation and data handling 
requirements, focusing on a risk-based, proportionate approach. 
• Reduce reporting obligations to what is strictly necessary, particularly for providers 
not primarily operating as digital platforms. 
• Avoid contradictory definitions and requirements by aligning terminology and 
regulatory scope across all digital acts. 
• Update and streamline the e -Privacy framework, focusing only on communications 
secrecy, and eliminate redundant provisions (such as on security and breach 
notifications).  
 
 
6. Full Harmonization of the NIS2 Directive 
 
• Issue: The NIS2 Directive has not been uniformly implemented  across Member States, with 
varying degrees of requirements and interpretations. Businesses active in multiple Member 
States face the burden of satisfying divergent national requirements.  
• Recommendation: The implementation of NIS2 should be fully harmonized across the EU, 
prohibiting additional or divergent national requirements. There should be explicit recognition of 
group-wide/compliance group audits, ensuring that multinationals can efficiently demons trate 
compliance through consolidated processes.  
 
Conclusion  
Telekom Austria remains firmly committed to ensuring compliant, secure, and competitive digital 
markets in Europe. We urge the European Commission to act swiftly and decisively to deliver legal 
certainty, administrative simplification, and the elimination of duplicative and contradictory 
regulatory requirements. The harmonization and streamlining of digital regulation is not only 
desirable but urgently needed to support a thriving, innovative, and resilient European digital 
economy.  
We appreciate the opportunity to contribute to this important process and would welcome further 
dialogue. Should you require any additional information or clarification regarding our 
recommendations, we are readily available to discuss these matters in more detail.

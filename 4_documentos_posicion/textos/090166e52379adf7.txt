  NIS2,  CRA,  DORA,  GDPR,  AI  Act   
Incident  Reporting   
Problem :  The  EU  must  consolidate  and  streamline  the  existing  multiple  reporting  obligations  for  cybersecurity  incidents  under  the  NIS2,  CRA,  DORA,  GDPR  and  AI  Act.  Companies  designated  as  ‘essential’  or  ‘important’  entities  must  report  incidents  under  NIS2,  often  duplicating  the  effort  already  required  for  personal  data  breaches  under  GDPR  or  for  product  vulnerabilities  under  the  CRA.  This  may  necessitate  reporting  to  multiple  authorities  using  different  forms,  content  and  deadlines  and  based  on  different  thresholds  and  terminology  required  by  each  law.   Solution :  The  EU  must  establish  a  single  reporting  regime  across  the  GDPR,  NIS2,  DORA,  CRA,  AI  Act  and  other  regulations  as  a  matter  of  urgency.  This  would  signiﬁcantly  contribute  to  the  European  Commission's  goal  of  reducing  reporting  requirements  by  at  least  25%  (35%  for  SMEs).  Reportable  incidents  should  be  ﬁled  under  one  format  to  one  EU  entity  -  for  example  the  national  notiﬁcation  entry  points  under  NIS2  -  which  can  cascade  information  to  adjacent  regulators  as  necessary.    Cyber  Resilience  Act  
Scope  (Article  2)  
Problem:  One  of  the  CRA’s  primary  areas  of  confusion  and  complexity  is  the  deﬁnition  of  remote  data  processing  and  accompanying  recitals  related  to  providers  of  cloud  solutions.  Such  providers  appear  to  be  excluded  from  the  CRA  but  are  then  brought  back  into  the  regulation  if  they  offer  mobile  applications,  which  in  today’s  mobile-ﬁrst  world,  is  often  a  requirement.  This  overlap  duplicates  requirements  for  cloud  providers,  making  compliance  with  both  the  NIS2  and  CRA  requirements  complex  and  costly.  Solution :  The  CRA  should  have  a  clear  exclusion  for  cloud  providers  that  are  in  scope  of  the  NIS2.  This  exclusion  is  intended  to  prevent  overlapping  regulatory  burdens  and  to  ensure  legal  certainty  for  cloud  service  providers  that  are  already  subject  to  the  extensive  cybersecurity  and  incident  reporting  obligations  under  NIS2.  Subjecting  these  providers  to  an  additional,  parallel  set  of  requirements  under  the  CRA  creates  unnecessary  administrative  and  ﬁnancial  burden  without  a  corresponding  increase  in  security.  
Vulnerability  Reporting  (Article  14)  
Problem :  Requiring  manufacturers  to  report  previously  unknown,  unpatched  vulnerabilities   to  EU  authorities  before  a  patch  is  ready  creates  severe  risks.  It   worsens  security  outcomes  by  forcing  vendors  to  rush  untested  patches  or  guidance,  potentially  introducing  new  bugs  or  failures  and  it  creates   fragmentation  and  risk  by  creating  a  "treasure  trove"  of  unpatched  vulnerabilities  for  malicious  actors,  especially  if  compiled  into  a  central  repository.  Solution:  Amend  Article  14  to  require  reporting  of  vulnerabilities  only  after  mitigating  or  corrective  actions  (i.e.,  a  patch  or  guidance)  have  occurred.  Vendors  must  be  granted  reasonable  time  to  safely  develop  and  release  the  ﬁx  to  customers  ﬁrst.  Instead  of  collecting  zero-day  data,  the  Commission  (via  ENISA)  should  focus  on  establishing  and  maintaining  a  public  catalog  of  Known  Exploited  Vulnerabilities  (KEV).  This  helps  companies  prioritize  patching  threats  that  are  actively  being  used  in  attacks,  improving  overall  cyber  defense  for  EU  users.

AI  Act   
Data  Governance  (Article  10)  
Problem:  Article  10  imposes  a  stricter  obligation  on  providers’  use  of  special  categories  of  personal  data  for  bias  testing  than  the  GDPR  (“strictly  necessary”  v.  “necessary”),  which  could  limit  providers’  abilities  to  effectively  mitigate  bias  in  AI  systems.   Solution:  Article  10  should  be  amended  to  ensure  full  alignment  with  the  GDPR.  The  Joint  EC/EDPB  Guidelines  on  the  AI  Act  and  GDPR  must  also  ensure  this  alignment  and  be  published  promptly  following  open  consultation  with  stakeholders.   
Harmonised  standards  (Article  40)  
Problem :  The  absence  of  harmonized  European  standards  intended  to  provide  a  presumption  of  conformity  for  the  EU  AI  Act's  high-risk  provisions  means  the  high-risk  requirements  are  set  to  become  applicable  before  companies  have  the  necessary  technical  and  legal  guidance  to  comply.  This  makes  it  practically  impossible  for  businesses  to  adapt  existing  systems  within  the  mandated  timeline.   Solution :  Article  40  should  be  amended  so  that  the  high-risk  requirements  become  applicable  only  12  months  after  the  relevant  harmonized  standards  are  published.  This  approach  would  provide  the  necessary  predictability  and  adequate  time  for  adaptation,  allowing  companies  to  achieve  compliance  effectively  and  fostering  innovation.

Data  Governance  Act  
International  Transfers  (Article  31)  
Problem:  The  Data  Governance  Act  introduces  inconsistent  requirements  for  data  transfers  that  go  above  and  beyond  the  GDPR’s  established  framework  for  personal  data  transfers.  This  is  challenging  for  companies  with  mixed  data  sets,  as  it  creates  impediments  to  companies’  ability  to  transfer  non-personal  data,  in  addition  to  the  personal  data  transfer  requirements,  resulting  in  administrative  burdens  that  outweigh  the  risks.  We  also  note  the  Data  Act  follows  a  similar  approach.   Solution :  For  this  reason,  where  the  provider’s  systems  store  personal  data,  any  valid  transfer  mechanism  under  the  GDPR  should  suffice  for  compliance,  without  the

duplication  of  obligations  under  the  Data  Governance  Act  or  the  Data  Act.  Open  Data  Directive   
New   
Problem :  The  current  Open  Data  Directive  faces  challenges  in  promoting  the  effective  reuse  of  public  sector  data,  particularly  in  the  domain  of  skills  and  labour  market  data.  This  data  often  lacks  the  necessary  granularity,  standardisation  and  timeliness  (interoperability  and  quality)  required  for  advanced  applications  like  labour  market  forecasting  in  the  age  of  AI  and  informing  training  initiatives.   Solution:  The  revised  Open  Data  Directive  should  consider  harmonized  technical  standards  and  data  models  for  key  high-value  datasets,  speciﬁcally  concerning  skills,  education,  and  employment  data.  This  would  require  Member  States  to  collect  and  publish  this  data  using  common  EU-wide  vocabularies  and  APIs,  signiﬁcantly  improving  its  quality,  granularity,  timeliness,  and  interoperability  for  reuse  in  AI-driven  labour  market  tools  and  upskilling  programmes.

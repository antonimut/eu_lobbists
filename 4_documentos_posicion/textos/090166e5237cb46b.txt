Call for evidence on Digital Omnibus - Cisco response  
The Draghi report on the Future of European Competitiveness highlighted the “rising weight 
of regulation” as a source of challenges for companies operating in the EU. Building on this 
conclusion, the European Commission has rightly embarked on a regulatory simplification 
exercise, with several ‘omnibus packages’ on the policymakers’ agenda in 2025.  
Cisco welcomes the opportunity to comment on the upcoming Digital Package. Amongst 
other priorities, we understand the focus will be on streamlining regulations about the 
reuse of public sector data, cybersecurity incident reporting obligations and the optimal 
application of the AI Act.  
Next to overregulation, the Draghi report also shed light on many of the challenges Europe 
is facing: a growing GDP gap, a talent gap, and low innovation capacity. The EU has lagged 
behind in the adoption of new technologies such as AI and cloud computing, and on the 
deployment of fiber and gigabit technologies, hampering the opportunities for growth and 
deepening the digital divide.  
The Digital Single Market was launched ten years ago and is still far from being complete.  
We still have fragmented national regulations creating inefficiencies, preventing cross-
border collaboration and scaling for European companies. With this paper, Cisco wants to 
propose several areas of regulatory simplification across the EU digital acquis, to remove 
particularly burdensome regulatory requirements that do not bring obvious benefits in 
terms of efficiency or security.  
1. Simplifying cybersecurity incident reporting obligations 
We have seen the proliferation of inconsistent cybersecurity reporting requirements in 
recent years, creating unnecessary and duplicative burdens for businesses. The General 
Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the revised Network and Information Security Directive 
(NIS2), the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) and the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) all 
require different thresholds, timelines and contents for incident reports. Under NIS2, the 
27 Member States will have their own reporting process, forms, or portals. This complexity 
can ultimately cause businesses to move resources away from mitigation, response and 
recovery from the incident itself to compliance, in turn, weakening the EU’s cyber 
resilience. We therefore believe the future digital simplification package must streamline 
cybersecurity reporting requirements in the following areas.  
• Main establishment  
Cisco supports greater use of, and consistent criteria for, the main establishment principle 
for EU cybersecurity and data governance regulations. This would allow companies to

• Reporting templates  
Together with a more consistent use of the main establishment principle, the European 
Commission should adopt one harmonised template for cybersecurity and data breach 
notifications across the EU, replacing the various templates that exist today. To simplify 
incident reporting under NIS2, the EU could consider developing a common EU-wide 
portal. Such a portal could contain the option of clicking on or checking off the relevant 
jurisdictions that should each receive the notification. The single EU intake portal could 
work for NIS2 reporting and other pieces of legislation as well.  
• Common EU-wide entry point for reporting  
To ensure harmonisation with the CRA, the NIS2 Directive should prioritise reporting to 
national CSIRTs over the competent authorities, potentially through a single, secure, EU-
wide intake portal, which could also serve as an intake portal for reporting obligations 
across other pieces of legislation. This approach would also improve trust and streamline 
the overall information-sharing framework.  
• Alignment on legal terms 
The NIS2’s use of ‘significant incidents’ differs from the CRA’s definition of ‘severe 
incident’, creating inconsistencies. While we recognise they cover incidents in different 
domains (provision of the service versus build environment of the manufacturer), we 
recommend the CRA should also set geographical limits that focus on impact within the 
EU, as found in NIS2.      
Also, all cybersecurity regulations must be aligned on what it means to ‘become aware’ of 
the incident or vulnerability. We recommend all cybersecurity regulations be aligned with 
the European Data Protection Board’s (EDPB) guidelines on personal data breach 
notification and NIS2 Implementing Regulation 2024/269 - which state that entities are 
considered to be aware of a breach or incident when they have a reasonable degree of 
certainty that a security incident has occurred.

creating inconsistencies. For example, Article 23(1) of EU NIS 2 requires service providers 
to report significant incidents to customers: “Where appropriate, entities concerned shall

However, the draft Dutch implementation of the law contains very similar language, but

The NIS2 Directive (Article 23(2)) also requires essential and important entities to report 
“significant cyber threats” only to recipients of services. Reporting threats to competent 
authorities or CSIRTs is purely voluntary. However, Member States have been transposing 
these provisions in an inconsistent manner. Hungary and Slovakia both require essential 
and important entities to report significant cyber threats to national authorities, and 
moreover, define “significant cyber threats” differently. 
To address these inconsistencies in legal interpretation and terminology between Member 
States’ transpositions of NIS2, the European Commission could provide legal guidance to 
ensure greater harmonisation and a common interpretation of NIS2 obligations.   
2. Simplifying compliance with NIS2 security requirements and audits 
With regards to NIS2, Cisco is concerned by the divergent security requirements and 
related security audits that entities need to follow in each jurisdiction. These are very time-
consuming and costly requirements, with no obvious cybersecurity benefit. Even with the 
main establishment principle, the NIS2 Directive illustrates how inconsistencies among 
Member States balloon to potentially prohibitive costs.   
For example, companies offering public electronic communications services (PECS) 
throughout the EU are subject to 27 separate laws purportedly covering the same topic but 
with inconsistent results.   
NIS2 results in a patchwork of security measures, with some Member States going above 
and beyond what is required under Article 21 of the Directive. Belgium added coordinated 
vulnerability disclosure policy; Slovakia added malicious code protection; Malta added 
logging and traceability of network and information systems; Hungary has security 
standards that map roughly to NIST 800-53, which are much more stringent than NIS2’s 
minimum security measures.  
In many cases, while each country requests its own audit, this audit would take place 
against the same set of central processes within a company, as systems are centralised for

In Hungary, for example, companies will have to carry out periodic audits by one of four

Cisco welcomes the inclusion of a Mutual Recognition Agreement on Cybersecurity in the 
recent EU-US Joint Statement. It should aim to deliver outcomes in two areas: first, to ease 
compliance with EU cyber regulations, the EU should allow companies to do their 
conformity assessments in the United States. Second, parties should work towards 
mutually recognisable cyber regulations and certifications. 
Finally, Article 32(2) of NIS2 directs Member States to ensure that competent authorities, 
when exercising their supervisory tasks in relation to essential entities, have the power to 
subject those entities to a variety of inspections and audits, including targeted security 
audits carried out by an independent body or competent authority. Cisco recommends that 
principles of mutual recognition and acceptance of audits also apply to such targeted 
audits as well to avoid having multiple duplicative audits of the same service provided 
across borders (e.g., public electronic communication services). 
3. Cyber Resilience Act 
Even if the Cyber Resilience Act does not seem to be part of what the Commission would 
like to address in the Digital Omnibus, we would like to make a few comments about the 
implementation of the CRA.  
• Timing of implementing guidance  
The CRA contains a complex set of product security and resilience requirements that 
hardware and software vendors will need to implement into their product development 
lifecycles once those requirements are finalised. These lifecycles often take multiple years 
from the time of a product is initially designed until it is fully developed for release on that

the market.  Thus, time is truly of the essence for vendors to receive definitive guidance on 
topics that are key to interpretating CRA requirements, as noted in more detail below.  
While the final overall implementation deadline for performing conformity assessments is 
11 December 2027, many of the most crucial requirements are not yet fully defined 
pending further interpretative guidance from the Commission. The timing of release of 
some of the key implementing guidance greatly needed by industry is within the EU 
Commission’s control, including the guidance referenced in CRA’s Article 26 concerning 1) 
the overall scope of CRA, 2)  the application of support periods to various categories of 
products with digital elements, and 3) the concept of substantial modification. We strongly 
urge the Commission to publish drafts of the guidance for industry consultation and 
finalise such guidance as soon as possible in order to give vendors sufficient time to 
incorporate the guidance given the long lead times needed to incorporate requirements 
into the development of their products.  
• Compliance deadlines 
CRA horizontal standards are currently being developed. Horizontal standards for Annex I 
part I are due for publication in Q1 2027, less than 12 months before the compliance 
deadline in December 2027. Standards for Annex I part II (vulnerability management) are 
currently scheduled for finalisation in October 2026, after the compliance deadline for 
incident and vulnerability reporting (September 2026). This timing is challenging for 
companies as they prepare for compliance.  
If the CRA harmonised standards are not ready in time, the European Commission should 
make it possible to postpone the application of CRA essential requirements. To give 
sufficient time to product manufacturers, the application date should be pushed back until 
12 months after relevant harmonised standards for Annex I are published. 
4. Simplifying data legislation  
The European Commission should use the upcoming Digital Omnibus to streamline 
existing rules on data. The Digital Omnibus should not only focus on the Data Governance 
Act and the Open Data Directive (as highlighted in the call for evidence) but also on the 
Data Act and the GDPR.  Merging the Open Data Directive and the Data Governance Act to 
streamline rules on the re-use of public data would have limited value to businesses 
operating in the EU.  
On the other hand, targeted simplification of the GDPR and the Data Act would have the 
potential to boost data innovation in the EU. For a thriving AI ecosystem in Europe, the

GDPR and data regulations must be “fine-tuned” to fit new technology and purposes, while

4.1. Targeted simplification of the Data Act is needed 
We understand that the European Commission thinks it is too early to review the Data Act 
and that a revision could only take place as part of the Digital Fitness Check or even later as 
part of the review process in 2028.  
We would however like to point to some issues to be resolved: 
• Data Act: Trade secrets  
The Data Act introduces provisions on compulsory sharing of trade secrets (Article 4) if all 
necessary technical or organisational measures have been taken prior to the disclosure to 
preserve their confidentiality. If the trade secret owner can demonstrate that it will suffer 
serious economic damage, it can refuse to provide access to the data, but the data 
user/requestor can challenge the decision.   
Compulsory sharing of trade secrets should be removed from the Data Act. The value of a 
trade secret is precisely because it’s a secret.  Disclosure, even with promises of 
confidentiality by the recipient, eviscerates the value of trade secrets. The value of a trade 
secret is often intangible and not easily measured or quantified. By placing a minimum bar 
of proving damages before trade secrets owners can exercise all of their protective rights, 
the protections are undermined. No other IP right places such a minimum bar to 
enforcement. It is imperative in a trade secret regime that trade secret owners have the 
final say as to whether or not to share information because sharing always bears a risk, and 
the trade secret owner cannot be made completely whole when a trade secret is 
misappropriated or copied by competitors.  
• Data Act/ Data Governance Act: International Data Transfers 
The Data Act and the Data Governance Act introduce new requirements for transfers of 
non-personal data that apply over and above the GDPR's framework for personal data 
transfers. This is particularly challenging for companies handling mixed data sets, creates 
impediments to companies’ ability to transfer non-personal data like those that the GDPR 
imposes on personal data and creates administrative burdens that are not commensurate 
to the risks presented.  
For this reason, Chapter VII of the Data Governance Act on International Access and 
Transfer, Article 28 and Chapter VII of the Data Act should be withdrawn to avoid these 
overlaps and unnecessary complexity.

4.2. GDPR simplification 
GDPR simplification should be part of the Digital Omnibus. Three issues should receive 
particular attention:   
• GDPR: Processing of pseudonymised personal data should be enabled.  
While GDPR purports to be risk-based, enforcement and regulatory interpretation has 
focused on what the data is, rather than the context and purpose for which it is used.  In 
order to streamline its impact, attention should be paid to the sprawling concept of 
personal data. To incentivise companies to adopt technical and organisational data 
protection controls, companies should be given additional latitude to process 
pseudonymous data with controls to prevent unauthorised re-identification.  
Recital 26 of the GDPR should make clear that, if a natural person is no longer identifiable, 
pseudonymised data shall be considered to be non-personal data and processed 
accordingly.  
• GDPR prevents legitimate (and necessary) personal data processing for AI 
training.  
As already mentioned above, a key concern in the current regime is the legal grounds for 
repurposing personal data for further use.  When such further use is envisaged, data 
controllers are required to either justify it via a compatibility assessment, or revert to 
obtaining data subject consent, which is impractical.  We recommend that the full range of 
legal grounds for processing personal data under Article 6(1) should remain available.  
Companies should be allowed to demonstrate that the balancing of legitimate interest 
(with appropriate controls) versus privacy intrusion weighs in favour of allowing the use of 
personal data for AI training.  
• Adopt a new approach to International Data Transfers under GDPR. 
The essential equivalence standard by which third country data protection laws and 
practices are assessed in order to establish adequacy of protection of transferred personal 
data is a hindrance to trade, creates legal uncertainty, and a disproportionate operational 
burden for companies, while failing to significantly contribute to the data protection 
posture.  In practice, only a handful of countries have been able to qualify for an ‘adequacy 
decision’ that requires an almost identical legal regime to be established in the third 
country.  Outside these limited decisions, companies are not able to rely on their own 
guarantees for protecting data but must self-assess and be accountable for the 
commercial, national security, and law enforcement regimes in the third country, over 
which they have no agency.

Beyond the EU legal framework, this is also creating issues in third countries which use 
GDPR as a blueprint and adopt similar transfer regimes without the equivalent privacy 
culture or institutions to enable and enforce it. If GDPR-like adequacy requirements are 
imposed on the EU Member States – legal framework, safeguards against commercial and 
government intrusion, independent oversight, and individual rights – each Member State 
would have to demonstrate compliance to foreign regimes via a multi-year, political 
process that gets periodically reassessed before the EU can receive data.  The adequacy 
regime offers only limited legal certainty for on-going data transfers. 
Our recommendation is to foster a multilateral approach to recognising personal data 
transfers by way of reference to an international, principle-based standard (e.g., the OECD 
Privacy Guidelines and Global Cross Border Privacy Rules) recognised by a treaty or other 
more durable international instrument. This would ensure a core baseline of protection 
and legal certainty while introducing a degree of flexibility that allows for mutual 
recognition of data privacy regimes around the world.  The current framework of unilateral, 
rigid and prescriptive assessments of each jurisdiction is untenable at scale. 
5. The ePrivacy Directive (and corresponding Member State legislation) is 
obsolete 
As the challenging legislative process and withdrawal of the ePrivacy Regulation 
demonstrates, applying a specific data privacy regime to the electronic communications 
sector is not straight forward.  The withdrawal of the ePrivacy Regulation means that the 
current ePrivacy Directive continues to be the legal instrument in this field. First adopted in 
2002, many of the provisions are obsolete, such as billing, caller ID, call forwarding and 
subscriber directory.  We contend that it is no longer fit for purpose and creates an unlevel 
playing field without adding any clear value. The consent banners we see on every EU 
website are distracting and useless – putting the onus on the data subject to understand 
and agree to the use of cookies, for example. Instead, companies should be held 
accountable for privacy-respectful and legitimate use of cookies without the use of 
consent banners that are rarely read and have caused notification fatigue.  
As such, the ePrivacy Directive should be removed from the acquis and national 
transpositions deleted. Created prior to the establishment of GDPR, which applies in any 
case to personal data processed in this sector, it includes provisions on security and 
confidentiality that are already well established in the more recent law.  In addition, it 
creates a convoluted and restricted set of grounds for processing communications, traffic, 
location data, and accessing terminal equipment, that would be better served by the 
general grounds for processing data in Article 6 of GDPR.

Cisco has been an active stakeholder in the process that led to the approval of the AI Act. 
As the first comprehensive legislative framework for AI, the Act stands for a complex 
structure encompassing compliance obligations for all actors along the AI value chain. As 
the EU AI Office guides its implementation, we believe that the role of stakeholders 
developing and deploying AI should continue to be central. Moreover, the implementation 
process should maintain as a central focus the objectives set out in the Draghi Report and 
in the ambitious announcements made on AI by President Von der Leyen: support 
investments and simplify to strengthen European competitiveness.  
• AI Act Implementation  
The EU AI Office’s role in implementing the AI Act should be clearly affirmed by the 
leadership of the European Commission. As the AI Act delegates significant portions of the 
regulation to secondary legislation, it is of fundamental importance to ensure that these 
rules are coherent, cohesive and in line with the text of the Act. Businesses would be hit 
with significant uncertainty, should the AI Office not be empowered to centralise this 
rukemaking process. A specific challenge in this space would be for other DGs to publish 
rules executing the mandate of the AI Act, without the necessary competences and 
understanding of the AI Act that the AI Office is instead building.   
• Stakeholder involvement  
The AI Office should also ensure that future consultations on the implementation of the 
Act, are published in a timely manner, and allow for enough time for stakeholders to 
prepare their responses. Given the clear timeline for the implementation of the Act, Cisco 
recommends that the future consultations are launched as soon as possible, to avoid any 
future issues in stakeholder representation.  
Furthermore, given the importance and sensitivity of these guidelines, we encourage the AI 
Office to carry out consultations on draft guidelines as early as possible, to better reflect 
the state of the art of AI development and deployment, and empower companies with 
information that can support their compliance journey. 
• Supporting compliance with the Act 
The obligations applying to High-Risk AI (HRAI) will heavily rely on standards to show 
compliance. The AI Office launched a verification exercise to estimate whether the 
standards drafting was in line with the timeline of the AI Act over the Summer. Following 
this exercise, the European Commission is currently reflecting on whether a grace period 
for compliance with HRAI requirements would be warranted. Given the current delay in 
finalising standards, paired with the necessity to produce and evaluate the HRAI

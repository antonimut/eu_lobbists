1. Cybersecurity  
 
1.1. Simplification of the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) 
 
Explanation: 
• The CRA does not cover products with digital elements made exclusively for the own 
use of the manufacturer. However, it does not include an explicit exception for prod-
ucts with digital elements that are provided exclusively within a corporate group. It 
therefore may be possible that not only products with digital elements made available 
to third parties but also products provided solely to other entities within a group of 
companies may fall within the scope of the CRA. This would mean a large number of 
group-internal products (in particular software) would need to be assessed, and rele-
vant products would need to fully comply with the requirements of the CRA (e.g., 
(third-party) conformity assessments, CE marking, information provision to users, re-
porting of relevant incidents).  
• The CRA requires that relevant products with digital elements comply with a variety of 
cybersecurity requirements starting end of December 2027. However, corresponding 
standards are not expected until at least end of 2026, which results in a very short im-
plementation period of about one year. 
• According to Art. 13(2), Annex I, Part I (2)(a) CRA products with digital elements shall 
be made available on the market without known exploitable vulnerabilities. However, 
manufacturers regularly learn of new vulnerabilities and make risk-based assess-
ments on the need to prioritize fixes. Also, in many cases, vulnerabilities may be iden-
tified that do not affect the security of a software product in practice because, e.g., 
they may only be exploitable in environments where the product is not intended for 
use.  
Proposal: 
• The Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) should not apply to products (including software) 
that are exclusively provided for internal use within a corporate group (Group Privi-
lege). This would acknowledge the lower risk of a purely group internal provision 
(e.g., due to a limited group of users and general cyber security standards and 
measures within corporate groups) and avoids unnecessary regulatory burden for 
such solutions. Alternatively, the applicability of the CRA for such products should be 
limited to compliance with the essential cybersecurity requirements set out in Annex I. 
This would already achieve a sufficiently high level of cybersecurity. Compliance with 
additional requirements (e.g., CE marking, provision of certain information for the 
user) make no sense due to the specifics of internal use (similar to own use) and 
would be a mere formality that would require significant effort. 
• The proposed 24-month implementation period should commence only once all rele-
vant standardization processes have been completed—currently expected by the end 
of 2026. This approach would ensure a more efficient and realistic implementation 
timeline, allowing organizations to align with finalized technical specification. Further 
development of existing laws should only proceed once the initial framework is com-
plete and its effectiveness demonstrated. If regulations include reporting require-
ments, these should also be harmonized across the board. In order to reduce the bur-

den on companies, accelerate the implementation of respective requirements and en-
sure legal certainty, we advocate the early publication of respecting reporting guide-

• The requirement to make products available without known exploitable vulnerabilities 
should be limited to vulnerabilities with a certain criticality. To avoid unreasonable 
burdens only high and critical vulnerability, based on the Common Vulnerability Scor-
ing System (“CVSS”), should be relevant.  
 
1.2. Harmonization of Reporting Obligations for CRA/NIS 2/DORA 
 
Explanation: 
• The cyber security related regulations CRA, NIS 2 and DORA as well as the poten-
tially relevant GDPR contain different procedures and provide for different competent 
authorities regarding the reporting of relevant incidents. Harmonizing these would 
create significant efficiency gains and simplifications. Thus, more resources could be 
allocated to address vulnerabilites. 
• NIS 2 and CRA provide particularly short deadlines of 24 hours for reporting relevant 
incidents upon becoming aware of such incident. While this may be justified in certain 
cases due to the criticality of the incidents, it poses significant challenges for compa-
nies. Especially for larger companies or groups of companies with complex internal 
structures and longer reporting lines, ensuring reporting within 24 hours requires con-
siderable effort.

Proposal: 
• The processes for reporting relevant incidents under different laws need to be stand-

this, a single European platform and digital procedure for reporting relevant incidents 
should be developed. This procedure should allow for all relevant reports (i.e., where

means the reporting entity is only required to submit a single unified report of an inci-
dent, which would serve as a notification to all relevant authorities (on national and

European level). In the future, the EUDI-Wallet could be used for authorization on 
such a reporting platform.  
• Such a platform should at least be established for notifications under NIS 2 and CRA 
(e.g., under the responsibility of ENISA). Later it could be extended to also include 
the reporting of relevant incidents under DORA in coordination and cooperation with 
the respective competent authorities.  
• Until this has been implemented, the first step should be to at least establish a single 
European reporting platform for reports under each law. While, for example, the CRA 
provides for the establishment of a single European reporting platform (through which 
reports can also be forwarded to other affected designated CSIRT), the NIS 2 Di-
rective requires reporting directly to national authorities. In the case of corporate 
groups including international companies, this leads to practical difficulties in the re-
porting process itself (e.g., language barriers) and results in significant additional ef-
fort due to potentially required multiple reporting to different authorities. Therefore, a 
single reporting platform for NIS 2-relevant incidents would be welcomed, especially 
as it would also ensure simplified coordination and collaboration between the relevant 
authorities of the member states when dealing with a (cross-border) security incident. 
• Early warning notifications within 24 hours should only be required for incidents in 
particularly critical areas. This means that under NIS 2, only essential entities should 
need to submit an early warning notification within 24 hours and under CRA, an early 
warning within 24 hours should only be required if an important or critical product with 
digital elements is affected. This approach allows to use existing resources appropri-
ately and ensure reliable reporting within 24 hours for particularly critical incidents. 
Other relevant incidents should be reported within 72 hours in alignment with the 
deadlines under the GDPR. 
 
1.3. Harmonization of Radio Equipment Directive (RED) and UNECE R155  
 
Explanation: 
• UN R155 makes cybersecurity and a CSMS a precondition for type approval in the 
EU (new types since July 2022; all new vehicles since July 2024). OEMs bear lifecy-
cle responsibility for monitoring and secure updates. 
• The Radio Equipment Directive (RED) 2014/53/EU empowers additional essential re-
quirements in Article 3(3)(d/e/f). Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/30 ac-
tivates these obligations for internet‑connected radio equipment from 1 August 2025, 
with incomplete sectoral carve‑outs. 
• Head units and TCUs in vehicles fall under RED/2022/30 due to internet connectivity 
although the vehicle is already regulated by UN R155 — creating double regulation 
with overlaps on network protection and integrity. 
• Critical gap: Article 2(2) of 2022/30 carves out motor vehicles for (e)/(f) but not for (d), 
so Article 3(3)(d) RED (network protection) still applies to vehicle radio equipment. 
 
Proposal:  
• Amend RED/2022/30 article 2 (1) so that Article 3(3)(d/e/f) do not apply to radio 
equipment that is integral to vehicles subject to UN R155, provided type‑approval 
conformity is demonstrated.

1.4. DORA 
 
Explanation: 
• The Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) is a regulation ensuring that financial 
entities can withstand, respond to, and recover from all types of ICT-related disrup-
tions and threats. It establishes a comprehensive framework for digital operational re-
silience, including requirements for ICT risk management, incident reporting, and test-
ing. 
• Why DORA was established? 
o Ongoing digital transformation of financial services. 
o Increasing number of cyberattacks. 
o Increasing interconnection of critical infrastructures and networks. 
• Objective: 
o Ensure financial industry can withstand and recover from cyber threat, IT disrup-
tions and other operational risks. 
o Ensure better transparency on interconnections and market concentration of 3rd 
party ICT providers. 
• The vehicle manufacturer are affected by DORA not only if they have an associated 
bank but also in some cases as an ancillary insurance intermediaries (Versicher-
ungsvermittler mit Nebentätigkeit). This results in significant additional administrative 
efforts for the manufacturers. At this point, it is questionable whether the activity as an 
insurance intermediary with ancillary activities is actually relevant to the stability of the 
financial market. Exemptions for insurance intermediaries with only a low insurance 
sales value often do not apply to the manufacturer, as the total turnover including the 
vehicle business and not just the turnover from insurance is taken into account. 
Proposal: 
• To determine whether simplified DORA rules can be applied, only the revenues from 
insurance intermediary business activities should be considered for insurance inter-
mediaries with ancillary activities. 
 
1.5. Harmonization of entry into force for CRA and UN R155 for Typ L-Vehicles 
 
Explanation: 
• The Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) provides an exemption for products to which Regu-
lation (EU) 2019/2144 applies due to the applicability of UN Regulation No 155. 
• There is also a proposal to exclude L-category vehicles under Regulation (EU) 
168/2013 from the scope of the CRA, as these will also be subject to cybersecurity 
requirements under the UN Regulation No 155. The application of UN Regulation No 
155 will be mandatory for the type approval of new L-category vehicles as of 11 De-
cember 2027, the date of application of (most of) the Cyber Resilience Act. Existing 
vehicle types will be subject to such requirements as of 11 June 2029. 
• However, reporting obligations according to Art. 14 of the CRA will apply from 11 
September 2026. 
 
 
6 
 
• This could lead to relevant L-category vehicles falling under the reporting obligations 
of the CRA from 11 September 2026 until entry-into-force/applicability of the excep-
tion. Reporting processes according to CRA would need to be implemented, only to 
be replaced or adjusted to align with requirements under UN Regulation No 155.  
• Also, since the background of the exception is the applicability of UN Regulation No 
155, there is a risk that the exception could be interpreted in such way that vehicles 
which are not (yet) subject to the requirements of UN Regulation No 155 still fall un-
der the CRA. This could lead to overlapping requirements for existing vehicle types, 
which the exception is intended to avoid. 
 
Proposal: 
• The exception for L-category vehicles from the CRA should be aligned with the ap-
plicability of the reporting obligations under the CRA (i.e., apply from 11 September 
2026), in order to avoid the need to implement CRA-compliant reporting processes 
for just a short transition period and therefore reduce unnecessary administrative and 
compliance burden and costs. 
• Further, it should be clarified that all L-category vehicles are exempt from the scope 
of the CRA upon the entry into force of the exception, regardless of when they were 
placed on the market and whether or when they fall under UN Regulation No 155 to 
avoid possible double regulation of existing vehicle types.  
 
1.6. NIS 2  
 
Explanation: 
• Reporting of incidents to the responsible supervisory authorities (“Reporting-to-Au-
thorities”): 
o A very short reporting period (early warning within 24 hours, follow-up reporting af-
ter a further 48 hours and sequentially) requires new reporting channels to supervi-
sory authorities and corresponding internal processes.  
o For providers of specific services (e.g., online marketplaces and social networking 
services platforms), Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 sets out harmonized 
provisions, such as criteria for when an incident is considered to be significant. The 
background of this is that these services typically have a cross-border nature and a 
coherent framework for the same service should be ensured across different mem-
ber states. According to the recitals and the chosen threshold in the Implementing 
Regulation, it seems that the model for regulating these services was primarily 
based on the classic large providers with particular criticality, such as multinational 
social media companies. However, the wording of the definitions of the regulated 
services is broadly defined, allowing even less significant and critical services to 
meet the requirements. This leads to situations where companies that meet the 
thresholds for essential/important entities and whose core business falls under a 
different sector can additionally be subject to the Implementing Regulation with 
other, less critical or essential services. E.g., an automotive manufacturer may also 
provide online marketplaces as a way to sell its products or offers apps, where e.g. 
customers can communicate with each other. In the case of such classification 
across multiple sectors, this leads to great uncertainty regarding which criteria the 
 
 
7 
 
entity must consider for which business activities and potentially resulting in in-
creased effort due to the application of different criteria within one company.  
o For entities other than digital providers, there is no EU-wide definition of when an 
incident is considered significant. Although the NIS2 directive includes the possibil-
ity for the Commission to adopt implementing acts for other entities, there is no ob-
ligation to do so. This leads to great uncertainties for entities and an inconsistent 
EU-wide reporting system, as individual member states may interpret the term "sig-
nificant incident" differently. At the same time, there are concerns that member 
states will adopt the criteria of the implementing regulation without reflection, which 
may not adequately apply to all important and critical entities, resulting in thresh-
olds too low/improperly and unnecessary reports. 
o Each EU member state defines its own locally responsible CSIRT or supervisory 
authority. Where appropriate, and in particular where a significant incident con-
cerns two or more Member States, the CSIRT or the competent authority shall in-
form the other affected Member States and ENISA of the significant incident. How-
ever, due to the large number of authorities and the lack of uniform processes on 
EU level a reliable exchange of information and coordination may not be guaran-
teed in such cases. Additionally, in cases involving a group of companies, it may 
happen that the same incident needs to be reported by multiple group companies 
to different authorities.  
• NIS 2 training for management bodies: The regulation includes no specific require-
ments (e.g. formats, scope,(mandatory) content, (mandatory) participants) regarding 
the required NIS 2 training for management bodies (cf. 20(2)). It is unclear what the 
legislative expectations are in this regard leading to uncertainties for the companies 
affected. 
• In case of international companies the necessary registrations with the relevant local 
supervisory authorities are difficult to implement due to different registration platforms 
and language barriers. 
 
Proposal: 
• Early warning notifications within 24 hours should only be required for incidents in 
particularly critical areas. This means only essential entities should need to submit an 
early warning notification within 24 hours. This approach allows to use existing re-
sources appropriately and ensure reliable reporting within 24 hours for particularly 
critical incidents. Other relevant incidents should be reported within 72 hours. 
• The definitions for certain types of digital providers should be adjusted so that entities 
that qualify as such only due to negligible business activities (in relation to their over-
all business operations) are excluded from the scope of application. 
• EU-wide definition of reporting criteria with differentiation according to the different 
NIS 2 sectors (or at least groups of sectors). The criteria should be coordinated with 
major associations in the respective sector. Further, it should be clarified how entities 
should handle situations where they fall into multiple sectors, particularly regarding 
when they need to apply which criteria regarding significant incidents. Since the crite-
ria of one sector may not necessarily fit activities in another sector, criteria for a spe-
cific sector should only be applied to incidents related to the business activities that 
fall within that sector.  
• Establishing a single EU-wide reporting platform for NIS 2-relevant incidents with 
clear guidelines on required information to facilitate the reporting process for entities 
 
 
8 
 
and ensure simplified coordination and collaboration between the relevant authorities 
of the member states when dealing with a (cross-border) security incident. At a mini-
mum, member states should be required to implement the reporting processes as ef-
ficiently and digitally as possible. Therefore at least, a fully digitized reporting portal 
should be established, equipped with efficient interfaces for automation, avoiding mul-
tiple reports, and providing a central point of contact. At the very least, the type of in-
formation, the format and the procedure of a notification should be further specified, 
and companies should have the option to submit reports in English to meet interna-
tional requirements. 
• EU-wide definition of criteria regarding format, scope, content, and participants of the 
NIS 2 trainings by the Commission via an implementing act. 
• Establishing a single EU-wide registration platform where entities can centrally, eas-
ily, and uniformly carry out the required registration.

2. AI  
 
2.1. Delay of Implementation Deadline for High-Risk AI Systems 
 
Explanation: 
• The AI Act constitutes a complex regulatory framework aimed at governing highly in-
tricate technology. A major risk is that regulation could hinder the use of technology. It 
is therefore crucial that both the legal interpretation be clearly defined, and the stand-
ards and guidelines precisely outline the measures for ensuring AI Act compliance. 
• After the AI Act has entered into force over a year ago and the rules for High-Risk AI 
systems will come into force in August 2026, EU Commission guidelines are still 
missing (e.g., on the Definition of High Risk, Transparency, etc.).  
• The development of the standards (through JTC 21) to permit High-Risk AI systems 
to conform to the AI Act is unlikely to be completed in time for companies to prepare.  
 
Proposal: 
• Therefore, the implementation deadline for High-Risk AI systems should be urgently 
delayed by 24 months. 
• In general, the implementation period should commence only once all relevant stand-
ardization processes have been completed and all guidelines have been released—
currently expected for the AI Act not earlier than Q4 2026. This approach would en-
sure a more efficient and realistic implementation timeline, allowing organizations to 
align with finalized technical specifications. Further development of existing laws 
should only proceed once the initial framework is complete and its effectiveness 
demonstrated. 
 
2.2. Harmonized definition of biometric data between the AI Act and the GDPR 
 
Explanation: 
• In comparison to the AI Act, the GDPR defines biometric data more narrowly by re-
quiring it to enable or confirm the unique identification of a natural person.  
• The AI Act lacks this specific identification criterion, leading to a potentially broader 
interpretation. 
• This discrepancy creates legal uncertainty and increases compliance efforts for AI 
system providers as a new data category needs to be evaluated. 
 
Proposal: 
• The AI Act definition of biometric data should be aligned with the definition used in the 
GDPR.

2.3. Relevance of the AI Act for Type Approved Vehicles 
 
Explanation: 
• One of the primary objectives of vehicle type approval is to mitigate risks e.g., to life 
and health of vehicle occupants and road users on a broad scale. Therefore, vehicles 
(including relevant systems) must meet extensive requirements und have to undergo 
corresponding conformity assessments. The UN regulations, which form the basis for 
system approvals, are technology-neutral and therefore encompass AI systems. 
• Additionally, the AI Act may also apply to AI components in the vehicle. An exception 
to the scope of application is only provided for safety components of type-approved 
vehicles, where the term 'safety component' is defined independently in the EU AI Act 
and is therefore not subject to the understanding under the relevant Regulations (EU) 
2018/858 and 2019/2144.  
• Consequently, the AI Act poses the risk of over regulation for vehicles or certain parts 
thereof that are relevant for type approval under Regulation (EU) 2018/858 and Reg-
ulation (EU) 2019/2144, in particular as UN regulations for needed system approvals 
reference standards such as ISO 26262 and ISO 21448.  
• Additionally, guidelines for the assessment of AI systems are currently under discus-
sion at the UNECE level (IWG AI). 
 
Proposal: 
• Delete the amendment to Regulation 2018/858 including the requirement for relevant 
delegated acts under Regulation 2018/858 to take into account the requirements of 
Chapter III, Section 2 of the AI Act. The AI Act should not apply to AI systems or mod-
els that fall within the scope of Regulation (EU) 2018/858 or Regulation (EU) 
2019/2144 - neither directly nor indirectly. Requirements for such AI systems or mod-
els should be regulated exclusively through type approval regulations to ensure sec-
tor-specific and appropriate provisions for the product.  
 
2.4. AI Act: No blanket classification of emotion recognition systems as high-risk 
 
Explanation: 
• Currently, all emotion recognition systems are classified as high-risk unless explicitly 
prohibited under Article 5(1)(f) AI Act. 
• Recital 44 justifies this based on concerns about scientific validity and risks of dis-
crimination or rights violations. 
• However, some use cases—such as lighting or sound based on mood—pose minimal 
risk. 
• A blanket classification discourages innovation and may drive development of such 
technologies outside the EU. 
 
Proposal: 
• Amend Annex III No. 1 lit. c) to limit high-risk classification to systems that may result 
in discriminatory outcomes or rights infringements. 
 
 
11 
 
• Alternatively, restrict high-risk classification to systems whose outputs are used to 
make decisions affecting individuals. 
 
2.5. AI Act: Clarification of No. 1 lit. b) Annex III: Exhaustive and reliable list of sen-
sitive or protected categories 
 
Explanation: 
• Annex III No. 1 lit. b) classifies AI systems used for biometric categorization based on 
sensitive or protected attributes as high-risk. 
• However, the AI Act itself does not define what constitutes "sensitive or protected" at-
tributes. Only Recital 54 of the AI Act refers to Article 9(1) GDPR, which includes a 
list of special categories of personal data. 
• This indirect reference creates ambiguity and legal uncertainty for AI providers, as it 
is unclear which attributes are covered. Also, there is a risk that authorities may later 
interpret the list under Article 9(1) GDPR as non-exhaustive under the AI Act, increas-
ing compliance complexity. 
 
Proposal: 
• Replace the vague term "sensitive or protected attributes" in Annex III No. 1 lit. b) with 
a clear and exhaustive list. 
• This exhaustive list should include but is not limited to: health data, genetic data, bio-
metric data for unique identification, and potentially ethnic origin. 
• This approach mirrors the clarity provided in Article 5(1)(g) AI Act and Article 9(1) 
GDPR.

3. Data  
 
3.1. RED III  
 
Explanation: 
• The Renewable Energy Directive primarily focuses on renewable energy systems 
also contains within Article 20a requirements in regard to vehicle data.  
• The Article 20a of the RED III brings significant requirements which are already laid 
down by earlier Regulation (EU Battery Regulation – EUBR, Euro 7, Data Act etc.) 
and is therefore causing double regulation. This introduces unclear scope of require-
ments for manufacturers and subsequently economic operators. 
• In addition, it is unfeasible to implement technical and data requirements in a energy 
directive, with overlaps with other regulations. Additionally, the potential variance in 
the national implementation poses a risk for the Single Market and causes massive 
compliance burdens for vehicle manufacturers.  
 
Proposal: 
• Deletion of Article 20a, paragraph 3 sentence 2 to avoid double regulation mainly with 
the EU Battery Regulation – EUBR, Euro 7 and the Data Act. 
 
3.2. Data Act 
 
Explanation: 
• Data holders as defined in the Data Act, i.e. usually manufacturers of IoT devices, 
face a significant burden under such new regulation by being obliged to provide mani-
fold information to authorities and data recipients in several respects. First, a compre-
hensive set of pre-contractual information per connected product and related service 
is required pursuant to Art. 3, para. 2, 3 Data Act . Whilst added value for the ad-
dressees of such information can be acknowledged at least to some extent, this does 
not apply to the other information provision duties.  
• Even in the absence of anyone’s dissatisfaction, complaint or other specific reason, 
the entirety of data holders is supposed to overwhelm the competent authorities with 
transparency concerning circumstances around (prima facie justified) withholding of 
data, see Art. 4 (2), (7), (8), 5 (10), (11) Data Act. This could be the case, e.g., when-
ever data holder and data recipient were not able to align on sufficient measures to 
protect trade secrets and consequently data was not shared. Even without a com-
plaint by the data recipient, the data holder still has to file a comprehensive report to 
the competent authorities about this process. This creates a veritable additional “re-
porting regime” and hence bureaucracy on the part of the data holder as well as a 
need for additional resources at the competent authorities in order to digest all such 
unsolicited input. It should also be borne in mind that any additional transparency re-
quirement entails the risk of jeopardizing legitimate interests of data holders. For in-
stance, the mere fact that certain (non-standard) product data exists and is available 
to the product manufacturer may constitute a trade secret whose disclosure becomes 
more probable whenever the underlying data leaves the sphere of the data holder. 
 
 
13 
 
• Similarly, Art. 8 (3), 9 (7), 20 (2) Data Act require the data holder to demonstrate that 
the conditions of the data provision fulfil the Act’s requirements in terms of non-dis-
crimination or compensation. In Art. 9 (7), not even a concern or request of the data 
recipient is a prerequisite for the information requirement to the detriment of the data 
holder. 
• In addition, Article 9 (7) of the Data Act requires data holders to disclose their cost 
structures (including cost calculations) – without any handbrakes or thresholds. A re-
quirement that is not only frequently difficult or impossible to fulfill due to contractual 
confidentiality obligations, but is, in many cases, outright prohibited under EU compe-
tition law. It is a well-established principle of antitrust law that the disclosure of pur-
chase prices between competitors is problematic. However, this is precisely what Arti-
cle 9 (7) of the Data Act appears to mandate. It must be taken into account that data 
holders and data recipients are often direct competitors with respect to key cost com-
ponents—for instance, when procuring data storage capacity from providers such as 
large cloud providers. Moreover, beyond the demand-side markets, data holders and 
recipients frequently compete in downstream markets as well (e.g., OEM and OES in 
the market for data-driven products for automotive repair services). As a result, data 
holders are placed in a legal dilemma created by two conflicting EU legal instruments. 
If they comply with the transparency obligation under the Data Act, they risk violating 
competition law. Conversely, if they refrain from disclosing their cost calculations, 
they face the risk that, in the event of a dispute, a court may consider their fees to be 
excessive. 
• In addition to provisions for data holders, the Data Act also includes regulations for 
providers of data processing activities. In that context Article 32 restricts the transfer 
of non-personal data to third countries. With this, the Data Act establishes, in effect, a 
parallel framework for international data transfers alongside the General Data Protec-
tion Regulation (GDPR) creating extra legal uncertainty for companies operating in-
ternationally. Creating such additional and often overlapping regime for data transfers 
is unnecessary and results in even more complex and burdensome compliance re-
quirements for companies.  
Besides that, it leads to uncertainty that there is no clear definition of who is qualified 
as a provider of data processing services whether in the recitals nor in the FAQs to 
the data act published by the EU Commission. Additionally, it is not clear what are the 
exact requirements to be fullfilled by a provider of data processing services. 
 
Proposal: 
• Even without extensive bureaucratic reporting obligations, if there is a potential initial 
suspicion the data recipient can use all usual legal instruments to claim their rights. In 
addition, Art. 37 (14) Data Act already mandates the relevant supervisory authorities 
to gather information about the compliance with the Data Act from the data holders. 
Therefore, in case of a substantiated complaint to the relevant supervisory authority 
(Art. 37 (5)(b), 38 Data Act) comprehensive information from the data holder to vali-
date the complaint can already be requested. In the aftermath, the reporting obliga-
tions foreseen in the respective articles of the Data Act (Art. 4 (2), (7), (8), 5 (10), 
(11), 8 (3), 9 (7), 20 (2)) should be deleted accordingly. This approach prevents the 
unnecessary generation of extensive documentation and the initiation of overly bu-
reaucratic processes and at the same time still securing the rights of any data recipi-
ent. 
 
 
14 
 
• The extensive disclosure obligations of data holders under Article 9(7) should be re-
moved accordingly. In the event of disputes regarding this matter, the ordinary legal 
process remains fully accessible. 
• Should an unexpectedly high number of lawsuits and substantiated complaints arise, 
further adjustments may be considered as part of the Data Act review scheduled for 
September 2027. 
• The international transfer provisions contained in Chapter VII of the Data Act should 
be fully deleted.  
At the very least, reliance on valid GDPR mechanisms like Standard Contractual 
Clauses and technical and organizational measures should be clearly deemed as suf-
ficient under the Data Act.  
 
3.3. GDPR – Research Data  
 
Explanation: 
• Ensure high privacy standards that enable innovation: Data is essential for driving in-
novation and maintaining global competitiveness. However, the current approach re-
garding scientific research in the GDPR does not include research done by compa-
nies.  
• This leads to disproportionate restrictions that unduly limit the competitiveness of Eu-
ropean companies. 
• As example in developing autonomous driving functions the recording and processing

a data protection impact assessment, a balancing of interests and showcase the legit-
imate interest. If a scientific research institute would perform the same research, they

Proposal: 
• The GDPR already provides exemptions for the processing of personal data for sci-
entific or historical research or statistical purposes (Article 89 (2)). These exemptions 
should also be extended to commercial research purposes. 
 
3.4. GDPR – Register of processing activities  
 
Explanation: 
• Article 30 GDPR requires documentation of all processing activities which include 
personal data, which in practice leads to an overarching number of entries—many of 
which are low-risk and offer little added value. 
• This results in a disproportionate administrative burden, especially for processing op-
erations with minimal impact on the rights of data subjects. 
• The risk-based approach of the GDPR should be applied to these documentation re-
quirements as well. This would allow organizations to focus documentation efforts on 
medium and high-risk processing activities. 
 
 
15 
 
• Low-risk processing operations, as processing that include limited personal data, 
(e.g. processing only names, user-IDs or/and email-addresses for the purposes of ac-
cess control or for contacting peoples for work-related activities), should be exempt 
from detailed documentation. 
• Streamlining documentation in this way would reduce compliance overhead and allow 
for more efficient resource allocation within the companies. 
 
Proposal: 
• Introduce a risk-based exemption in Article 30(5) for processing activities classified as 
low risk. 
 
3.5. GDPR – Group Privilege 
 
Explanation: 
• Within a company group the exchange of personal data needs to meet the same strict 
requirements as the exchange of personal data outside the company group. 
• Without complex legal intercompany frameworks, it is not possible to exchange data 
between subsidiaries. As many processings are centralized in such a group, the data 
exchange is crucial and data protection requirements may cause a huge bureaucratic 
effort.  
 
Proposal: 
• The data exchange between subsidiaries of a company group should be treated in 
the same way as the data exchange within one company to reduce regulatory bur-
den, and enable companies to reduce compliance costs.  
 
3.6. Harmonized compensation for data provision 
 
Explanation: 
• The provision of data is invariably associated with costs—even when data is already 
available to the manufacturer of a connected device. These costs may include trans-
mission fees (e.g., mobile network usage) as well as expenses related to cloud and 
compute infrastructure. 
• The EU Data Act acknowledges this and includes compensation for data provision in 
its cross-sectoral framework, including data requested by public sector bodies (B2G), 
as outlined in Article 20(2). 
• However, existing sector-specific regulations often prescribe inconsistent or no com-
pensation mechanisms for data sharing with public authorities (B2G). This creates le-
gal uncertainty and operational complexity. 
• To ensure consistency and legal clarity, the cost calculation model introduced by the 
Data Act should be adopted as the baseline for all B2G data provision.

• This approach discourages unnecessary data requests while ensuring fair compensa-
tion for the infrastructure and effort required to make data available as well as reduc-
ing administrative burdens. 
• Examples of regulations in the automotive sector that currently do not provide com-
pensation regimes for data provision:

o Battery State of Health submission mandated in Euro 7 (EU) 2024/1257) 
o Automated Driving Systems Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/1426 
o Driver Control Assistance Systems (DCAS) OJ L 2024/2689  
o Intelligent Speed Assistant (ISA) Delegated Regulation (EU) 2021/1958 
o Automated Lane keeping Systems (ALKS) 
o On Board Fuel Consumption Monitoring (OBFCM) Commission Regulation

Proposal: 
• For B2G data provision, the same compensation principles as laid down in Article 
20(2) of the Data Act should apply across all public sector data requests (B2G), 
where the data from connected devices is transmitted from the manufacturer directly 
to the authority. 
• All future regulations concerning B2G data provision shall refer to the compensation 
framework for data sharing as stipulated in Article 20(2) of the Data Act. This applies 
only to data obtained from connected devices and transmitted over-the-air. Existing 
regulations should be revised accordingly to align with this standard. 
 
3.7. GDPR – Information Obligation 
 
Explanation: 
• Article 14 (5) of the GDPR currently acknowledges exemptions in some given cases 
of information obligations for the controller (for example when the personal data is ob-
tained due to legal obligations). Nevertheless, these exemptions for the controller 
only apply in case that the personal data has not been obtained directly from the data 
subject.  
• When data is obtained directly from the data subject this lack of exceptions causes 
enormous documentation and bureaucratic efforts as data protection authorities and 
courts tend to expect increasing detail in the information about data processing pro-
vided to the data subject. 
• For example, when data was obtained bevor the entry into force of the GDPR but 
needs to be processed due to a legal obligation afterward, the information obligations 
are causing enormous efforts as for example the data subject cannot be reached eas-
ily. In case the data would not have been obtained directly the exemptions in Article 
14 (5) would help to reduce bureaucratic efforts.

Proposal: 
• The exceptions set forth in Article 14 (5) GDPR should also apply to Article 13 GDPR.

   Response to the Call for evidence on Simplification – digital package and omnibus   carVertical is a Lithuanian scale-up that helps European consumers make informed decisions about second-hand vehicle purchases and avoid fraud. Odometer fraud costs EU consumers an estimated €9 billion annually through manipulated mileage on used cars, leading to significant financial losses.  We thank the Commission for the opportunity to provide suggestions for the upcoming Digital Omnibus on data legislation, cybersecurity incident reporting, and the AI Act. In our submission to the Call for Evidence, we focus on data legislation.  While we recognize that the full scope of challenges in obtaining data may be more relevant to the forthcoming Digital Fitness Test, we nevertheless wish to share several practical, cost-related issues we encounter regularly. We would also like to thank the Commission for recognizing that small and mid-caps must be considered in the simplification of data rules. This is an important shift that allows not only SMEs but also European small and mid-caps to benefit from the simplification package—helping fast-growing European tech companies scale and enhance Europe’s global competitiveness. Although, in our opinion, to truly unlock the potential of European companies data rules need to be amended, the goal of reducing compliance costs related to data access by harmonizing rules and their application is a step in the right direction. Moreover, where amendments to EU rules are not feasible, clarifications and recommendations to Member States are the next-best alternative. Please find our additional comments below.   1. Access to national registries: registries are financially disincentivized to open data; GDPR remains the main barrier  1.1. Costs. Currently, the average cost of connecting to a single registry (in our case: vehicle registries) is at least €200k per registry, with 27 Member States’ registries theoretically amounting to approximately €4-5.4 million. The upkeep of the connection is at least €5k/month per registry, totaling €135k/month EU-wide; that is €60k per registry annually = ~€1.6 million per year. This illustrates why registries are financially motivated not to open valuable datasets and to continue selling them commercially.

1.2. Slow access & inaccurate data provided. The access to registers is not only costly, but also slow - averaging 18 months in our case. Moreover, the data provided is often incomplete and erroneous.  1.3. Suggested solution. Financially incentivize national registries to open high-value datasets in areas the EU and Member States deem worthy and socially beneficial (e.g., reducing fraud in the second-hand vehicle market, improving vehicle safety, and lowering vehicle-related incidents). A prime example is Latvia, which compensates its registries for lost revenue when opening data, helping them readjust and develop new operating models.  2. Access to Periodical Technical Inspection (PTI) databases: legal framework between registries and other databases is fragmented   2.1. Costs. The average cost of connecting to PTI databases is at least €3.5 million EU-wide, with the average cost per request ranging from €1 to €12. 2.2. Fragmentation. Access to PTI databases is fragmented from vehicle registries, and the legal processes between them are likewise fragmented, resulting in even higher costs for companies like ours. 2.3. Suggested solution. Harmonize the legal framework between vehicle registries, periodical technical inspection (PTI) databases and third-parties that should have access to them. While our specific examples concern vehicle data, similar fragmentation likely exists in other areas as well.  3. Access to service shops: upkeep alone costs €300k/month; GDPR remains the major issue  3.1. Costs. There are roughly 520,000 vehicle service shops in the EU. Establishing access to them costs about €50 million, with upkeep of at least €300k/month, plus data fees.  3.2. The GDPR effect. Despite these high costs, the main obstacles are regulatory: in many Member States, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) prevents service shops from sharing vehicle identification number (VIN) data, leading shops to refuse data sharing for fear of liability. This is due to VINs being classified as personal data under the GDPR, which results in  costly, case-by-case frameworks and adding up to two thirds to existing costs.  3.3. Suggested solution. In the upcoming Digital Fitness Test, issue recommendations to Member States to create legal exemptions for VINs (treating them as technical data rather than misclassified personal data (along with other non-sensitive data types)), to curb the over-application of the GDPR.  
4. Creating an integrated, consumer-based vehicle history database would take 70 years under current EU data rules and practices   4.1. Integration drag. Nearly 50 separate government vehicle databases exist across the EU, making integration lengthy and costly. At the current pace and with the current regulatory framework, building a fully integrated, consumer-focused European database would take over 70 years.  4.2. Gatekeeping data within the EU. Centralized vehicle databases exist in some EU Member States, such as Belgium, the Netherlands, Slovakia, but they are often closed to external  entities (even to those within the EU), or are limited to owners without the possibility of potential buyers of second hand vehicles to obtain such information from official sources, leaving them with less safety than the owner/seller.  4.3. Lack of centralization. Finally, in most of the EU, vehicle data is not collected in a single, unified system, many countries and organizations either do not collect it at all or, if they do, it is not centralized.  4.4. Suggested solutions. Centralize vehicle data collection in a single, unified system, thus helping  fight odometer and vehicle history fraud and helping protect the European consumers. Supporting European, market-based solutions rather than building a state-run platform that public entities may be unable to maintain is also important. Finally, exploring new incentives for Member States to share data with companies based outside their own country would help solve “Single Market failures” that we currently face.

Digital Omnibus – Key Asks and Recommendations 
Inter IKEA Group 
1) AI Act and high-risk requirements 
Do not delay high-risk obligations 
Ask: Keep high-risk requirements on track. If a delay is unavoidable, apply obligations first to providers, 
especially VLOPs, with deployer obligations phased in later. 
Why: We see a growing number of voices calling for a general “stop the clock” for the AI Act, but we 
believe this would slow our internal transformation and increase vulnerability for deployers of digital 
technologies. High-risk safeguards are important because they give deployers like us the authority to 
refuse non-compliant tools and create predictable quality signals from vendors. If delay is unavoidable, 
obligations should first apply to providers, especially large platforms and VLOPs, with deployer 
obligations phased in later and lighter rules for SMEs. This sets the bar upstream, reduces downstream 
assessment costs, and encourages vendors to invest in conformity and documentation before placing 
systems on the market. This is especially important in the absence of specific AI liability requirements. 
Mandatory vendor declarations 
Ask: Require vendors to declare when a product qualifies as an AI system, its risk classification and 
material model facts, including when AI is added via software feature updates. Declarations should be 
machine readable and referenceable in contracts and procurement portals. 
Why: We are not always informed when an AI capability is introduced. For example, HR tools we procure 
have received updates that added AI functionality without explicit notice. Today there is an overreliance 
on the deployer reading technical documentation to classify a tool. A mandatory, standardised 
declaration would create one source of truth and reduce the risk of missed classifications. 
Human oversight 
Ask: Define what counts as effective human oversight for deployers and the expected competence or 
training for staff who perform it, including role profiles, independence requirements, escalation paths 
and documentation minimums. 
Why: Clear baselines enable consistent staffing, training and auditability across business units. Without 
concrete expectations, oversight risks becoming a checkbox exercise rather than reliable risk control. 
Record-keeping and event logs 
Ask: Clarify what deployers must log under Article 12, including events to capture, retention periods and 
acceptable formats. Provide templates that cover model version, configuration, data inputs, output 
rationale, human interventions and outcomes. 
Why: Current provisions focus on providers. Deployer clarity is needed to select tools, size storage and 
design processes. Interoperable, template-based logs would enable audits and incident investigations 
without custom solutions in each team and company. 
Fundamental Rights Impact Assessments (FRIAs) 
Ask: Define clear triggers and methodology for FRIA, provide templates and examples, and explain how 
FRIA relates to GDPR DPIA, and other HRIA’s including when a single assessment can cover both. 
Why: Overlaps create uncertainty and duplication efforts. A trigger list and a FRIA–DPIA crosswalk would 
let companies plan assessments early in the lifecycle and avoid overcompliance. 
Data quality requirements 
Ask: Provide practical criteria and best practice examples for data quality in high-risk systems. Clarify 
which obligations sit with providers, integrators and deployers, and when providers must supply model 
and dataset documentation.

Why: Vague wording leaves accountability unclear for deployers and leads to inconsistent controls. Clear

Clarification of overlaps with adjacent regulation 
Ask: Clarify how the AI Act interacts with machine safety, accessibility, product liability, corporate 
sustainability, labour and platform work frameworks, and provide a mapping of precedents. 
Why: A harmonised mapping avoids double reporting and conflicting instructions, and lets companies 
reuse test reports, sustainability reports and assurance across regimes. 
Content authenticity 
Ask: Fast track the adoption of European labelling systems of AI-generated content and disclosure when 
people interact with AI systems. Endorse embedded metadata standards, such as C2PA, and encourage 
interoperable labelling for images, audio, video, and text. 
Why: A harmonised approach avoids a patchwork of incompatible labels and reduces implementation 
cost. We are developing an internal approach to label AI-generated content, but an EU-endorsed 
standard would reduce rework and ensure our labels are recognised across the digital ecosystem. 
AI literacy guidance 
Ask: Provide practical literacy guidance with examples and templates for training, oversight and 
prohibited practices, and show best practices from real use cases, divided by sectors. 
Why: Scaling literacy requires usable materials, not only principles. When the first literacy obligations 
under the AI Act appeared there was limited guidance and we had to develop our own programs and 
seek examples from peers. Standardised materials would accelerate compliant upskilling. 
On Article 50 
Section 1  
Ask: Clarify whether simply naming a system something like “AI” or “GPT” is sufficient to meet the 
requirement to inform users that they are interacting with AI. 
Why: While vulnerable groups such as elderly people and children may require more information, for a 
reasonable adult user, the name itself might be sufficient to ensure compliance with Article 50 (1). 
Section 3 
Ask: Provide more clarity and examples of AI systems that are “allowed” for emotion recognition and 
biometric categorisation. 
Why: The article references emotion recognition and biometric categorisation systems that differ from 
those prohibited, but the guidelines released on prohibited systems mainly give examples of banned use 
cases. It remains unclear which systems fall under the permitted category. 
Section 5 
Ask: Clarify whether we should expect similar suggestions for how to present transparency information 
under Article 50 (clear and distinguishable manner), and whether the codes of practice mentioned in 
Article 50 (7) will help with it. 
Why: EU Data Protection Authorities have shared best practices in terms of how cookie banners should 
effectively and clearly explain data processing and collection to users. We should expect comparable 
guidance for transparency information under Article 50. 
 
2) Cookies and ePrivacy Directive 
Consent set once, applied everywhere 
Ask: Enable consent management at the browser or operating system level and require services to 
respect these settings, including recognition of global privacy signals. Provide a reference API for services 
to query consent reliably.

Why: This reduces banner fatigue and the “consent chase” across sites. It also improves accessibility for 
people who rely on assistive technologies. 
Risk-based consent 
Ask: Require explicit consent only for high-risk tracking, for example cross-site profiling, fingerprinting 
and data sharing with third parties. Permit lighter conditions for low-risk uses, for example first-party 
analytics with aggregation, retention caps and no sharing. 
Why: Protection is focused where risks are highest, while low-risk measurement can proceed with 
proportionate safeguards and transparency. 
Align ePrivacy with GDPR 
Ask: Align definitions and legal bases with GDPR, clarify when legitimate interest can be used for low-risk 
analytics, and reduce duplicate notices. 
Why: A coherent framework cuts legal uncertainty, reduces double consent prompts and helps 
companies apply one consistent compliance logic. 
Consistent rules for cookie banners 
Ask: Establish an EU-level standard for compliant banners, including equal prominence of “accept” and 
“reject”, clear categories, and prohibition of manipulative design. Encourage DPAs to apply that standard 
consistently. 
Why: Divergent national interpretations lead to repeated redesigns and inconsistent enforcement. A 
single pattern reduces rework and complaint risk. 
Cutting compliance costs 
Ask: Require platforms and major browsers to offer built-in consent and preference tools that services 
can adopt and recognise these tools under enforcement guidance. 
Why: Third-party CMP pricing tied to visitor volume increases costs for high-traffic sites without clear 
user benefit. Built-in options make compliance more predictable and cost-effective. 
 
3) Cybersecurity  
Incident reporting streamlining 
Ask: Streamline incident reporting by aligning channels, formats and deadlines across regimes, and 
encourage a one-stop reporting portal with a standard template. 
Why: Consolidated reporting reduces duplication for operators subject to multiple obligations and 
improves the quality and timeliness of information to competent authorities. 
Align reporting timelines and processes 
Ask: Align NIS2 reporting with existing GDPR procedures by setting 72 hours as the 
standard timeframe for notifying a significant incident, and ensure the reporting timeline 
starts only once an incident has been confirmed as significant 
Why: The 24-hour early warning creates a parallel process to GDPR and forces teams to 
report before an incident is properly assessed. This duplicates effort and draws resources 
away from handling the actual event. 
Focus reporting on confirmed significant incidents 
Ask: Limit mandatory reporting to verified significant incidents and remove the 
requirement to report suspected or attempted intrusions. Refine the rule on recurring 
incidents so that only clearly linked events with material impact are counted together. 
Why: Large organisations detect attempted intrusions on a daily basis. Reporting every 
suspected event would overwhelm both companies and authorities.

Clarify what counts as a significant impact 
Ask: Provide clear guidance on how to assess when a slowdown or outage qualifies as 
“limited availability” or “considerably slower than average, ” and standardise how to 
estimate the number of users affected, including for internal and franchise service 
models. 
Why: Current wording is open to interpretation. Clear thresholds would create 
consistency across Member States and ensure that reporting focuses on genuinely 
impactful events. 
Clarify scope and authorities for reporting 
Ask: Clarify when incidents outside the EU that affect shared corporate networks fall 
under NIS2, and publish an EU-wide registry of competent authorities and CSIRTs with 
their sector responsibilities and contact details. 
Why: Multinational companies operate shared systems across regions. Clear scope and 
contact points would make reporting more predictable and prevent duplicate work. 
Publish a timeline for implementing regulations and standards 
Ask: Provide a clear timeline for the remaining implementing regulations and standards 
under NIS2, covering all relevant business activities beyond Managed Service Providers. 
Why: Only one detailed implementation regulation has been issued so far. A predictable 
timeline would help companies prepare compliance processes in an orderly and cost-
efficient way. 
Coordinated requirements across NIS2, CRA and RED delegated acts 
Ask: Align overlapping obligations on vulnerability handling, incident and breach reporting, and baseline 
controls, including common thresholds, timelines and taxonomies, and coordinate enforcement dates. 
Why: Parallel and partly duplicative requirements raise complexity for manufacturers and operators. 
Alignment reduces duplicate work, especially when multiple frameworks cover the same product or 
service. 
Clearer scope and terminology with concrete examples 
Ask: Provide clear definitions and examples of in-scope products and explain how to interpret “direct” 
and “indirect” internet connectivity in RED delegated acts, for example devices that connect via a 
gateway or hub. Clarify treatment of modules and local-only connectivity. 
Why: Ambiguity drives uneven application and unnecessary cost. Examples like smart lighting, 
connected home devices and appliances would help manufacturers determine scope consistently. 
Continuous compliance for updatable products 
Ask: Provide lifecycle guidance, including in the Blue Guide, for products with frequently updated 
software, with a distinction between material and non-material updates, reuse of prior assessments and 
safe-harbour approaches for security patches. 
Why: Agile updates can otherwise trigger constant reassessment, slowing R&D and increasing cost 
without proportional security gains. 
 
4) Digital identity and trust services 
Predictable rollout 
Ask: Set clear milestones and deadlines for when European Digital Identity Wallets will be available in 
each member state, including interim readiness gates. 
Why: Businesses need dependable timelines to plan adoption and integration.

Validation of wallet and trust service providers 
Ask: Maintain an EU-level register of recognised wallet providers and qualified trust service providers, 
with transparent status and assurance information. 
Why: Companies need a reliable way to check compliance status before integration and contracting. 
Consistent user experience 
Ask: Provide reference UX flows and implementation patterns, so wallet use is simple and consistent 
across services and contexts, including retail checkout, delivery and in-store workforce scenarios, with 
online and offline flows and fallbacks for users without smartphones. 
Why: A harmonised journey builds familiarity and trust, reduces support needs and accelerates 
adoption. 
Supplier assurance 
Ask: Issue standard contractual clauses and evidence requirements for identity and trust service 
suppliers, for example breach notification timelines, availability targets, conformity attestations and 
audit rights. 
Why: Clear model clauses simplify due diligence, align supplier obligations with corporate governance 
and reduce negotiation friction. 
 
5) Data acquis (Data Act and Data Governance Act) 
Overreaching complexity on the Data Act structure 
Ask: Simplify the structure by clarifying the interplay between the two regimes in the Data Act, namely 
data holder obligations and data processing obligations, and their overlap with GDPR, the Data 
Governance Act and the Trade Secrets Directive. 
Why: The two regimes can overlap, which makes application confusing, for example when an entity is 
not a data holder yet is still a data processing service provider. Interplay with GDPR, the DGA and Trade 
Secrets adds to complexity. For instance, a data holder’s sharing obligation may be limited where 
personal data is involved. Clearer boundaries and examples would improve compliance. 
Concept of providers of data processing services 
Ask: Clarify the definition and scope of “providers of data processing services,” and explicitly exclude 
entities that only facilitate or pass on services, such as franchise systems that redistribute third-party 
services within a group. 
Why: Chapter VI applies to providers of data processing services, yet the term “provider” is not defined. 
It is unclear whether it covers only sellers or fee-based operators, or also facilitators and pass-through 
models. Without exclusions, entities that merely facilitate could be captured by obligations they are not 
equipped to fulfil. 
Clarification of overlaps with adjacent regulation 
Ask: Clarify how the Data Act and Data Governance Act interacts with corporate and product related 
sustainability regulations – especially requirements for Digital Product Passport and the vision going 
forward on how a European Trust Infrastructure, including digital identity services, will enable 
compliance across multiple regulatory areas. 
Why: We have received indication from DG ENVI that the Digital Product Passport should be thought of 
as a ‘mandatory product information data space’ and can therefore be enabled by the data sharing 
ecosystem, architectures and digital identity services developed by DSSC and CIRPASS. Clarification on 
what are accepted standards, explicit overlaps in requirement and known EU funded support systems 
would enable acceleration towards compliance with both digital and sustainability regulations. 
Concept of providers of data processing services 
Ask: Clarify the definition and scope of providers of data processing services, ensure alignment with the 
broad definition of data, and specify what types of services can be subject to data-switching requests.

Why: The term provider is unclear and could capture facilitators that only pass on or redistribute 
services, such as franchise systems. Clearer boundaries would prevent unintended inclusion and improve 
predictability for businesses. 
Switching charges 
Ask: Amend Article 29 to clarify what is included in switching charges and what costs are excluded. 
Why: Ambiguity risks inconsistent interpretation across Member States and unpredictable pricing for 
customers. 
Definitions and governance 
Ask: Amend Article 2(12) to specify how multiple users of a connected product are included and their 
rights allocated and amend Article 2(13) to confirm that data holder covers connected products and 
related services. Clarify that data holders may use non-personal data to improve products. 
Why: Current definitions do not reflect multi-user contexts and leave uncertainty over how connected 
product data may be used for innovation. 
Supervisory authorities 
Ask: Clarify how national authorities appointed under Article 37 will coordinate with existing Data 
Protection Authorities. 
Why: Overlapping supervision risks inconsistent enforcement and duplicate reporting. Clear division of 
roles would improve coherence and reduce compliance burden.

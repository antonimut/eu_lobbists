Amazon response to the public consultation on the EU Digital 
Simplification Omnibus, 13 October 2025 
 
 
INTRODUCTION 
 
Amazon welcomes the opportunity to contribute to the European Commission's consultation 
on digital simplification. Through our experience serving many European customers and 
businesses with diverse digital services, we have gained direct insights into the complexities 
and overlaps within the current digital regulatory framework. We believe thoughtful 
simplification can reduce administrative burdens while maintaining high standards of security, 
privacy, and consumer protection. Our proposals below focus on practical solutions that would 
benefit the entire EU digital environment, from large enterprises to SMEs, while ensuring 
Europe remains competitive in the global digital economy. 
 
 
1) CYBERSECURITY 
 
Cybersecurity Incident Reporting  
The issue: Duplicative and conflicting cybersecurity incident reporting requirements have 
emerged as the EU has recently legislated to ensure a high level of cybersecurity across the EU. 
For example, in the case of NIS2 (Network and Information Security Directive) and the Cyber 
Resilience Act (CRA), there are overlaps and inconsistencies between reporting terminology, 
thresholds, timelines and intake methods for such reports.  
The impact: These overlaps and inconsistencies create regulatory burdens for organisations. 
For example, reporting the same information on more than one reporting platform or to 
multiple regulatory authorities; updating authorities according to different timescales, creating 
increasing reporting requirements at particularly sensitive times when incidents may be 
unresolved; and facing uncertainty as to whether different reporting requirements are 
triggered, due to differing definitions and thresholds of what constitutes a reportable incident.  
Solution: We support efforts to align reporting across the legislative acts, following the 
approach taken in the NIS2 Directive. This approach would allow for consistency across 
organisations involved in the supply chain, including alignment with the different 
responsibilities they hold (e.g., between providers and customer), and support effective 
reporting based upon a common and aligned understanding of regulatory requirements.  
Conclusion: Aligning reporting requirements will allow resources to be dedicated towards 
resolving incidents and ensuring effective compliance, rather than duplicative and onerous 
administrative tasks.

Promotion of automation in cybersecurity reporting and compliance 
The issue: In implementing cybersecurity reporting and compliance requirements, there is an 
opportunity to use digital tools and automation/AI to reduce administrative burdens and 
manage the challenges derived from modern ICT (e.g., scalability). 
The impact: Automation and AI will enable cybersecurity teams to dedicate more time and 
resources to resolving cybersecurity incidents and allow public administrations to receive and 
process significant volumes of data in a way that encourages actionable outcomes. 
Solution: The EC should enable compliance process automation where possible with current 
technology when setting down the requirements for cybersecurity reporting. 
Conclusion: Use of automation and AI in compliance processes will support effective and 
efficient collaboration between organisations and supervisors, allowing organisations to focus 
on actionable outcomes rather than paper-based processes. 
 
Development of the One Stop Shop Principle     
The issue: The One Stop Shop Principle included in NIS2 risks not delivering expected outcomes 
for the Digital Single Market, due to different interpretations applied by Member States.  
The impact: These divergences are leading to individual Member States requiring registration 
with responsible authorities and can ultimately lead to duplicative requirements being imposed 
by several Member States on organisations offering services across the EU. This runs counter to 
the objective of the One Stop Shop. 
Solution: The EC should enforce the intent behind the One Stop Shop and ensure that 
organisations are registered and supervised by only a single Member State, determined by the 
NIS jurisdictional criteria. In addition, the EC should implement further mutual recognition 
provisions. These should provide that for organisations that are assessed by their responsible 
authority as being compliant with their obligations under NIS2, this assessment would be valid 
for other EU Member States’ authorities also, including for comparable horizontal or sectoral 
regulatory regimes. 
Conclusion: The One Stop Shop should deliver on its expected results for cross-border 
organisations by expanding mutual recognition to reduce cross-border frictions for digital 
service providers. 
 
Prioritization of Harmonized Standards as opposed to Common Specifications 
The issue: The "Omnibus IV" package aims to introduce changes across a series of regulations 
and directives. One horizontal objective aims to increase the reliance on Common 
Specifications, as opposed to harmonized standards which is particularly critical for 
cybersecurity. Due to the lack of guardrails and governance processes aligned to our European 
Standardization System’s principles, this shift towards Common Specifications raises concerns 
about potential market fragmentation and reduced stakeholder participation in standards 
development (e.g., CEN/CLC JTC13 WG9 for EU CRA). 
The impact: The proposal to extend the use of Common Specifications represents a departure 
from traditional European Standardisation System practices, potentially creating barriers 
between the EU and international markets. Such changes could add complexity to both 
European businesses seeking international expansion and companies operating within the EU. 
3 
 
This shift could also prompt third countries to implement similarly restrictive or divergent 
policies. 
Solution: We advocate an approach that prioritises key stakeholders and European consumers 
in guiding standardisation processes for cybersecurity. Priority should be given to identifying 
genuine customer challenges and developing appropriate standardisation mechanisms. 
Common Specifications should be limited to exceptional cases, specifically when European 
Standard Organisations decline EC standardisation requests. In such instances, the Commission 
must establish clear guidelines governing the implementation and maintenance of Common 
Specifications for cybersecurity.  
Conclusion: While acknowledging the intention to ensure legal certainty through Common 
Specifications when harmonised standards are absent, the systematic implementation of this 
approach poses significant risks for ongoing cybersecurity activities developing harmonised 
standards. Preserving the fundamental principles of the European Standardisation System is 
crucial for digital simplification and maintaining alignment between regulations and market 
requirements, preventing unnecessary regulatory burden. A focus on customer needs and 
stakeholder engagement promotes simplification, better adoption, innovation, and quality in 
harmonised standards for cybersecurity. 
 
 
2) ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE ACT 
 
Ensure sufficient time for compliance  
The issue: While some legal provisions already apply, provisions for high-risk AI and on 
transparency (Art. 50) will only apply from August 2026. The crucial legal specifications in the 
Code of Practice on Art. 50 as well as Guidelines and CEN CENELEC’s technical standards 
necessary for AI Act implementation will not be complete before mid-2026. Yet companies 
must comply from August 2026.  
The impact: Companies will not have the time needed to prepare for compliance based on the 
required legal specifications, which creates high legal uncertainty. 
Solution: Priority should be given to allow adequate time for businesses to properly implement 
the AI Act, following legal specifications becoming available. This requires adjusting the legal 
compliance deadline. This should be coupled with aligning CEN CENELEC's technical standards 
with international standards (e.g., OECD, G7 frameworks), which helps to accelerate the 
process and reduces compliance costs through legal fragmentation. 
 
Reduce collateral damage for positive use cases 
The issue: Many aspects of the AI Act continue to place unnecessary barriers on positive and 
innovative uses of AI that benefit both consumers and society. For example, many AI systems 
that pose no significant risk of harm to health, safety or fundamental rights don't clearly fit the 
current Article 6(3) conditions, potentially leading to over-classification. This will limit EU 
availability of innovative solutions that benefit customers elsewhere. 
The Impact: For example, AI systems that can enable more helpful and responsive customer 
interactions, or chatbot applications able to analyze images, may face unnecessary high-risk 
obligations despite posing no material risk to any individual. 
4 
 
Solution: Article 49(2) should be available for such systems that, while not falling directly within 
enumerated conditions, pose no significant risk of harm. This would maintain appropriate 
safeguards for genuinely high-risk applications while enabling uses that meet evolving customer 
expectations for AI interactions. Specifications through harmonized standards will be key to 
promoting compliance certainty. 
 
Streamline and harmonize enforcement 
The issue: National Market Surveillance Authorities (MSAs) will enforce non-GPAI provisions. 
Different individual MSAs will need to decide how to regulate a specific use case, even if the 
same use case is provided in several member states. The AI Act does not oblige MSAs to align 
these activities. 
The impact: MSAs’ discretion on how to regulate creates a significant risk of fragmented 
interpretations and enforcement across the EU. Similar use cases risk being subject to different 
AI Act provisions, depending on the MSA. This creates procedural duplication and bureaucratic 
hurdles, burdening the deployment of high-risk and low risk AI systems falling under Art. 50. 
Solution: To address this, we suggest amending the AI Act to make the AI Office responsible for 
enforcing requirements for low-risk AI systems that fall under Art. 50. This aligns with the AI 
Office's role of facilitating the development of a Code of Practice in this area. For high-risk AI 
systems, where sector-specific expertise is needed, we recommend a transparent mechanism 
for mutual recognition of MSAs' decisions to ensure consistent interpretation of requirements.  
 
Reduce reporting burdens and overlap with data protection impact assessments 
The issue: Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) required by the GDPR are also covering 
AI-related issues and, thus, overlap with AI Act requirements. Article 27(4) AI Act generally 
allows alignment between DPIA and the AI Act's Fundamental Rights Assessments (FRAs). 
The Impact: Depending on the decisions of individual enforcers, companies may need to 
provide overlapping assessments, increasing compliance burdens without adding value. 
Solution: AI providers should be able to determine whether DPIAs suffice for FRA requirements, 
reducing duplicate work while upholding comprehensive risk evaluation. 
 
 
3) DATA ACQUIS 
 
EU Data Act Article 35: Interoperability Standards Implementation  
The issue: The European Commission plans to establish an EU standard repository (Q4 2025), 
mandating compatibility with specified interoperability standards and specifications for certain 
data processing service providers (cloud PaaS, SaaS, and edge services). This initiative proceeds 
despite absence of evidence of market failure, lack of demonstrated added value beyond 
existing industry solutions, negative consequences to innovation and limited stakeholder 
consultation. 
The impact: These requirements will increase regulatory burden through mandatory costs for 
compatibility implementation and monitoring. It will redirect resources from service innovation 
to compliance activities creating barriers to entry for resource-constrained enterprises (start-
ups/SMEs). Constraints on technical specification choices may result in reduced service 
5 
 
development flexibility and impede market-driven solutions. It risks creating second-tier EU-
specific services by global providers where implementing compatibility to the standard or 
specification adds no customer value and/or would impede innovation for customers in other 
regions.  
Solution: The Data Act provides discretionary authority rather than mandatory implementation 
requirements. A measured implementation is needed deferring mandatory standards adoption 
until clear market necessity is established following a thorough impact assessment and 
comprehensive industry consultation process to ensure alignment with market needs and 
technological industry practice.  
Conclusion: Immediate implementation of mandatory standards is not required by the Data 
Act. A measured, evidence-based approach starting with a voluntary and informative list of 
interoperability standards (similar as the approach in the Interoperable Europe Act), coupled 
with robust stakeholder engagement would better serve market development, while ensuring 
regulatory objectives are met. 
  
A level playing field in consumer policy and enforcement 
The Issues: The current landscape of EU consumer protection faces several significant 
challenges. Complex and fragmented regulations across EU member states create a maze of 
compliance requirements for businesses. Information requirements for repetitive transactions 
have become overly burdensome. The current regulatory framework proves inflexible for 
emerging AI shopping assistants, potentially stifling innovation. Additionally, fragmented 
national rules on price reductions for perishable goods create inconsistencies in the market. 
These issues are compounded by information overload affecting customer experience, 
redundant requirements in digital environments, and multiple overlapping legal frameworks 
including Digital Services Act (DSA), Unfair commercial practices directive (UCPD), and GDPR. 
The Impact: The effects of these challenges manifest in two primary areas: business operations 
and consumer experience. For businesses, the impact includes increased operational costs, 
disproportionate burdens on digital media suppliers, and particular challenges for SMEs in 
meeting multilingual requirements. Innovation in AI-driven solutions is being hindered by rigid 
regulatory frameworks. From the consumer perspective, information overload makes it difficult 
to process relevant information, leading to cumbersome shopping experiences, especially with 
AI assistants. Consumers face potential barriers to accessing beneficial services and confusion 
arising from overlapping regulations. 
Solutions: Solutions can be categorised into short-term and long-term measures. In the 
immediate term, implementing flexible information requirements for AI shopping assistants 
would provide relief. Eliminating redundant formal requirements, such as model withdrawal 
forms, and transitioning to digital-only format for non-essential product information would 
streamline processes. The implementation of Digital Product Passport (DPP) for multilingual 
information would enhance accessibility. Looking ahead, long-term solutions include 
developing transparent AI agent self-identification mechanisms and creating structured 
dialogue for monitoring emerging regulatory barriers. Harmonising labeling and standardized 
information requirements, establishing a broader consumer strategy to prevent national 
fragmentation, and leveraging the existing Consumer Rights Directive with simplifications 
would create a more cohesive regulatory environment. 
6 
 
Conclusion: The simplification of EU consumer laws represents a crucial initiative that must 
carefully balance efficiency with protection. While reducing administrative burden is essential, 
it should not come at the cost of consumer safeguards. The path forward requires enabling 
innovation, particularly in AI-driven solutions, while harmonising regulations across member 
states. Embracing digital solutions for information delivery and addressing overlaps in various 
legal frameworks will be key to creating a more coherent regulatory environment. Success in 
this endeavor will benefit both businesses and consumers, creating a more efficient and 
effective marketplace while maintaining robust consumer protection. 
  
 
4) EUROPEAN DIGITAL IDENTITY FRAMEWORK 
 
Regulatory fragmentation and overlapping compliance requirements 
The issue: eIDAS 2.0 (electronic identification, authentication, and trust services) regulation 
creates overlapping obligations with multiple frameworks (NIS2, DORA (Digital Operational 
Resilience Act), DSA, GDPR, CSAM (Child Sexual Abuse Material) Regulation, each with distinct 
incident reporting requirements, compliance obligations, supervisory authorities and 
enforcement mechanisms. Organisations face duplicative reporting through different channels, 
formats, and timelines. Additional complexity arises from emerging use cases for online 
platforms which intersect with DSA and CSAM requirements. 
The impact: Organisations must maintain separate compliance programs, report the same 
incidents multiple times and engage with multiple supervisory authorities. This creates 
administrative complexity during critical incident response periods, increases compliance costs 
significantly, and creates legal uncertainty from potentially conflicting requirements. SMEs are 
disproportionately affected due to limited resources. 
Solution: Establish a unified reporting mechanism allowing organisations to fulfill multiple 
regulatory obligations through single submission. Extend the "One Stop Shop" principle from 
NIS2 to eIDAS 2.0. Create clear regulatory hierarchy and mutual recognition between 
frameworks - where organisations comply with equivalent requirements under NIS2 or DORA, 
this should be recognized for eIDAS 2.0. Align definitions, thresholds and timelines across all 
frameworks. Ensure coordination between sectoral legislation (DSA, CSAM) and cybersecurity 
frameworks. 
Conclusion: Harmonising compliance requirements across frameworks will reduce 
administrative burdens, allow organisations to focus on security and service delivery rather 
than regulatory complexity and ensure consistent enforcement across the EU. 
 
International standards and global certification recognition 
The issue: eIDAS 2.0 establishes EU-specific technical standards and certification schemes that 
diverge from established international standards (ISO/IEC 27001, FIDO Alliance, W3C, global PKI 
frameworks). This creates regulatory isolation requiring solutions to be redesigned specifically 
for the EU market, while not recognising equivalent international certifications providers may 
already possess. 
The impact: Organisations face duplicate certification costs and barriers to entry. EU access to 
innovative global solutions is limited. European providers face competitive disadvantages 
7 
 
internationally when their eIDAS certifications aren't recognised globally. This undermines the 
development of truly global digital identity solutions and contradicts EU digital single market 
objectives. 
Solution: Prioritise alignment with international standards (ISO, ITU-T, FIDO, W3C) in 
developing technical specifications. Establish mutual recognition agreements with international 
certification bodies. Adopt risk-based approach accepting international certifications meeting 
equivalent standards. Design EU-specific requirements as European refinements to 
international standards rather than replacements. Create a clear equivalence assessment 
framework similar to GDPR adequacy decisions. 
Conclusion: Embracing international standards and mutual recognition will position the EU as a 
leader in global digital identity interoperability while maintaining high security standards and 
reducing unnecessary barriers for all providers. 
 
Technical implementation complexity and timeline pressures 
The issue: Implementing acts and technical standards for eIDAS 2.0 remain under development, 
creating uncertainty around liability, functional requirements and exact specifications. 
Providers must implement EUDI (European Digital Identity Regulation) Wallet support, upgrade 
identity verification systems and ensure interoperability across national schemes by specific 
deadlines (e.g., PSPs by December 2027) without clear guidance. Security requirements, 
including encryption standards, face potential conflicts with other legislation like CSAM. 
The impact: Organisations face uncertain implementations, increased compliance risks and 
difficulty planning investments without clear guidance. Technical complexity requires 
substantial investments in new technologies, API integrations, and skills. Potential encryption 
weakening for CSAM compliance could undermine EUDI Wallet security and conflict with NIS2 
requirements. 
Solution: Provide clear technical guidance based on international standards well before 
compliance deadlines and allow adequate time for businesses to properly implement legal 
specifications when they become available. Establish a phased implementation approach. 
Ensure the Technology Roadmap on Encryption maintains strong on security, resilience and 
safeguarding of human rights. Create centralised guidance mechanisms for consistent 
interpretation and conduct regular stakeholder consultations. 
Conclusion: Adequate implementation timelines with clear, risk-based technical guidance will 
enable organisations to build secure, interoperable systems rather than rushing compliance 
under regulatory uncertainty. 
 
User adoption and digital inclusion 
The issue: The wallet-centric approach requires users to adapt to new tools across multiple 
services. Not all users have compatible devices (near field communication smartphones) or 
technical skills. Some identity documents remain non-electronic. Fragmented implementation 
across Member States creates inconsistent user experiences. 
The impact: Digital divide affects elderly, rural, and lower socioeconomic populations. 
Inconsistent cross-border recognition creates user frustration. Risk of excluding citizens from 
essential services. 
8 
 
Solution: Implement digital literacy programs. Ensure alternative access methods for 
incompatible devices. Maintain traditional verification during transition. Establish minimum 
accessibility standards and harmonised UX guidelines across the EU. 
Conclusion: Inclusive implementation will maximize eIDAS 2.0 benefits while preventing new 
barriers to essential services, supporting digital rights for all EU citizens. 
 
 
5) GDPR and ePRIVACY 
 
Updating ePrivacy rules 
The issue: EU’s rules on cookies and other tracking technologies laid down by the ePrivacy 
Directive should be simplified.  
The Impact: The current framework has led to an over-reliance on consent mechanisms in the 
form of cookie banners, resulting in a sub-optimal user experience and the well-documented 
phenomenon of “consent fatigue” that undermines users’ privacy choices and control. It is 
implemented variably across Member States and not all data protection authorities have 
competence over ePrivacy, which leads to legal uncertainty and fragmentation. The recent 
EDPB guidance has additionally broadened the application of Article 5(3) of the Directive, 
requiring GDPR‑standard consent for a wide range of tracking technologies without consistent 
exemption guidance.  
Solution: Amendments to Article 5(3) should allow for a pragmatic, risk-based and technology-
neutral approach to support use of cookies or similar technologies for legitimate and 
transparent purposes without impacting privacy. Allowing legitimate and transparent low-risk 
processing activities that advertisers, publishers and their AdTech partners consider critical to 
deliver commercially viable advertising campaigns without consent would lessen consent 
fatigue and enhance regulatory clarity. This includes, for example, use of storage or access 
technologies for purposes related to security, fraud prevention, product maintenance, software 
updates, contextual advertising, aggregated analytics and frequency capping. When personal 
identifiers are present, data collected via cookies should be able to be processed under any of 
the legal bases set out in the GDPR, notably ‘legitimate interest’. This alignment would reduce 
over‑reliance on consent, ensure consistency in the treatment of personal data, and establish a 
single harmonised framework across the EU, reducing fragmentation and regulatory 
uncertainty. Efforts to reduce consent fatigue should not involve shifting to centralised user 
controls such as browser, or OS‑level consent management systems. These approaches assume 
that consumer preferences are not context‑specific, which research consistently shows is not 
the case. They also risk failing GDPR’s specificity requirements, as most users do not alter 
default settings, as well as potentially concentrating power in the hands of large browser 
providers, raising competition concerns. It is important that website owners retain the ability to 
engage directly with their customers in terms of data use and personalisation.  
Conclusion: For the use of storage or access technologies for low risk-processing activities 
exemptions to consent requirements should be introduced. Requiring separate consent under 
Article 5(3), the collection of personal data using cookies and similar technologies should fall 
under the GDPR to create a unified and consistent legal regime.

Maintain GDPR as a horizontal and risk-based regulation 
The issue: Simplification of the GDPR can bring efficiencies, foster growth and enable 
innovation across the European economy. However, limiting simplification efforts to SMEs, 
while well intended, risk creating regulatory uncertainty, triggering unintended consequences 
across the broader supply chains and undermining the GDPR principle of accountability.  
The impact: Data flows are increasingly interconnected, with SMEs playing a crucial role in 
supply chains and handling processing on behalf of larger organisations. Lower compliance 
standards for SMEs could heighten the risk of supply chain attacks that would impact 
data subjects' privacy and the wider economy. Large company controllers often rely on SMEs to 
process data on their behalf. Under the GDPR’s principle of accountability, controllers must be 
able to demonstrate that all processing activities — including those carried out by processors — 
comply with data protection rules.  To protect themselves, larger controllers may avoid working 
with SMEs altogether or only work with SME processors that agree to adhere to the higher 
standards of data protection. This would undo any simplifications and harm competitiveness 
and innovation.  
Solution: Simplification of the GDPR should apply horizontally to all entities and not be limited 
to small and medium enterprises (SMEs). The Commission can achieve its objective to reduce 
companies' administrative burdens by ensuring regulatory clarity through guidance applicable 
to all companies and consistency across Member States.  
Conclusion: Adopt a balanced and inclusive approach to GDPR simplification that applies 
uniformly to all companies rather than focusing on SMEs only, to ensure legal clarity and 
consistent data protection standards.

Ecommerce Europe reply to the Call for Evidence on 
the Digital Omnibus 
Ecommerce Europe welcomes the simplification agenda of the EU rulebook by the Commission  as legal 
clarity is essential for businesses to operate and expand their operations across Member States. We 
especially appreciate the focus on improving the alignment and consistency of existing rules , rather than 
introducing additional layers of regulation. Kindly find below or initial feedback on the proposed changes.  
I. Cookies and related technologies  
Cookies and other tracking  technologies are a key driver of online advertising and an enabler of success 
for businesses selling products and services online. The ability to effectively reach potential customers 
through digital channels is not merely a competitive advantage, it is a  prerequisite for innovation, 
competition, and sustained economic growth for our sector. Personalised advertising, in particular, plays a 
critical role in ensuring that consumers are shown relevant content while enabling businesse s to optimise 
their limited marketing budgets. 
• Addressing cookie fatigue  
As underlined in the Call for Evidence (CfE), the outdated rules on cookies and other tracking technologies 
under Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive continue to govern both the protection of the user’s terminal 
equipment, as well as the access and collection of information from said equipment. In practice, this cookie 
rule has fuelled widespread consent fatigue . The proliferation of banners and click -throughs has led to 
user disengagement, undermining the transparency objective . It  is a situation neither desired by 
consumers nor businesses. We therefore welcome the Commission’s efforts to address this challenge. At 
the same time, we stress the importance of identifying the right approach: one that reduces unnecessary 
friction while upholding high standards  of data protection, ensur ing fairness across the digital ecosystem, 
and delivering real convenience for both consumers and businesses.  
• Modernising the framework through stronger alignment with the GDPR  
The CfE suggests including modernised rules on cookies under the GDPR framework when personal data 
are collected through such technologies. Ecommerce Europe welcomes this objective and is supportive of 
modernising the current framework to make it fit for the future of digital commerce. In that regard, we call 
for maximum alignment of any future cookie -related rules with the GDPR  and believe tha t Article 
5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive should be aligned with the six legal bases for data processing 
established under Article 6 GDPR. 
 
Article 5(3) ePrivacy Directive currently applies a basic and rigid “on/off” mechanism that relies solely on 
consent, in contrast to Article 6 GDPR, which offers a more balanced and flexible framework through six

Aligning Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive with the GDPR would enable the application of a risk-

on consent alone . Under a modernised framework, the use of cookies and related technologies could, 
where appropriate, be justified on the basis of legitimate interest (Article 6(1)(f) GDPR), as long as robust 
safeguards are in place to protect consumers’ rights and expectations.  
 
In addition, w e would welcome reducing cases where consent must be obtained. Amendments to Article 
5(3) should be made to provide clear, harmonised exemptions for legitimate and transparent low risk 
processing activities. For example use of storage or access technologies for purposes related to security, 
fraud prevention, product maintenance, software updates, contextual advertising, aggregated analytics and 
frequency capping , functional cookies for personali sation and user -friendly features  (for example to 
remember online shopping pre ferences, language settings, longer login sessions, etc. ) should be exempt 
from consent requirements. 
• Accelerating PETs uptake in Europe 
Ecommerce Europe sees great potential in the development of Privacy Enhancing Technologies (PETs) . 
Yet, the current landscape in Europe is underdeveloped , technically comple x, and lacks pan-European 
harmonisation. Most importantly, the ePrivacy Directive subjects many PETs to opt -in consent 
requirements. This situation places the development of PETs on the same legal ground as cookies, creating 
a major disincentive for business adoption. If the industry must bear the significant costs and technical 
complexity of deploying PETs, only to face the same consent -related friction and low opt -in rat es, the 
business case for innovation is non-existent.  
 
The upcoming Digital Omnibus could play an important role in addressing these challenges. To unlock the 
potential of PETs, the policy narrative must shift from restriction to strategic enablement, fostering an 
environment where privacy by design is not a compliance burden but a commercially viable driver of 
innovation. For PETs to deliver on their potential, they must be scalable and accessible to all market 
participants and not limited to expensive, bespoke solutions. Broad, cost -effective adoption is essential to 
ensure that privacy protections are not a premium feature, but a baseline standard across the ecosystem.  
To this end, Ecommerce Europe calls for the development of a common, interoperable PET infrastructure 
for advertising, developed with key tech leaders in the market.   
 
By allowing the usage of PETs without strict consent requirements , which could be achieved by 
aligning Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive with Article 6 GDPR, legitimate interest and its 
balancing test  could apply to PETs, provid ing a strong incentive for increased investment and 
research in PETs development across Europe.

• Keeping control over consent management 
Aligning Article 5(3) ePrivacy Directive with Article 6 GDPR would allow businesses to rely less on consent 
and make use of the GDPR’s broader range of legal bases for data processing. This would significantly 
reduce cookie fatigue and overreliance on consent banners. However, for cases where consent would still 
be required, we strongly call for EU-wide practical guidance on consent requirements for cookies and other 
tracking technologies, such as pixels or device identifiers, to ensure compliant use cases.  We should 
however not forget that diverging interpretations across Data Protection Authorities (DPAs) continues to 
create significant compliance challenges for businesses.   
 
Lastly, we believe that additional measures should not involve changes to centralised user controls, 
including browser or operating -system-level functionalities . These systems b ear important 
competition concerns, with deployers risking becoming gatekeepers which needs to be avoided, especially 
as cookies and other tracking technologies are particularly important for smaller businesses . While such 
systems may reduce the number of cookie banners, they should not be considered as the ultimate solutions 
to addressing cookie fatigue. The choices of consumers about their privacy are highly context-driven, and 
online businesses must be allowed to engage directly with their customers on the way they use their data, 
the purpose of their processing, and their approach to personalisation. Moreover, there is a risk that 
centralised cookie management tools always risk a generalised reliance on  centralised management 
systems would impose a burden on users  who would have to proactively change their default settings. It 
will make the consent even more abstract than it is now, and more confusing. There will be a risk that 
consumers deactivate features that improve their experience, leading to a bad experience on the website 
without realising that it is a consequence of their abstract choice. Ultimately, such approach would weaken 
transparency and consumer empowerment rather than strengthening it.     
• Looking ahead to the Digital Fitness Check 
The mandate of the current European Commission is driven by a desire to simplify rules and ensure a 
higher level of consistency between old and new rules. In this context, we expect the launch of a Digital 
Fitness Check as a follow up to the Digital Omnibus , with the aim of assessing  the coherence and 
cumulative impact of the EU digital acquis.  In this context, we would like to reiterate our call for caution 
regarding any potential reopening of the GDPR. Since its entry into force, the GDPR has established a 
robust, well -balanced, and globally recognised framework that ensures a high level of protection and 
consumer trust in digital services.  It has also become a cornerstone  of the EU’s digital acquis, with its 
principles serving as a foundation for an expanding body of legislation.  
 
E-commerce players  have invested significantly in ensuring c ompliance, incorporating its principles into 
their day-to-day operations, while navigating its various interpretations across Member States, a process 
that remains resource intensive. At this stage, the level of compliance and regulatory maturity achieved 
under the GDPR should be safeguarded. We therefore  strongly believe that efforts to achieve the 
objectives of the Digital Omnibus , particularly with regard to  cookies and related technologies , 
should not lead to a reopening of the GDPR.

• Aligning reporting requirements and deadlines 
Today, cybersecurity incident reporting (under NIS2, GDPR data breaches, DORA, etc.) involve multiple 
authorities and conflicting notifications deadlines. There are some deadlines of mere 4h for ZPS, 24h for 
security vulnerabilities and 72h for GDPR incidents . There are also concerns around  notification sharing 
provisions where for instance in Germany, BSI or BaFin might share a notification with D ata Protection 
Authorities (DPAs). These overlaps and inconsistencies create regulatory burdens for businesses including: 
reporting the same information on more than one reporting platform or to multiple authorities, updating 
authorities according to different timelines, increasing reporting requirements at particularly sensitive times 
when incidents may be unresolved, facing uncertainty as to whether different reporting requirements are 
triggered, due to differing definitions and thresholds of what constitutes a reportable incident.  
 
We therefore support aligning reporting requirements to allow for resources to be dedicated towards 
resolving incidents and ensuring effective compliance, rather than duplicative administrative tasks. In this 
context, a solution could be a “single reporting window” : a single portal at Member State level that 
forwards information to the right authorities (NIS2, DPA, consumer authority). We suggest that 72 hours 
should be the initial notification deadline, with detailed follow-up within 7 to 14 working days.  
• Promoting automation in cybersecurity reporting and compliance 
When it comes to cybersecurity reporting and compliance requirements, there is an opportunity to leverage 
digital tools and AI to reduce administrative burdens and manage the challenges derived from modern ICT 
(e.g., scalability and composability) . Automation and AI will both enable cybersecurity teams to dedicate 
more of their time to resolving cybersecurity incidents and allow public administrations to receive and 
process potentially significant volumes of data in a way that encourages actionable outcomes. In the future, 
the Commission, when setting down the requirements for cybersecurity reporting , s hould maximi se 
opportunities for machine-readable formats (e.g., OSCAL), standardi sed data formats and use of APIs, to 
allow for process automation and usage of AI where possible with current technology.  
• Developing the One Stop Shop Principle     
In addition, we fear that t he One Stop Shop Principle included in NIS2 risks not delivering the expected 
outcomes, due to different interpretations applied by Member States.  We encourage the Commission to 
enforce the intent behind the One Stop Shop and ensure that entities are registered and supervised by only 
a single Member State, determined by the NIS jurisdictional criteria. In addition, the Commission should 
implement further mutual recognition pro visions. These should provide that for entities that are assessed 
by their responsible authority as being compliant with their obligations under NIS2, this assessment would 
be valid for other Member States’ authorities, including for comparable horizontal or sectoral regulatory 
regimes.  
III. Data acquis  
When it comes to the data acquis, we believe that future efforts should aim to link the GDPR, ePrivacy, the 
Data Act, and the Data Governance Act, to ensure data protection while enabling responsible data sharing 
for innovation and competitiveness.  Clarifying and specify ing to what extent these instruments actually 
enable and allow data sharing to enable data driven use cases would be welcome.

More specifically, t he Data Governance Act  requires data intermediation service providers to establish 
separate legal entities for their data intermediation activities, distinct from their other business operations  
(Art. 12), which imposes a rigid solution to a problem that could be addressed through lighter, operational 
measures. The requirement adds substantial legal, administrative, and financial burdens, forcing 
companies to duplicate infrastructure, staff, and compliance processes. Instead of mandating structural 
separation, the Commission could introduce organisational and operational safeguards such as transparent 
pricing, clear internal controls, technical measures to prevent conflicts of interest, and contractual 
obligations to guarantee neutrality. These measures would ensure trust and inde pendence while 
maintaining flexibility, reducing regulatory burden, and supporting innovation in data intermediation 
services. 
IV. Artificial Intelligence Act  
• Ensuring sufficient time for compliance  
While some legal provisions already apply, provisions for high -risk AI and on transparency (Article 50) will 
only apply from August 2026. However, the legal specifications in the Code of Practice on Article 50 as well 
as Guidelines and CEN CENELEC’s technical standards  will not be complete before mid -2026. Yet 
companies must comply from August 2026.  Companies will not have the time needed to prepare for 
compliance based on the required legal specifications, which creates high legal uncertainty.  We therefore 
believe that priority should be implementing a 'stop-the-clock' mechanism that delays AI Act implementation 
until at least 1 year after legal specifications become available, allowing for more realistic compliance 
timelines. This should be coupled with al igning CEN CENELEC's technical standards with international 
standards (e.g., OECD, G7 frameworks), which helps to accelerate the process and reduces compliance 
costs through legal fragmentation. 
• Reducing collateral damage for positive use cases 
Many aspects of the AI Act place unnecessary barriers on positive and innovative uses of AI that benefit 
both consumers and society. For example, many AI systems that pose no significant risk of harm to health, 
safety or fundamental rights don't clearly fi t the current Article 6(3) conditions, potentially leading to over -
classification. This will limit EU availability of innovative solutions that benefit customers elsewhere. For 
example, AI systems that can enable more helpful and responsive customer intera ctions, or chatbot 
applications able to analyse images, may face unnecessary high-risk obligations despite posing no material 
risk to any individual. We believe that Article 49(2) should be available for such systems. This would 
maintain appropriate safeguards for genuinely high-risk applications while enabling uses that meet evolving 
customer expectations for AI interactions. Specifications through the harmonised standards will be key to 
promoting compliance certainty in a risk-balanced manner. 
• Streamlining and harmonising enforcement 
National Market Surveillance Authorities (MSAs) will enforce non -GPAI provisions. Different individual 
MSAs will need to decide how to regulate a specific use case, even if the same use case is provided in 
several member states. The AI Act does not oblige MSAs to align these activities. MSAs’ discretion on how 
to regulate creates a significant risk of fragmented interpretations and enforcement across the EU. Similar 
use cases risk being subject to different AI Act provisions, depending on the MSA. This creates procedural 
duplication and bureaucratic hurdles, burdening the deployment of high-risk and low risk AI systems falling

• Reducing reporting burdens and overlap with DPIAs 
 
There are unclear obligations imposed on the deployer role. This applies in particular to the use of off -the-
shelf COTS/SaaS and GPAI models and the overlap with consumer  and data protection legislation. For 
deployers of off -the-shelf high-risk AI systems, we recommend adopting a proportionate ‘deployer -light’ 
documentation package focusing on use -context, safeguards and training, with GPAI providers retaining 
core model evaluation duties.  
Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) required by the GDPR are also covering AI -related issues 
and, thus, overlap with the AI Act requirements. Article 27(4) AI Act generally allows alignment between 
DPIA and the AI Act's Fundamental Rights Assessments (FRAs). Depending on the decisions of individual 
enforcers, companies may need to provide overlapping assessments, increasing compliance burdens 
without adding value. We believe that AI providers should be able to determine whether DPIAs suffice for 
FRA requirements, reducing duplicate work while upholding comprehensive risk evaluation. More generally, 
we call for a single Joint Impact Assessment covering AI Act, GDPR and consumer law.  
V. European Digital identity Framework 
• Regulatory fragmentation and overlapping compliance requirements 
eIDAS 2.0 creates overlapping obligations with multiple frameworks (NIS2, DORA, DSA, GDPR, CSAM 
Regulation), each with distinct incident reporting requirements, compliance obligations, supervisory 
authorities, and enforcement mechanisms. Organi sations face duplicative reporting through different 
channels, formats, and timelines. Additional complexity arises from emerging use cases like age verification 
for online platforms, which intersect with DSA and CSAM requirements. Organi sations must maintain 
separate compliance programs, report the same incidents multiple times, and engage with multiple 
supervisory authorities. This creates administrative complexity during critical incident response periods, 
increases compliance costs significantly, and creates legal uncertainty from potentially conflicting 
requirements. SMEs are disproportionately affected due to limited resources.  
To address this, we propose e stablishing a unified reporting mechanism allowing entities to fulfil multiple 
regulatory obligations through single submission , and extending the "One Stop Shop" principle from NIS2 
to eIDAS 2.0 . We would also welcome clear regulatory hierarchy and mutual recognition between 
frameworks: w here entities comply with equivalent requirements under NIS2 or DORA, this should be 
recognised for eIDAS 2.0.  As well as clear  alignment on definitions, thresholds, and timelines across all 
frameworks.  
• International standards and global certification recognition

eIDAS 2.0 establishes EU-specific technical standards and certification schemes diverg ing from 
established international standards (ISO/IEC 27001, FIDO Alliance, W3C, global PKI frameworks). This 
creates regulatory isolation requiring solutions to be redesigned specifically for the EU market, while not 
recognising equivalent international certifications providers may already possess.  Organisations face 
duplicate certification costs and barriers to entry , and European providers face competitive disadvantages  
internationally when their eIDAS certifications aren't recognised globally.  
To address this issue, we recommend prioritising alignment with recognised international standards (such 
as ISO, ITU -T, FIDO, and W3C) when developing technical specifications.  The EU should also explore 
establishing mutual recognition agreements with international certification bodies and adopt a risk -based 
approach that accepts foreign certifications meeting equivalent standards. EU-specific requirements should 
be designed as extensions of international standards rather than as replacements. Lastly, clear equivalence 
assessment framework similar to GDPR adequacy decisions could be explored. 
•  Technical implementation complexity and timeline pressures 
Implementing acts and technical standards for eIDAS 2.0 remain under development, creating uncertainty 
around liability, functional requirements, and exact specifications. Providers must implement EUDI Wallet 
support, upgrade identity verification systems, and ensure i nteroperability across national schemes by 
specific deadlines (e.g., PSPs by December 2027) without clear guidance. Security requirements, including 
encryption standards, face potential conflicts with other legislation like CSAM. Organisations face uncertain 
implementations, increased compliance risks, and difficulty planning investments without clear guidance. 
Technical complexity requires substantial investments in new technologies, API integrations, and skills. 
Potential encryption weakening  for CSAM compliance could undermine EUDI Wallet security and conflict 
with NIS2 requirements. 
We encourage the Commission to p rovide clear technical guidance based on international standards well 
before compliance deadline s, and we suggest introduc ing a “stop-the-clock” mechanism, delaying 
enforcement until at least one year after the relevant standards are published.  A phased implementation 
approach would allow organisations to adapt gradually . We also recommend establishing centralised 
guidance mechanisms  to ensure consistent interpretation across Member States, alongside regular 
stakeholder consultations to identify implementation challenges early. 
• User adoption and digital inclusion 
The wallet-centric approach requires users to adapt to new tools, yet not all have compatible devices (NFC, 
smartphones) or technical skills. Some identity documents remain non -electronic and fragmented national 
implementation risks creating inconsistent user experiences.  Meanwhile, the d igital divide may exclude 
elderly, rural, and lower socioeconomic populations  from essential services.  To ensure inclusivity, we 
recommend implementing digital literacy programs, ensuring alternative access methods for inco mpatible 
devices, and m aintaining traditional verification during transition. Establish ing minimum accessibility 
standards and harmoni sed UX guidelines across EU  will help maximise the benefits of eIDAS 2.0 while 
safeguarding digital rights and ensuring no one is left behind.

1  Introduction 
Europe’s competitiveness is under significant pressure, as highlighted in recent reports by Draghi 
and Letta. The EU is lagging behind the United States and China in terms of economic growth, 
productivity, and innovation. There is a growing risk that Europe will fall behind in critical technolo-
gies. The saying “the US innovates, the EU regulates” is becoming increasingly accurate. 
The EU has introduced a comprehensive digital regulatory framework in the last years. Amongst 
them, the Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act), the Data Act, the NIS2 Directive and the Cyber Resilience 
Act (CRA). While those (horizontal) regulations aim to shape Europe’s digital landscape, some also 
pose challenges for the industry. In particular if they conflict or overlap with further data regulations 
that also partly deal with data. To be mentioned in this respect were the amendment to the Renewa-
ble Energy Directive (RED) or EURO 7 amongst others. The widely spread regulation for handling 
data and providing it to various stakeholders for various purposes challenges not just existing pro-
cesses and the digital business models within the Volkswagen Group. The uncertainty that is cur-
rently being created by different governance regimes and different compensation rules provided by 
the plentiful regulations is considerable. 
The sheer volume of unharmonized data-related legislation has led to an unprecedented workload, 
consuming financial and human resources that could otherwise be invested in research, develop-
ment, and innovation — all essential to maintaining global competitiveness. In this context, the 
Volkswagen Group strongly welcomes the European Commission’s announcement of the Digital Om-
nibus (Digital Package on Simplification), which aims to streamline and simplify digital regulation. 
Policy Recommendations at a Glance 
The Volkswagen Group calls on the European Commission to incorporate the following elements into 
the Digital Omnibus in order to reduce the burden for one of Europe’s key industries, cut red tape and 
reporting requirements. And thus enhances Europe’s competitiveness by unblocking the full poten-
tial of digital transformation. 
To achieve this, we propose the following three overarching actions: 
1. Reduce Reporting Obligations 
Several digital legislative acts, introduced by the European co-legislators, include extensive 
reporting requirements. To alleviate the overall regulatory burden and free up resources for 
research, innovation, and the development of new business models, reporting obligations 
with respect to data handling and provision should be streamlined throughout the existing 
regulations. Further, simplification of reporting must become a consideration in the legisla-
tive process. Additionally, the simplification of reporting obligations for data holders and 
data handlers should be a major concern throughout the data lifecycle on EU level as well as 
in the provisions for the legislators of the member states. 
2. Reduce Regulatory Complexity  
In order to foster innovation and ensure legal clarity across the European digital and indus-
trial landscape, it is essential to streamline regulatory frameworks and avoid overlapping or 
conflicting provisions. This position is exemplified by two particularly illustrative cases, 
which underscore the need for regulatory alignment and simplification: (1) The Data Act al-
ready establishes harmonized and mandatory requirements for data sharing across all con-
nected products with digital elements. A comprehensive compensation model can be de-
rived from this regulation, too. Supervisory bodies and dispute settlement mechanisms

2  Simplifying the EU’s Digital Regulations 
To advance Europe’s digital transformation, the EU must pursue a harmonized, low-bureaucracy, and 
innovation-friendly approach to implementing the recently adopted digital regulations. 
 
2.1  ePrivacy Directive 
The ePrivacy Directive (ePD) is a key EU regulation that governs (among other things) cookie installa-
tion, messaging data, and direct marketing by electronic means. It aims to protect the physical integ-
rity of users' devices by requiring consent for device access and storage. Limited exemptions exist 
for access that is required for transmission or where “strictly necessary” for provision of an ISS. This 
has resulted in absolutist interpretations which de facto has transformed the ePD regime in a “con-
sent-only” regime incoherent with other laws including the GDPR and the Data Act and incompatible 
with activities that are inherent to how the Internet works and how the Internet of Things economy 
can be developed.  Implemented into national law, the ePD allows Member States to establish addi-
tional rules, with the result that interpretation and enforcement vary among Data Protection Authori-
ties (DPAs) and with Telecommunications Regulators. 
Status quo: The ePD is considered outdated and burdensome, lacking balance with other rights and 
interests. It overlaps with the GDPR and the Data Act, leading to contradictions and inefficiencies 
and, in the case of the Data Act, impossibility to achieve its legislative goals. Key issues include: 
• Consent absolutism 
• Excessive burden on companies, in particular regarding the collection of anonymous data or 
use cases with no/minimal privacy impact (e.g., analytics)  
• Impact on safety and integrity 
• Malfunctioning enforcement causing legal uncertainty 
• Contradictions with other EU legislations, notably the GDPR and the Data Act 
• Absolutist interpretation by data protection regulators 
• Fragmented construction and enforcement per Member State: different kind of regulators 
and no country-of-origin principle 
These challenges necessitate a reevaluation to ensure a balanced and effective regulatory environ-
ment that supports the policy and legislative purposes of EU Digital Strategy, including privacy pro-
tection and economic growth. 
With regards to the automotive sector, the outdated and unnecessarily strict approach based on 
general consent with very few exemptions leads to a severe impact on innovation capabilities. We 
estimate a loss of up to 80% of data relevant to further develop technology which is relevant for gen-
eral traffic safety and new mobility solutions. 
Proposed simplification: The ePrivacy Directive (ePD), last updated in 2009, does not reflect the real-
ities of today’s digital ecosystem. Article 5(3) - best known for its "cookie consent" rules - does not 
appreciate the essential role that cookies play in the digital era. It has led to distrust and excessive 
banner fatigue, without meaningfully enabling an informed choice. Furthermore, Article 5(3) raises a 
barrier to the development of privacy enhanced technologies and innovation that can contribute to a 
higher level of traffic safety. The overreliance of the cookie regime on consent is incompatible with 
the development of a digital economy and is not coherent with more modern data protection instru-
ments like the GDPR and the EU’s Data Act.

To address this, we propose that Article 5(3) be modernized by either (i) disapplying ePD 5(3) alto-
gether insofar as the GDPR also applies, or (ii) incorporating the full spectrum of legal bases under 
article 6 of the GDPR, rather than being limited to consent. This would enable a risk-based approach 
to data use through cookies and similar technologies, aligning protections with actual risk to data 
subjects. 
By aligning cookie rules with GDPR, the EU can: 
• Reduce compliance burdens on users and organizations, 
• Improve user experience by reducing unnecessary pop-ups, 
• Enable data-driven innovation in areas such advanced driver assistance systems and auton-
omous driving systems. 
At the very least, the ePrivacy Directive should create exemptions from its consent requirement for 
private innovations that, once deployed, also serve a public purpose such as traffic safety and new 
mobility solutions. 
2.2  General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) 
Status quo: it is important to outline that targeted and meaningful reforms of GDPR and especially 
its interpretation and implementation are vital to remove unnecessary hindering blocks for innova-
tion. Implementing the GDPR in practice often involves significant bureaucracy and legal uncer-
tainty, which can hinder the free flow of data and stifle innovation. 
Proposed simplification: To ensure the free movement of data while safeguarding fundamental 
rights, data protection rules should be critically reviewed and adapted with greater emphasis on the 
risk-based and proportionality principles embedded in the GDPR. Many practical problems stem 
from inconsistent and severely unbalanced interpretation and enforcement by supervisory authori-
ties, which urgently needs to be addressed. The GDPR itself also offers significant potential for im-
provement on a material level, for example in the areas of legal bases for processing, transparency 
obligations, and anonymisation criteria—issues that cannot be fully resolved through secondary leg-
islation alone. The aim of any reform should be to achieve effective data protection without lowering 
standards or fundamentally altering the established regulatory framework. 
(a) Risk-Based Approach 
A keyway to genuinely ease the burden on businesses is to reform the principles governing data pro-
tection processes. Most importantly, the risk-based approach should be firmly established as the 
guiding principle of the GDPR. This would mean that obligations for data controllers are not imposed 
in a blanket manner but are instead tailored to the actual need for protection in each specific case. 
For example, if personal data is only a minor by-product of processing, or if the focus is purely on 
technical information, extensive information and documentation requirements should not be neces-
sary. Currently, however, the GDPR must be fully observed even when there is no real risk to individu-
als, tying up resources, increasing costs, and stifling innovation - without any clear benefit to data 
protection. A clearer definition of ‘personal data’ in Article 4(1) GDPR would help focus protection 
where it is truly needed and relieve companies whose aim is not to use personal data, but rather 
technical insights. Such clarification would make the GDPR more practical and efficient. 
For intra-group data transfers, an exception should be made so that such transfers are treated as in-
ternal processing, not as disclosure to third parties.

The GDPR is based on a risk-based approach, aiming for a fair balance between the risk of data pro-
cessing and the necessary safeguards. Only by properly considering the degree of risk to individuals 
can appropriate solutions be found for the wide range of situations covered by the GDPR. However, 
this approach is not consistently applied in regulatory practice. It is essential to give greater weight 
to the varying risks of different processing activities when assessing data protection issues. This ap-
plies especially to special categories of personal data under Article 9 GDPR which should also ur-
gently be reviewed.  
We do not consider it appropriate to link data protection privileges strictly to the number of employ-
ees of a company. Larger companies may also carry out routine processing with minimal risk, while 
small firms can process highly sensitive data on a large scale. Therefore, compliance requirements 
should depend on the actual risks of the processing activities. 
Finally, the current proposal will only provide limited relief for SMEs in practice. Larger companies, 
which are not covered by the exemptions, will still need to demand full documentation from their 
service providers and partners—even if those partners qualify for exemptions - in order to meet their 
own accountability obligations under Article 5(2) GDPR. As a result, practical benefits will remain 
limited. 
(b) Legal Bases 
Ensuring that the legal bases for data processing under the GDPR are practical is crucial—not only 
for the survival and growth of data-driven business models, but for all business activities in the digi-
tal age. Currently, these legal bases are increasingly reaching their limits when it comes to innovative 
technologies and data-intensive applications, leading to significant legal uncertainty that hampers 
both analogue and digital innovation in Germany and across the EU. 
Although Article 6 GDPR sets out several equal legal grounds for processing personal data, in prac-
tice, data protection authorities often prioritise consent. Consent has become the standard mecha-
nism for legitimising data processing, but it is not always legally or practically suitable - especially in 
situations with complex or evolving processing contexts or low-risk data uses. The scope and re-
quirements for consent should be made more realistic and practical, with clearer rules on its effec-
tiveness. Differentiating requirements based on processing risk would better reflect actual protec-
tion needs and strike a fairer balance between data protection and usability. This includes clarifying 
the rules for consent under Article 9(2)(a) GDPR, for example by providing standard examples of ac-
ceptable processing purposes, and considering whether low-risk processing activities could be ex-
empt from consent requirements altogether. 
The ‘legitimate interests’ clause in Article 6(1)(f) GDPR is vital for businesses across all sectors. How-
ever, some authorities are narrowing its scope inappropriately, and the balancing test for legitimate 
interests is often fraught with uncertainty, hindering day-to-day business and innovation. To prevent 
undue restriction, Recital 47 should clarify that processing for commercial purposes can generally be 
considered a legitimate interest, and that direct marketing for customer acquisition is also covered. 
Further processing of personal data for purposes other than those originally agreed upon is another 
area of legal uncertainty. Article 6(4) GDPR states that such processing depends on the compatibility 
of the new purpose with the original one, but it remains unclear whether a separate legal basis is 
needed even when purposes are compatible. The intended privileges for further processing for ar-
chiving in the public interest, scientific or historical research (Article 5(1)(b) GDPR) have not yet pro-
vided meaningful relief for businesses, due to a lack of clarity on when such processing is ‘not in-
compatible’ with the original purpose. This uncertainty is a significant barrier. Clear rules are needed

when a separate legal basis is required for further processing, and more detailed guidance is needed 
on the requirements for privileged processing under Article 89(1) GDPR. In particular, it is unclear 
what constitutes ‘appropriate safeguards’ under this article. 
Finally, in light of new EU digital regulations, exceptions may be needed for certain processing activi-
ties, such as those related to data sharing obligations under the EU Data Act. 
(c) Data Subject Rights 
Transparency in data processing is essential for strengthening citizens’ data sovereignty. However, 
the information and access obligations under Articles 13, 14, and 15 GDPR apply equally to all con-
trollers and processing scenarios. To ease the burden on businesses, there must be more practical 
ways to handle data subject rights. Responding to access requests- especially under Article 15 - is 
often highly resource-intensive and can result in disproportionate effort, with limited benefit for the 
individual. Excessive information can reduce clarity and usability, and studies show that fewer peo-
ple are reading privacy notices since the GDPR came into force. Modular, standardised privacy no-
tices with icons—originally considered during the legislative process (see Recital 60 GDPR)—would 
be a helpful solution. 
To manage clearly unfounded or excessive requests more effectively, legal clarifications, deadlines, 
limitations, or standard procedures should be introduced. For example, individuals should be re-
quired to specify which data or processing activities their request concerns; vague or blanket re-
quests should be rejectable. Information obligations should also be reviewed to strike a better bal-
ance between user rights and administrative feasibility. 
A tiered approach to information delivery should be legally established, especially regarding the for-
mat and timing. Where limited formats are used (e.g. phone-based data collection), it should be le-
gally permissible to provide only core information initially, with further details made available elec-
tronically—provided the individual is clearly informed at the time of collection. This approach is al-
ready reflected in Recital 58 GDPR and the Article 29 Working Party’s transparency guidelines, but 
inconsistent implementation calls for formal legal anchoring, potentially via an amendment to Article 
12 or Recital 58. 
Additionally, Article 13 GDPR should be expanded - similar to Article 14(5)(b)—to include an exemp-
tion for cases of ‘disproportionate effort’. Even in direct data collection, there are scenarios (e.g. brief 
business calls or in-person meetings) where providing full information is impractical and purely for-
malistic. 
Also, the concept of “abuse of the legal rights” should be introduced as we see increasing cases 
where data subject rights are being used as a tool to achieve other purposes outside of data protec-
tion; mostly to put pressure on the controller by increasing costs in order to achieve monetary or 
other non-privacy related gains. 
Finally, it should be clarified that the right to a copy under Article 15(3) GDPR refers only to the per-
sonal data being processed—not to full copies of underlying documents or files. 
(d) Enforcement 
As part of a reform of data protection enforcement, the role of supervisory authorities should be 
modernised. In addition to safeguarding personal data, their responsibilities should also include sup-
porting the free flow of data—both within the EU and, with appropriate safeguards, beyond—as well 
as promoting innovation and the competitiveness of data-driven businesses. A broader, more bal-
anced mandate would help ensure a fair equilibrium between data protection and other fundamental

freedoms. Under Article 57(1)(e) and (f) GDPR, authorities should not only support individuals but 
also advise controllers and processors. 
The role of the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) should also evolve. In the absence of con-
sistent case law on key GDPR issues, the EDPB’s guidelines and opinions are vital for practical, busi-
ness-friendly implementation. However, its decision-making processes are often severely unbal-
anced and lack expertise. This is partially due to the severe lack of stakeholder involvement—contra-
dicting the right to good administration under Article 41 of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights. 
To improve transparency, the process for developing guidelines and recommendations should re-
quire early and ongoing stakeholder involvement—through public consultations or expert dia-
logues—before drafts are published. This would improve quality, increase acceptance, and enhance 
practical implementation. The EDPB should also shift from a primarily reactive body to a proactive, 
innovation-friendly partner. A proposed “EDPB Innovation Forum” could institutionalise dialogue be-
tween regulators, businesses, and researchers to support legally compliant innovation and guide 
emerging technologies. 
To support this, we propose targeted amendments to the GDPR: 
• Article 70(4): 
“The Board shall consult relevant stakeholders and give them the opportunity to comment 
within a reasonable timeframe. This consultation must take place before the adoption of 
opinions, guidelines, recommendations, or best practices. The Board shall duly consider the 
input and publish the results, including a summary and explanation of how the feedback was 
taken into account. ” 
• Article 58(3)(k): 
“On its own initiative or upon request, adopt guidelines, recommendations, and best prac-
tices to help controllers and processors comply with the GDPR. Before adoption, the author-
ity shall consult relevant stakeholders and take their views into full consideration. ” 
• New Article 58(3a): 
“In exercising the powers under paragraph 3, the supervisory authority shall take into ac-
count relevant case law and interpretations of this Regulation by EU institutions and bodies. ” 
(e) Strengthen the Role of the EU Commission 
As one of the most important measures to be taken to address severe implementation issues related 
to GDPR, the role of the European Commission needs to be strengthened; especially related to cross-
border cases—and to ensure a consistent, balanced, and innovation-friendly data protection frame-
work. For that purpose, the Commission needs to be empowered to issue binding rules through im-
plementing acts and introduce standard templates for data processing agreements. By that, key as-
pects of general importance of GDPR implementation or sector specific relevant interpretations of 
the law in key areas of innovations could be funneled into a reliable process that respects the bal-
anced approach the lawmaker has intended without over-representation of one single fundamental 
right outweighing others. 
Furthermore, the Commission should take a leading role in coordinating the various legal instru-
ments under the EU Digital Strategy, which currently lack sufficient alignment. A central coordina-
tion body at EU level could provide guidance on how these regulations interact, helping ensure co-
herence and legal certainty across the digital landscape.

2.3  Cybersecurity 
Avoid Cybersecurity double-regulation and simplify certification 
Status quo: With several horizontal regulations like CSA, CRA and NIS-2 the regulatory landscape of 
Cybersecurity has increased tremendously. Additionally, several harmonized European standards 
(hENs) have been defined and are under negotiation. Beside the horizontal regulation there are verti-
cal, i.e. industry-related regulations like Radio Equipment Directive (EU) 2014/53, (EU) 2018/1139 
and (EU) 2019/2144 which cover cybersecurity and corresponding certification of those systems 
and components. This increasingly complex situation makes it hard for the relevant industry to en-
sure compliance by understanding the different scope statements, interrelationships and interde-
pendencies between the horizontal, vertical regulations and hENs in the right way. 
In 2019, the European Cyber Security Act (EU CSA) was enacted with a view to strengthen the EU 
Agency for cybersecurity (ENISA) and introduces an EU-wide cybersecurity certification framework 
for ICT products, services and processes. While the EU Common Criteria Scheme (EUCC) has been 
finalised, the EU Member States still could not agree on the criteria to be integrated into the EU 
Cloud Scheme (EUCS). Since the enactment of the CRA, and thereby mandatory cybersecurity re-
quirements for all products with digital elements that are placed on the European market, the EU 
CSA’s cybersecurity certification schemes have become obsolete. 
Proposed simplification: The Volkswagen Group strongly recommends limiting the complexity of Cy-
bersecurity-related regulation and certification approaches to the minimum. Systems and compo-
nents provided by suppliers who can prove that they have implemented and follow the vertical, in-
dustry-related regulation and certification and thus fulfil the technical specifications and cybersecu-
rity measures and processes for their systems and components in accordance with the relevant 
standards are not subject of horizontal Regulations. Otherwise, systems, components and separate 
technical units designed and constructed would be subject to the requirements of the horizontal 
Regulation. 
Withdraw product-group-specific cybersecurity certification schemes of the European Cyber Secu-
rity Act (EU CSA). The Commission shall repeal EU CSA completely. All aspects regarding the estab-
lishment of ENISA should be addressed in a new ENISA Act. The number of hEN to be defined for EU 
CRA shall be considerably reduced. Instead, established and existing standards from vertical, indus-
try-related regulation shall be considered. 
Radio Equipment Directive (RED) Delegated Regulation: Withdraw the Act

integrate cybersecurity requirements seamlessly, reduce regulatory complexity, and enhance overall

NIS 2 Directive: EU-wide harmonisation of implementation 
Status quo: Since the co-legislators opted for a Directive as the regulatory instrument when drafting 
NIS 2, the implementation of its requirements varies significantly across the EU’s 27 Member States. 
This has led to divergent approaches in areas such as entity classification (ranging from single- to 
three-tier systems), sectoral scope (with some Member States expanding coverage), and size-cap 
thresholds. As a result of this regulatory fragmentation, organisations may face different compliance 
obligations across jurisdictions—even when offering identical services. Therefore, compliance with 
NIS 2 in one Member State does not automatically ensure compliance in another. 
Proposed Simplification: The directive-based approach requires companies to navigate differing na-
tional interpretations and implementation timelines. This includes inconsistent definitions of scope, 
varying security frameworks, different reporting thresholds, and diverging supply chain require-
ments—creating significant complexity for cross-border cybersecurity management and compliance 
monitoring. To reduce implementation costs for businesses and ensure a risk-appropriate level of 
cyber resilience across the Union, all Member States should implement the NIS 2 Directive in a har-
monised manner, avoiding additional national requirements. This should be supported by centralised 
guidance to ensure a pragmatic and consistent interpretation of key terms, such as “establishment. ” 
Furthermore, the “main establishment” principle should be extended to other highly interconnected 
sectors, such as transport. To further harmonise cybersecurity obligations for entities, the European 
Commission should consider adopting a Regulation when revising the NIS 2 Directive. 
NIS 2 Directive: Reporting obligations (Article 23) 
Status Quo: Article 23 of the NIS 2 Directive mandates that all essential and important entities report 
any cybersecurity incident that has a significant impact on their services. In the event of such an inci-
dent, organisations are required to submit between three and five separate reports. The initial notifi-
cation must be submitted within 24 hours of becoming aware of the incident, followed by a more de-
tailed report within 72 hours. Upon request by a CSIRT or, where appropriate, the competent author-
ity, an interim report may be required to provide status updates. Additionally, a final report must be 
submitted no later than one month after the initial notification. If the incident is still ongoing at the 
time of the final report, the organisation must submit a progress report and then a final report within 
one month of resolving the incident. 
Proposed Simplification: Timely sharing of information on current cyberattack vectors is essential to 
strengthening Europe’s cyber resilience. However, incident reporting will only contribute meaning-
fully to this goal if the data submitted to national authorities is actively analysed and integrated into 
a real-time cybersecurity situational overview. The current requirement of submitting up to five re-
ports per incident is overly bureaucratic. Furthermore, the inconsistent definitions of reportable inci-
dents across Member States—some requiring reports beyond significant incidents, others applying 
different criteria for cross-border impact—force companies to implement extensive monitoring sys-
tems and maintain country-specific incident response procedures. This increases resource demands 
and elevates compliance risks.

To reduce administrative burden without compromising cybersecurity, the Volkswagen Group calls 
for harmonised incident reporting under NIS 2 across all EU Member States. Specifically, the number 
of mandatory reports per incident should be limited to three: 
1. Initial Report within 48 hours, containing only basic information (e.g. company name and vis-
ible impact). 
2. Intermediary Report only upon request by the national authority. 
3. Final Report one month after resolution, detailing the incident, its cause, mitigation 
measures, and any cross-border effects. 
This streamlined approach would lower bureaucratic costs, allowing companies to focus resources 
on incident response, prevention, and detection. Standardised templates and clear definitions would 
further simplify the process and ultimately strengthen Europe’s overall cyber resilience. 
NIS 2 Directive: Critical components (Article 24 paragraphe 2)  
Status quo: In case of insufficient levels of cybersecurity, under NIS 2 Article 24 paragraph 2 the Eu-
ropean Commission is empowered to adopt delegated acts, in accordance with Article 38, to sup-
plement the NIS 2 Directive by specifying which categories of essential and important entities are to 
be required to use certain certified ICT products, ICT services and ICT processes or obtain a certifi-

tion (EU) 2019/881. This could lead to overlaps with the horizontal requirements under the CRA.  
Proposed simplification: The CE marking according to CRA should be recognised as a sufficient re-
quirement for ICT products under NIS-2. 
NIS 2 Directive: Simplify requirements for affiliated companies  
Status quo: Currently, affiliated companies within a corporate group are treated like independent 
companies in the open market under NIS2. 
Proposed simplification: If a company offers services that are regulated under NIS2 (Annex I No.8 
Digital Infrastructure) in conjunction with EU Implementing Regulation 2024/2690 exclusively to af-

from the requirements of EU Implementing Regulation 2024/2690. 
2.4  Artificial Intelligence 
The AI Act should be simplified by shifting to sector specific regulation of Industrial AI by excluding 
Annex I. The Implementation Deadlines for Annex I and Annex III should be extended by at least 24 
months starting with the implementation of the delegated acts. Transition periods should be linked 
to the availability of harmonised European standards. The GPAI Code of Practice needs to become 
more user oriented by revising the thresholds for systemic risk and avoid an inappropriate shifting of 
responsibilities from model providers to finetuners and users of these models. The AI Act should 
specify under which conditions finetuned models may be exempt from certain requirements, using 
clear definitions and thresholds. The AI Act should ensure that its transparency and data protection 
requirements are consistent with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

AI Act: Implementation Deadlines for Annex I and Annex III 
Status quo: The implementation of the AI Act is phased, with various provisions becoming applicable 
between February 2, 2025, and August 2, 2027. High-risk AI systems under Annex I must comply 
within 36 months from the entry into force of the AI Act, i.e., by August 1, 2027. High-risk AI systems 
under Annex III must comply within 24 months from the entry into force of the AI Act, i.e., by August 
1, 2026. The standardization of the high-risk requirements is progressing much more slowly than 
originally planned. However, the availability of high-quality standards is essential for a practical and 
innovation-friendly implementation of the AI Act. Furthermore, a clarification is needed whether the 
AI Act related standardization is applicable on AI systems falling under Art. 6 Para. 2 AI Act or not. If 
the current deadlines for the high-risk requirements are kept too, many companies will postpone in-
vestments in high-risk AI systems, as the legal uncertainty is too great without standards being final-
ized in time. In addition, we see slow progress in establishing competent authorities at national level, 
in clarifying various points in the AI Act that require interpretation (high-risk definition, AI literacy, 
transparency requirements, materiality threshold for changes to AI systems, etc.) and in clarifying the 
interaction with other horizontal and sectoral legislation. 
Proposed simplification: We propose an extension of the implementation deadlines for the high-risk 
requirements of the AI Act by 24 months each for Annex I and Annex III. AI Act: Legislative Interplay 
with horizontal legislation. 
AI Act: Annex I + Recital 49  
Status quo: The overlapping requirements and potential conflicts between the AI Act, the Data Act, 
and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) can create legal uncertainty and increase the 
compliance burden for companies. For example, the GDPR sets out comprehensive data protection 
obligations which apply to AI as well. 
Proposed simplification: The AI Act should ensure that its transparency and data protection require-
ments are consistent with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). This would provide clear 
guidelines for companies on how to handle personal data in AI systems without conflicting with 
GDPR obligations. We also call for regulatory clarification in the interpretation of cases involving the

2.5  Data economy 
General remark: The Data Act has the potential to serve as a powerful framework and enable the 
joint European Data Market. The Data Acts core provisions - such as the definitions governing access 
to data from connected products, the obligations for manufacturers and service providers, and the 
rules for data sharing (including financial compensation) - must not lead to legal uncertainty by over-
lapping with/overriding existing horizontal or vertical legislation. 
Renewable Energy Directive III (RED III): Repeal Article 20a (3) (EU) 2018/2001 amended by Di-
rective (EU) 2023/2413 of 18 October 2023 
Status quo: Art. 20a paragraph 3 of the Renewable Energy Directive brings new and significant re-
quirements which have partly been laid down by previous Regulation, e. g. the EU Data Act. This in-
troduces an unclear scope of requirements for manufacturers and confuses customers and third par-
ties in many ways, creating legal uncertainty for all European data market participants. Additionally, 
Art. 20a RED contains a deviant compensation regime to the one of the Data Act. Moreover, since 
this is a directive, a worst-case scenario could be 27 different national implementations, each with 
varying obligations.  
Proposed simplification: If new requirements regarding data provisioning of Battery Management 
System data were to be introduced, this should be done by changing (EU) 2023/1542, EUBR, whilst 
adhering to the Data Act with respect to governance and compensation regimes.  
Further, sufficient lead time has to be allowed, taking into consideration that various horizontal and 
sectoral legislations with respect to data have recently been introduced with a considerable impact 
on large, medium and small businesses as well as Startups in one of Europe’s key industries: Auto-
motive.

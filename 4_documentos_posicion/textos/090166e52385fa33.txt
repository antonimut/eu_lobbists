Introduction 
DOT Europe welcomes the opportunity to contribute to the European Commission’s call for evidence 
on the Digital Package. In line with the Draghi Report and the Communication on “A Simpler and Faster 
Europe”, we believe it is essential to stress that consistent application of rules and greater legal clarity 
are fundamental for Europe’s competitiveness. Over the past years, landmark legislations such as the 
GDPR, the DSA, the Data Act, and the AI Act have crea ted a dense and sometimes fragmented 
regulatory environment in the digital sphere , with uneven national implementation and divergent 
approaches to interpretation and oversight. At the same time, technology continues to evolve rapidly, 
requiring Europe to reassess its digital rulebook to ensure it remains fit -for-purpose and supports , 
rather than hinders, innovation, growth, and competitiveness. 
DOT Europe has actively contributed to numerous consultations, including those linked to the AI 
Continent Action Plan, the ApplyAI Strategy, Single Market Strategy and the Data Union Strategy, in 
order to bring forward the perspective of our members. In line with the Call for Evidence’s structure, 
we have structured our response around 2 key areas: the data framework, including  dedicated 
sections to the ePrivacy Directive, and international data flows , and the AI framework. As requested 
by the European Comm ission, the last part of the document will take a broader and more practical 
approach and will provide concrete recommendations on how the digital acquis could be simplified in 
order to ensure less administrative burden and more proportionality and legal clarity. Our aim is to 
support the European Commission in ensuring that Europe’s regulatory environment is coherent, 
predictable, and innovation-ready, while fully reflecting European values and protecting fundamental 
rights. 
Data Framework  
DOT Europe was particularly concerned by some proposals in the Data Union Strategy questionnaire 
(Section 3, question 4 on the DGA and question on the ODD) that would impose a different regime for 
non-EU companies, VLOPs (under the DSA) and gatekeepers (under the DMA)  for re-using public 
sector data compared to European businesses, under the Data Governance Act and the Open Data 
Directive. Such an approach would create unjustified and discriminatory barriers, denying some

businesses in the EU. These companies are integral to the digital ecosystem, offering widely used 
services that enhance daily life and economic activity across Member States. 
Discriminating between specific actors in the data economy based on size is in tension with the EU’s 
commitment to a n evidence- and risk-based regulation and fair and open digital single market, but

Moreover, the European Commission plans to set up an EU standard repository for interoperability in 
cloud, SaaS, PaaS, and edge services by September 2025. This move lacks evidence of market failure, 
demonstrated added value, or sufficient stakeholder consultation. Man datory compliance would 
impose significant costs, divert resources from innovation, and create barriers for SMEs and start-ups, 
while limiting flexibility and potentially leading to EU -specific “second -tier” services with little 
customer benefit. Instead, the Data Act allows for a discretionary approach. A phased, evidence-based 
implementation, starting with voluntary standards and informed by thorough consultation and impact 
assessment, would better align with industry practice, support innovation, and still meet regulatory 
objectives. 
Finally, the Data Governance Act requires data intermediation service providers to establish separate 
legal entities for their services, aiming to ensure neutrality and prevent conflicts of interest. However, 
this rigid structural separation lacks evidence of necess ity, imposes heavy financial and operational 
burdens, and disrupts efficient integrated service models. Establishing new entities duplicates 
infrastructure, personnel, and compliance obligations without guaranteeing neutrality. Instead, the 
same objectives could be achieved through less burdensome measures such as transparent pricing, 
internal controls, technical safeguards, and clear contractual terms. Replacing structural separation 
with operational and transparency requirements would reduce unnecessary b arriers, foster 
innovation, and provide a more predictable compliance environment. 
If the EU aim to foster a thriving, competitive, and inclusive data economy, all actors must be allowed 
to compete on equal terms. The success of the Data Union depends on clear, coherent, and future -
proof rules that support innovation rather than constrain it. Open and  non-discriminatory access to 
public sector information should remain a foundational principle, ensuring all companies , regardless 
of origin or size, can contribute to Europe’s digital transformation. 
ePrivacy Framework  
Building on the need for a harmonised and non-discriminatory data framework, it is essential that the 
EU also addresses inconsistencies and inefficiencies within the current ePrivacy Directive (ePD). 
Originally adopted in 2002 and updated only incrementall y, the ePD has become a patchwork of 
overlapping provisions that regulate a diverse range of services and pursue conflicting policy 
objectives, from privacy and consumer protection to market regulation and law enforcement. This 
complexity contributes significantly to legal uncertainty and regulatory fragmentation, especially as it 
interacts inconsistently with the GDPR and other digital rules.  Taken together, this has made data 
protection rules unstable and unpredictable for many providers of digital services. 
 
 
 
 
 
3 
 
A clear opportunity exists to simplify and modernise the ePrivacy framework by streamlining its scope 
and aligning it more coherently with broader EU data protection and telecoms legislation. Specific 
provisions, such as those on billing, call blocking, subscriber directories, and call forwarding, would be 
more appropriately addressed under the European Electronic Communications Code (EECC). 
Provisions on data retention (Article 15) could be reconsidered in the context of a new, dedicated 
proposal and the ProtectEU Strategy. Crucially, the rules on the processing of traffic and location data 
(Articles 5-6 and 9), as well as those governing direct marketing (Article 13), could be absorbed in the 
GDPR, where they would benefit from a single, well-established legal framework. 
One of the most pressing issues within the current ePD is Article 5(3), often referred to as the "cookie 
rule." As it stands, the requirements for prior consent to access or store information on a user’s device 
are overly rigid . Crucially, they are out of step with technological advances  and what constitutes 
essential technology to provide a digital service today or is low risk to user privacy. This also burdens 
users with repetitive and intrusive cookie banners , contributing to widespread "consent fatigue"  
diminishing user engagement with digital privacy tools and undermining the transparency objective 
which is one of the key principles of the GDPR. 
DOT Europe proposes targeted amendments to Article 5(3) to create explicit and clear exemptions 
from the consent requirement for legitimate, low-risk purposes which currently require consent and 
cannot be deployed where users ‘reject all’ cookies . These should include uses essential to security, 
fraud prevention, product maintenance, software updates, contextual advertising,  measurement, 
frequency capping and aggregated analytics. By modernising the legal basis for such uses, the EU can 
strengthen trust, enhance user experience, and reduce compliance burdens, especially for SMEs that 
rely on these tools to maintain service functionality and improve usability. A further route for targeted 
simplification of Art.5(3) would be to  integrate Article 5.3 in to GDPR and  issue targeted regulatory 
guidance to clarify that, when data is collected via cookies or similar technologies, Art. 5(3) no longer 
applies and data  may be processed using any of the legal bases set out in GDPR Arts 6 and 9 (as 
applicable). Since most high -risk processing using cookies or similar technologies will inherently 
involve personal data, the GDPR provides a more suitable, modernized regulatory framework.   
Importantly, we also recommend clarifying that cookies not requiring consent can be omitted from 
banners to  ensure greater user attention on uses which require a consent decision and reduce 
confusion . Information on essential cookie uses can then be provided in an alternative, accessible 
form such as in a privacy policy.  However, this clarification should not be interpreted as a general 
solution to the issue of cookie banners, particularly for services that rely on advertising to support 
their offerings. Instead, it should be seen as a targeted fix aligned with the scope o f existing 
exemptions.  
In addition, the current framework fails to create meaningful market incentives for investing in 
privacy-enhancing technologies. Since businesses must obtain consent regardless of whether they use 
a privacy-preserving technology or not, there is little commercial rationale to choose the more privacy-
friendly option , particularly as such privacy technologies often  come with higher implementation 
costs. As a result, companies may default to cheaper, more intrusive technologies, even when 
alternatives exist. A  smarter regulatory design that reduces consent obligations when privacy 
technologies are used, with the appropriate and necessary safeguards for users,  could correct this 
imbalance. It would open the way to investment in solutions that preserve data utility while enhancing 
privacy. For users, this would mean stronger privacy protections by default, fewer intrusive prompts, 
and greater confidence that their data is handled responsibly where these technologies are used. 
 
 
 
 
 
4 
 
Finally, the provision on the confidentiality of communications content (Article 5(1)) should also be 
adapted to today’s reality of increasingly diverse and competitive communications services. 
Automated processing for user-benefiting functionalities, such as translation, accessibility features, or 
cyber protection, should be permitted under appropriate safeguards, while data -at-rest should be 
clearly governed by the GDPR. 
In short, a reformed ePrivacy framework should be leaner, more coherent, and functional for how 
digital services operate today  with a focus on a risk-based approach which balances innovation with 
the protection of user privacy rights. By aligning its provisions with the GDPR and other relevant 
legislation, and by introducing proportionate, risk-based exemptions, the EU can reduce 
fragmentation, increase legal certainty, and ensure that European users and businesses alike can 
benefit from secure, innovative, and user-centric digital services. 
Data flows 
A functional European data framework must be complemented by a robust and pragmatic strategy 
for international data flows. Just as fragmented domestic rules hinder the EU’s digital economy, rigid 
or unclear cross-border data transfer rules threaten its global competitiveness, particularly in strategic 
sectors like AI, cloud computing, and industrial digitisation. 
Seamless and secure data flows are essential for developing, deploying, and scaling European 
technologies. In practice, the distinction between personal and non -personal data is increasingly 
blurred, especially in AI training, edge computing, and complex d ata ecosystems. A successful Data 
Union must therefore adopt a holistic, risk -based approach to international transfers , one that 
protects user rights while enabling cross-border innovation. 
Proportionate safeguards 
The GDPR’s current framework for international transfers , relying heavily on Standard Contractual 
Clauses (SCCs) and Transfer Impact Assessments (TIAs) , has become overly complex following the 
Schrems II ruling. Businesses are often required to assess foreign legal systems based on hypothetical 
risks, a burden that disproportionately affects SMEs and diverts resources from meaningful privacy 
protections. 
To restore legal clarity and promote data-driven growth, two key reforms are essential: 
1. Self-Certification for Intra-Group Transfers 
A self-certification regime under Article 46 GDPR would allow multinational organisations to 
commit to EU-level data protection standards, supported by technical and organisational 
safeguards. This would reduce the need for repetitive TIAs and streamline routine internal 
transfers  for corporate groups that have already implemented high, group-wide data 
protection standards. 
2. Risk assessments based on evidence 
TIAs should focus on the practical likelihood of unjustified access, grounded in verifiable 
evidence and precedent, rather than on purely theoretical risks. This pragmatic approach, 
already adopted in other jurisdictions, would align enforcement with real-world risks and 
support stable, secure data flows.

Legal certainty and trust 
While adequacy agreements like the EU-U.S. Data Privacy Framework are important, they cover only 
a small share of global data flows. Most transfers still depend on complex compliance mechanisms. 
Without clearer, harmonised guidance, businesses , especially smaller players, face legal uncertainty 
and rising costs. 
To address this, the European Commission should engage with stakeholders to develop practical tools 
and guidance, including: 
• Standardised templates for TIAs (including a dedicated TIA for high-risk process/flow); 
• Support for assessing non-adequate jurisdictions; 
• Common criteria for evaluating government access risks. 
Such initiatives would significantly reduce compliance burdens and reinforce Europe’s commitment 
to trusted, responsible innovation. 
The Data Act introduces important clarifications regarding international data transfers and 
government access to data, including safeguards to prevent unlawful governmental access from 
outside the EU and requirements to ensure that data transfers do not co nflict with EU or Member 
State law. However, significant uncertainty remains regarding the practical implementation of these 
provisions. The absence of harmonised procedures for cross -border law enforcement requests and 
the potential for divergent interpre tations among Member States continue to create compliance 
challenges for technology providers operating in Europe. Further guidance from the Commission, and 
where possible the establishment of a single, predictable standard for government access to data 
when it comes to both personal and non -personal data flows, would help maintain trust, ensure 
cybersecurity, and support the competitiveness of international cloud and platform services in the EU. 
 AI Act Framework  
DOT Europe strongly supports the objectives of the AI Act. At the same time, its implementation 
must be carefully managed to enable innovation rather than unintentionally hinder it. Without a 
pragmatic approach, there is a risk of creating regulatory obligations that increase costs, generate 
uncertainty, and slow down Europe’s ability to compete globally in AI. To this end, we recommend 
several targeted simplification measures that would make the AI Act more effective, workable, and 
aligned with Europe’s broader digital ambitions including promoting the adoption of AI. 
Simplified ecosystem for AI development and digital innovation 
To effectively support AI development and adoption, the EU’s legislative framework must become 
significantly more coherent, predictable, and proportionate. Currently, businesses face a fragmented 
patchwork of overlapping requirements and inconsistent enforcement and interpretation across 
instruments including the GDPR, Data Act, AI Act, ePrivacy Directive, and sector -specific rules. This 
legal complexity increases compliance burdens, undermines legal certainty, and makes it harder for 
digital services to scale across the Single Market. 
As the European Commission prepares its Digital Omnibus , it is important to recognise the evolving 
data needs for AI training, particularly the value of access to diverse and high -quality datasets to 
 
 
 
 
 
6 
 
develop AI to benefit European consumers and businesses, particularly access to multilingual data. 
Facilitating responsible and innovation -friendly data access, while fully respecting privacy, security, 
and intellectual property rights, can help ensure tha t AI systems developed in Europe are 
representative of its linguistic and cultural diversity. Careful consideration should be given to avoid 
unintended barriers that could limit the ability of developers to contribute to the development of AI 
solutions that benefit European consumers and businesses. Ongoing dialogue with stakeholders and 
regular assessment of the impact of data access provisions will be essential to strike the right balance 
between enabling innovation and upholding fundamental rights. 
Simplification and regulatory coherence must be treated as strategic priorities for the EU. This does 
not mean weakening protections; it means focusing regulatory efforts where they are most needed, 
eliminating duplication, and fostering a technology-neutral, scalable legislative environment. With the 
Digital Package approaching, the EU has a crucial opportunity to build a future -proof governance 
framework that empowers innovation while maintaining trust and accountability across the Single 
Market. 
Finally, simplification efforts relating to the GDPR  should be done through guidance for better 
implementation, and should ensure effective and consistent enforcement, rather than  reopening or 
revisiting the core text of the GDPR.  Implementation should be simplified for all companies rather 
than focusing on SMEs and SMCs alone, who, despite their size may still engage in high-risk processing 
and can be subject to vulnerabilities. This is essential to avoid fragmentation, ensure legal clarity and 
uphold strong data protection standards across the entire digital economy. 
Implementation and standards 
 
The effectiveness of the AI Act depends heavily on the timely availability of harmonised EU standards. 
These standards will guide providers and deployers in demonstrating compliance with core 
requirements. However, with the current timeline suggesting that the first  EU standards will not be 
ready before mid -2026, forcing implementation earlier would leave developerwithout a reliable 
framework for conformity. This would inevitably lead to fragmented, inconsistent approaches and 
higher compliance risks, particularly for SMEs. DOT Europe therefore calls for the implementation of 
all provisions of the  AI Act that rely on standards  to be delayed by at least one yearafter relevant 
standards become available. This would ensure that compliance efforts are meaningful, harmonised, 
and supportive of Europe’s broader goal of building a competitive AI ecosystem.  Any impacts for 
downstream firms should be addressed at the same time. 
 
Streamlined implementation and oversightand international alignment 
 
The AI Act sets out several transparency obligations for AI systems, including watermarking and 
labelling of synthetic content. While the AI Office will play a role in developing codes of practice to 
support these obligations, the current text leaves open questions on who will ultimately enforce them. 
Leaving enforcement fragmented across national authorities risks diverging interpretations, creating 
an uneven playing field across Member States. Article 82 (Compliant AI Systems Which Present a Risk)  
provides that if a high -risk AI system is compliant but still poses risks to health, safety, fundamental 
rights, or public interests, market surveillance authorities may require the operator to promptly take 
measures to eliminate those risks.  We believe that n ational authorities should not introduce 
additional enforcement or compliance rules, as this could lead to unchecked fragmentation and an 
expansion of the AI Act’s scope.  Centralising enforcement of transparency obligations for low-risk AI 
 
 
 
 
 
7 
 
systems within the AI Office would bring much -needed consistency, reduce administrative burden, 
and provide clarity for providers and deployers alike. 
 
At the same time, European AI regulation does not exist in isolation. Companies operating in global 
markets already engage with international initiatives such as those developed by the OECD and the 
G7. Ensuring that the AI Act’s implementation is aligned w ith these frameworks is essential to avoid 
creating compliance complexity or discouraging global players from deploying AI in Europe.  A clear 
case for international alignment is the forthcoming Code of Practice under Article 50. The Code should 
be consistent with global standards and remain flexible in its approach to watermarking and labelling, 
allowing actors to adopt techniques that reflect their business models, the state of the art, and the 
specific risks posed by their AI systems.  By actively engaging in international standardisation 
discussions, the EU can reinforce its role as a leader while ensuring European businesses are not 
disadvantaged and supports transparency efforts as the necessary technology matures. 
A rational approach to exemptions 
The AI Act already contains specific exemptions based on company size, particularly to support SMEs 
and start -ups. While DOT Europe supports proportionality, we caution against the introduction of 
additional size-based carve-outs during the simplification process. The reality of the AI ecosystem is 
that it is highly interconnected: in Germany, for example, 95% of AI start-ups operate in B2B contexts, 
and across the EU, 63% of AI start-ups build on foundation models developed by larger companies. It 
is a central principle of the AI Act that there is a fair allocation of responsibility within the supply chain 
and that would be undermined by size-based exemptions. 
Furthermore, th e issue of size-based exemptions extends beyond the developers of foundational 
models. A vast ecosystem of European startups and established companies depends on fine -tuning 
these models for specialised industrial applications. If the foundational models are prevented from 
learning from European public data, their quality and relevance within the EU are severely diminished, 
which in turn undermines the entire European ecosystem that builds upon them and places domestic 
companies at a significant competitive disadvantage.Instead, simplification should be guided by risk. 
A risk-based approach across the value chain ensures that obligations are focused on use cases where 
real risks to rights or safety exist, while reducing unnecessary burdens for systems that pose minimal 
risk. This approach would create a more coherent and innovation -friendly framework without 
compromising safeguards. 
Refinements to risk classification 
 
A key area of simplification lies in clarifying and refining the classification of high -risk AI systems. 
Article 6 establishes criteria for determining high -risk applications, but in practice there is a risk of 
over-classification, capturing many systems t hat do not pose material risks. The forthcoming 
guidelines on Article 6(5) will therefore be decisive. These should include a comprehensive and 
practical list of examples , distinguishing between high -risk and non -high-risk use cases, giving 
companies legal certainty and supporting consistent application across Member States. 
 
Article 6(3) exceptions also require clarification. Many AI systems that have minimal impact on 
individuals, such as emotion recognition tools used purely for aggregated market research or 
advertising effectiveness, should not fall under high -risk obligations. At the same time, the scope of 
high-risk classification for emotion recognition should be narrowed to target genuinely problematic 
 
 
 
 
 
8 
 
uses, such as manipulative profiling.  Furthermore, when a provider of AI systems provides concrete 
justifications that its AI system does not pose a significant risk of harm to the health, safety or 
fundamental rights of natural persons per Article 6(3), it should not be required to register its system 
in the high-risk AI database per Article 49 . Furthermore, basic customer service applications that 
simply enable more natural interactions between users and AI should be excluded. Such refinements 
would strike a better balance between protecting individuals and enabling innovation in AI -driven 
services. 
We would encourage a more considered approach towards the delineation between developers, 
deployers and the grey area in between, so that the compliance burden does not become increasingly 
all-encompassing as the industry develops and every party is fine-tuning before deployment and ends 
up being categorised as a quasi-developer; and the significant inefficiency and operational burden this 
would create across the chain of parties involved. 
We would also urge reconsideration of the compliance burden on both the part of companies and the 
AI office that will come from registration requirements. This will likely create significant deployment 
delay in a fast developing market and mean that the EU is increasingly likely to lag behind in both the 
innovation and deployment space, and it is unclear what benefit such a process will yield in terms of 
mitigating risk. 
Taken together, these simplification measures would help the AI Act achieve its objectives while 
avoiding unnecessary complexity. By focusing on readiness of standards, centralised and consistent 
enforcement, alignment with international frameworks, risk-based simplification, and clear guidance 
on risk classification, the EU can create a regulatory environment that safeguards rights and trust while 
also enabling faster and more effective AI adoption across the economy. For Europe to remain 
competitive in the global AI race, simplification is not just desirable, it is essential.  
Policy Recommendations 
Beyond the scope of the immediate regulatory adjustments through the Digital Omnibus, DOT Europe 
wishes to share its practical recommendations for streamlining the digital acquis. We therefore wish 
to contribute to the broader discussion that will inform the upcoming Fitness Check with concrete and 
actionable proposals covering the simplification of existing rules;  improvements in implementation 
and governance at the EU and Member State level; and principles to improve the law-making process. 
These recommendations have been drafted by DOT Europe as part of a study called “Streamlining the 
EU Digital Rulebook” published in April this year.1  
Simplifying existing rules 
The EU digital rulebook has expanded substantially over the past 5 -10 years. The resulting complex 
regulatory framework has created significant overlaps, duplications and sources of friction across a 
range of obligations imposed on companies. We propose a number of measures broken down by 
different types of obligations. An important thread throughout this exercise is the joint use of 
documentation, audits, transparency obligations etc, including risk/impact assessments, when similar 
obligations exist across different pieces of legislation.

Improving implementation and governance 
At the EU level 
This section introduces recommendations to improve the transparency, accountability and quality of 
EU-level bodies with de facto rule -making powers (such as the EDPB, AI Board, EU Digital Services 
Board etc.) We recommend the following: 
• Introduce common statutory duties for all Member State digital regulators, EU-level regulator 
fora and the European Commission to have regard to the impact of regulation on competition, 
innovation, and growth, with a view to supporting the EU ’s competitiveness goals as well as 
to weigh and balance different fundamental rights amongst each other. 
• Introduce additional duties for all digital regulators, in cases of overlapping competences, to 
co-operate in order to:  
o Reach decisions that respect both laws/do not create conflicts among laws, 
avoid overlaps and do not create disproportionate burdens. 
o Ensure companies are not subject to multiple investigations into the same practices / 
behaviours by different regulators through implementation of appropriate 
coordination mechanisms. A useful example is the Data Act provision, which allows 
authorities to exercise their competence only if the individual company is not subject 
to enforcement proceedings by another competent authority. 
• Institute pre -implementation dialogues  between the European Commission and  Member 
States to ensure supervisory convergence and consistency in the interpretation of key 
provisions of new legislative acts to streamline implementation and enforcement at the 
national level. 
• Have political and legal ownership. Although EU -level bodies should be impartial and 
independent from policymakers, this does not mean that there should not be an 
accountability mechanism in place where they have regulatory decision-making powers. This 
includes being accountable to a judicial authority for their decisions. 
• Be required to proactively consult widely with stakeholders on draft opinions, decisions, 
guidelines, and their annual work programmes to inform their priorities and ensure guidance 
is proportionate, effective , practical  and workable , while being flexible enough to 
accommodate the diversity of digital businesses in the market today . This should include 
consultation with regulated entities, concurrent regulatory bodies and relevant academics. 
• Introduce procedural rules to allow for the exercise of the right of defence  by regulated 
entities and where relevant that should include a direct right of appeal and the right to be 
heard within a reasonable deadline. 
 
 
 
 
 
12 
 
• Introduce a requirement for oversight bodies to offer guidance and compliance support 
before enforcement actions . Guidance should be concise, concrete and relevant for 
practitioners. It should also be provided in a timely manner to coincide with the entry into 
effect of new regulatory frameworks. 
• Investigations under various legal instruments should be concluded within a reasonable 
timeframe to avoid prolonged legal uncertainty. 
At the Member State level 
Rule-making does not stop at adoption and publication in the Official Journal An often overlooked 
aspect of legislation is the implementation at the Member State level. Member States and/or national 
authorities should: 
• Carefully sequence implementation to ensure guidance precedes the entry into force of new 
rules and oversight bodies acquiring enforcement powers. 
• Ensure coordination to avoid different national regulators looking into the same practice 
multiple times. Again, a useful example is the Data Act provision, which allows authorities to 
exercise their competence only if the individual company is not subject to enforcement 
proceedings by another competent authority. 
• Introduce a requirement for oversight bodies to  develop guidance and compliance support 
before any enforcement actions. 
• Guidance should be concise, concrete and relevant for practitioners, as well as actionable and 
workable. 
• Foresee efficient and effective co -operation mechanisms between country supervisory 
authorities, especially in cross-border cases and where responsibilities intersect. 
• Codify the digital rulebook to work towards consistency, prevent further legal overlaps and

functioning of the policy-making system. These focus on ensuring coherence and a holistic look at new 
policy and proposed legislation to foresee and avoid overlaps and conflicts at an earlier stage in the 
process.

Conclusion 
Europe has an opportunity to ensure its digital framework empowers innovation while safeguarding 
trust and user rights. To achieve this, simplification and coherence must become strategic priorities. 
By addressing overlaps across horizontal legislations, clarifying enforcement responsibilities, and 
modernising outdated provisions such as elements of the GDPR and the ePrivacy framework, the EU 
can create a regulatory environment that supports global competitiveness while remaining firmly 
anchored in European values. 
DOT Europe stands ready to support the European Commission in this endeavour. We believe that a 
simplified, innovation-ready, and globally connected regulatory framework will not only strengthen 
Europe’s digital economy but also ensure that citizens and businesses can thrive in the digital age. 
The upcoming Digital Omnibus and Digital Fitness Check provide the right moment to make this 
vision a reality, and we look forward to contributing constructively to this process.

The Finnish Food and Drink Industries´ Federation (ETL), representing food industry in 
Finland, appreciates the opportunity to participate in the European Commission’s 
consultation on the simpliﬁcation of digital regulation. We strongly support the 
Commission’s efforts to streamline digital regulation and reduce unnecessary 
administrative burdens, particularly in the context of the food sector, where the 
regulatory framework has become disproportionately complex in relation to 
technological development. 
The European Commission has taken the right direction by launching broad initiatives to 
simplify regulation and reduce administrative burdens. This is absolutely necessary and 
commendable also in relation to digitalisation and data, as the current regulatory 
environment, such as GDPR, NIS2, CER, the AI Act and the Data Act, has become heavy and 
complex. Simpliﬁcation is essential to ensure legislation remains up to date and supports the 
competitiveness of Europe’s food system. Regulation should act as an enabler that supports 
investment, growth and sustainable production - not hinder them. 
Key messages from the Finnish Food and Drink Industries´ Federation: 
1. Proposals to streamline the GDPR
The GDPR should be revisited to ensure it does not hinder the smooth ﬂow and utilization of
data. Overly strict or unclear interpretations of the GDPR have, for example, led to situations
during the implementation of the EU Deforestation Regulation where necessary data sharing
within the value chain has been blocked. The scope of the GDPR should be limited in cases
where personal data processing is temporary and not linked to an identiﬁable individual (non
personal use of personal data -model). The assessment of sufficient pseudonymisation
remains unstructured and unclear. In the future, it should be sufficient to remove identiﬁers
from datasets so that, for a third party, the data would no longer be considered personal data.
2. Proposals to streamline the Data Act
The obligation to share device data outside the EEA under the Data Act should be
reconsidered due to the changed security landscape. Protection of trade secrets and
retention of data-driven expertise within European companies must be strengthened. A
harmonisation procedure similar to the GDPR should be implemented to avoid situations
where an IoT product is simultaneously subject to decisions and penalties from multiple
national authorities. The mandatory contract obligation in extensive supply chains should be
examined and clariﬁed. Articles 32 and 28(1)(b) of the Data Act should be removed due to
their redundancy with Chapter V of the GDPR.
3. Proposals to streamline the AI Act
The AI Act should be clariﬁed to ensure that European companies can beneﬁt from AI. Article
59 of the AI Act outlines the conditions under which the original purpose of personal data may
be deviated from within a secure testing environment, for example when developing AI
models for socially signiﬁcant purposes. These exemptions should be clariﬁed and
broadened to also encompass the development needs of companies. The roles and

responsibilities among providers, deployers, and downstream providers within extensive 
supply chains require clariﬁcation. The implementation of the more technical obligations of 
the AI Act should be deferred until standardised frameworks are established for companies, 
allowing them adequate time to incorporate these standards into their operations. The 
provision allowing market surveillance authorities to access the source code of an AI system 
(Article 74(13)) should be removed. 
4. Proposals to streamline NIS2 and CER cybersecurity regulations
Administrative burden on companies should be reduced by implementing the “once only” 
principle, where a single notiﬁcation suffices for all authorities (NIS2, CER, GDPR, CRA).
Reporting schedules and notiﬁcation requirements should be harmonised across the
regulatory frameworks.  Terminology and deﬁnitions in NIS2 and other cybersecurity
regulations should be aligned to make regulation clearer and easier to apply. A common
minimum grace period should be agreed upon, as most EU Member States have not yet
implemented the NIS2 Directive. Member States’ guidance and interpretation should also be
harmonised to ensure that internationally operating companies can operate under similar
practices regardless of the Member State to which they report. Voluntary, harmonised
templates should be developed for assessing cybersecurity in the supply chain to make the
process more efficient and less burdensome, especially for SMEs. Regarding risk
management obligations, particularly under the CER Directive, companies’ existing business-
driven risk management practices should be recognised and accepted.

egta – Contribution to the Call for Evidence on a Digital Omnibus 
 
egta is the international trade body of multiplatform TV and audio businesses. We represent 
more than 180 members in over 40 markets. Our mission is to contribute to the commercial 
sustainability of the media industry. We enable our members to flourish as they  connect 
brands with audiences. Through our unique network of experts, we foster collaboration and 
engage with policy makers. We drive the industry forward through cooperation, benchmarking 
and alignment on standards. egta is a knowledge-sharing and innovation hub for its members 
and the broader industry. 
egta welcomes the opportunity to contribute to the call for evidence on a Digital Omnibus 
proposal introduced by the European Commission . The Commission’s push for the 
modernisation and simplification of the complex EU’s digital framework represents a unique 
opportunity to improve the EU’s competitiveness, as well as the audiovisual and advertising 
industries. This exercise however must ensure that the proportionality and coherence of 
existing legislations are maintained, while providing for future -proof solutions guaranteeing 
business sustainability, privacy and user trust. 
 
I. Article 5(3) ePrivacy: Need for clarification  
The ePrivacy framework requires an urgent update to provide simplification and clarity for 
businesses while delivering user trust and privacy . The lack of agreement on the proposed 
ePrivacy regulation and ‘Cookie Pledge’ initiative highlighted the significant issues when it 
comes to tailoring a framework adapted to the needs of media companies in the digital era. 
This particularly applies to Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive, as the current interpretation 
of cookie consent rules has led to disproportionate administrative burdens and  “consent 
fatigue” affecting users, with a fragmented implementation and enforcement across Member 
States. Such burdens are especially detrimental to small and medium -sized enterprises 
(SMEs). At the same time, benefits for users remain limited, if at all. 
A solution would be to simplify consent requirements while maintaining safeguards to lighten 
the compliance burden without affecting user privacy. 
 
1) A risk-based approach aligned with GDPR 
The Digital Omnibus should aim to align ePrivacy more closely with the GDPR by adopting a 
similar risk-based approach when it comes to data processing. 
Such an approach would allow low -risk, technically necessary or privacy -friendly operations 
to be exempted from a generalised blanket requirement of consent. A flexible framework would 
thus improve balance and consistency between privacy and innovation, with proportionate 
obligations corresponding to the level of risk for users.

2) Lighter consent requirements and possible exemptions 
Another solution, that could be combined with a risk -based approach, would be to adopt a 
non-exhaustive whitelist of processing activities that would not de facto require consent from 
users. Such activities would only include innocuous and functionally necessary use cases, 
such as: 
- Audience measurement carried out by the media service and audience 
measurement carried out by an independent third party on behalf of the 
provider of the media service : performance monitoring , technical 
measurement and frequency capping,  essential for the media industry to 
be able to efficiently monetise their content 
- Core service delivery and access management (login, updates, service 
maintenance, accessibility features...) 
- Security and fraud prevention 
Lighter consent requirements would greatly increase our industry’s competitiveness, reducing 
compliance costs significantly as well as tackling “consent fatigue”, as it would allow users to 
focus only on meaningful privacy-related choices. At the same time, essential operations that 
qualify as low -risk for privacy could function under appropriate safeguards but without 
unnecessary burdens. The best example is audience measurement1, as it enables publishers 
and media services as a whole to understand their audiences and sustain their business 
models. 
 
II. Avoiding centralised consent 
The European Commission should however explicitly discourage the notion of centralised 
consent. Introducing browser-level consent mechanisms would put  control entirely in the 
hands of gatekeepers, leading to significant intermediation risks and depriving smaller players 
of any control over their services. 
Centralised consent would have a negative impact on publishers’ revenue streams, as well as 
ceding control to potential bad actors. This could inadvertently result in ill-advised user choices 
to the detriment of privacy and trust. 
Moreover, centralised consent would drastically disrupt the direct relationship between users 
and individual services, particularly crucial for SMEs and the media sector.  To sustain 
publishers and broadcasters’ business model, a bilateral relationship with customers needs to 
be maintained. 
Overall, the European Commission should avoid replicating the shortcomings that were 
already pointed out during the negotiation around the ‘Cookie Pledge’ initiative. Efficient 
consent should remain decentralised and service-specific in order to ensure transparency, 
accountability, and proportionate control, while offering a level-playing field for all actors in the 
digital ecosystem.

III. Conclusion 
The Digital Omnibus presents a timely opportunity to introduce some coherence and balance 
to the EU’s privacy framework. Adopting a risk-based approach would deliver a modernised 
framework that fosters innovation and competitiveness while maintaining strong privacy 
obligations. 
A revision of Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive, while necessary, should be accompanied 
by a comprehensive impact assessment, to properly assess the opportunities to improve 
privacy as well as the economic consequences of the proposed changes on the digital and 
media markets. 
Finally, in order to ensure legal certainty and good administration, a better and clearer 
coordination between the European Commission and the European Data Protection Board 
(EDPB) is crucial, as it would foster a consistent interpretation of the rules and enhance their 
proper enforcement across all Member States down the line.

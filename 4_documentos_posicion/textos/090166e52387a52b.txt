CALL FOR EVIDENCE ON A DIGITAL OMNIBUS: EBU POSITION 
--------------------------------- 
The European Broadcasting Union is the world’s leading alliance of public service media (PSM). The EBU has 
115 member organizations in 56 countries who operate nearly 2,000 television, radio and online channels and 
services and reach an audience of more than one billion people in 160 languages.  
PSM organizations are entrusted with the performance of a service of general economic interest, which 
consists, inter alia, of the provision of high-quality content that fulfils the cultural and democratic needs of the 
society they serve. 
--------------------------------- 
The European Broadcasting Union (EBU) welcomes the opportunity to answer the European 
Commission’s call for evidence on a Digital Omnibus proposal. 
 
Public Service Media (PSM) play a unique role in promoting democracy, cultural diversity, and 
social cohesion. They are strong supporters of the EU Digital rulebook and look forward to its full 
implementation to the benefit of EU citizens and businesses.  
 
We understand that the current call for evidence will be focusing on specific laws (Data Acquis, 
AI Act, Cybersecurity Act, ePrivacy rules), while inviting input for a future Digital Fairness Check 
aimed at  assessing the broader cumulative impact of digital rules, including data protection 
aspects. PSM support the Commission’s objective to address the complexity of current rules and 
facilitate their implementation while maintaining a strong protection for fundamental rights and 
citizens.  
 
We would like to submit the following observations to the attention of the European Commission 
with the objectives of ensuring proportionality and aligning the legal framework with the realities 
of PSM’s role as major content producers and disseminators in the digital era and public service 
institutions, while upholding fundamental rights.    
1. ePrivacy rules 
The current rules have led to complex compliance processes , as well as a fragmented 
implementation. The interpretation of cookie consent rules has also significantly evolved in the 
past years,1 which warrants an appropriate reflection about a possible simplification the current 
legal framework.  
 
a. Lighter consent requirements for specific use cases 
 
Ideally, existing ePrivacy rules should be better aligned and harmonised with the General 
Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).  
 
In particular, the risk-based approach of the GDPR could be more appropriately reflected 
by lightening certain consent requirements for specific low-risk uses cases, such as:

b. Risk of consent centralisation/intermediation  
 
A concept promoted in recent regulatory discussions on the Digital Omnibus, particularly 
around the simplification of privacy rules, is the centralisation of user consent 
management, e.g. through browsers. While simplifying how end users set their consent 
preferences is a commendable objective, it requires careful  assessment of the available 
technical solutions.  
 
We would like to underline that this idea is not new and it has already been considered in 
the past  in the context of  the ePrivacy Regulation (Article 10) as well as the European 
Commission’s cookie pledge. PSM have consistently warned that such solution s, if 
introduced without adequate safeguards, could create negative consequences: 
 
• Intermediation of user -service relationship: the automation/centralisation of 
consent through browser settings risks creating friction by intermediating the 
relationship between consumers and individual services. Users trust each individual 
service differently: a trusted local PSM is not perceived the same way as an unknown  
or non-EU service provider which may not be subject to the same legal standards. 
Consent set at browser level is likely to reflect caution toward the least trusted 
services, undermining user choice for more trusted ones. 
 
• Lack of granularity and context : central consent tools often reduce complex data 
practices into "allow all/block all" options. This approach fails to capture important 
nuances, such as non -commercial or public -interest uses, or cookies that play a 
functional role without harming privacy. As a result, users may inadvertently block 
services they rely on or disable beneficial functionalities. 
 
• Gatekeeping risks: centralising consent in browsers enables browser providers to 
shape how consent is handled by imposing their own preferences  or prioritizing 
business interests. For example, dominant browser providers may take an overly

2. AI Act implementation 
With the general application of the AI Act set to begin on 2 August 2026, many aspects remain 
unclear at this stage. The Digital Omnibus could serve as a helpful tool for exploring potential 
ways to streamline the implementation of the rules. 
 
a. Clarifications 
 
Despite the recent adoption of several guidance documents  by the European 
Commission,4 certain concepts in the AI A ct remain unclear. This is for example the case 
for the definitions of “provider” and “deployer”, where in practice drawing a line  between 
the two notions may prove difficult, especially in cases where a user modifies, integrates, 
or fine-tunes an off-the-shelf AI system and places it on the market or puts it into service. 
 
b. High-risk AI systems 
 
The qualification of an AI system a s high-risk under Article 6(2) is based on the simple 
criterion of whether a system is used for one of the purposes listed in Annex III.  
 
While Article 6(3) tries to provide more flexibility to account for specific contexts where the 
use of AI systems may actually not create significant risks for individuals, it remains vague. 
 
The Digital Omnibus could attempt to clarify Article 6(3) to enable a more nuanced 
application of what should be considered as a high -risk AI systems, in particular whe re 
data protection laws already provide a high level of protection to citizens . As an example, 
biometric identification systems  can be  used occasionally by media  organisations to 
support editorial and journalistic work,  e.g. through content archive management or fact 
checking. The current provisions do not enable to factor in possible safeguards put in place 
by deployer s to limit and mitigate any adverse impact on individuals (data retention 
periods, limitations on the type and amount of data processed). They also fail to take into 
consideration the purpose and context of the high -risk use, e.g. non-commercial uses, 
journalistic purposes, existence of a public service mission.

c. Interplay between the AI Act and the GDPR  
 
PSM welcome the upcoming  guidelines on the relationship between the General Data 
Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the AI Act  which are currently being drafted by the  AI 
Office and the European Data Protection Board.  
 
Although we understand that the guidelines will only be subject to a public consultation in 
2026, we would like to encourage the European Commission to consider including certain 
clarifications already in the Digital Omnibus proposal. 
 
In particular, the Commission should address the overlap between the obligations to carry 
out Data Protection Impact Assessment s (Article 35 GDPR) and Fundamental Rights 
Impact Assessment s for high -risk AI systems (Article 27 AI Act) . To reduce duplication, 
compliance with  these two obligations should be streamlined , ideally through a single, 
harmonised template that organisations can use to fulfil both requirements. 
 
d. Protecting intellectual property rights 
 
The simplification of digital rules  must not come at the expense of critical protections 
provided by the AI Act, particularly those safeguarding intellectual property rights.  
 
These protections are vital in ensuring that human creativity is respected in an increasingly 
AI-driven world. Weakening copyright-related provisions in the AI Act could undermine the 
incentives for innovation in creative industries and erode legal certainty in protecting 
original works.  
 
Article 53 of th e AI Act  in particular  mandates transparency for the use of copyrighted 
material to train datasets and requires GPAI model providers to put in place a policy to 
comply with Union law on copyright and related rights, notably to comply with reservations 
of rights.  
 
These provisions ensure that datasets used to train AI models, such as those generating 
music, art, or text, comply with copyright laws. Simplifying these provisions risk creating 
loopholes where AI operators could exploit copyrighted content without accountability, 
effectively distorting the balance between innovation and intellectual property protection.  
 
Furthermore, weakening these rules could have a disproportionate impact on PSM who 
depend heavily on protecting the rights of creators to sustain cultural diversity and high -
quality content production. The media industry al ready faces challenges from generative 
AI systems producing content that risks replicating copyrighted material. Ensuring that 
robust copyright protections remain intact in the AI Act is essential to uphold the legal 
framework that supports cultural and creative indu stries while promoting  ethically 
responsible innovation in AI development. It is important that these rules apply also to 
SMEs, as they can infringe rightsholders’ rights as much as larger companies.

3. Data Acquis (Data Governance Act, Free Flow of Non -Personal Data Regulation, Open Data 
Directive). 
The simplification of the EU acquis should be in line with the Data Union Strategy to ensure a 
full consistency in EU Policy making. 
 
PSM organisations have already highlighted to the European Commission (in the context of 
the public consultation on the Data Union Strategy ) that the simplification of these legal 
frameworks should not come at the expense of the crucial protections that exempt PSM from 
unduly burdensome obligations. These existing exceptions are the following:  
 
• Exception in the Data Governance Act (Regulation 2022/868/EU): data held by public 
service broadcasters and their subsidiaries for the fulfillment of a public service 
broadcasting remit are exempted from Chapter II provisions under Article 3(2)(b) of the 
Data Governance Act. 
 
• Exception in the Open Data Directive (Directive 2019/1024/EU) : public service 
broadcasters and their subsidiaries are exempted from the Open Data Directive under 
Article 1(2)(i). It is important that this exemption is maintained in any future revision, as 
the case of Public Service Broadcasters is fundamentally different from that of the public 
service bodies which fall within the scope of the Open Data Directive. 
 
The above mentioned exceptions  are constitutionally mandated safeguards that protect 
media freedom, editorial independence, and the democratic public service remit of PSM. We 
take the view that t hese should be explicitly maintained in any simplified framework for the 
following reasons: 
 
a) The nature of the system of public service broadcasting remit and its legal 
framework  
 
PSM already have as part of their public service remit the task of ensuring that citizens 
have the widest possible access to a broad range of content. Regardless of the 
information that PSM gather and irrespective of the programmes they produce, 
commission, acquire or preserve in their archives, the objective is to make them available 
to the public. Setting out the conditions under which public service broadcasters have to 
give access to their content would interfere and overlap with the definition of the publi c 
service remit, which - according to the Amsterdam Protocol - falls within the exclusive 
competence of the Member States.  
 
b) Protecting broadcasters' own and acquired intellectual property rights  
 
Almost all information and data held by PSM are subject to copyright protection. Beyond 
the day-to-day exploitation of intellectual rights held by PSM, in particular the exclusive 
rights held by broadcasters as producers of audio or audiovisual programmes, third-party 
rights (e.g. music rights) are an integral part of virtually all broadcast material, and not 
only of acquired or commissioned productions but also of programme material produced 
entirely by PSM themselves. This would effectively exclude nearly all potentially suitable

In any event, requiring PSM to make available their material for which third parties hold

c) Protecting editorial independence and journalistic sources  
 
PSM hold sensitive data including (i) journalistic sources requiring absolute 
confidentiality (protected inter alia  under Article 11 of the Charter of Fundamental 
Rights), (ii) editorial content in development stages, (iii) research materials for 
investigative journalist, or (iv) viewer data reflecting consumption of sensitive content 
(political programming, minority interest content).  
 
In order to protect editorial freedom and the confidentiality of sources, PSM must not be 
obliged to provide access to unpublished journalistic material.  
 
It is for the PSM to decide whether or not to make their broadcast, non -broadcast or 
unpublished content and related material available to third parties, in the light of various 
considerations and, in particular, protection and confidentiality of journalistic sources. 
Acting otherwise, their editorial freedom and the principle of protection of sources would 
be seriously jeopardised. In addition, making available unpublished material would give 
rise to potential conflicts with the moral rights of the journalists involved.

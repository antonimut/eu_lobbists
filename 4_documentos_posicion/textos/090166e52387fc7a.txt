 
 
 
 
 
AI ACT 
It must be ensured that the upcoming Digital Omnibus comprehensively eliminates am-
biguities between the AI Regulation and the GDPR. SMEs must not be left with any un-
certainty regarding the application of open-source AI-models, as otherwise their use by 
companies will be out of the question. An important factor here is the design of the 
guidelines announced in Article 96 of the AI Regulation, which are to be drawn up by 
the Commission and address the implementation of AI-systems. They must be feasible 
for SMEs and ensure that no additional or unexpected obligations arise, particularly for 
craft businesses. An example of these potential ambiguities can be found in Article 3 
(23) of the AI Regulation, which concerns "significant changes" to an AI system. 
In particular, it must be clarified: 
  Which specific types of changes are not considered substantial changes 
  That providers of AI-systems allow, through testing/conformity procedures, for the 
necessary flexibility in order to make practical operations and the further develop-
ment of the systems possible without triggering a new and costly assessment each 
time 
  That the guidelines under Article 96 permit the 'fine-tuning' of AI systems by allowing 
companies to enter their own operational and customer data to optimize the system 
within the scope of the intended purpose as intended use. Businesses that, for exam-
ple, use their own data to adapt AI systems to their workflows must not themselves 
fall into the role of ‘provider’ through this step. The responsibility for conformity as-
sessments remains with the manufacturers. Only if a craft business makes a funda-
mental change of purpose or independently implements substantial further develop-
ment can it legally become a ‘provider’. 
 
Demands on the Digital Omnibus 
We welcome the European Commission's efforts to create legislative simplifications for com-
panies as part of the planned Digital Omnibus. Only by harmonizing complex rules, reducing 
overlaps, and modernizing the regulatory framework can we succeed in strengthening Europe 
as a center for business and innovation. The Digital Omnibus must strike a balance between 
simplifying compliance, streamlining obligations, and ensuring competitiveness. However, it 
should not be about deregulation and groundbreaking laws such as the Data Act must not be 
weakened under the pretext of “simplification”.

Legal Uncertainty Regarding the Use of (Modified or Unmodified) 
Open-Source AI Models 
The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) highlighted another source of legal uncer-
tainty for users of (modified or unmodified) open-source AI models in its Opinion 
28/2024 (https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2025-05/edpb_opin-
ion_202428_ai-models_de.pdf) 
According to the EDPB, the unlawful processing of personal data during the training of AI 
models may have an impact on the lawfulness of the subsequent use of such models by 
another operator. “The assessment of the potential impact of the unlawfulness of the 
initial processing on the subsequent processing carried out by another controller should 
be made by supervisory authorities on a case-by-case basis.” (ibid., para. 128). 
This means that if a model was trained unlawfully, its subsequent use may also be prob-
lematic – even if the user themselves has not committed any wrongdoing. 
Authorities will therefore decide on a case-by-case basis – resulting in significant legal 
uncertainty for users. 
 If the assessment of data protection compliance depends on case-by-case evaluations, 
the resulting uncertainty will inevitably discourage substantial investments in the adap-
tation of open-source models or even in the necessary hardware for local deployment. 
Open-source models are typically developed by much smaller providers than OpenAI or 
Anthropic and are further adapted by the community. Structurally, this often goes hand 
in hand with less comprehensive documentation of the training data used, meaning that 
later users may not be able to determine whether data protection rules were violated 
during the training process. 
This interpretation of the law therefore creates significant competitive disadvantages, 
particularly for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), and limits their ability to use 
open-source models. 
A potential solution to this legal uncertainty would be to clarify that the unlawfulness of 
training an open-source AI model should only affect its subsequent use if the user knew 
or should have known that the model had been trained unlawfully.

GDPR 
We welcome the exemption from maintaining a record of processing activities as pro-
posed in the Omnibus IV package. In addition, further adjustments should be made in 
line with the risk-based approach. 
The obligations under Articles 13 and 14 GDPR are in no way consistent with the princi-
ple of proportionality inherent in the risk-based approach. Given that data processing by 
tradespeople is typically minimal and low risk, the processing can be considered virtually 
risk-free. 
There is, in the view of the craft sector, no identifiable interest on the part of website 
visitors in receiving such information. A consistent application of the risk-based ap-
proach should therefore lead to a transformation of the information obligation into a 
right of access for the customer: a customer who wishes to receive specific information 
should have the right to do so. However, there is no need for unsolicited information 
about legal bases, deadlines, or rights that are of no practical relevance to the customer. 
 
GDPR / Use of AI 
In line with the risk-based approach, the regulatory burden for AI-related data pro-
cessing with minimal risk must be reduced. 
Firstly, the concept of legitimate interest should be strengthened as a legal basis for the 
processing of personal data in important AI use cases. 
Even for low-risk data processing activities, which are appropriately safeguarded 
through techniques such as pseudonymisation, the legitimate interest basis should be 
applicable. 
Where re-identification is not possible, it should be explicitly clarified that such data do 
not constitute personal data. 
Data Act 
Finally, we wish to highlight an essential aspect concerning the Data Act. The regulation 
strengthens SMEs and supports open and competitive markets. For this reason, it is cru-
cial that the Data Act is now implemented consistently and without any alteration. Only 
in this way can it achieve its full potential and strengthen digital services for crafts and 
small enterprises.

For consistent implementation, the European Commission should therefore: 
• Reject any attempts to make data sharing voluntary 
• Maintain mandatory notification obligations with respect to trade secret and cyberse-
curity exceptions, keeping the burden of proof with the data holder and ensuring robust 
and accessible review mechanisms. 
Article 5 (9–11) of the Data Act already establishes specific and strong legal safeguards 
to balance access rights with the protection of trade secrets and sensitive business infor-
mation. 
The agreed text is fit for purpose: the scope of data-sharing obligations is limited to 
readily available data, not to a product’s internal data. The potential for reverse engi-
neering of manufacturers’ intellectual property from such data is therefore very limited 
and is not expanded by the Data Act. 
The Data Act must not be weakened under the pretext of “simplification.” Its strength 
lies in enforceable rights, fairness, procedural justice, and legal clarity – principles that 
are essential for Europe’s long-term resilience, innovation, and competitiveness.

Executive Summary 
Fastweb welcomes the European Commission’s initiative to simplify and streamline the 
Union’s digital regulatory framework through the Digital Omnibus. 
As a major operator in the telecommunications and digital services sector, with substantial 
investments in Artificial Intelligence (AI) , data-driven innovation, and cybersecurity, we 
strongly support the objective of creating a more coherent, efficient, and innovation -
friendly European digital ecosystem. 
Our contribution highlights three policy areas where simplification, alignment, and 
proportionality could deliver the greatest benefits: 
• Cybersecurity – ensuring coherence across overlapping national and EU 
frameworks (NIS2, Cybersecurity Act, Cyber Resilience Act, DORA) while 
maintaining high protection standards. 
• Data legislation and privacy – modernising the ePrivacy Directive (2002/58/EC) to 
ensure technological neutrality, fair competition, and consistent rules on cookies, 
tracking technologies, and data protection. 
• Artificial Intelligence (AI Act)  – clarifying the interaction between the AI Act, the 
Copyright Directive (EU) 2019/790, and the General Data Protection Regulation 
(GDPR), to provide legal certainty and prevent disproportionate obligations for EU-
based operators. 
We believe that a unified, technology-neutral, and proportionate regulatory approach  — 
one that preserves security and fundamental rights while enabling innovation — will be 
essential to strengthen Europe’s digital competitiveness and sovereignty. 
Fastweb remains fully committed to cooperating with the European Commission and 
relevant stakeholders in the follow-up discussions and technical consultations supporting 
the Digital Omnibus initiative. 
 
Response to the European Commission’s Call for Evidence – “Digital Omnibus” 
1. General context and position 
We welcome the European Commission’s initiative to simplify and streamline the Union’s 
digital regulatory framework through the forthcoming Digital Omnibus. 
As a telecommunications operator investing heavily in artificial intelligence, data -driven 
innovation, and cybersecurity, we strongly support the Commission’s objective to reduce 
administrative burdens, eliminate overlaps, and ensure a more coherent and 
proportionate implementation of the EU digital acquis. 
The following observations focus on two critical domains of the digital ecosystem — 
cybersecurity, data and privacy, and artificial intelligence — where coordination, 
simplification and legal clarity can most effectively enhance both compliance efficiency 
and Europe’s digital competitiveness.

2. Cybersecurity 
Over the past years, the European and national cybersecurity frameworks have evolved 
rapidly, reflecting growing awareness of cyber threats and the strategic importance of 
digital resilience. 
While this progress has significantly strengthened Europe’s overall security posture, it has 
also resulted in a complex and fragmented regulatory landscape. 
Telecommunications operators, as essential providers under Directive (EU) 2022/2555 on 
measures for a high common level of cybersecurity across the Union (NIS2 Directive), are 
often subject to multiple overlapping regimes — both European and national — that 
pursue similar objectives through different procedures and timelines. 
Against this background, the Digital Omnibus provides a unique opportunity to streamline 
compliance processes, improve consistency across legal instruments, and reduce 
redundant administrative obligations while maintaining a robust level of protection. 
2.1 Streamlining national security and perimeter procedures 
In Italy, the coexistence of the Golden Power framework (Italian Decree-Law No. 21/2022, 
converted by Law No. 51/2022) and the National Cybersecurity Perimeter (Perimetro di 
Sicurezza Nazionale Cibernetica – PSNC) has created a dense and sometimes redundant 
set of procedural obligations for operators managing critical networks and infrastructures. 
The implementation of these rules, through multiple Decrees of the President of the 
Council of Ministers (DPCM), often entails repetitive filings, overlapping notifications, and 
extended timelines — especially for operators involved in mergers or organisat ional 
changes. 
Simplifying these procedures would significantly enhance legal certainty and operational 
agility. 
For instance, companies should be allowed to remove previously notified assets from 
annual plans when operating conditions have materially changed, such as when Fixed 
Wireless Access (FWA) services are migrated to mobile networks.  
Similarly, when authorities require the replacement of existing suppliers, a compensatory 
mechanism should be foreseen to address the technical and economic infeasibility of 
substituting end-of-life radio equipment. 
Such measures would not weaken national security; rather, they would focus oversight on 
truly critical risks and align administrative practice with the principle of proportionality 
enshrined in EU law. 
2.2 Enhancing certification and evaluation processes 
A second area where simplification would yield tangible benefits concerns the procedures 
of the National Evaluation and Certification Centre (Centro di Valutazione e Certificazione 
Nazionale – CVCN), which assesses the security of ICT products and services  used in 
critical infrastructures. 
While the CVCN plays a key role in safeguarding national cybersecurity, the current 
evaluation process can be lengthy and resource-intensive.

To streamline operations and foster predictability, it would be highly beneficial to establish 
a public or semi -public list of ICT products already assessed by the CVCN.  
Operators using these pre -evaluated products could then follow a simplified notification 
process limited to their integration into networks, avoiding redundant reviews of identical 
components. 
Moreover, certification schemes developed under Regulation (EU) 2019/881 on the 
European Cybersecurity Certification Framework (Cybersecurity Act – CSA) should be 
recognised as equivalent or preferential within national technology evaluations. 
This alignment would encourage the adoption of EU-certified solutions, reduce duplication 
between national and European certification paths, and contribute to a more integrated 
and trusted digital single market for secure technologies. 
2.3 Strengthening Europe’s cybersecurity posture and technological sovereignty 
Beyond administrative simplification, cybersecurity policy must also promote strategic 
autonomy and technological sovereignty. 
To that end, it would be appropriate to require — that, at least in high -risk or nationally 
sensitive domains, on a risk -based basis and without undermining the functioning of the 
internal market, entities covered by the NIS2 Directive should procure cloud and AI 
services developed and hosted within the European Union. 
Such a measure would enhance resilience by ensuring that critical data and workloads 
remain within jurisdictions bound by EU law and subject to EU security oversight. 
Far from limiting competition, this approach would create a virtuous cycle between 
investment in advanced European technologies, stronger supply -chain security, and 
reduced exposure to extraterritorial risks. 
2.4 Coordinating and rationalising incident reporting 
Another key challenge faced by operators concerns the coexistence of different incident 
reporting obligations stemming from the NIS2 Directive, the National Cybersecurity 
Perimeter (PSNC), and the Directive (EU) 2022/2557 on the Resilience of Critical Entities 
(CER Directive). 
Each regime introduces its own definitions, thresholds, and timelines for what constitutes 
a reportable incident. 
This multiplicity often results in duplicated reports, inconsistent data flows, and 
unnecessary administrative burdens — with limited added value for authorities. 
The Digital Omnibus should therefore promote a harmonised and coherent reporting 
framework, ensuring that a single incident notification, submitted through a common or 
mutually recognised interface, satisfies multiple legal obligations. 
Aligning deadlines, templates, and escalation procedures would significantly increase 
efficiency, enabling operators to focus resources on incident response and remediation 
rather than on bureaucracy. 
2.5 Harmonising risk-management measures and implementation timelines

The entry into force of the Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 of 17 
October 2024 laying down rules for the application of the NIS2 Directive further specifies 
the risk-management measures required under the NIS2 Directive. 
However, the timing and scope of these measures must remain consistent with national 
transpositions to avoid divergent interpretations and implementation schedules across 
Member States. 
A coordinated approach between EU and national authorities — possibly through the 
European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) — would ensure uniform application, 
prevent regulatory fragmentation, and strengthen mutual trust among competent 
authorities. 
2.6 Towards a unified and interoperable cybersecurity framework 
Finally, the progressive evolution of the Union’s cybersecurity legislation — 
encompassing NIS2, the Cyber Resilience Act - Regulation (EU) 2024/2847  (CRA), the 
Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) and the Cybersecurity Act (CSA) — has 
created a landscape that, while comprehensive, can appear fragmented and complex for 
operators active across multiple sectors. 
A more unified and interoperable framework, underpinned by recognised international 
standards such as the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) 
Cybersecurity Framework, would provide a common baseline for risk management and 
compliance. 
This would improve predictability, facilitate cross -sector implementation, and make 
Europe’s cybersecurity ecosystem more transparent, resilient, and innovation-friendly. 
 
3. Data legislation, including rules on cookies and other tracking technologies 
The evolution of digital communications has rendered the current ePrivacy framework 
increasingly outdated and inconsistent with today’s technological and market realities. 
Adopted at a time when electronic communications were largely provided by traditiona l 
telecommunications operators, the ePrivacy Directive imposes obligations that are no 
longer technology-neutral. 
Over the past two decades, information society service providers  — including over-the-
top (OTT) platforms and digital application providers — have become key players in the 
delivery of messaging, voice, and content-sharing services. Yet, the regulatory framework 
continues to place disproportionate compliance requirements on telecommunications 
operators, while similar services delivered via internet platforms often fall outside 
equivalent obligations. 
To restore fair competition and regulatory consistency, the ePrivacy framework should be 
either comprehensively reviewed or replaced with a new legislative approach that ensures 
technology- and service-neutral obligations across all actors in the digital value chain. 
In particular, the rules governing cookies and other tracking technologies must be 
modernized. Since the adoption of the original Directive, tracking tools have evolved

significantly, ranging from traditional cookies to a wide array of digital identifiers and 
device-based profiling technologies. Regulation should focus on the purpose and impact 
of data processing, not on the specific technology used. 
A modernized framework should therefore: 
• Forbid harmful uses of cookies, identifiers, and tracking tools irrespective of the 
provider’s nature—whether telecommunications operators, OTTs, or other digital 
service providers. 
• Promote technological neutrality, ensuring that similar services are subject to 
equivalent obligations and safeguards. 
• Enhance user trust by maintaining high standards of privacy protection while 
avoiding regulatory fragmentation or competitive distortions. 
Such reform would align data protection and privacy legislation with the Digital Services 
Act and European data strategy, supporting a coherent, innovation -friendly regulatory 
environment that reflects how people communicate and interact today. 
 
 
4. Artificial Intelligence – Coordination, compliance, and proportionality 
4.1 Coordination between the AI Act and Directive (EU) 2019/790 
Over the past years, the Union has adopted two foundational legal pillars for the digital 
ecosystem: Directive (EU) 2019/790 on Copyright in the Digital Single Market and 
Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 on Artificial Intelligence (the AI Act).  
While both pursue complementary objectives, overlaps and regulatory gaps have 
emerged that require clarification. 
Articles 3 and 4 of the Copyright Directive establish exceptions for text and data mining 
(TDM) — processes central to AI model training. At the same time, Article 53(1)(c) of the 
AI Act obliges general -purpose AI model providers to implement policies ensu ring 
compliance with EU copyright law, including recognition of opt -outs made under Article 
4(3) of the Directive. 
Uncertainty remains regarding how providers can comply with opt -out requests made by 
rightholders after model training. While reservations can be technically observed during 
data collection (e.g. via robots.txt), no reliable methods exist to remove such content from 
a trained model. 
This regulatory and technical gap risks creating inconsistency and undermining 
competitiveness. 
The Commission is therefore invited to clarify: 
• whether opt-outs apply only prior to training or also ex post; 
• how reservations should be technically represented (machine-readable metadata, 
standardised formats);

• whether any protocols could realistically enable the removal of reserved content 
from pre-trained models. 
Until such methods exist, obligations that are technically unachievable should be avoided. 
A harmonised and realistic approach is needed to ensure legal certainty and competitive 
neutrality. 
4.2 Data protection and the right to erasure 
A related issue concerns the interaction between the AI Act and the General Data 
Protection Regulation (GDPR), particularly Article 17 (right to erasure).  
Once AI models are trained, personal data may become statistically embedded and non-
retrievable. Selective removal is not technically possible without compromising model 
integrity. 
We encourage the Commission, in coordination with the European Data Protection Board 
(EDPB) and the European Data Protection Supervisor (EDPS), to issue guidance 
clarifying how data subject rights should apply in this context. 
Consideration could also be given to creating an AI -specific right to erasure, adapted to 
the technical realities of model training and evolution. 
4.3 Ensuring a level playing field 
Strict enforcement of EU copyright, data protection, and AI obligations must not result in 
structural competitive disadvantages for EU -based companies vis -à-vis global 
hyperscalers. 
We therefore encourage the Commission to consider regulatory symmetry mechanisms, 
such as equivalence clauses or market -access conditions, ensuring that all AI providers 
offering products or services in the Union are subject to equivalent obligations on 
transparency, security, data sovereignty, and fundamental-rights protection. 
A coherent and balanced regulatory approach — combining legal certainty, 
proportionality, and competitive neutrality — is indispensable to preserve Europe’s 
competitiveness and uphold the values underlying its digital governance framework. 
 
 
5. Conclusions 
The Digital Omnibus represents a crucial opportunity to consolidate, streamline, and 
modernise the European Union’s digital rulebook. 
Across the fields of cybersecurity, data protection and privacy, and artificial intelligence, 
the initiative should aim to ensure a coherent, technology -neutral, and proportionate 
regulatory environment that supports both trust and innovation. 
Simplification and alignment across existing instruments — including the NIS2 Directive, 
the Cybersecurity Act (CSA), the ePrivacy framework, the General Data Protection 
Regulation (GDPR), and the AI Act — would deliver significant benefits for operators and 
authorities alike.

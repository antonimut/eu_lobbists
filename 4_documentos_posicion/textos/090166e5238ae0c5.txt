Feedback to call for evidence on the Digital Omnibus 
 
Philips welcomes the opportunity to contribute evidence to the upcoming proposal for the Digital Omnibus. 
 
At Philips, we have been innovating to improve people’s health and well-being for over 130 years. People’s needs are 
at the very heart of how we innovate and design for sustainable impact, with a ‘safety and quality-first’ mindset. This 
is reflected in our portfolio of patient - and people -centric innovations in hardware, software, AI and services, 
supporting care in the hospital and in the home. We see the future of healthcare as the one that extends beyond 
hospital walls into the home and the community, with digital technology connecting care across settings. 
 
While the medical technology sector has been one of the most innovative in Europe, the last ten years have seen a 
decline in placing innovative medical devices on the European market. As the EU regulatory landscape has become 
extremely complex with the recent adoption of multiple legislation around data, AI and cybersecurity, often 
overlapping and incoherent with the existing legislation, the attractiveness of the European market is declining. 
 
If no action is taken, medical device manufacturers will further deprioritize Europe. Together with the  other EU 
initiatives on competitiveness and Life Sciences Strategy, the upcoming Digital Omnibus is an opportunity to reverse 
this trend by ensuring a clear, coherent and predictable regulatory environment in the area of AI, data and 
cybersecurity, providing legal certainty and aiming at full integration of the ( Digital) Single Market.  It would be 
beneficial if Digital Omnibus aimed for uniformity and simplification across all aspects of the law  and regulations in 
scope of the regulatory adjustments envisioned. This means that consideration must be given not only to the 
directives and regulations, but also to the  tools supporting implementation such as  FAQs and other guidance 
documents that are subsequently issued. 
 
Below, we list the issues and challenges that, in our view, the Digital Omnibus should tackle to achieve these goals. 
 
Artificial intelligence – AI Act 
 
A robust regulatory framework not only provides certainty to all stakeholders involved but also is crucial to create 
trust in AI by health systems and a level playing field between all market players.  
 
The AI Act includes many sensible requirements to ensure the safety of users and patients, by requiring, for example, 
transparency in algorithms or robust data governance to ensure traceability and accountability.  
 
However, any medical device that is also an AI system, as defined in the AI Act, is today already regulated under the 
Medical Device Regulation (MDR), which includes strict requirements to bring safe and performant medical devices, 
incl. AI-enabled, on the European market.  
 
While the AI Act partly addresses the interplay with the Medical Device Regulation (where it offers the possibility for 
providers to integrate AI Act compliance aspects into existing documentation and procedures), concerns remain 
around the capacity issues of Notified Bodies as experienced in transition from Medical Device Directives (MDD) to 
the MDR, lack of timely and continuous guidance for AI -based medical devices to support implementation of new 
requirements and address any remaining overlaps and gaps, and lack of alignment between horizontal (AI) standards 
and vertical (medical device) standards.  
 
More concretely: 
- The transition from MDD to MDR has resulted in longer average certification time  for (AI) medical devices 
(from 9-12 months to 12- 24 months), higher conformity assessment procedure costs  (on average 260%), 
increased time spent on preparation of technical documentation (+157%) and 9% more regulatory staff are

for AI -enabled medical devices by including  the relevant high- risk AI requirements in the MDR , as well as an 
extension of the date of application to allow the readiness of the entire ecosystem . A well -designed and clear 
regulatory framework will enable more innovation, better market access and help us deliver better care for more 
people in the EU. 
 
Data-related legislation 
 
Regarding the consolidation of data acquis, we see some regulations becoming obsolete such as Free -Flow of Non-
Personal Data Regulation and Open Data Directive since both the Data Act and the Data Governance Act have similar 
scopes. In view of simplification, these regulations should be consolidated to offer coherent and predictable 
frameworks for data sharing and data-based innovation. 
 
Further simplification of the Data Act should consider the following: 
 
- Contractual obligation imposing on data holders the renegotiation of existing complex contracts represents 
considerable administrative burden, disproportionate costs and introduces legal ambiguity. In this regard, 
the regulatory simplification exercise represents an opportunity to either remove this obligation or make it 
voluntary.  
 
- Another simplification in the Data Act should alleviate the procedures for the use of certain safeguards for 
data holders –  including protection of trade secrets and cybersecurity risks –  when assessing data sharing 
requests. These safeguards are framed too generically and place an excessive procedural burden on the data 
holders who will have to thoroughly substantiate their refusal decision, demonstrate the risk of serious 
economic damage and notify the competent authority. 
 
Cybersecurity legislation 
 
Stringent and harmonised security legislation at European level is important to protect patients’ safety and data, as 
well as prevent negative financial impacts on hospitals and health systems. Medical device manufacturers currently 
need to comply with security requirements in the MDR, GDPR, and NIS2 Directive. Other security requirements apply

Implementation of the Cyber Resilience Act  is currently experiencing difficulties, including harmonisation and 
reference of harmonis ed standards in the Official Journal, designation of Notified Bodies, and clarification of key 
questions around scope and requirements. A pragmatic approach needs to be found to this implementation, 
including reasonable adjustment of the timeline for development and harmonisation of standards. 
  
The Radio Equipment Directive (RED) Delegated Act applies to many products placed on the European market from 
August 2025 onwards. Demonstrating compliance under its strict conformity assessment rules -  either by applying 
the harmonised 18031 standards or notified body assessment - requires a significant investment from manufacturers.

From December 2027 onwards, the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) will apply and although the overall security objectives 
are very similar, the way that this must be demonstrated in technical documentation is different, adding additional 
work for manufacturers to also demonstrate compliance to the CRA. 
 
Some of this burden could be relieved . If the European Commission allowed a transition period of 2 to 3 years for 
products already in scope of the RED Delegated Act , which are complying to the RED harmoni sed 18031 standards 
or have undergone notified body assessment to satisfy Article 6 (a), manufacturers would only need to demonstrate 
compliance to Annex I, Part II as stated in Article 6 (b). 
 
Security requirements under the Medical Devices Regulation need to be updated, starting with the MDCG 2019-16 
Guidance on Cybersecurity for medical devices, to ensure legal clarity and alignment with the current state -of-the-
art in standards, technology and other legislation. 
 
A harmonised approach to cloud security across the EU is needed to strengthen trust and support uptake of cloud 
and Software -as-a-Service by hospitals and healthcare providers.  This should also reduce divergences in 
requirements at national and local level.  
 
Regarding the ePrivacy Directive, currently countries within the EEA have differing consent rules regarding analytics 
tracking cookies. Exempting first party analytics cookies  from prior consent , in accordance with recent measures 
under the UK's Data (Use and Access) Act, would help achieve consistency across various jurisdictions in the EEA.  
 
Making the Digital Single Market Work Better for Medical Devices 
To support a stronger digital single market, the EUDAMED digital database under the MDR should give clear and easy 
access to information for everyone involved in the medical device sector across Europe.  
 
As digital rules change, it  is important to look at how EUDAMED connects with other EU laws —like those on 
packaging, batteries, and electronic waste. Future updates should make sure these areas work well together. 
 
Medical device approvals by Notified Bodies should also move to digital formats. This will save time, reduce 
paperwork, and make the process more consistent. The new rule (Commission Implementing Regulation 2025/1234) 
allowing electronic instructions for professional users should be expanded to all users, like in the US —where paper 
copies are available on request. Digital product labels can lower costs and support sustainability in the sector.

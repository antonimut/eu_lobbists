secunet – response to European Commission public consultation on 
Simplification – digital package and omnibus 
secunet welcomes the European Commission’s Digital Omnibus Package as a 
timely and strategic initiative to reinforce Europe’s resilience and global 
competitiveness. The rapid advancement of digital technologies, coupled with 
the growing complexity of EU regulatory frameworks, present both significant 
opportunities and pressing challenges for European industry. To ensure that 
Europe remains at the forefront of innovation, it is essential to establish a 
coherent and future-proof regulatory environment that safeguards strategic 
autonomy and technological sovereignty – across the data acquis, throughout 
our supply chains, and within our public procurement systems.   
As Germany’s leading provider of high-security IT solutions, secunet has been a 
trusted partner to public authorities, critical infrastructure operators, and 
industry leaders for over 2 decades. With deep expertise in cybersecurity, digital 
identity, secure cloud infrastructure and AI governance secunet plays a pivotal 
role in enabling secure digital transformation across Europe. Our mission aligns 
closely with the objectives of the Digital Omnibus Package: to streamline 
regulatory obligations, reduce administrative burden, and ensure that European 
values – privacy, trust and technological independence – are embedded in the 
foundations of the European Digital Single Market.  
 
Challenges of fragmentated cyber incident reporting 
The EU’s suite of cybersecurity legislative acts including: the NIS2 Directive, 
Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), the Cybersecurity Act and the Digital Operational 
Resilience Act (DORA) – as well as the General Data Protection Regulation 
(GDPR), and the Critical Entities Resilience Directive (CER) aims to enhance 
privacy and digital resilience. However, overlapping and unclear requirements 
for reporting cyber incidents across these policies and between Member States 
pose significant operational and compliance challenges for entities under 
scope, including:  
• Multiple notifications: organisations are often required to notify several 
national bodies of the same incident, leading to duplicative efforts. This 
complexity is heightened by the fact that different authorities may be 
responsible for different aspects of an incident – for example, in 
Germany, both the BfDI and the BSI may need to be notified, as well as 
with De-Mail. The growing number of regulations further complicates the 
landscape. Moreover, organisations with multiple sites across Europe may 
face internal coordination challenges to determine which entity should 
report, and whether a single report suffices from a national legal 
perspective. This varies by regulations: NIS2 requires national-level 
reporting and may necessitate multiple submissions, whereas the CRA is 
centralised via ENISA and does not involve national authorities.

• Jurisdictional inconsistencies: Entities operating across borders face 
inconsistent obligations with varying formats, languages, and definition 
of significant incidents. These inconsistencies stem not only from 
differences between legislative acts but also from how laws are 
transposed nationally which can lead to competitive distortions and 
operational uncertainty.  
• Divergent timelines and thresholds: reporting windows, thresholds and 
obligations differ not only between Member States but also across EU-
level policies. Somes countries have introduced shorter reporting 
windows, broader incident classification, and requirements to report 
significant threats.  
• Supply chain complexity: Additional reporting obligations arise from 
supply chain relationships requiring proactive communication with 
vendors and partners.  
This fragmentation diverts essential cybersecurity resources from mitigation 
and recovery, ultimately weakening the EU’s overall cyber resilience.  
Recommendations:  
To enhance cybersecurity resilience across the European Union, secunet 
proposes a series of targeted reforms to streamline incident reporting, reduce 
administrative burden, and foster a more secure and cooperative digital 
environment. These recommendations aim to align with the overarching goals 
of the Digital Package while ensuring practical implementation across Member 
States. 
A key priority is the establishment of a single and harmonised reporting 
regime. This should be based on a one-stop-shop principle, enabling entities to 
report cybersecurity incidents through a single point of entry. Such a centralised 
approach would ensure that incident data is stored and managed by EU 
Member States, while national Computer Security Incident Response Teams 
(CSIRTs) coordinate with sectoral regulators as needed. This model would 
significantly reduce duplication, streamline communication, and enhance 
regulatory coherence across jurisdictions. 
secunet also advocates for risk-based and proportionate notification 
requirements. Definitions of incident severity thresholds must be clarified and 
standardised, particularly regarding their impact on confidentiality, integrity, 
and service availability. This would allow organisations to more easily determine 
when and how to report incidents, primarily through one national authority. 
That authority would then be responsible for forwarding relevant information to 
EU-level bodies such as ENISA. This approach ensures that regulatory attention 
is focused on incidents with significant impact, while avoiding unnecessary 
reporting. 
A fair compliance process is essential to foster trust and transparency. Before 
enforcement actions are taken for non-compliance, businesses should be given 
a reasonable opportunity to submit compliant incident reports. This should 
follow a formal notification from regulators, allowing organisations to adapt 
constructively to evolving requirements. Such a process encourages a

collaborative rather than punitive regulatory environment and supports 
continuous improvement. 
Another critical area is liability clarification. Currently, both the NIS2 Directive 
and the Cyber Resilience Act lack explicit provisions on liability. The legal 
framework should ensure that the act of notification itself does not increase 
liability for spill-over effects. This would create a more controlled and secure 
environment for information sharing, encouraging organisations to report 
incidents without fear of legal repercussions. 
secunet also highlights the need for comprehensive supply chain security. 
Existing regulations should be strengthened to address vulnerabilities that 
extend beyond current measures. This includes implementing more rigorous 
supplier assessments, establishing certification schemes, and promoting 
coordinated threat intelligence sharing across the ICT supply chain. A holistic 
approach to supply chain security is vital to protect critical infrastructure and 
maintain trust in digital services. 
To support the effective implementation of these recommendations, secunet 
proposes several concrete initiatives: 
• A Centralised European Reporting Platform should be developed to 
enable direct incident reporting, with data managed by Member States. 
This platform would serve as a secure and interoperable hub for all 
entities subject to EU cybersecurity regulations. 
• A “Report Once, Comply Many” mechanism should be introduced. This 
would allow a single incident report to satisfy the requirements of 
multiple regulations and authorities, significantly reducing administrative 
burden and improving efficiency. 
• Reporting timelines across different frameworks should be aligned 
across all legislations including the AI Act (Article 73). Establishing a 
common progression—from initial notification to follow-up reports—
would help organisations manage their obligations more effectively and 
ensure consistency across sectors. 
• Cross-recognition agreements should be established among EU 
regulatory authorities. These agreements would allow reports submitted 
under one framework to be accepted as valid under others, promoting 
regulatory convergence and reducing duplication. 
• Investment should be made in an open-source, EU-certified reporting 
tool. Designed for integration with existing security systems, this tool 
would offer organisations a reliable and standardised way to report 
incidents securely and efficiently. 
• A dedicated SME Support Programme should be launched to provide 
technical assistance to small and medium-sized enterprises. This initiative 
would help SMEs navigate the reporting process and comply with 
cybersecurity regulations through training, templates, and expert 
guidance tailored to their specific needs. 
Together, these recommendations and initiatives form a comprehensive 
strategy to modernise cybersecurity incident reporting in the EU. They reflect

A sovereign Cloud  
In light of growing geopolitical tensions, increasing regulatory demands, and 
the strategic importance of data autonomy, secunet strongly supports the 
European Commission’s efforts to develop a sovereign cloud infrastructure as 
a cornerstone of digital sovereignty. The EU’s reliance on non-European 
hyperscalers—who currently control over 70% of the European cloud market—
poses significant risks to data protection, operational autonomy, and strategic 
resilience. 
secunet welcomes the Commission’s work on developing the Cloud and AI 
Development Act, which aims to triple Europe’s data processing capacity by 
2030 and promote sovereign cloud solutions for strategic EU use cases. We 
support the proposal to position public administrations as anchor clients for 
sovereign cloud offerings, thereby creating demand certainty and accelerating 
adoption.  
As the first provider of a sovereign cloud solution approved for classified 
information in Germany, secunet has demonstrated that secure, scalable, and 
open-source-based cloud infrastructure is not only feasible but essential. Our 
SINA Cloud platform, approved by the German Federal Office for Information 
Security (BSI), enables public authorities and critical industries to process 
sensitive data in compliance with national secrecy regulations. This marks a 
significant step toward reducing dependence on non-European providers and 
ensuring legal and operational control over critical digital assets.  
We recommend the following policy actions to support the EU’s sovereign cloud 
ambitions: 
• Mandate EU jurisdictional control over data stored and processed in 
sovereign cloud environments. Hosting data in Europe is insufficient if 
providers remain subject to foreign laws such as the U.S. CLOUD Act or 
FISA Section 702.  
• Restore sovereignty criteria in the EU Cybersecurity Certification 
Scheme (EUCS), including immunity requirements for High+ certification 
levels. These provisions are essential to ensure that data cannot be 
accessed by non-EU entities and to foster trust in European cloud 
services.  
• Promote open-source and interoperable cloud architectures, such as 
those used in secunet’s SINA Cloud, to prevent vendor lock-in and support 
transparency. This aligns with the principles of reversibility, portability, 
and compliance-by-design promoted by initiatives like GAIA-X.  
• Support European cloud providers through targeted public 
procurement, investment incentives, and access to capital. Bitkom’s 
proposal for a €30 billion investment in sovereign cloud, chips, and AI 
infrastructure is a step in the right direction.

• Encourage federated cloud models that allow national and regional 
providers to collaborate under shared standards, ensuring scalability 
while preserving sovereignty. This approach is central to the GAIA-X 
initiative and should be further institutionalised.  
• Ensure alignment with broader EU digital legislation, including the Data 
Act, AI Act, and Cyber Resilience Act. Sovereign cloud infrastructure must 
be integrated into the EU’s digital regulatory framework to ensure 
coherence and legal certainty.  
secunet considers digital sovereignty to be a foundational element in ensuring 
Europe’s long-term economic stability, democratic resilience, and technological 
competitiveness. In this context, the development and certification of sovereign 
cloud infrastructure represent a strategic priority. Such infrastructure should be 
designed to align with European legal frameworks, uphold fundamental rights, 
and support innovation across sectors, contributing to a secure and 
autonomous digital ecosystem within the EU. 
 
Considerations on the EU AI Act 
secunet supports the European Union’s ambition to establish a global 
benchmark for trustworthy and secure artificial intelligence through the EU AI 
Act. As Europe’s first comprehensive legal framework for AI, the Act introduces a 
risk-based approach that aligns regulatory obligations with the potential 
impact of AI systems on health, safety, and fundamental rights. While the Act is 
a critical step toward responsible AI governance, its successful implementation 
will depend on clarity, proportionality, and technical feasibility. 
The AI Act classifies AI systems into four categories: minimal risk, limited risk, 
high risk, and unacceptable risk. Secunet supports this tiered approach but 
emphasises the need for clear and actionable guidance on classification 
criteria, particularly for high-risk systems under Annex III. We recommend that 
the Commission to publish sector-specific examples and decision trees to help 
providers and deployers determine whether their systems fall under high-risk 
categories.  
secunet welcomes the AI Act’s emphasis on accuracy, robustness, and 
cybersecurity for high-risk AI systems, as outlined in Article 15. However, the 
practical implementation of these requirements remains undefined. To address 
this:  
• Harmonised technical standards should be established for AI-specific 
cybersecurity protections, including resilience against adversarial 
attacks, data poisoning, and model manipulation. As many harmonised 
standards are not expected to be finalised until late 2026, it may be 
appropriate to consider extending the timeline for applying essential 
requirements, provided that conformity assessment bodies have been 
accredited. 
• Certification schemes for AI security should be developed and aligned 
with the Cybersecurity Act and the NIS2 Directive.

European Digital Identity Framework & EU Business Wallet 
secunet strongly supports the European Union’s ambition to establish a 
sovereign, secure, and interoperable digital identity framework through the 
revised eIDAS 2.0 Regulation. The introduction of the European Digital Identity 
Wallet (EUDI Wallet) represents a strategic opportunity to reinforce digital 
sovereignty, reduce dependency on non-European authentication providers, 
and empower citizens and businesses with trusted identity solutions across 
borders. 
The European Digital Identity Framework must be built on sovereign 
infrastructure, governed by EU law and operated by entities subject exclusively 
to European jurisdiction. This is essential to ensure that identity data—arguably 
the most sensitive category of personal information—is protected from 
extraterritorial access under foreign legislation such as the U.S. CLOUD Act. 
Sovereignty in digital identity is not merely a technical requirement; it is an 
imperative that underpins trust in public services, legal certainty in cross-border 
transactions, and strategic autonomy in the digital age. 
secunet advocates for a public-led and open-source-based architecture for 
the EUDI Wallet, with reference implementations provided by the European 
Commission and certified by national cybersecurity authorities. This approach 
will ensure transparency, vendor neutrality, and long-term maintainability. The 
wallet must be interoperable across Member States, support mutual recognition 
of credentials, and integrate seamlessly with existing national eID schemes and 
trust services. 
To achieve this, the EU must invest in standardisation and certification 
mechanisms that guarantee high levels of assurance, privacy, and usability. The 
wallet should support qualified electronic signatures, seals, timestamps, and 
other trust services, enabling secure and legally valid digital interactions across 
sectors. These services must be accessible to both natural and legal persons, 
including SMEs, public administrations, and regulated industries such as finance, 
healthcare, and energy. 
secunet also supports the development of the European Business Wallet 
(EUBW) which will extend the benefits of sovereign digital identity to legal 
entities. The EUBW should offer a free version for SMEs and micro companies 
giving free access to all government services. The EUBW should enable secure 
B2B and B2G interactions, automate Know-Your-Business (KYB) procedures, 
and facilitate the issuance and verification of Digital Product Passports (DPP), 
supply chain credentials, and regulatory attestations. Its success will depend on 
early involvement of private stakeholders, seamless integration with enterprise 
systems, and alignment with existing EU legislation such as the Single Digital 
Gateway Regulation.

To ensure true digital inclusion and competitiveness, it is essential to provide a 
free version of this wallet for sole proprietors and small businesses, granting full 
access to all government services. This would lower barriers to entry, foster 
entrepreneurship, and align with EU objectives for SME digitalisation and equal 
participation in the Single Market. 
Finally, the European Digital Identity Framework must be embedded within a 
broader strategy for digital sovereignty, encompassing cloud infrastructure, 
cybersecurity, and data governance. The wallet should not be an isolated tool 
but part of a cohesive ecosystem that enables secure, user-controlled, and 
legally protected digital interactions across the EU. 
 
Conclusion 
The European Commission’s Digital Omnibus Package presents an important 
opportunity to advance the EU’s digital policy objectives. As digital technologies 
and regulatory frameworks continue to evolve, a balanced approach is needed 
to support innovation, strengthen resilience, and ensure legal certainty for all 
stakeholders. 
secunet’s recommendations focus on harmonising cybersecurity incident 
reporting, promoting sovereign cloud infrastructure, supporting effective 
implementation of the AI Act, and establishing a secure and interoperable 
digital identity framework. These measures are intended to help reduce 
administrative complexity, foster cross-border cooperation, and enhance trust 
in digital services. 
By encouraging open-source solutions, public-led architectures, and 
interoperability, the EU can further develop a robust digital ecosystem that 
benefits citizens, businesses, and public authorities. secunet remains committed 
to supporting the European Commission and Member States in the 
implementation of these initiatives, drawing on its experience in cybersecurity, 
digital identity, and secure infrastructure. 
Ongoing collaboration between policymakers, industry, and civil society will be 
essential to ensure that Europe’s digital transformation is inclusive, secure, and 
sustainable.

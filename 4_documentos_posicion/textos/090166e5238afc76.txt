NCC Group’s response to the European Commission’s consultation on the 
Digital Omnibus 
 
NCC Group (trading as Fox -IT in the Netherlands) is a leading pan -European cybersecurity 
company. With over 25 years’ experience of delivering digital resilience services , we protect 
many of the digital products and systems that are used daily by European Union (EU) citizens. 
We are proud to be  trusted partners to governments in Europe and around the world , 
participating in numerous regional efforts to combat cybercrime  and contributing to national 
and supranational cyber resilience objectives. 
 
We welcome the opportunity to reply to the European Commission’s consultation. The Digital 
Omnibus signifies a strong opportunity to simplify and align cybersecurity rule s, while 
maintaining high standards of resilience. The net result would be reduced administrative 
burdens, clarity of obligations and greater investment in the EU’s collective digital resilience. 
Specifically, we ask that the following three areas be prioritised:   
 
1) Alignment of reporting requirements in cybersecurity 
 
The strengthening of the cybersecurity acquis in the previous term is a significant win  for 
critical infrastructure, businesses and citizens alike, creating a safer cybersecurity ecosystem 
for all and raising the standards across all industries and products. However, this new 
framework, while comprehensive, is not without flaws.  
 
As it stands, entities reporting cybersecurity incidents face an overly complex web of 
overlapping frameworks, with duplicate obligation s and requirements to report similar 
information to different entities and under different timelines. This can lead to inefficiencies in 
reporting where, for instance, it is necessary for an organisation to report under NIS2, GDPR, 
DORA, the AI Act , the Cyber Resilience Act  (CRA) and/or sector-specific regulation. In the 
case of NIS2, this is exacerbated by the fact that that file is a Directive, leading to slightly 
different regimes across the Member States . The European Cyber Security Organisation’s 
NIS2 Implementation report , for example, found that there were emerging differences in 
incident reporting requirements such as requiring initial incident reports within 6 hours 
compared to NIS2's baseline 24 -hour requirement and expanding beyond NIS2's focus on 
"significant" incidents by introducing multi-tiered classification systems1.  
 
Simplification should not mean eliminating rules, but rather streamlining reporting timelines, 
aligning definitions and clarifying scope and interplay between legislations. We also support 
the Commission’s proposal to establish a single reporting platform that would allow information 
on incidents and vulnerabilities to be transmitted to the right authority as needed . This would 
not only reduce the administrative burden for regulated organisations, but it would enable 
greater information sharing across legislative regimes and agencies, providing a more accurate 
picture of the EU-wide threat landscape.

2) Maintaining a strong level of cybersecurity in Europe 
 
Though we strongly support the EU’s commitment to reducing compliance burdens, we note 
that simplification cannot lead to a backsliding of cybersecurity rules, the foundation of which 
was built in the previous Commission.  
 
In a digitised economy that is underpinned by complex supply chains, strong cybersecurity is 
essential to resilience and growth. However, against an unstable geopolitical backdrop, the 
rate, severity and sophistication of cyberattacks and hybrid threats continues to grow.  Last 
year marked a record number of recorded global ransomware cases, with Europe being victim 
to around 1 in 4 ransomware incidents 2. These attacks have real -world consequences on 
governments, businesses and, ultimately, citizens . Cybersecurity is no longer just about 
protecting data, it is essential to the provision of public services and economic security. Indeed, 
studies show that most European SMEs would struggle with business continuity,  and more 
than half would even face bankruptcy, when faced with a single major cyber incident3. 
 
Legislation therefore acts as a crucial guardrail, ensuring that organisations implement strong 
cybersecurity protocols, that the products and services we all rely on are secure, and that the 
overall security threshold across the Union remains high. In other words, we believe that efforts 
to reduce compliance burden s should focus on streamlining processes and improving 
coherence, but caution against the lowering of the overall level of protection that safeguards 
Europe’s economy. 
 
3) Collaboration with likeminded partners 
 
Cybersecurity transcends political borders. The criminal landscape is complex, involves many 
actors and the complicit involvement of nation states, with cyberattacks very rarely originating 
from the EU alone. International cooperation with like -minded partners such as the UK  is, 
therefore, critical. 
 
We welcome the EU and Member States’ efforts of recent years to enhance global cooperation 
on cybersecurity through partnerships like the Counter Ransomware Initiative, and urge 
continued operational collaboration , information sharing  and alignment on  regulatory 
requirements where appropriate . As highlighted in the Commission’s recent International 
Digital Strategy , further work is also needed to enhance the mutual recognition of 
cybersecurity labs in trusted third countries. Such labs will be necessary to deliver the surge 
capacity required for compliance assessments under the Cyber Resilience Act , while also 
allowing EU-based labs to serve similar regimes in other markets in the longer-term.  
 
We thank you once more for the opportunity to feed into this consultation and remain at your 
disposal for any further input.

 
Digital omnibus – Consultation  
The consulting engineering perspective on the upcoming Roadmap 
 14 October 2025 
 
Introduction 
The European Federation of Engineering Consultancy Associations (EFCA), representing 
Europe’s consulting engineering firms active in the construction and infrastructure sector, 
welcomes the European Commission’s initiative to simplify and streamline the EU ’s digital 
acquis. Consulting engineers play a pivotal role in designing and delivering sustainable and 
innovative infrastructure, often using data -intensive and AI -enabled tools (e.g. BIM, digital 
twins, predictive modelling). As key integrators between public authorities, contractors, clients, 
and technology providers, consulting engineers face unique challenges in navigating 
overlapping digital, environmental, and construction -specific regulatory frameworks, 
particularly in cross -border projects.  A clear, proportionate, and harmonised regulatory 
framework is essential to support innovation while keeping compliance manageable for SMEs 
and mid-caps. 
A. Artificial Intelligence  
Artificial Intelligence Act (2024/1689) 
AI systems are increasingly being deployed in the construction sector for applications such as 
structural health monitoring, predictive maintenance, safety automation, design optimisation, and 
digital twins. Many of these systems may qualify as high-risk AI, triggering obligations related to 
documentation, human oversight, robustness, cybersecurity, and conformity assessment , and 
consequent liability, under the AI Act. Consulting engineers, in particular, are adopting AI tools for 
predictive maintenance, structural analysis, energy efficiency optimisation, and risk management, 
meaning that a growing share of engineering AI applications will need to com ply with these 
regulatory requirements. 
Concerns and recommendations: 
Consulting engineers face significant challenges in deploying AI in the construction sector, 
particularly SMEs that often lack the legal and technical capacity to fully understand and implement 
compliance obligations such as risk assessments, conformity ch ecks, and record- keeping. 
Uncertainty also arises around how AI Act requirements interact with sector -specific rules,

To address these challenges, it is essential to clarify the classification of high-risk AI for construction

B. Data acquis 
Free Flow of Non-Personal Data Regulation (2018/1807) 
Consulting engineering services rely heavily on cloud-based design tools, BIM platforms, and data 
analytics, which require secure and efficient cross -border data flows.  National localisation 
requirements or unclear porting obligations can impede cross-border collaboration, slow digital 
project delivery, and consequently even raise costs. 
Concerns and recommendations: 
Despite the Regulation, some barriers to data mobility persist due to national security or public-
interest justifications, which can complicate cross-border projects. Therefore, EFCA requests that 
the prohibition on data -localisation requirements be reinforced as such  requirements in 
procurement contracts can limit the ability to use the most efficient solutions. At least, transparent 
criteria for when restrictions are permitted should be there. 
When it comes to facilitating data porting and vendor interoperability, consulting engineers see 
much value in sector-specific guidance on file formats, transfer protocols, and backup procedures 
for large engineering datasets.

In addition, SMEs face uncertainty in assessing whether cross-border data storage complies with 
national rules. Therefore, support for SMEs and mid-caps is needed with simplified compliance 
measures and clear contractual guidance for cloud services. 
Lastly, one could consider providing harmonised enforcement guidance to avoid fragmentation 
between Member States that could hinder cross-border projects. For engineering consultancies 
coordinating multinational project teams and data environments, consistent enforcement is key to 
maintaining continuity across the project lifecycle and ensuring compliance for all stakeholders 
involved. 
Open Data Directive (2019/1024) 
Access to public sector information is critical for design, planning, risk assessment, and digital twin 
creation. Fragmented national implementations create barriers, especially for SMEs and mid-cap. 
Consulting engineers are major users and re -users of public -sector information, particularly in 
infrastructure planning, environmental impact assessments, and digital twin development. 
Concerns and recommendations: 
EFCA suggests that there is harmonisation and simplification of rules on access and re-use of public 
sector data, with explicit references to construction-relevant datasets. That is also to ensure the 
optimisation of delivery for cross -border projects. Engineering consultancies often act as the 
coordination point where data from multiple authorities, contractors, and jurisdictions converge, 
which amplifies the impact of inconsistent access and re-use conditions across borders. 
Moreover, high-value datasets, such as those related to geospatial, transport, and environmental 
information, are often not made available in a timely, machine-readable, or affordable manner, 
which increases integration costs. To address this, data should be shared in standardised, machine-
readable, and API -accessible formats, with clear and low -cost licensing conditions, thereby 
accelerating access and usability while reducing barriers for integration and application.  SMEs 
should also be provided with clear guidance and simplified compliance mechanisms to avoid 
administrative burdens that hinder innovation.  
Data Governance Act (2022/868) 
 
Consulting engineers increasingly rely on shared data platforms, infrastructure datasets, BIM 
models, sensor and IoT streams, and construction monitoring data to deliver projects efficiently. The 
Data Governance Act (DGA) facilitates data-sharing partnerships between public and private entities 
but can be complex for SMEs and mid-sized firms, particularly regarding contractual arrangements, 
licensing, and liability. For consulting engineers, the DGA is especially relevant in relation to data-
sharing mechanisms, the reuse of public-sector data, and the role of data intermediaries. Engineering 
projects now depend heavily on access to high-quality datasets, including geospatial information, 
environmental monitoring data, transport flows, and energy system data.

Concerns and recommendations: 
 
Key challenges for consulting engineers include the complexity of rules governing the reuse of 
protected data, such as commercially confidential or sensitive public-sector information, and the 
burdensome compliance obligations for companies acting as data intermediaries in project-related 
exchanges.  
 
Divergent implementation across Member States further risks uneven access to data, undermining 
cross-border projects. To address such issues, it is essential to simplify and harmonise rules for the 
reuse of public -sector data, create standardised, sector -specific templates for data -sharing 
agreements, and ensure interoperability and standards for engineering datasets, including 
geospatial data, BIM models, and digital twins. However, before simplification can be effective, the 
current complexity of contract ual relations between stakeholders, software vendors, and cloud 
providers must be clearly acknowledged and analysed. Existing collaborative platform contracts 
often fail to capture the cascade of responsibilities involved, particularly for SMEs. Therefore,  
regulation should first promote clear guidance that maps these contractual workflows and 
interdependencies, serving as a foundation for more realistic and effective simplification measures. 
 
Clear guidance on liability and data quality is needed to help consulting engineers understand 
responsibilities when shared datasets are incomplete or erroneous, while proportionate compliance 
requirements for SMEs and harmonised enforcement across Member States will enable efficient, 
secure, and consistent cross-border collaboration in construction and infrastructure projects. Given 
that engineering consultancies frequently coordinate diverse actors within a single project 
ecosystem, clarity on these cascading obligations is vital to avoid regulatory gaps or overlaps in cross-
border consortia 
 
Data Act (2023/2854) 
Although EFCA understands that the Data Act is not part of this Call for Evidence, we would like to 
briefly stress, as per recent communication to DG CNECT (letter sent 16 July to CNECT.G, G.1 & 
CNECT.01) that we support the timely and undiluted provisions of the Act, particularly its core 
provisions in Chapters II, III, and VI and urge the Commission to uphold their full implementation 
as scheduled. 
Conclusion 
EFCA welcomes the Digital Omnibus as an important step to reduce fragmentation, legal 
uncertainty, and administrative costs in the EU’s digital acquis. For consulting engineers in the 
construction sector, simplification and harmonisation across all relevant instruments are essential 
to unlock the benefits of data-driven and AI-enabled innovation while keeping compliance feasible 
for SMEs and mid-sized firms. Consistent enforcement and harmonised application of rules across 
Member States are crucial to facilitate cross-border engineering projects and enable consulting 
engineers to focus on delivering sustainable, safe, and resilient infrastructure for Europe’s green 
and digital transitions.

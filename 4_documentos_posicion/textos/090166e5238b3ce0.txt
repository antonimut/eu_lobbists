 
 
BZDV - Bundesverband Digitalpublisher und Zeitungsverleger e. V. 
MVFP - Medienverband der freien Presse e. V .   
Comments  
on the Call for Evidence – Digital Omnibus 
 
14 October 2025 
 
BDZV and MVFP welcome this opportunity to provide their feedback to the Call for Evidence on 
the Digital Omnibus, which could aƯect several pieces of legislation of great relevance for the 
press sector. While we share the Commission’s simpliﬁcation objectives and welcome the 
intention to ease the administrative burdens and costs for businesses, we are alarmed by the 
prospect set out in the Call for Evidence to centralise consent management options.  
Were that to happen, in no way would this constitute a mere simpliﬁcation exercise. It would 
severely undermine publishers’ ability to provide and ﬁnance their digital newspapers and 
magazines, threatening press freedom and pluralism at a time when our information space 
is ﬂooded with disinformation. 
Press publications play an essential democratic role by providing citizens with professional 
editorial information, contributing to an open, dynamic and informed public debate. Press 
publications are also businesses that need to be able to provide their product and remain 
ﬁnancially viable in an increasingly competitive digital single market. The democratic 
mission of the press is intertwined with its business side and market reality, especially in this time 
of geopolitical and economic tension. A free press can only be free, if it can ﬁnance its operations 
in the market. 
Simpliﬁcation eƯorts should not exceed the simpliﬁcation mandate and de facto turn into a 
means of introducing fundamental changes to secondary law.  As the Digital Omnibus will not be 
supported by an impact assessment, it is all the more crucial that it truly remains limited to 
simpliﬁcation adjustments. 
Any change based on erroneous, non-evidence-based presumptions, including for example the 
claim that centralising consent management would cut costs for business, not only raises 
questions in terms of democratic legitimacy, but would in fact cause the Omnibus to deviate 
from the spirit of the original law and backﬁre against the very businesses it aims to support. 
 
I. Centralised Consent 
BDZV and MVFP are highly concerned about the reference to “central cookie management 
mechanisms” in the Call for Evidence for the Digital Omnibus. While such solution may appear 
sensible at ﬁrst, in practice it would destroy the business models of the digital press and other 
digital businesses. The damage that centralised consent mechanisms would bring to the 
press sector cannot be overstated. It would eƯectively jeopardise the provision of digital

newspaper and magazines, ultimately leading both to a decrease in media pluralism and for 
consumers the amount of freely accessible, advertising ﬁnanced newspapers and magazines.  
Ever stricter data protection laws have led to a legal framework in which the digital press can 
only operate, let alone be ﬁnanced, with the consent of a large number of individual readers. 
The design and delivery of editorial content, protection of integrity and against ad blockers, 
audience measurement, ﬁnancing through advertising and sales to digital readers, including 
subscriptions, are typical conditions for the existence of the digital press, many of which are not 
possible without speciﬁc consent. For this reason, and contrary to a common misconception, 
the digital press does not rely on consent and data processing only for advertising purposes. 
The press relies on consent and data processing also for the provision of digital press oƯerings, 
including paid content as well as editorial and business development. 
As such, where individual consent is required by law, it must be a fundamental principle to ensure 
that (i) publishers can obtain and manage consent in a practical manner (i.e., right to request; 
no prohibition on requesting), and (ii) that any consent given is directly implemented and 
cannot simply be ignored by software settings, consent managers or other gatekeepers (e.g., 
obligation to implement for browsers, personal consent management tools, etc.) 
In other words, digital publishing – including provision, operation, editorial purposes, and 
ﬁnancing – is only possible with consent for various purposes. No digital publication can be 
successful without data processing and analytics. As such, we are, to say the least, surprised 
by the unsubstantiated claim in the ‘Call for Evidence’ that centralising consent would cut costs 
for businesses by reducing the number of cases in which consent must be obtained.  
With regard to the ﬁnancing aspect, personalised online advertising is an essential source of 
revenues for press publishers, allowing them to monetise visits by users who do not have a 
subscription – and to keep the price of subscriptions more accessible. Publications that mainly 
rely on digital subscriptions for their ﬁnancing also need personalised advertising, as most of 
them have a selection of articles accessible and ﬁnanced through advertising, that are essential 
for attracting subscribers by allowing them to test the content ﬁrst. 
If the EU were to make obtaining consent more diƯicult, or even impossible, through centralised 
consent management – and considering that there are no scalable alternatives to personalised 
ads in the digital environment – not only would publishers lose signiﬁcant revenue, but the gap 
between European companies and digital Gatekeepers would also widen, further 
strengthening the latter’s data supremacy and dominance in the digital advertising market. 
In fact, Gatekeeper platforms with their walled gardens and login models will not face any 
diƯiculties in obtaining consent, while publishers would be technically blocked from requesting 
consent by centralised signals. 
As highlighted by the consensus that emerged among media companies and press publishers at 
Commission workshops on the so-called ‘Cookie Pledge’ , contextual advertising alone is 
currently not ﬁnancially viable for the press and media, as the value of the advertising is 
signiﬁcantly lower compared to targeted ads. Examples such as the Dutch online publication 
Tweakers, which reverted to personalised advertising for ﬁnancial reasons after a period of using 
contextual advertising alone, conﬁrm that publishers cannot ﬁnance their editorial operations 
without relying on personalised advertising.

The so-called ‘consent fatigue’ is generally cited as a reason for such measures. In this context 
it is important to point out, on the hand, that the necessity to obtain consent is not caused by 
dishonest compliance by the industry. On the contrary, it stems from the legal requirements of 
the European data protection acquis for online service providers to obtain consent for the 
majority of purposes necessary for providing and ﬁnancing a viable online business in 
compliance with data protection rules. The increase in consent requirements is a result of EU 
legislation and guidance by the EDPB and national Data Protection Authorities (DPAs). 
On the other hand, it is worth putting the scale of the so called ‘consent fatigue’ into perspective. 
Figures from press publishers in Europe paint a diƯerent picture compared to the one often 
shared in public debates. Examples from publications show that that up to 90% of readers of 
digital press publications are not continuously confronted with consent requests. In other words, 
millions of digital press readers are already able to access their favourite publications 
without being asked for consent each time because they allow consent cookies to be stored 
when they ﬁrst give their consent. 
For example, in the case of a digital magazine, over a period of a month, approx. 29 million 
readers, which equates 73% of their readership, were able to access the magazine without having 
to give their consent again. Over the same one-month period, 80% of the visits to several other 
magazines were done without having to ask for consent again because the readers had stored on 
their device the consent given on the ﬁrst visit. Equally, several digital newspapers with a 
cumulative daily reach of over 10 million users only had to show consent banners to around 10% 
of their readers, as the remaining 90% stored their consent and were therefore able to access the 
digital newspaper directly. 
As mentioned above, we also believe that centralised consent would only further reinforce 
existing gatekeepers, consolidate their data hegemony and further distort competition in 
the advertising market in their favour – or establish the providers of centralised consent 
solutions as new gatekeepers. 
In light of the above, we were alarmed to see that centralised consent is under consideration for 
the Digital Omnibus, with no Impact Assessment, when nothing less than the very digital 
business models and ﬁnancial sustainability of the press and most digital services is at stake. We 
do not consider such a proposal to be a technical adjustment, after negotiations on the e-
Privacy Regulation proposal were blocked for years, partly due to the acknowledgement by 
decision-makers of the existential threat that centralised consent would pose to the digital 
economy and the digital press in particular. Criticism over centralised consent also 
contributed to the decision to abandon the ‘Cookie Pledge’ initiative. This goes to show that there 
is no consensus on centralised consent in the institutions and the industry rejects it, so an Impact 
Assessment would at the very least be necessary before such a proposal is even considered. 
While we agree with the need for simpliﬁcation, removing consent at website-level would not 
constitute simpliﬁcation, but a massive threat to the digital press and its business models. 
Alleged ‘consent fatigue’ should not be used to promote centralised consent as a tool for 
simpliﬁcation and reducing burdens, as these measures are considered highly problematic by 
the industry and have not been able to obtain political or industry backing during the negotiations 
on the e-Privacy Regulation.  
Nonetheless, simpliﬁcation could to some degree be achieved by limiting the purposes for 
which consent is required, the level of granularity and the information to display. If the EDPB

II. Room for Simpliﬁcation  
We agree with the need to avoid legal overlaps, excessive red-tape, fragmentation and 
uncertainty. The Digital Omnibus proposal may be helpful in this regard, for example by bringing 
together rules that concern the same area but are currently spread across various pieces of 
legislation. 
To avoid overwhelming businesses and SMEs, it is important that any measure is proportionate 
to their scale and level of risk posed. This would prevent situations where the administrative and 
compliance burdens eƯectively discourage innovation, investments or legitimate practices1. 
As the e-Privacy Directive (ePD) is in the scope of the Omnibus, we would like to express our 
support for to at least exclude certain low-risk practices from the consent requirement. 
Doing so would also help simplify consent banners and, as a result, not overload consumers with 
information. For example, the EDPB acknowledged that the exceptions from the consent 
obligation for security updates and audience measurement proposed in the e-Privacy 
Regulation are related to speciﬁc types of processing with very limited privacy risks for the users. 
Particularly, as audience measurement is essential for media to gauge their audience, assess 
content performance and take investment decisions, we support an exception for measurement 
regardless of the speciﬁc conﬁguration of the collaboration between the publication and the 
measurement company and of whether certain technologies or methodologies are used. 
Furthermore, we would also support an exemption for the use of cookies necessary for purposes 
related to the correct provision and functioning of press websites, such as content and ad 
performance, logins, fraud prevention, analytics, the use of metered models etc.  
Against this background, we would like to highlight that a consistent approach between the 
Commission and the EDPB is a precondition to avoid legal uncertainty. Currently, the 
Commission’s goals for simpliﬁcation are not reﬂected in the EDPB’s attempts to extend the 
requirement for consent to more and more purposes, to the detriment of user experience and of 
other legal basis for data processing foreseen in the General Data Protection Regulation.

It is indisputable that one of the core objectives of the AI Act is ensuring compliance with Union 
law by AI Actors and securing a level playing ﬁeld whereby AI companies operating in the Union 
market are subject to a common set of rules, irrespective of whether those providers are 
established or located within the Union or in a third country. 
Against this background, BDZV and MVFP oppose any potential use of the Digital Omnibus as a 
means to either dilute, in any manner, delete altogether, or postpone the entry into application of 
the copyright-relevant obligations incumbent on providers of general-purpose AI models per Art. 
53(1)(c) and (d), which represent but an initial, yet still insuƯicient step that must necessarily be 
built upon.  
On the contrary, as the Commission seeks to achieve optimal implementation of the AI Act, the 
simpliﬁcation process presents an opportunity to revisit and greatly improving the AI Act 
implementation package adopted in July this year, comprising the General-Purpose AI (GPAI) 
Code of Practice, the AI OƯice’s transparency template for GPAI model providers to summarise 
the training content, and the GPAI guidelines, which represented a missed opportunity to provide 
meaningful protection of intellectual property rights in the context of generative AI and fell short 
of the EU AI Act’s own objectives. Only in this way will the objective laid down in the AI Act of 
helping rightsholders exercise and enforce their rights under Union law may be achieved and legal 
certainty for all stakeholders while be acquired, while also ensuring that the EU’s AI and digital 
frameworks remain coherent, innovation-friendly, and ﬁrmly respectful of intellectual property 
rights.

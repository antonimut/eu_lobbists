Digital Omnibus and Digital Simpliﬁcation Agenda 
  
October 14, 2025  
  
The Business Software Alliance (BSA) welcomes the opportunity to provide an answer to the European 
Commission’s Call for Evidence on the Digital Omnibus and Digital Simpliﬁcation Agenda. 
  
BSA is the global trade association of the enterprise software industry, representing companies 1 that are 
leaders in artiﬁcial intelligence, cybersecurity, cloud computing, and other cutting-edge technologies. We 
work in over 20 markets in the US, Europe, and Asia, advocating for policies that build trust in technology 
so that every industry sector and the public can beneﬁt from innovation.

The EU’s Digital Ambition: Balancing Innovation and Regulation 
 
The European Union (EU) aims to be a global leader in digital innovation and regulation. Its ambition —to 
build a digital economy that safeguards rights, fosters trust, and fuels competitiveness —is both 
commendable and essential for Europe’s long-term resilience and prosperity. 
 
Yet, Europe’s digital landscape has grown increasingly complex. The rapid succession of horizontal and 
sectoral digital laws—from the Data Act and AI Act to NIS2, DORA, and the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA)—
has created a dense and often fragmented web of obligations. The result is a regulatory architecture that, 
while well- intentioned, could risk undermining innovation, competitiveness, and investment due to 
overlapping, inconsistent, and sometimes contradictory, requirements. 
 
To deliver on its Simpler and Faster Europe agenda, the Commission must focus on practical simpliﬁcation 
that enables compliance, coherence, and innovation —especially for SMEs and mid -cap ﬁrms driving 
Europe’s digital transformation. 
 
BSA Recommendations for Legislative Simpliﬁcation 
 
BSA’s recommendations build on the Commission’s Digital Omnibus initiative and the forthcoming Digital 
Fitness Check. They are structured around ﬁve pillars: 
1. Ensuring cross-legislation consistency and coherence; 
2. Reducing overlaps and compliance duplication; 
3. Strengthening proportionality and diﬀerentiation;  
4. Fostering a culture of co-regulation and dialogue; and 
5. Sector-Speciﬁc Simpliﬁcation Priorities.

1. Ensure Cross-Legislation Consistency 
 
Current Challenge 
 
The EU’s digital rulebook is fragmented across multiple instruments, each with its own scope, deﬁnitions, 
and enforcement mechanisms. Businesses —especially those operating cross -border—must interpret 
overlapping terms such as “data controller,” “data holder,” “provider,” or “incident,” often without guidance 
on their interrelation. 
 
What BSA Recommends the Commission To Do: 
 
• Codify the Digital Acquis: 
Create an accessible, consolidated codiﬁcation of the EU’s digital legislation and its implementing 
acts. This should provide a clear map of overlaps, contradictions, and implementation timelines, 
allowing regulators and businesses to identify where simpliﬁcation is possible. 
• Standardise Core Deﬁnitions: 
Develop a shared digital lexicon—harmonising recurring terms (e.g., “data holder,” “AI provider,” 
“gatekeeper,” “signiﬁcant/severe incident”) across acts. This will reduce interpretation gaps, 
prevent inconsistent national transpositions, and support legal certainty. 
• Horizontal Implementation Guidance: 
Publish cross-cutting guidance on the interplay between key legislative frameworks (e.g., GDPR, 
Data Act, AI Act, CRA). This should clarify compliance in hybrid cases—such as AI models trained on 
mixed datasets or cross-border cloud infrastructure. 
• Inter-Agency Coordination Taskforce: 
Establish a Digital Regulation Coherence Forum bringing together DG CONNECT, DG JUST, DG 
COMP , DG FISMA, ENISA, the EDPB, and the AI Oﬃce to align implementation, interpretation, 
timing, and enforcement practices. 
• Pre-Legislative “Interoperation Check”: 
Introduce a mandatory coherence test in the Better Regulation process to assess proposed 
legislation against existing frameworks before adoption. 
• Implementation Timing Alignment: 
Ensure new obligations enter into force only once supporting secondary legislation, guidance, and 
standards are published—avoiding premature compliance uncertainty. Moreover, provide for a 
certain amount of time (at least 3 years) once the legislation (and its entire secondary acts) apply 
before starting any review and/or amendments to it. In line with simpliﬁcation, it would provide 
institutions, businesses, citizens to apply or comply with the legislation, and suﬃcient them for the 
legislator to assess the eﬀect of such application and compliance in practice before considering any 
changes. 
 
2. Reduce Overlaps and Compliance Duplication 
 
Current Challenge

incident or process often triggers multiple notiﬁcations, audits, and reporting duties to diﬀerent 
authorities. These ineﬃciencies undermine resilience by d iverting resources away from actual risk 
management. 
 
What BSA Recommends the Commission To Do: 
• Uniﬁed Incident Reporting Framework: 
Establish a single, harmonised EU cybersecurity reporting template and incident classiﬁcation 
taxonomy under ENISA’s coordination. Apply a “one-stop-shop” principle across NIS2, DORA, CRA, 
and GDPR. 
o Introduce tiered reporting thresholds to prevent overreporting of minor incidents. 
o Remove impractical “weekend reporting” requirements. 
o Allow cross-border operators to report to their main establishment authority, which then 
coordinates at EU level. 
• Audit and Certiﬁcation Mutual Recognition: 
Develop a policy requiring Member States to mutually recognise cybersecurity audits conducted 
under another Member State’s regime, provided equivalence in scope. This would reduce audit 
multiplication and costs—estimated to exceed €1 million per multinational operator under NIS2, 
while allowing for harmonization, and thus simpliﬁcation, across the EU. 
• Data Act Implementation Alignment: 
Postpone the full application of the Data Act until national authorities are designated and 
interoperability standards ﬁnalised. Encourage a phased approach focused ﬁrst on interoperability, 
before access and switching obligations become binding. 
• Simpliﬁed Reporting Portal: 
Build a digital-by-default reporting platform consolidating all compliance submissions (incident 
reports, certiﬁcations, transparency disclosures) across laws. 
• Regulatory Compromise Mandate: 
Introduce a statutory obligation for digital regulators to consult each other, seek compromise and 
avoid contradictory enforcement in overlapping areas, consistent with the Treaty’s principle of 
sincere cooperation (Article 4(3) TEU). 
• Cross-Law Harmonised Decision-Making: 
Require regulators to coordinate enforcement outcomes for overlapping laws, particularly between 
data protection, cybersecurity, and AI authorities, under the aegis of the EU Commission. 
 
 
3. Strengthen Proportionality and Diﬀerentiation 
 
Current Challenge 
 
EU digital laws often apply identical compliance burdens to high-risk and low-risk activities, consumers and 
businesses, and to SMEs as well as large enterprises. This one -size-ﬁts-all approach discourages smaller 
innovators and limits competitiveness. A tailored approach, that would ﬁt the level of obligations to the 
level of risk/impact, as well as to the nature (consumer or business) and size of the entity (SMEs to large 
enterprises) facing the obligation, should be promoted. 
 
What BSA Recommends the Commission To Do: 
• Systematic Proportionality Clauses: 
Embed proportionality tests in all implementing acts, distinguishing obligations by risk level, 
business model, and size (e.g., diﬀerent templates for SMEs, mid-caps, and large enterprises). 
• Simpliﬁed Compliance Toolkits: 
Develop Commission-published model templates and FAQs for SMEs—similar to GDPR Codes of 
Conduct—to help small ﬁrms comply eﬃciently with the Data Act, CRA, and AI Act. 
• Broader, Risk-Based SME Exemptions in GDPR Implementation: Introduce practical exemptions to 
ease SME compliance under GDPR. 
o Article 7 (Consent): Waive formal proof and record-keeping for low-risk SME processing. 
o Article 28 (Processor Contracts): Allow use of simpliﬁed Commission templates except 
when handling sensitive data. 
o Article 33 (Breach Notiﬁcation): Exempt SMEs from notifying minor, low-risk breaches if 
documented and mitigated. 
o Article 35 (DPIA): Lower DPIA thresholds for SMEs; provide simpliﬁed templates. 
o Article 37 (DPO): Clarify “large-scale processing”; allow exemptions for limited, non-core 
data activities. 
• Phased Implementation: 
Align compliance timelines with product lifecycles—especially under the CRA and AI Act—to avoid 
forcing premature redesigns while taking into account the evolutive nature of such technologies. 
• Promote Self-Assessment: 
Allow self-assessment as the default for low-risk entities, reserving third-party audits for genuinely 
high-risk products or systems. 
• Voluntary Certiﬁcation Alternatives: 
Expand voluntary certiﬁcation under the Cybersecurity Act and AI Act, building on ISO/IEC 
standards. Promote equivalence with international schemes (e.g., SOC 2, ISO 27001). 
• SME Support Mechanisms: 
Channel Digital Europe Programme funding into compliance capacity-building—e.g., regulatory 
sandboxes, training, and technical assistance hubs – for companies, and in particular for SMEs. 
 
 
4. Foster a Culture of Co-Regulation, Partnership and Dialogue 
 
Current Challenge 
 
The pace of technological change outstrips traditional legislative cycles, and enforcement often remains 
siloed. Sustainable simpliﬁcation requires continuous dialogue between regulators, industry, and civil 
society. 
 
 
What BSA Recommends the Commission To Do: 
• Scale Up Regulatory Sandboxes: 
Expand and interlink existing sandboxes under the AI Act, Data Governance Act, and eIDAS. 
Sandboxes should allow controlled real-world testing, mutual learning, and ﬂexible compliance 
experimentation. 
• Establish a Permanent Digital Simpliﬁcation Forum: 
Create a standing platform for structured dialogue between industry, regulators, and the 
Commission on simpliﬁcation priorities. 
• Co-Regulatory Mechanisms: 
Encourage co-developed Codes of Conduct and technical standards, overseen by the Commission, 
to translate legislation into practical compliance models. 
• Stakeholder Consultation Mandate: 
Require regulators to consult aﬀected sectors timely before issuing guidance or implementing rules 
with signiﬁcant operational impact. 
• International Standards Alignment: 
Mandate the use of globally recognised standards (ISO/IEC, NIST , OECD) across certiﬁcation, audit, 
and interoperability frameworks to prevent EU-speciﬁc fragmentation. 
• Practical Regulatory Guidance: 
Ensure all implementing guidelines are clear, operational, and accessible. Include SMEs and civil 
society in their drafting to ensure they are feasible and balanced. 
  
5. Sector-Speciﬁc Simpliﬁcation Priorities 
 
Cybersecurity: Unify reporting under ENISA’s coordination; ensure risk -based, proportionate incident 
thresholds; and align CRA and NIS2 implementation timelines and authorities. 
 
EU Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA): 
• Contractual Flexibility: Permit ﬁnancial entities and ICT providers to agree reasonable alternative 
clauses meeting DORA’s objectives rather than prescriptive contractual wording. 
• Oversight of Critical ICT Providers: Allow self-certiﬁcation or third-party audits demonstrating 
compliance to reduce intrusive or duplicative supervision. 
 
Data Regulation: Consolidate rules on data access, reuse, and transfer; align Data Act and GDPR 
mechanisms; safeguard trade secrets; and modernise ePrivacy to reduce consent fatigue. 
 
EU Data Act Clariﬁcations: 
• Legacy Contracts: Clarify that early termination penalties equal to the remaining contract value, 
recognising pre-existing expectations and investments made before the Act’s entry into force. 
• New Contracts: Permit proportionate early termination penalties covering reasonable losses—e.g., 
cloud operations, personnel, discounts, or dedicated infrastructure—so providers can recover fair 
costs without distorting competition. 
 
ePrivacy Simpliﬁcation and Harmonisation: 
• Expand the scope of cookies exempt from explicit consent to include ﬁrst-party analytics and 
functional cookies that enhance service performance and user experience. 
• Call for a binding European Commission decision harmonising cookie consent rules across Member 
States, eliminating divergent DPA interpretations and establishing uniform, practical standards. 
 
Artiﬁcial Intelligence: Empower the AI Oﬃce as the single coordinator of implementation; postpone high-
risk obligations until harmonised standards are published. 
 
Digital Identity and Trust Services: Streamline accreditation for trust service providers; adopt 
interoperable open standards; and harmonise supervision under eIDAS 2.0. 
 
 
* * * 
 
To unlock Europe’s full digital potential, the EU must move from legislative proliferation to strategic 
coherence. Simpliﬁcation should aim not to weaken EU digital legislation, but to make them practical, 
proportionate, and predictable. 
 
The Commission should prioritise eﬀective implementation of existing rules, evaluate real- world impact, 
and only then propose new initiatives when demonstrably necessary. 
 
Through coordinated simpliﬁcation —coherence across laws, reduced duplication, proportionate 
compliance, and structured co-regulation—the EU can achieve its twin goals: a competitive, innovative, 
predictable and trusted digital economy. 
 
BSA stands ready to work with the European Commission, Member States, and stakeholders to ensure that 
simpliﬁcation delivers tangible beneﬁts for Europe’s economy, citizens, and global digital leadership.

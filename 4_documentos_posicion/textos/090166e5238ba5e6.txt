Introduction 
Intesa Sanpaolo welcomes this Call for Evidence as the need for simplification 
and clarification of the EU digital regulatory framework is urgent. Financial 
institutions face significant burdens and regulatory risks due to fragmented 
legislation, overlappi ng regulatory regimes, and inconsistent interpretations 
across multiple authorities which prevent legal certainty and predictable 
outcomes. These challenges increase costs, hinder competitiveness, and may 
divert resources away from operational and security  priorities. Any 
simplification initiative must lead to in precise rules which, in a technology-wise 
manner, enable adaptation to evolving scenarios and operational 
innovation. Equally important is institutional -level coordination, ensuring that 
European and national competent authorities communicate effectively.  
Within the current Call for Evidence, this means addressing: 
- The clarification and streamlining of core definitions which often rely on 
excessively broad concepts, leaving interpretative discretion. This is 
particularly evident in the distinction between personal and non -personal 
data, where case law and regulatory guidance have failed to provide 
clear operational boundaries. Similarly, the definition of AI systems remains 
difficult to apply in practice, while incident reporting obligations are 
fragmented across DORA, CRA, GDPR, NIS2 and other frameworks. A single 
shared taxonomy, with aligned thresholds and a unified EU reporting 
channel could significantly reduce duplicated compliance efforts. 
- The contractual imbalance in the digital ecosystem, where companies are 
frequently bound by hardly negotiable or non -negotiable standard terms 
imposed by ‘big tech’ players. Furthermore, within the AI environment, AI 
system deployers and downstream provide rs often face significant

1. Definition review (personal/non -personal data, AI systems, and IT 
incident) 
There is an urgent need to establish rules and definitions that do not rely 
exclusively on broad or abstract principles, which may leave excessive 
interpretative discretion and ultimately result in cumbersome compliance 
approaches. In particular, it is worth noting that: 
- The definition of non -personal data under the Free Flow of Non -
Personal Data Regulation builds on the definition of personal data 
under the GDPR. However, the definition of personal data is 
extremely broad, and recent case law from the Court of Justice of 
the European Union has confirmed how difficult it is to delineate its 
boundaries. The definition of non -personal data is also critical to 
capturing the opportunities offered by AI systems. The challenges of 
reconciling GDPR - which applies to personal data - with AI systems 
are well-known. Attempts to determine when data used for training 
no longer qualify as personal data (e.g. by the Hamburg Authority) 
have not been endorsed by the EDPB, leaving interpretive 
boundaries uncertain. 
- The current definition of AI systems is excessively complex and 
operationally burdensome, making it difficult for stakeholders to 
consistently determine whether a given system falls within the 
regulatory scope. Existing guidance brought by the European 
Commission Guidelines on the definition of an artificial intelligence 
system established by Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act) has not 
provided sufficient clarity, for instance, with regards to the concept 
of “inference”. Further clear guidance on the practic al application 
of the definition is needed to enable immediate and consistent 
identification of AI systems in scope, regardless of sector or technical 
ecosystem in which they are used. 
- Clarification is also needed for the definition of incidents, the cases 
in which notification is required, and the timelines for notification. The 
draft guidance on reporting serious AI incidents recently issued by 
5 
 
the Commission already provides a clear illustration of the 
complexity involved, even within its limited scope, in determining (i) 
when an incident must be notified, (ii) how it should be notified, and 
(iii) the applicable timelines. A simplification could  involve unifying 
notification procedures through a single channel, with clear rules - 
without relying on general principles - for specific types of incidents, 
and timelines starting from the closure of the incident allowing 
stakeholders to focus on managi ng the incident and protecting 
infrastructures and individuals. As mentioned below in the 
Streamlined Institutional Architecture section, we support the 
introduction of an EU Hub (Articles 19 - 21 DORA) to centralize the 
reporting of (at least major) ICT-related incidents. The Hub should be 
the primary reporting point, avoiding duplication of reporting 
obligations to individual authorities. Competent authorities should be 
responsible for coordinating among themselves to verify notifications 
and carry out the required follow-ups (if any).

2. Significant imbalance in the digital ecosystem 
The current regulatory framework insufficiently addresses the significant 
contractual asymmetries within the digital ecosystem, where stakeholders are 
often forced to accept standard terms imposed by ‘big tech’ players. This 
would be increasingly critical if we consider that AI technologies are leading 
to significant market share concentration: for instance, lacking a specific 
regulatory action, deployers or downstream stakeholders integrating 
General-Purpose AI models to AI Systems may face increasing diff iculties 
setting up fair contractual arrangements due to reduced bargaining power.  
Further consequences may include imbalanced allocation of responsibilities 
and asymmetrical liabilities regimes governed by such standard contractual 
terms imposed unilaterally by model providers. This could result in situations 
where stakeholders are prev ented from complying with key regulatory 
obligations (e.g., auditability, risk management, etc.).  
In light of the above, the European Commission should consider targeted 
interventions to address such imbalances.

3. GDPR and AI interplay 
European Institutions should take a clear, coordinated and forward -looking 
position on the use of personal data for the training of artificial intelligence 
systems, by providing consistent, practical and unambiguous guidance on 
how such processing should be conducted in compliance with data 
protection principles as well as other applicable laws (e.g., employment law, 
intellectual property law, etc.). 
In doing so, it shall be ensured a uniform interpretation and application of the 
GDPR across the Union, including in the context of AI development and 
deployment. A consistent approach is essential to avoid fragmentation, 
reduce legal uncertainty and ensur e a level playing field for all stakeholders 
operating in the European ecosystem. When addressing the interaction 
between AI and data protection law, EU institutions and authorities should also 
focus on the substance rather than the form of compliance mech anisms, 
aiming at the effective protection of data subjects’ rights and interests over 
purely formal or procedural considerations. This process may require clarifying 
or, where appropriate, adapting the interpretation and practical application 
of GDPR obli gations to reflect the specificities of AI technologies and 
concrete data usage. 
Against this background, it would be advisable to: 
- Clarify and provide a structured alignment between the GDPR and the AI 
Act, particularly regarding the interplay between the notion of 
“automated decision-making” processes under Article 22 GDPR and “high-
risk AI systems” under the EU AI Act. While we agree on the fact that the AI 
Act should not derogate the GDPR, we believe that a lack of clarity persists 
where AI tools significantly contribute to employment -related decisions 
without making the final decision, potentially triggering both regulatory 
regimes simultaneously, despite the exemptions provided by Article 6 AI 
Act. Furthermore, it would be helpful to coordinate Article 22 GDPR with 
Article 86 AI Act, as well as practical indications about how human 
8 
 
oversight could be granted and explanation obligations can be effectively 
fulfilled. 
- Enhance coherence and reduce duplication of efforts between Article 36 
GDPR regarding the Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) and Article 
27 AI Act regarding the Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (FRIA). 
Considering the scope of such assessments, w e encourage that the FRIA 
template that will be issued by the AI Office shall also provide clear 
guidance on how the DPIA should be complemented to meet obligations 
under Article 27 AI Act by references, e.g., to structured human oversight, 
decision traceability, relevant security measures, etc. 
- Transparency obligations’ interplay under the GDPR and the AI Act could 
be clarified to avoid potential duplication of information contained in 
privacy notices and AI transparency notices. Ideally, where the information 
is addressed to the same recipient, the information contained in one 
notice should not be repeated in the other document.

4. Streamlined institutional architecture  
The role and coordination of competent supervisory authorities at the EU level 
should be (re)designed to address the growing fragmentation that financial 
institutions face when applying multiple regulatory frameworks. Entities 
operating across different Me mber States often need to interact 
simultaneously with, among others, data protection authorities, AI supervisory 
bodies, financial regulators, cybersecurity agencies and consumer protection 
authorities, all of which have overlapping competences on the sam e issues. 
This results in legal uncertainty, duplication of requests for information, 
inconsistent interpretations of regulatory obligations and unnecessary 
administrative complexity.  
More structured and centralised supervisory architecture could therefore be 
considered. One option would be to identify a single “lead authority” which 
could oversee cross -cutting digital regulatory areas such as AI, data 
protection and cybersecurity, so t hat entities operating across the European 
Union (and/or the European Economic Area) may benefit from a consistent 
regulatory and enforcement approach. 
Likewise, incident definitions and notification timelines must be coordinated 
and clarified, ideally with a single channel for reporting and clear timelines. 
While a big step towards coordination of incident reporting has been made 
with DORA, there are sti ll frameworks with diverging timelines, taxonomies, 
thresholds, and templates (e.g., GDPR, SSM requirements). The financial 
services sector will undoubtedly benefit from harmonized incident reporting 
requirements and a shared taxonomy with consistent thres holds for 
“significant”, “major”, or “severe” incidents. As for the notification channel, 
the preferred option would be the creation of a single European reporting 
hub as per Articles 19 - 21 DORA. Information submitted through this entry point 
could be tr ansferred to other relevant authorities (including those under the 
Cyber Resilience Act, GDPR, etc.), with additional positive outcomes in terms 
of mutual information -sharing, crisis management, and enhanced digital 
resilience. Additionally, joint supervis ory opinions or coordinated decision -

5. IP rights and AI-training datasets 
A thriving data economy requires a strategic shift towards exchanges of data 
across sectors. The European Union can help overcome persistent obstacles 
in AI development: for example, enhancing high data quality accessibility 
would enable more efficient model training, lower negotiation and 
transaction costs, and facilitate the emergence of sophisticated, data -
intensive AI systems.  
However, in our view, any data sharing among institutions, except when a 
legal obligation already exists, should remain purely voluntary, and exclusively 
market, rather than regulatory top-down, driven. This is particularly true for the 
financial services sector, which is already subject to data sharing obligations 
under the PSD2 (currently under revision). For instance the European 
Commission’s proposal for a Regulation on Financial Data Access (FiDA) 
would add another mandatory sector-specific framework which, considering 
the absence of a clear customer / market demand, may unreasonably 
undermine the competitiveness of European financial institutions, impose 
unjustified costs and divert resources that could be allocated to other 
projects, such as funding k ey strategic sectors and investments for 
technological innovation in digital transformation. 
As far as intellectual property rights over the content used and to be used for 
training AI systems are concerned, as well as the rights over outputs 
generated by or with AI systems, clear and predictable rules are essential to 
avoid legal uncertainty, allowing stakeholders to determine in advance what 
may or may not be done, and who will hold the rights to AI-generated works. 
At the same time, there is a need for simplification, flexibility, and 
harmonization at European level for such use.  
Any regulatory streamlining in this area must respect the existing acquis, 
notably Directive 96/9/EC on database protection and Directive (EU) 
2016/943 on trade secrets. To provide businesses with greater legal certainty, 
the European Commission should con sider the introduction of harmonised

contractual templates for licensing copyrighted content also for AI training 
purposes.

6. AI liability allocation 
While we acknowledge the recent withdrawal of the AI Liability Directive 
proposal, we believe that soft -law guidance for AI -related liability should be 
nevertheless developed to address damages caused by AI systems, aiming 
to eliminate the current fragmentation and overlapping rules that exist across 
multiple legal domains. Indeed, as of today, responsibilities may fall under 
national civil law, consumer protection regulations, and product liability 
provisions, each of which may operate differently at natio nal and 
international levels. This lack of coherence generates legal uncertainty, 
complicates cross-border enforcement, and increases the risks of inconsistent 
judgments. Such guidance should not be designed as mere incremental 
obligations or liabilities t o the existing system, avoiding presumptions of 
causality, potentially distorting the risk allocation and undermining the 
balanced application of existing liability frameworks. Should it be decided to 
resume the AI Liability Directive adoption process, to ensure consistency we 
would suggest opting for a Regulation to ensure consistency among the EU 
jurisdictions.

7.  Overlapping cybersecurity and reporting obligations (CRA and 
DORA) 
The interaction between the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) and the Digital 
Operational Resilience Act (DORA) requires urgent clarification, as the current 
overlap creates significant implementation challenges for the financial sector. 
These two legislative ins truments pursue similar objectives - namely, 
strengthening cybersecurity and operational resilience - but do so through 
different mechanisms, scopes and compliance obligations. In the absence of 
clear coordination, this regulatory layering risks imposing r edundant or even 
conflicting requirements on financial institutions, undermining legal certainty 
and increasing compliance costs. This situation may contradict the European 
Commission’s objective of ensuring regulatory coherence and supporting the 
European Union’s global competitiveness. Cyber resilience of financial entities 
and their ICT providers are already comprehensively regulated under DORA 
and related implementing legislation, which establish detailed rules on ICT risk 
management, incident reporting , resilience testing and oversight of critical 
third-party service providers. In this regard, one of the most critical issues 
concerns the duplication of reporting obligations. Under such framework, 
financial entities could be required to report cybersecur ity incidents both 
under DORA and CRA, often to different authorities, with different formats, 
procedures and timelines. By way of reference, the table below summarizes 
the main reporting timelines across the EU framework for financial institutions 
(including also additional pieces of legislation providing for reporting 
obligations, as NIS2, CER, and GDPR).  
Overlapping reporting obligations create a major burden, particularly cross -
border, diverting resources from proactive security measures. Major areas of 
cost include staff, systems/tools, legal and consulting. A similar duplication 
occurs with respect to t he obligation to maintain and update registers of 
information related to ICT products and services. 
To address these problems, the Commission may consider to adopt clear 
guidance or legislative amendments to define the respective scopes of DORA 
and CRA, ideally by explicitly confirming DORA as lex specialis for the financial

8. GDPR simplification (security measures, extra-EEA data transfer, Article 
10) 
Innovation has rapidly outpaced certain provisions of the GDPR, raising 
legitimate questions about their long -term adequacy. Article 32 GDPR 
currently requires controllers and processors to adopt appropriate technical 
and organisational measures, explicitl y citing encryption as an example. 
However, emerging technologies such as quantum computing may soon 
render traditional encryption methods obsolete. Therefore, maintaining an 
explicit reference to encryption may encourage reliance on outdated 
techniques, t hereby weakening protection standards. To ensure regulatory 
resilience, Article 32 GDPR could be amended to remove this explicit 
reference, and existing authorities may issue soft-law guidance, guidelines, or 
implementing rules to identify security measure s corresponding from time to 
time to the state of the art, also taking into account the specificities of 
different business sectors. A technology -neutral approach would also bolster 
the role of privacy and cybersecurity certification schemes by detaching 
them from potentially obsolete references.  
Likewise, the current rules on international data transfers impose heavy 
operational burdens that are often disproportionate to the risks involved. 
While the requirement to assess foreign legal frameworks and implement 
supplementary measures enhances safeg uards, it applies even to low -risk 
transfers involving limited data volumes or non-sensitive categories of data, or 
among entities part of the same group. To restore proportionality, the 
European Commission may consider introducing a legislative threshold 
determining when supplementary measures are in fact necessary (e.g., in 
case of transfers involving third -party recipient entities). Transfers below this 
threshold could rely solely on internal risk assessment and certain security 
measures to protect the d ata flow perimeter (e.g., to prevent third parties 
gaining access to transferred data), whereas transfers of high-risk data - such 
as health, criminal, or financial data - would continue to fall within current rules 
of Chapter V GDPR.

Finally, under Article 10 GDPR, the processing of personal data relating to 
criminal convictions and offences can be carried out only under the control 
of official authority, or when the processing is authorized by Union or Member 
States law providing for appropriate safeguards. Such approach has led to 
considerable fragmentation, with divergent or absent national rules creating 
legal uncertainty and high compliance costs for organisations operating 
across borders. This is especially true within the financi al sector where due 
diligence processes may require the processing of data close to the definition 
set forth by Article 10 GDPR (e.g., self -declaration of absence of judicial 
proceedings). The current framework may undermine the free flow of data 
within th e internal market and weaken fundamental rights protection. To 
address this structural gap, European Commission may consider harmonising 
the national legal frameworks by introducing exceptions and/or EU-level legal 
bases for processing criminal data under specific circumstances and with 
adequate safeguards (e.g., modelled on Article 9 GDPR for special 
categories of data). We would also like to suggest considering the 
introduction of a requirement for all Member States to keep registers of 
criminal convictions, offenses, and related security measures, organized by 
type of offense and status of proceedings. Without such registers, it can be 
rather challenging to effectively comply with EU regulations like the AML 
Package, as well as some local laws that requir e checks on the reputation 
and professionalism of certain professionals, such as financial advisors. 
Introducing such specific requirement could help avoiding potential conflicts 
with the minimization principle, while supporting regulatory compliance and 
simplify the application of GDPR.

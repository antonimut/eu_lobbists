1 Preliminary remarks: the Digital Omnibus, an opportunity not to 
be missed 
Unipol Group (hereinafter “Unipol”) welcomes the European Commission’s plan to 
simplify digital regulations with a “Digital Omnibus” initiative.  
We strongly support  the Commission’s commitment to regulatory simplification, as 
demonstrated by the announcement of the first Sustainability Omnibus in February 2025 
and the subsequent proposals in areas such as investment, SMEs, defence readiness. We 
share this approach and encourage the Commission to apply the same ambition to digital 
regulation, ensuring that simplification efforts are extended to a broader set of legislative 
acts. 
Improving digital regulations is essential to reduce administrative burdens , to ensure a 
more predictable and practical regulatory environment and to unlock a wave of 
innovation that is necessary for boosting productivity and competitiveness. This 
opportunity must not be missed: the Digital Omnibus is a key enabler of the Single Market. 
We therefore call on the EU institutions to launch the Digital Omnibus as a bold and 
comprehensive initiative , which should include  – beyond the  GDPR – also recent 
legislative files such as the AI Act, the Data Act, the Financial Data Access Regulation and 
the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA). 
The main objective of this initiative  should be to ensure  a level playing field and find a 
better balance between horizontal and sectoral regulation when developing digital rules. 
With this respect, we would like to take this opportunity to reiterate the following 
proposals.  
2 Proposed amendments to Access to financial data (FIDA) 
While the objectives of the Financial Data Access (FIDA) proposal are commendable —to 
foster innovation and competition within the European financial sector —the proposed 
regulation in its current form has deep flaws that risk leading to unintended 
consequences, including competition distortions, the strengthening of existing data

oligopolies, and the imposition of disproportionate costs on the industry. 
We believe that without significant amendments, the regulation could hinder the 
achievement of its original objectives. Considering that co-legislators have not yet agreed 
on appropriate solutions to overcome contentious key provisions,  we recommend 
withdrawing FIDA’s proposal. The key areas of concern are as follows. 
First, the proposed data-sharing model is unbalanced and risks distorting competition in 
the market. It grants Financial Information Service Providers (FISPs) access rights to 
financial data without corresponding reciprocal obligations. This one-way data flow risks 
creating a competitive disadvantage for data holders (financial entities) and  could 
unintentionally reinforce the market position of foreign gatekeepers, who are uniquely 
positioned to aggregate and leverage vast datasets as well as to obtain clients’ consent 
for accessing this data. While we believe that competition promotes innov ation and 
consumer welfare, we cannot ignore the potential risks associated with Big Tech firms 
utilising FISP authorisation to strengthen and enlarge their  data oligopolies. There is a 
tangible concern that these firms might exploit the acquired financial data to offer 
services in direct competition with established financial firms, particularly in credit and 
insurance intermediation 1. Recital 31 of the FIDA proposal is indeed worrying as it 
envisages the possibility for FISPs to “provide financial products and services to customers 
in the Union”. Given the extensive access to consumer data and superior analytics 
capabilities of Big Tech firms, financial entities find themselves at a clear disadvantage. 
This disparity, acknowledged by numerous scholars, pos es a threat to competition, 
consumer welfare, and the overall stability of the financial sector2.  
Second, the proposal poses a significant  risk to the protection of intellectual property  
that underpins the insurance sector. Mandating the disclosure of a non -life insurance 
product’s price in combination with detailed customer data (such as address, age, and 
vehicle type) creates a clear pathway for the reverse engineering of proprietary pr icing

Given the gravity of these issues – for which the co -legislators have yet to agree on 
possible solutions - we believe the most prudent course of action is to withdraw the 
legislative proposal to allow for a thorough reassessment of the necessity for such a wide-

jurisdictions where open finance is growing, there are not legislative frameworks 
comparable to FIDA’s proposal, in terms of scope and prescriptiveness.  
Should the co -legislators choose to move forward with FIDA’s negotiations, 
notwithstanding the above reasoning, we highlight that the following points must be 
addressed to create a viable and appropriate framework: 
(i) The exclusion of gatekeepers and their controlled entities from registering as 
FISPs. In line with the German Presidency proposal, this is a necessary safeguard 
to ensure customer protection, fair competition, and financial stability. 
(ii) A clearer and narrower definition of the scope of data sharing, explicitly excluding 
enriched and inferred data . To protect proprietary know -how, it is crucial that 
data such as the price of an insurance policy, when combined with other customer 
data, is not subject to mandatory sharing. Furthermore, clear guidance on data 
retention periods for terminated contracts is needed to avoid imposing indefinite 
storage obligations.

(iii) The adoption of realistic and phased implementation timelines . The complexity 
of the required technical and governance frameworks necessitates a more 
gradual approach than is currently envisioned. 
3 Proposed amendments to Data Act – Access to in vehicle data 
The entry into force of Regulation (EU) 2023/2854 (Data Act) represents a significant step 
forward regarding the free flow of data across all economic sectors in the Union. The Data 
Act establishes the user's right to access data generated by their connected products and 
the faculty to assign this right to a third -party service provider of their choice, such as 
insurance companies. This framework aims to put all providers on a level playing field, 
ensuring that data access is fair and non-discriminatory, with the ultimate goal of fostering 
innovation and competition. 
However, despite the provisions of the Data Act, achieving full access to vehicle data for 
independent service providers, like insurers, remains challenging within the mobility 
sector. In fact, there is still a partial exchange of data between OEMs (Original Equipment 
Manufacturers) and insurance companies, primarily due to a contractual imbalance 
between the parties that hinders the development of innovative and competitive services. 
The following structural criticalities prevent the effective circulation of vehicle data and 
cannot be fully overcome by the Data Act: 
1. Unsustainable service costs. Although the Data Act mandates the application of 
FRAND (Fair, Reasonable, and Non -Discriminatory) conditions, in practice, the 
economic terms imposed by carmakers for data access do not always align with 
the actual marginal costs. The current level of se rvice costs imposed by OEMs 
constitutes a significant economic deterrent for the development of business 
cases, discouraging insurance companies from utilizing in -vehicle data streams. 
This data would otherwise be crucial for mu ltiple purposes, such as precise risk 
assessment, more accurate anti -fraud controls, and the offering of accessory 
mobility-related services. 
2. Data scope and technology controlled by OEMs. The manufacturer maintains an 
advantageous position not only in the data sharing methods, but also in 
determining the type of data to be made available: the Data Act only mandates 
the provision of data "designed to be retrieved." OEMs, therefore, decide 
whether to design specific data points (e.g., data provided by accelerometers, 
vehicle speed, GNSS-based localization, and odometer values, all of which are of 
particular interest to insurance companies) as direc tly retrievable from the 
vehicle. Furthermore, even for "readily available" data (cf. Art. 4, par. 1, Data Act), 
for which users are guaranteed indirect access via back -end servers, the OEM 
retains the faculty to determine the specific data points to be recorded and made 
accessible. Consequently, already at the design stage, carmakers can limit the 
 
5 
 
scope of vehicle data that can be shared, choosing to render a series of data 
(including non-enriched or non -inferred data, such as acceleration, speed, and 
vehicle position data) as "non-retrievable" or not "readily available," despite their 
availability being vital for the development of innovative use cases by service 
providers. 
3. Lack of technical standardization.  The absence of a minimum data set and the 
freedom of each OEM to define the format and technology for accessing vehicle 
data for every production line creates technological heterogeneity in the market. 
This complexity increases operational difficulties an d management costs for 
insurance companies. 
Given all the above, while the Commission's intent to address the issue is appreciated, we 
observe that the recent Guidelines on Vehicle Data (Commission Communication 
C/2025/5026) do not resolve the criticalities outlined. They fail to tackle the industrial and 
negotiating imbalance between OEMs and service providers (including insurance 
companies) and, conversely, appear to confirm the privileged position of OEMs regarding 
the selection of data to be shared and the methods of making it available. 
For these reasons, as previously stated (cf. Unipol’s Response to the European 
Commission's public consultation "Public consultation on the revision of the Union 
legislation on vehicle type-approval with regard to access to in-vehicle generated data for 
the purpose of providing vehicle-related and mobility service," 02/08/2022), we reiterate 
the need for specific, binding, and detailed sectoral legislation. Such legislation must 
primarily overcome the anticipated imbalance and inclusively assess the economi c 
interests at stake across the entire value chain. Only a vertical regulatory act that restricts 
the carmaker's expected discretion in identifying the data to be shared (through direct or 
indirect access) will effectively guarantee all aftermarket service providers genuine access 
to vehicle data and resources under FRAND  technical and economic conditions, thereby 
enabling operators to develop more innovative and competitive services for the benefit 
of the entire community and competition. 
Subordinately, and in the immediate term, the Commission's attention is drawn to the 
necessity that the upcoming Commission guidelines on the calculation of reasonable 
compensation pursuant to Article 9(5) will address the current imbalance between service 
providers and OEMs by imposing lower prices than those currently applied, which now 
hinder the economic viability of the development of innovative services based on vehicle 
data. 
4 Proposed amendments to AI Act  
The EU AI Act was founded on commendable principles: a risk-based approach that bans 
AI systems incompatible with fundamental rights, regulates high -risk applications, and 
leaves other systems with minimal requirements. In theory, such an approach should 
 
6 
 
build the necessary trust and transparency for the widespread adoption of artificial 
intelligence.  
As Unipol, we have expressed our support for such an ambitious legislative initiative as it 
could help building trust around the use of AI systems. However, while we support the 
risk-based approach of the AI Act , we also recognize its limitations as the provisions on 
high-risk AI systems ’ classification  are overly broad  and vague  and pose major 
implementation challenges , thus hindering  the development of AI use cases and 
hampering the EU digital economy.  
Therefore, moving forward with the adoption of a Digital Omnibus package, we call on 
the Commission to address the following requests. 
I. Removing AI systems intended to be used for risk assessment and pricing in 
health and life insurance from Annex III 
AI systems used for risk assessment and pricing in life and health insurance should be 
removed from Annex III of the AI Act, as they do not  meet the criteria for high -risk 
classification. The main arguments of this proposal are:  
▪ The insurance sector is already governed by strong sectoral regulatory 
frameworks (e.g. Solvency II, IDD, DORA) , which includes also guidance on AI 
governance and risk management recently issued from EIOPA. These frameworks 
adequately manage risks related to AI use. 
 
▪ AI improves risk assessment accuracy, which can enhance inclusion by allowing 
coverage for high -risk individuals. While a common narrative suggests that 
granular risk assessment enabled by AI might lead to exclusion or discrimination 
through unaffordable premiums, this logic assumes that limiting the information 
available to insurers promotes financial inclusion. On the contrary, better risk 
knowledge, enabled by AI, allows insurers to better pursue their mission of 
mutuality, expanding the risk pool and pr oviding coverage even to individuals in 
high-risk categories that would otherwise be excluded or forced to pay very high 
premiums. It is the inaccurate risk assessment that forces insurance companies to 
raise premiums or to exclude coverages. Introducing barriers to the adoption of 
AI in the risk assessment and pricing of insurance policies would thus result in 
worse customer outcomes. 
 
▪ AI systems in underwriting typically act as decision -support tools, not 
autonomous agents . The final decision is retained by qualified human 
underwriters. Customers retain access to review and appeal mechanisms, 
through the insurer or external supervisory bodies, a stark contrast to the 
immediate and difficult-to-reverse impact of genuine high-risk systems. 
 
Therefore, removing these AI systems from Annex III would not exacerbate risks of 
infringements of fundamental rights. 
 
7 
 
As outlined in the Unipol position paper on Artificial Intelligence and in the response 
submitted during the “Targeted Stakeholder Consultation on Classification of AI Systems 
as High -Risk” (July 2025) , we therefore  urge the Commission to consider a more 
proportionate and effective approach , based on sector -specific guidance from 
competent EU authorities, rather than blanket inclusion in Annex III. This would ensure 
continued innovation and consumer protection without imposing undue burdens. 
II. Allowing insurers to benefit from the exemptions provided by Art. 6 of AI Act 
Article 6(3) on “Classification rules for high -risk AI systems” states that “ an AI system 
referred to in Annex III shall always be considered to be high -risk where the AI system 
performs profiling of natural persons”. Such a provision risks encompassing systems that 
are legitimately and routinely used in certain sectors  and may inadvertently include AI 
applications that are intrinsic to the business models of industries such as insurance, 
where profiling is a technical necessity rather than a risk factor. 
Precisely, actuarial risk assessment should not be classified as “profiling” under Article 
6(3) of the AI Act , and therefore should not automatically be subject to high -risk AI 
system requirements. The key points are: 
▪ Different purpose and function : unlike generic profiling, which often serves 
commercial interests, actuarial risk assessment is a technical process aimed at 
fulfilling a client -requested contract. Its goal is to fairly and accurately evaluate 
risk to support the sustainability of the insurance pool. 
 
▪ Promotes inclusion:  AI systems used in this context can help overcome 
discriminatory proxies (e.g., geographic location) and enable coverage for 
individuals in traditionally high-risk categories. 
 
▪ Existing robust regulation : the insurance sector is already governed by strict 
frameworks (Solvency II, IDD, DORA), which ensure sound practices and customer 
protection. EIOPA has confirmed these are sufficient to manage AI-related risks. 
 
▪ Regulatory overlap: automatically classifying these AI systems as high-risk due to 
“profiling” creates unnecessary regulatory burdens and could negatively impact 
customer outcomes. 
Hence, as already expressed in the position paper submitted during the “ Targeted 
Stakeholder Consultation on Classification of AI Systems as High -Risk” (July 2025) , we 
reiterate the request to the European Commission t o clarify that actuarial risk 
assessment and pricing activities do not constitute “profiling” under Article 6 of the AI 
Act, and should therefore be eligible for the exemption applicable to AI systems 
performing support tasks. 
Should recommendations as formulated respectively under Sections I and II not be 
addressed in the Digital Omnibus Package, we call on the Commission to take into account 
 
8 
 
former Italian Prime Minister Mario Draghi’s recent call to pause high-risk AI rules “until 
we better understand the drawbacks.”3 
III. Empowering AI systems’ users with clearly defined, enforceable rights  
The AI Act adopts a predominantly procedural and disclosure -based compliance model, 
lacking substantive rights for individuals and groups affected by AI systems. Notably,  it 
does not explicitly provide a right to challenge automated decisions with material 
adverse effects.  This omission represents a missed opportunity to build trust through 
genuine user empowerment. A more outcome -oriented and proportionate approach, 
focused on tangible rights, would have been preferable for a Level-1 framework, fostering 
innovation while ensuring accountability. 
In contrast, an instructive policy strategy has been adopted in the US, where the White 
House Office of Science and Technology Policy published a non-binding white paper  
aimed at supporting future policies on AI development on the basis of five main principles:  
1. Safe and Effective Systems : AI systems must be designed to protect individuals 
from harm and inefficacy. Developers should collaborate with diverse 
stakeholders to assess risks, conduct pre-deployment testing, and ensure ongoing 
monitoring. Independent evaluations confirming safety and effectiveness should 
be made publicly available whenever possible. 
2. Algorithmic Discrimination Protections: AI systems must be designed and used in 
ways that prevent discrimination based on protected characteristics (e.g., race, 
disability, sexual orientation). Developers should implement equity assessments, 
use representative datasets, conduct disparity testing , and enable independent 
audits to safeguard vulnerable groups. 
3. Data Privacy : users must be protected from abusive data practices and retain 
control over their personal information. AI systems should minimize the 
collection of personally identifiable information, ensure clear and concise consent 
mechanisms, and respect user decisions  regarding data access, sharing, and 
deletion. Enhanced safeguards are required for sensitive data, and surveillance 
technologies should be subject to strict oversight. 
4. Notice and Explanation: Individuals should be informed when automated systems 
are in use and understand how these systems influence outcomes. Clear, 
accessible explanations of system functionality and decision -making processes 
must be provided. 
5. Human Alternatives and Fallback Mechanisms: users should be able to opt out of 
automated systems when appropriate and access timely human intervention to

resolve issues. Fallback procedures must be accessible, especially in cases of 
system failure, errors, or contested decisions. 
In light of the above, we urge that any AI Act review shift its focus from purely procedural 
obligations on providers – which act as a de fact barrier to innovation - to empowering 
users with concrete, enforceable rights. 
➢ Narrow the scope and requirements of AI systems deemed at high-risk. 
➢ Discard the principle according to which high -risk AI systems cannot benefit 
from the exemption provided for ancillary systems to the extent that they 
involve the processing of personal data. 
➢ Bestow enforceable and effective rights in favour of AI systems’ users.  
Specifically, we propose granting individuals the right to: 
a. A clear explanation for any AI -driven decision that significantly impacts 
them.  
b. Contest an automated decision and have it reviewed by a qualified 
human with the authority to overturn it.  
c. Opt-out of certain non -essential AI systems and be provided with a 
human alternative where feasible. 
Lowering the barriers to the development of AI systems while increasing the rights of user 
may be the key to reach the right balance between fostering innovation and protect 
fundamental rights. 
5 Proposed amendments to the GDPR 
The GDPR has established a robust and harmonised framework for privacy protection 
across the EU, fundamentally reshaping how personal data is handled and empowering 
individuals with greater control over their information.  
As Europe enters a new phase of digital transformation —driven by the implementation 
of the Data Act, the AI Act, and other emerging legislation—GDPR remains a cornerstone 
of the EU’s regulatory landscape. 
However, six years of implementation have revealed that GDPR’s horizontal and high -
level nature can lead to unintended consequences in data -intensive sectors, such as 
financial services and insurance. These industries rely heavily on personal and sensitive 
data to del iver essential services and have invested substantial resources to ensure 
compliance. Yet, the complexity and rigidity of certain GDPR provisions may inadvertently 
hinder technological innovation and the adoption of new solutions.  For instance, the 
reliance on explicit consent makes it operationally unfeasible to train fraud -detection AI 
models on large, historical datasets of claims, which often contain health data. This 
 
10 
 
inability to use comprehensive data for model training directly hinders the development 
of more accurate and efficient systems to protect all policyholders. 
Hence, we call on the European Commission to take the opportunity of issuing a Digital 
Omnibus Package to promote greater consistency across the EU by amending Article 9(2) 
of the GDPR to introduce a new exemption for the processing of health data where it is 
necessary for the performance of a contract. This is not about “reopening the GDPR” but 
to enhance its effectiveness. 
The current formulation of Article 9(2) of the GDPR prohibits the processing of special 
category data unless one of the listed exemptions applies. Unlike Article 6, which permits 
processing general personal data where it is necessary for the performance of a contract, 
Article 9 does not include this as a legal basis for special category data. This  creates a 
significant regulatory gap for sectors such as (re)insurance, where processing sensitive 
data is often indispensable for fulfilling contractual obligations. 
In practice, this omission forces (re)insurance companies to rely solely on explicit consent 
to process special category data, as confirmed by the European Data Protection Board 
(EDPB) in its Guidelines 05/2020. However, this reliance is problematic. Conse nt in the 
insurance context is rarely “freely given,” as refusal may result in denial of essential 
services, thereby undermining the voluntariness required under GDPR Article 7(4).  
This regulatory paradox is particularly evident in mandatory insurance scenarios, such as 
motor vehicle liability insurance under Directive 2009/103/EC and its amendment 
Directive 2021/2118/EU. Here, both the policyholder and the insurer are legally oblige d 
to enter into a contract, which may necessitate the processing of health data (e.g., in 
personal injury claims). The unpredictability and urgency of such processing make prior 
consent impractical and legally fragile.  
Moreover, the current framework places disproportionate compliance burdens on 
insurers, especially SMEs, and risks delaying claim settlements, potentially exposing 
companies to administrative penalties under sectoral supervisory rules. 
Article 9(4) permits indeed national laws to impose further limitations on the processing 
of genetic, biometric, and health-related data, whereas Article 9(2) does not allow for the 
introduction of new legal bases for processing health data beyond those ex plicitly listed. 
As a result, the legal framework for processing special category data—particularly health 
data—remains fragmented across Member States. This limits the potential for 
harmonised and legally clear approaches to health data processing, especially in sectors 
where such processing is essential for service provision, such as insurance and healthcare. 
Therefore, as outlined in the Unipol ’s position paper to the EU Commission’s report on 
the GDPR (February 2024) , we urge the European Commission to consider amending 
Article 9(2) of the GDPR to include a new exemption for the processing of special 
category data where it is necessary for the performance of a contract,  particularly in 
regulated sectors such as insurance. This amendment would: 
 
11 
 
▪ align GDPR with sector-specific legal obligations and practice; 
▪ resolve the ethical and legal contradiction of coerced consent; 
▪ ensure that data subjects retain meaningful rights without compromising access 
to essential services; 
▪ promote legal certainty and operational efficiency for data controllers. 
This amendment would also complement existing national frameworks and foster a more 
coherent and predictable regulatory environment for both individuals and organisations. 
6 Proposed amendments to DORA an d NIS to streamline cyber 
incidents reporting  
In the critical moments following a cyber incident, when every resource must be 
dedicated to investigation and remediation, current legislative frameworks force a 
counterproductive diversion. Regulations such as DORA, NIS, the AI Act, and GDPR oblige 
entities to submit multiple, disparate reports on the same event to numerous authorities. 
Each report demands specific details formatted for different templates, governed by 
separate procedures, and constrained by conflicting deadlines. For any complex group, 
this avalanche of paperwork creates a severe operational burden at the worst possible 
time, diverting crucial attention and expert personnel from the vital work of recovery to 
redundant administrative tasks. The opportunity cost is immense, as hours spent 
satisfying bureaucracy are hours not spent patching vulnerabilities and restoring services. 
Such a system stands in direct opposition to the foundational principles of administrative 
efficiency and proportionality. It is a systemic failure that forces reporting entities to bear 
the weight of fragmentation, even as the competent Authorities themse lves are 
mandated to collaborate and share information. This approach is not merely inefficient; 
it is self -defeating. By hampering an organisation's ability to respond effectively, the 
reporting process itself undermines the very security and resilience t he legislation was 
designed to promote. 
As Unipol has consistently advocated in the past (see answer to FSB consultation on FIRE 
and ESA’s survey of centralised hub for ICT incidents reporting), the optimal and logical 
solution is a single, centralised EU hub for all mandated cyber incident reporting. This hub 
would implement a "report once" principle, acting as the sole point of entry for all incident 
notifications. From there, it would automatically and sec urely disseminate the relevant 
information to the appropriate national and EU authorities based on their jurisdiction. 
The European Supervisory Authorities (ESAs) themselves have acknowledged the 
compelling advantages of this model, including: 
(a) more consistent, standardised, high-quality and timely incident reporting data at 
EU level; 
 
12 
 
(b) simplification of reporting channels, avoiding parallel reports present in the 
baseline scenario; 
(c) shorter dissemination times for incident reports, facilitating a faster response to 
cross-border incidents; 
(d) development of advanced and centralized analytical capabilities, accessible to all 
users, including Competent Authorities and financial entities. 
The positive effects of a centralised hub extend far beyond administrative simplification. 
A critical and necessary component of this proposal is to grant access to the aggregated 
data collected by the hub, as this is what allows for the diffusion of cyber  insurance, a 
proven driver of overall resilience. Cyber insurance is widely acknowledged to increase an 
entity’s resilience because it creates a powerful incentive structure based on market 
principles. It encourages proactive investments in risk reduction, as insurers often require 
specific security controls or offer lower premiums for stronger defences, while facilitating 
more effective responses and recovery from attacks through expert support and financial 
coverage. Access to a common framework of aggre gated, anonymised incident data 
would create the reliable dataset insurers need to enhance risk measurement, pricing, 
and management, strengthening this virtuous cycle of resilience. 
Currently, the growth of this market is hindered by two primary obstacles, as highlighted 
by the IAIS: a lack of shared data and a lack of a shared taxonomy. Without a common 
language to describe incidents, comparing data becomes impossible, and its utility 
evaporates. The IAIS rightly notes that this f ailing "undermine[s] the possibility of 
combining internal and external data […] and forming a consistent view of cyber risk when 
accessing external or public data” (IAIS – Cyber Risk Underwriting). A central ised hub 
directly confronts these shortcomings. It would solve the fundamental market failure of 
adverse selection —where a lack of accurate data forces insurers to set high average 
premiums, discouraging the most secure firms from participating. With bette r data, 
pricing becomes more accurate, premiums can decrease, and the market can expand to 
protect more businesses. 
This course of action is endorsed by a clear consensus of regulatory bodies, who signal 
that the current fragmented approach is untenable. EIOPA, in its Strategy on Cyber 
Underwriting, has already called for data on cyber incidents to be "broadened and 
appropriately standardized." Similarly, the FSB, in its “Recommendations to Achieve 
Greater Convergence in Cyber Incident Reporting,” noted that policymakers should aim 
"To provide a consolidated source of incident data, trends, threats and/or risks," explicitly 
including a "data repository to support underwriting." 
Therefore, Unipol reiterates the following proposals: 
➢ Establish a single, centralised hub built on a common taxonomy for all incident 
reporting mandated under EU law (DORA, NIS, AI Act, GDPR). This will eliminate 
redundant reporting, strengthen cross -border resilience through timely and 
coherent data analysis, and improve collective response capabilities. 
 
13 
 
➢ Allow interested parties to access aggregated and anonymised data from this 
hub. For the insurance sector, this access is the catalyst needed to expand the 
cyber insurance market—a proven mechanism for enhancing the cyber resilience 
of all European firms and a strategic investment in the digital security of the Union 
itself. 
7 Conclusion  
The Digital Omnibus initiative represents a n opportunity that must not be missed to 
recalibrate the EU's digital regulatory agenda. Achieving the right balance between 
competitiveness and robust risk mitigation is paramount, particularly as recent legislative 
actions risk creating unintended barriers to economic and technological development. 
A truly effective simplification effort shall go beyond a mere quantitative reduction of 
burdens and address a number of challenges. This includes narrowing the AI Act's high-
risk classification and requirements to ensure proportionality and adapting GDPR's 
provisions to better align with sectoral realities. In this context, given its unresolved risks 
to fair competition, customer protection, and financial stability , the FIDA legislative 
proposal should be withdrawn to allow for a necessary reassessment. Furthermore, 
ensuring fair access to in -vehicle data requires dedicated sectoral legislation that moves 
beyond the Data Act. A primary objective of such legislation must be to lower data access 
costs, which are currently imposed at a level so high as to render most innovative business 
cases unviable. Finally, establishing a single hub for cyber -incident reporting would 
represent an essential step toward streamlining compliance and enhancing collective 
resilience. 
These targeted adjustments are vital for creating a coherent and predictable regulatory 
environment that serves as a catalyst, not an impediment, for a secure, innovative, and 
competitive digital Single Market.

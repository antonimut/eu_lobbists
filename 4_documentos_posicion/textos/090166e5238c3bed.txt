Comment 
of the German Insurance Association (GDV)  on the  Eu-
ropean Commission Call for Evidence  
Digital Omnibus  (Digital Package on Simplification)  
Digitalisation is essential to modern insurance services, enabling efficient claims 
handling, improving customer experience, and strengthening risk management. 
The German insurance industry remains strongly committed to supporting the dig-
ital transition and welcome the European Commission’s approach to review 
and simplify existing digital regulations. Insurance companies already face ex-
tensive digital and non -digital requirements. We therefore believe simplifications 
such as the “Stop the clock” for the Insurance Recovery and Resolution Directive, 
for example, which will cause significant additional administrative effort for insur-
ers, are needed.  
 
The Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) ensures a high level of cybersecu-
rity, and the financial sector has made great efforts to implement its requirements 
in a very short time. Beyond avoidable bureaucracy, and even with the same pro-
tection goal, the application of the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) requirements to 
insurance undertakings offers no value.  We therefore call for the Commission to 
exempt DORA-regulated financial companies from the equivalent – but not 
identical – CRA requirements. 
 
Furthermore, the use of AI in the insurance industry should not be slowed by 
overlapping regulatory requirements. Overlapping requirements under GDPR, 
AI Act and national regulations lead to significant compliance costs. In particular , 
the parallel application of the GDPR and the AI Act is hampering innovation. Ad-
justments are urgently needed in this area.

1. Avoid parallel regulation alongside DORA 
The Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA)  requires insurance companies 
to uphold a high level of cybersecurity through comprehensive obligations, includ-
ing those related to internal processes and systems. DORA covers all risks arising 
from, or linked to, information and communication technologies  (ICT). It comple-
ments the existing supervisory framework under Solvency II, which primarily fo-
cuses on financial stability, by extending regulatory oversight to include the entire 
digital infrastructure and cybersecurity landscape. 
In addition to this robust regulatory framework insurers are subject to other legal 
instruments with overlapping, but distinct, requirements. A prime example is inci-
dent reporting: DORA, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) , the 
Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), and the AI Act each impose reporting obligations, 
but differ in format, scope, and content. The lack of interoperability between these 
frameworks creates operational challenges and leads to redundant structures and 
processes. This fragmentation a ffects not only incident reporting but also areas 
such as cybersecurity, data governance, and risk management. 
1.1 Harmonise outsourcing rules under Solvency II and third-party risk 
management under DORA 
Regulatory treatment of insurance companies' use of third-party service providers 
varies by context. Service providers may qualify as an ICT third-party under DORA, 
as a relevant outsourcing arrangement under Solvency II, or both.  Insurers are 
currently required to establish and maintain parallel compliance structures. Har-
monising these rules would streamline compliance and improve operational effi-
ciency without compromising oversight.  
However, this should not lead to the requirements of DORA being transferred to all 
contracts with third parties. DORA’s broad ICT-services definition has already led 
to numerous time-consuming discussions and difficulties in practice. The main bur-
den lies in negotiating mandatory contractual requirements, not risk management 
considerations. Extending DORA requirements to all third parties would worsen 
this and increase burden. Instead, there are minimally invasive options for adapting 
the Delegated Regulation on Solvency II to bring it into line with DORA. 
Proposal: 
• Services that qualify both as ICT services under DORA and as outsourcing 
arrangements under Solvency II should be subject to a unified regulatory 
approach. The requirements of Article 274 (4) lit. (b) and (f) of Delegated 
Regulation (EU) 2015/35 on the written agreement between an insurance 
undertaking and a service provider for critical or important operational func-
tions or activities should only be mandatory “where appropriate”.

It would contribute to simplification and less bureaucracy if compliance with 
DORA requirements is regularly sufficient in these cases. The de-prioritiza-
tion of Article 274 (4) (b) and (f) is also justified because detailed service 
level agreements make the right to issue instructions and the obligation to 
comply with the insurance company's policies obsolete in many cases. 
1.2 Exempt DORA-regulated financial entities from the Cyber Resilience 
Act 
The Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) aims to improve products’ cybersecurity through 
minimum standards, transparency requirements, and reporting obligations. Insur-
ance services are not directly covered but may be indirectly affected, as an exam-
ple, when insurance companies provide policyholders with apps for submitting 
claims. These apps  must meet high cybersecurity standards , and  insurers al-
ready fulfil these requirements under the Digital Operational Resilience Act 
(DORA). Additional inclusion under the CRA woul d therefore represent an added 
and, in our view, unnecessary regulatory burden. 
DORA and the CRA offer similar protection but differ in implementation. For in-
stance, security incidents under DORA must be reported to  the relevant na-
tional supervisory authority, while under the CRA, they must be reported to the 
Computer Security Incident Response Team ( CSIRT) and the European Union 
Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA). This creates duplicated reporting obligations. 
This duplication is disproportionate as insurers are not traditional manufacturers, 
importers, or distributors of products with digital elements. Any risks are already 
managed through the robust risk management frameworks, including vulnerability 
analyses, mandated by DORA. Additional inclusion under the CRA would not 
enhance cybersecurity but increase the administrative burden on insurers. 
Proposals: 
• Financial undertakings already covered by DORA should be excluded 
from the scope of application of the CRA, or 
• The European Commission should make use of the authorisation provided 
in Art. 2 (5) CRA to fully or partially exempt DORA -regulated financial un-
dertakings from the CRA’s provisions through a delegated act, based on 
existing comparable regulation. 
1.3 Reducing bureaucracy and enhancing proportionality in DORA 
The implementation of DORA has required insurance companies to invest in sig-
nificant resources. This has strained resources in other areas and has limited the 
sector’s ability to fully leverage its innovation potential.

Proposals: 
• The current requirements for creating and maintaining the register of in-
formation – e.g. complex data model and subcontractor chain documen-
tation – are disproportionate to the actual benefits and should be simplified. 
• The current definition of ICT services  is overly broad, capturing many 
irrelevant applications. Additionally, inconsistent interpretations across 
Member States, supervisory authorities, users, and ICT service providers 
lead to legal uncertainty. A clearer, more focused definition is needed to 
ensure consistency and relevance. 
• The simplified ICT risk management framework should be made avail-
able to a broader group of entities, such as small and non-complex un-
dertakings (SNCUs) already defined under Solvency II, who are already 
subject to proportional requirements. 
• Independent of size, undertakings with a low-risk profile should face re-
duced requirements. For example, flexibility in the format of the register 
of information and the ability to combine the ICT risk management function 
with other roles should be allowed. The principle of proportionality should 
also apply to all DORA documentation (e.g., policies, procedures, commu-
nication, recovery plans) in both scope and detail. Review intervals should 
span multiple years, and testing obligations (in terms of number, s cope, 
and frequency) should be scaled accordingly. 
• Currently, determining whether an incident is reportable is highly time-con-
suming. Reporting should focus on system-critical incidents affecting 
availability to reduce unnecessary administrative burden. 
• The financial threshold for classifying a major incident (e.g., EUR 100,000) 
is often reached quickly, particularly when forensic services are involved, 
even if the incident is not of high severity. Higher thresholds would re-
duce unnecessary reporting. 
• A rigid application of time-based thresholds (e.g., 2 -hour outages) may 
overstate severity in some contexts (e.g., insurers), while understating it in 
others (e.g., payment services). Flexibility should be introduced to account 
for sector-specific impact. 
• Interim reports should allow the use of estimates or rounded figures ra-
ther than requiring exact data, to reduce the reporting burden during crisis 
management. 
• Additional national requirements should be avoided . For example, in 
Germany, insurance holding companies are also subject to DORA and in-
surers face an additional comprehensive annual audit as part of the audit 
of the annual financial statements. This leads to significant financial and 
staffing burdens, undermining proportional implementation.

1.4 Use of recognised certifications and audit results of critical ICT service 
providers 
Financial institutions must continuously monitor the ir third-party ICT service pro-
viders. When an ICT service supports a critical or important function, Article 8(3) 
of Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1773 stipulates that institutions must not  rely 
solely on third-party certifications or audit reports over time. Consequently, even 
with recognised certifications, financial institutions are still required to con-
duct their own audits. 
This obligation applies even when the European Supervisory Authorities (ESAs) 
have already audited the same service provider under their powers concerning 
critical ICT service providers. Such audits require significant preparation and are 
costly. As a result, it is not feasible to extend these audits to an unlimited number 
of providers. This may increase concentration risk, as institutions tend to rely on a 
few 'known' service providers. 
Proposals: 
• Enable more efficient use of recognised certifications , so that not all 
financial entities are required to perform identical audits independently. 
• Allow financial undertakings to access the audit results conducted by 
the ESAs’ Joint Examination Teams under DORA. This could potentially 
eliminate the need for duplicative audits by each institution of the same 
service provider. 
1.5 Reduce duplication within corporate groups 
Financial companies regulated under DORA are exempt from the parallel obliga-
tions of Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (NIS2 Directive) (see Recital 28 NIS2 Directive).  
However, intra-group IT service providers—being non-financial entities—do not 
benefit from this exemption and are therefore subject to the full scope of NIS2  
requirements. As a result, differing reporting obligations and formats can lead to 
significant duplication. A single cyber incident may trigger up to five separate re-
ports: 
• The group-internal IT service provider reports the incident to financial enti-
ties within the group in accordance with DORA. 
• The financial entities report the incident to the national supervisory author-
ity. 
• The supervisory authority notifies other relevant bodies (e.g., in Germany: 
the Federal Office for Information Security, BSI); 
• The group-internal IT service provider reports the same incident to the BSI 
under NIS2. 
• The BSI may, in turn, notify BaFin. 
0 8  C O M M E N T0 8  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Since group-internal IT service providers are typically integrated into the group’s 
unified systems, processes, and reporting lines, current regulations require corpo-
rate groups to establish duplicative structures. This creates unnecessary adminis-
trative burdens. In the long term, the objective should be a unified, single-report 
mechanism per incident. 
Proposals: 
• Exempt purely group -internal IT service providers  from the require-
ments of the NIS2 Directive, or 
• Allow purely group-internal IT service providers to voluntarily follow DORA-
aligned reporting obligations, in which case they would be exempted from 
NIS2 requirements. 
2. Reducing bureaucracy, legal uncertainty and administrative bur-
den within the GDPR 
The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) provides a high level of protection 
for individuals' data. However, its complexity and broad interpretation create un-
certainty and administrative burdens  for data-intensive and highly regulated sec-
tors like insurance. Insurers already process personal data in accordance with 
strict sector-specific rules and supervisory oversight, yet they face overlapping and 
sometimes conflicting compliance obligations under different legal frameworks.  
This fragmented regulatory landscape reduces legal clarity, operational efficiency, 
and the sector’s capacity to innovate and make responsible use of emerging tech-
nologies like AI. 
While it is not explicitly within the scope of the upcoming Digital Omnibus, we be-
lieve that targeted GDPR adjustments would significantly support the package’s 
goals of simplification, legal coherence, and innovation. It offers a chance to align 
data protection rules with parallel digital legislation, such as the AI Act, and to elim-
inate unnecessary duplication. 
2.1 Automated Individual Decision-Making (Article 22 GDPR) 
German insurers processed approximately 24 billion claims last year, around 
65,000 cases per day. Automated individual decision-making can significantly ac-
celerate this process. In simpler cases, processing times can be drastically re-
duced, allowing customer requests to be fulfilled much more quickly. The capaci-
ties freed up in this way could benefit customers with more complex claims. 
However, this potential is limited by a restrictive legal interpretation: Article 22 of 
the GDPR is currently interpreted by both the European Court of Justice (see judg-
ment of December 7, 2023, C -634/21 – SCHUFA Holding), the European Data

Protection Board (EDPB), and national data protection authorities as a general 
prohibition of fully automated decisions—even though the wording of the provision 
explicitly frames it as a right of the data subject. This restrictive reading often pre-
vents di gital solutions, such as online insurance contracts or automated claims 
processing. As a result, Article 22 GDPR no longer adequately reflects insurers’ 
realities or customer expectations for fast digital service. 
Proposals: 
• Clarify that Article 22 GDPR grants a right, not a prohibition. Automated deci-
sions should be permissible unless the data subject objects. 
• Ensure that the exceptions provided in Article 22(2) and (4) GDPR are not un-
dermined by restrictive interpretations. 
• Introduce an exemption for processing claims of data subjects who are not 
contractual parties of the controller (e.g., third-party claimants in liability insur-
ance). 
This issue aligns with the Digital Omnibus’ objective to foster legal clarity and digital 
innovation, especially by clarifying outdated interpretations that hinder automation. 
2.2 Processing of Health Data in the Insurance Sector 
Processing of health data is essential for underwriting and claims handling, as well 
as for reinsurance. However, there is no uniform legal basis across the EU. Some 
Member States (e.g., Austria with §11a of the Insurance Contract Act)  have en-
acted specific rules, but Germany does not. 
As a result, German insurers rely on the data subject’s consent. As a rule, consent 
is not a sufficiently reliable and efficient legal basis for the performance of an in-
surance contract because it can be revoked at any time . Some data protection 
authorities have even challenged the consent as involuntary. Thus, even with con-
sent, legal certainty is not ensured. Diverging  national interpretations also hinder 
cross-border collaboration, especially for German reinsurers who need access to 
health data from other countries without direct contact with the data subject. 
Proposal: 
• Introduce a clear statutory legal basis in Article 9(2) GDPR for processing 
health data for the conclusion and performance of insurance contracts (includ-
ing reinsurance), to ensure legal certainty and a level playing field across the 
EU. 
This offers a fitting opportunity to harmonize sector-specific rules, reduce fragmen-
tation, and ensure legal certainty for data-intensive services across borders – one 
of the objectives of this Omnibus.

2.3 Anonymization of Data 
New EU legal acts on data sharing , like Article 18(5) of the Data Act , require the 
anonymization of data when transmitting it to public authorities. Anonymized per-
sonal data is essential for many types of data analysis, increasingly important in 
digitalization. It is used to further develop existing applications and to train and test 
new applications, products, and systems. 
However, about it remains unclear  when data is considered sufficiently anony-
mized. Although the EDPB is expected to publish guidelines on anonymization 
soon, its Guidelines 01/2025 on pseudonymization have already shown that the 
EDPB is likely to set very high standards in this regard. 
Proposals: 
• Companies need reliable legal rules on when data is considered sufficiently 
anonymized and therefore no longer subject to the GDPR. 
• The identifiability of data should be determined relatively, based on whether 
the data controller can reasonably identify an individual. 
• It should be clarified that anonymization does not require a legal basis under 
Article 6 or Article 9 GDPR. 
The Digital Omnibus is the right vehicle to provide practical criteria and harmonized 
definitions for anonymization, thereby aligning data protection law with other digital 
regulations like the Data Act. 
2.4 Data Transfers 
2.4.1  International Data Transfers 
Supervisory authorities interpret Chapter V of the GDPR strictly, rejecting a risk-
based approach. This is particularly evident in the use of Standard Contractual 
Clauses (SCCs) and Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs). Despite safeguards in place 
when no adequacy decision applies, companies must assess even low-risk data 
case-by-case, like contact details in video calls. 
It is incomprehensible why the risk-based approach established in the GDPR (Ar-
ticles 24 and 32 GDPR) should not be applied to measures taken for data transfers 
to third countries. 
Proposals: 
• Introduce the risk-based approach (Articles 24 and 32 GDPR) to measures for 
data transfers to third countries (e.g. by explicitly anchoring it in Articles 44 et 
seq. GDPR). 
11  C O M M E N T11  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
• Recognize that a return transfer from a processor to a controller in a third coun-
try does not constitute a "new" data transfer. 
• Simplify and expedite adequacy decisions for third countries. 
• Provide clear and practical rules for using cloud services in third countries if the 
risk to data subjects is low. 
The Digital Omnibus can streamline international data transfers by anchoring a 
risk-based approach and reducing duplicative legal hurdles, improving coherence 
with the EU’s open data strategy. 
2.4.2  Binding Corporate Rules (BCR) 
 
Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs) as defined in Article 47 of the GDPR are, in prin-
ciple, a sensible instrument for corporate groups data transfers to third countries. 
However, EDPB’s recommendations 1/2022 expanded the requirements that had 
previously applied to Controller BCRs (see WP 256 and WP 264 of the Article 29 
Working Party) after only a short period of application. They now must reflect the 
full range of GDPR requirements. 
In addition, BCRs must include a wide array of supplementary measures that go 
beyond the requirements of Article 47 GDPR. These extensions significantly ex-
ceed what is necessary to implement the Schrems II ruling. Above all, the lengthy 
approval process ma kes BCRs increasingly unattractive in practice as a tool for 
data transfers to third countries within corporate groups. 
Proposals: 
• Scale back BCR requirements to what is strictly required by Article 47 GDPR. 
• Accelerate the approval process. 
2.4.3  Intra- Group Data Transfers 
To improve efficiency, tasks are delegated and centralized within insurance groups 
– as in other industries. Yet, the GDPR treats intra-group data transfers as if be-
tween separate entities. Beyond administrative purposes, only two legal frame-
works apply depending on the roles of the entities involved: Article 28 GDPR i f 
there is a controller–processor relationship, or Article 26 GDPR if the entities are 
joint controllers. Both require additional agreements between group entities. This 
adds bureaucracy and delays customer services. 
Article 6(1)(f) GDPR cannot justify processing sensitive data and therefore cannot 
serve as a legal basis in such cases. Obtaining consent from all customers is im-
practical and would also unnecessarily strain customer relations: frequent consent 
requests feel disruptive, and rode trust—especially without clear benefit.

Proposal: 
• Establish a specific legal basis within the GDPR for intra -group processing, 
particularly for special categories of personal data under Article 9 GDPR. 
2.5 Reducing Information and Access Obligations 
Meeting GDPR Articles 13, 14, and 15 information and access obligations require 
significant personnel and technical resources from companies. Since the GDPR 
came into force, the number of access requests from data subjects has multiplied. 
As a result, many companies now have several employees working daily on pro-
cessing these requests. 
In practice, however, data subjects are often not interested in the extensive infor-
mation as outlined in the GDPR and the EDPB guidelines. In many cases, the right 
of access is used to exert pressure on insurers in the context of legal disputes or 
to "fish" for specific information (e.g., by former employees or in customer con-
flicts). Especially in the B2B sector, it seems unnecessary for employees to have 
access rights against business partners or former employers. 
Particularly problematic is the EDPB’s demand that data from backup systems 
must also be made accessible. These systems are not intended for regular access 
but serve solely for disaster recovery. Searching them involves considerable tech-
nical and personnel effort, without providing any clear benefit to the data subject. 
Proposals: 
• To avoid unnecessary bureaucratic burden and overwhelming data subjects 
with information, the disclosures required under Articles 13 and 14 GDPR 
should not apply in the B2B context to business partners and their employees. 
Business partners and their staff could, for example, no longer be considered 
data subjects within the meaning of the GDPR, but rather part of the controller, 
insofar as they act in that capacity. 
• In general business operations, the necessity, nature, scope, and timing of pro-
actively provided information should be determined based on a risk-based ap-
proach and the context of the data processing. If, under the circumstances, no 
information is typically expected, it should not have to be provided proactively 
but instead be made available electronically and only sent upon request. 
• The law should clarify that, in cases involving large volumes of data, a two-step 
approach as described in Recital 63, sentence 7 of the GDPR is permissible, 
and that full details must only be provided upon the explicit request of the data 
subject. 
• Backup data and archive systems should be explicitly excluded from the right 
of access. 
13  C O M M E N T13  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
• Abusive use of the right of access must be legally limited, for example in cases 
of clearly vexatious or harassing requests. 
• The obligation to provide copies should not constitute a separate right; provid-
ing the personal data itself should be sufficient. 
• There should be no right of access where data must be kept confidential due 
to special confidentiality obligations of the controller, or where disclosure would 
jeopardize or facilitate the opposing party’s legal claims. 
• The exception under Article 15(4) GDPR should also apply to Article 15(1), to 
protect trade secrets or the controller’s legal position. 
We see the Digital Omnibus as an appropriate context to rebalance these obliga-
tions considering practical relevance, risk, and purpose—promoting proportionality 
and avoiding bureaucratic overload. 
3. Reconciling Data Protection and Artificial Intelligence 
 
Digitalisation and AI offer major opportunities for insurers and customers. Innova-
tion and digital transformation are at the forefront of the industry. Current rules, 
while protecting customers , may hinder or slow down innovation  and create 
unnecessary obstacles for both customers and insurers. 
Article 2(7) of the AI Act stipulates that the AI Act shall not affect the application of 
the GDPR. However, the simultaneous applicability of both regulatory frameworks 
leads to inconsistencies and overlaps. Examples are the lack of legal basis for AI 
Training (see 4.1) or the overlap between the data protection impact assessment 
under Article 35 GDPR and the fundamental rights impact assessment required 
under Article 27 of the AI Act (see 4.5).  
 
3.1 Legal Basis for AI Training 
AI models, IT applications, products, systems, and analytical tools can often be 
developed using synthetic or anonymised data. However, to deploy them effec-
tively, safely, and without discrimination, testing and training with real personal 
data is necessary. 
A clear example is the following scenario: insurers often need to review medical 
treatment plans and invoices, for instance, from dentists. These documents are not 
standardised, and the review often concerns individual procedures rather than the 
entire treatment. Yet, the interaction and medical necessity of these procedures 
must be assessed. To carry out such an assessment, insurers employ specialists 
with a medical background. These specialists make assessments that are often 
very complex. In many cases, however, these could also be easily taken over by 
AI.  
14  C O M M E N T14  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
AI speeds up simple cases. This means insured individuals receive their ben-
efits faster, and experts can focus on more complex cases. But to perform reliably, 
it must be trained on real, realistic data. Supporting such review processes with AI 
requires highly specific training data with real -world examples. Generalised or 
overly simplified data is not sufficient. 
A key issue is the lack of a clear legal basis for using personal data in AI 
training. Article 10(5) of the AI Act only applies when data is used to detect bias 
in high-risk AI systems; it does not apply to General Purpose AI models or non -
high-risk AI applications, even though the risk of bias is not limited to high -risk 
systems. No broader legal basis exists for using sensitive data under Article 
9 of the GDPR. 
Without a specific legal basis, AI training must rely on existing GDPR provisions. 
In practice, this will often have to be based on legitimate interest under Article 
6(1)(f) GDPR, since obtaining further consent after the conclusion of contract is 
frequently impractical or disproportionately costly. However, this legal basis cannot 
be used for sensitive data under Article 9 GDPR. 
In summary, the lack of a specific legal basis hinders innovation. Not only does the 
EU market lose out, as innovation must take place "elsewhere", whether in the U.S. 
or Asia, but EU customers miss out on the benefits, and investments in innovation 
and digitalisation are made outside the EU. This affects not only insurers but also 
service providers and infrastructure operators. 
Proposals: 
• Introduction of a specific legal basis, beyond Article 10(5) AI Act, allowing 
the use of personal data , including special categories (in particular, health 
data in private health insurance), for development and testing of AI models, 
IT applications, products, systems, and analytical tools when necessary. 
• Apply a less stringent standard when processing non-sensitive personal 
data. 
3.2 Avoiding an Overly Broad Definition of Personal Data 
The EDPB, Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU), and supervisory au-
thorities increasingly interpret personal data broadly, which imposes disproportion-
ate compliance burdens and hinders digitalisation and AI development. 
For example, in its guidelines on training AI models, the EDPB, following the CJEU 
case law on personal data, assumes that an AI model can only be considered 
anonymous if the direct extraction of personal data or the intentional or uninten-
tional querying of such data is unlikely, taking into account “all means reasonably 
15  C O M M E N T15  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
likely to be used by the controller or any other person.” Data protection authorities 
are expected to assess the anonymity of an AI model on a case -by-case basis, 
based on the measures taken by the controller. Companies are expected to re-
duce the use of personal training data  and the identifiability of individuals, to 
program AI models in a way that minimises the risk of direct output of personal 
data, and to verify the effectiveness of anonymisation measures themselves. If 
companies use an existing AI mode l, they must ensure that it was not trained on 
personal data or must anonymize it before use. These high data protection re-
quirements result in significant hindrances to the use of AI models by insur-
ance companies. 
In practice, it is also problematic when primarily factual data is considered 
personal data simply because it could be linked to a person. For example, infor-
mation about registered vehicles may relate to the owner, the policyholder, the 
driver, or even the mechanic servicing the vehicle. Geodata such as soil quality or 
potential flood zones may be linked to the property owner or tenant as value -de-
termining factors. 
This complicates the use of such data for intelligent public management, for exam-
ple, using vehicle location data to display occupied parking spaces in city centers 
or using flood zone data for traffic control after heavy rainfall. It also affects the 
statistical use of primarily factual data in the insurance industry, for instance, in 
calculating risk-based premiums. 
Proposal: 
• For data in existing AI models, it should be regulated that a personal reference 
is not assumed. Users of existing AI models should only be required to comply 
with data protection laws in their inputs and the processing of outputs from such 
models. Primarily information should be freely usable for the training of AI mod-
els. This could be regulated, for example, as a privileged purpose change in 
Article 6(4) of the GDPR. 
3.3 Simplifying the Balancing of Interests 
The requirements for specificity and the duty to inform in the context of a balancing 
of interests under Article 6(1)(f) GDPR must not be excessively stringent. In its 
judgment of 9 January 2025 (Case C-394/23), the Court of Justice of the European 
Union (CJEU) stated in paragraph 56 that the assessment of the necessity of pro-
cessing under Article 6(1)(f) GDPR does not take into account societal practices 
or conventions, and that this legal basis, as emphasised in paragraph 27 of the 
judgment, is to be inte rpreted strictly. Furthermore, reliance on this legal basis is 
excluded if data subjects have not been informed in accordance with Article 13 
GDPR about the specific legitimate interests pursued and the essence of the bal-
ancing of interests.

This creates the risk that, going forward, very detailed balancing assessments may 
be required, potentially differentiated by each individual category of personal data 
processed, and that such detailed information must be provided in order to rely on 
Article 6(1)(f) GDPR. In contexts involving the processing of large volumes of 
data—such as in the insurance sector or AI training—this is practically unworkable 
and would overwhelm data subjects with information. Providing information on the 
purposes and legal  bases of processing, as well as the types of personal data 
involved, should be sufficient. 
Proposal: 
• The information obligations under Articles 13(1)(d) and 14(2)(b) GDPR should 
be removed. At a minimum, there should be a clarification that incomplete ful-
filment of the information obligations under Articles 13 and 14 GDPR does not 
affect the validity of the legal basis, in particular Article 6(1)(f) GDPR. 
3.4 Data Minimisation 
When data is analysed using self -learning AI systems, it is often not possible to 
foresee in advance the full scope of data the AI will require. This contradicts the 
principle of restricting processing to what is strictly necessary, as prescribed by the 
principle of data minimisation in Article 5(1)(c) GDPR. 
Moreover, this principle also conflicts with the goal of preventing discrimination, 
which is a central aim of the AI Act proposed by legislators. Experience has shown 
that discrimination often arises when outdated or limited datasets are used . 
This issue can be mitigated by using a broad and unfiltered dataset. 
Proposal: 
• Deletion or relaxation of the data minimisation principle. 
3.5 No need for Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (Art. 27) Conform-
ity Assessment (Art. 43) 
There are already a whole range of regulations for the protection of fundamental 
rights, such as anti -discrimination regulations, the GDPR and insurance -specific 
regulations, which makes the Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment an unnec-
essary and additiona l burden. Furthermore, this is an additional specific require-
ment for deployers of high-risk AI systems in accordance with Annex III No. 5 (b) 
and (c), where additional regulations for the protection of insurance and banking 
customers already apply.  
In particular, the interaction between the fundamental rights impact assess-
ment and the data protection impact assessment (Art. 35 GDPR) remains

unclear. Fulfilling the requirements of both frameworks in parallel not only results 
in increased bureaucratic effort and additional costs but also creates significant 
legal uncertainty for companies. It remains unclear which set of rules takes prece-
dence in specific situations, inevitably leading to potential legal conflicts. The over-
lapping obligations should be clarified and streamlined, especially since Art. 35 
GDPR refers to personal data of a data subject in case personal data is processed 
whereas Art. 27 AI Act refers to “categories of natural persons or group of persons 
identified” by the deployer in case of a High-Risk AI System. In principle, the GDPR 
should not impose additional restrictions on the use of artificial intelligence 
beyond those already established by the AI Act. 
Furthermore, it remains unclear what ‘specific risks of damage affecting natural 
persons’ (Art. 27(1)(d)) means and how these risks should be assessed. It is also 
currently unclear whether a template questionnaire provided by the AI Office (Art. 
27 No. 4) would be mandatory to use and whether it would make things easier for 
deployers. 
Conformity Assessment (Art. 43 AI Act, Annex VI and VII) 
Companies have already implemented a quality management system in accord-
ance with Article 17 and a risk management system in accordance with Article 9 to 
fulfil these requirements. This must already be fully documented, which involves a 
significant amount of time and effort. 
The conformity assessment requires additional documentation for the evaluation 
of the quality management system and technical documentation, which is therefore 
an additional and unnecessary burden. 
Proposal: 
• Deletion of the requirement for a Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment un-
der Article 27 and the Conformity Assessment under Article 43 AI Act for sec-
tors already subject to specific fundamental rights and risk management regu-
lations (e.g. insurance, banking). 
3.6 Centralised market surveillance of AI systems in the financial sector 
We welcome the fact that Article 74 (6) of the AI Act provides for the financial su-
pervisory authority to be the competent market surveillance authority for the finan-
cial services sector. 
However, the restriction that the respective AI application must be directly related 
to the provision of financial services is insufficient. Instead, the financial supervi-
sory authority should be responsible for all applications. This will preserve func-
tioning structures and avoid duplication of supervisory activities.

For the supervised companies, separating AI applications according to different 
areas would create additional effort without adding value. In any case, the use of 
AI systems in companies cannot be separated by area. The development and im-
plementation of AI systems follows a uniform procedure; there is no distinction ac-
cording to areas of application.  
Shared responsibilities create the risk of demarcation issues and redundancies. 
Uncertainties regarding responsibilities and overlapping responsibilities will slow 
down processes and delay innovation.  
Proposal:  
• Adjustment and clarification of centralised market surveillance of AI systems 
in the financial sector in the AI Act 
3.7. Need for further clarification on the definition of AI 
In view of the recent uncertainties expressed by some member state authorities, it 
is necessary to reaffirm the exclusion of traditional statistical models (such as linear 
or logistic regression methods) from the AI definition and, therefore, from the scope 
of the AI Act. It should also be clarified that ‘generalised linear models’ (GLMs) are 
excluded from the AI definition, as they merely provide a predetermined output 
based on pre-programmed rules and do not draw any independent conclusions.

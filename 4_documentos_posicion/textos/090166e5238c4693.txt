Response to the Call for Evidence on the Digital Omnibus 
 
Introduction 
INVERCO is the Spanish Association of Collective Investment Institutions and Pension Funds. It represents 
more than 860,674 million euros in assets under management, channelling the savings of 24 million unit -
holder accounts in Investment Funds and Investm ent Companies and more than 10 million in Pension 
Funds. INVERCO represents 124 Spanish Investment and Pension Fund Managers (responsible for the 
management of 99% of the total assets under management in Spain  —jointly referred hereinafter as, the 
“Asset Management” industry), more than 30 international Managers and 32 Associate Members, including 
the main services providers to collective investment managers: audit firms, depositaries, law firms, platforms 
and IT providers.  
INVERCO welcomes the opportunity to comment on the Commission’s Call for Evidence on the 
simplification of the EU digital acquis. The Asset Management industry already operates under a stringent 
regulatory environment in the areas of data protection, cybersecurity and conduct of business, and therefore 
has a strong interest in ensuring that any streamlining initiative reduces unnecessary complexity while 
preserving legal certainty and high standards of investor protection. 
In this response, we focus on those areas where regulatory overlaps and uncertainty most directly affect 
Asset Management industry.  
 
1. The Data acquis 
For the Asset Management industry , fragmented and overlapping data rules create the most acute 
compliance challenges. The Commission’s approach to simplification rightly includes the Data 
Governance Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/868), the Free Flow of Non -Personal Data Regulation 
(Regulation (EU) 2018/1807), and the Open Data Directive (Directive (EU) 2019/1024).  
However, the Digital Omnibus exercise notably and without proper justification excludes the 
General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679), which remains the cornerstone 
of the Union’s data protection framework. If the purpose of the Digital Omnibus is to streamline and 
rationalise the EU’s data framework, the GDPR should form an explicit part of the review , at least to 
clarify its interplay with horizontal and sector specific rules. Without such clarification, overlaps and 
inconsistencies wi ll persist, driving duplicative compliance obligations, increasing 
administrative and supervisory burden, and ultimately undermining the effectiveness and 
credibility of the Commission’s simplification agenda. 
As outlined in section 3 below, several provisions of the  GDPR duplicate obligations already 
contained in other legislative acts , including the AI Act and DORA. These overlaps should be 
addressed through targeted adjustments to the GDPR, which, as argued above, should be explicitly 
included in the scope of the Digital Omnibus simplification initiative.

Achieving greater clarity and legal certainty is essential to foster innovation in areas such as portfolio 
optimisation, risk management, and fraud detection, while ensuring strong protection of investors’ rights. 
To this end, it might be worth exploring th e establishment of a European Financial Data Space under 
the Data Governance Act, which could provide a trusted framework with anonymisation toolkits, 
interoperable APIs and clear liability rules, thereby enabling secure data pooling and responsible data 
sharing. 
 
2. DORA 
The most effective lever for genuine simplification in the field of incident reporting (either by cyber 
threats or ICT-related events) is the reduction in the number of reporting fields and the extension 
of reporting deadlines in ICT incident reporting. Together, these adjustments would considerably 
ease the compliance burden on financial entities while ensuring that supervisors continue to receive 
timely and meaningful information. 
INVERCO notes that discussions around the potential establishment of a single EU-level reporting hub 
have gained prominence in the context of supervisory convergence
1. While such a hub has not been 
explicitly proposed in this Call for Evidence, it is recognised as an issue of strategic relevance for the 
financial sector and therefore merits further analysis and consultation. At this stage, however, we see 
value in approa ching the debate with caution. The Asset Management industry has already made 
significant investments to comply with the DORA framework, and any additional layer of centralisation 
would need to be carefully weighed against the costs, potential duplication of effort, and the risk of 
disrupting existing communication channels with national supervisors. 
In this regard, a pragmatic step could be to consider centralisation at national level, which would 
preserve proximity and efficiency in the dialogue with supervisors while contributing to greater 
consistency in reporting practices. It should also be recognised that, where asset managers form part 
of a banking or financial group, the reporting obligation can be fulfilled through a single notification by 
the parent company to the point of contact, covering all affected entities within the group.  Any future 
initiative to move towards a more ambitious EU -level structure should be the subject of broad 
stakeholder engagement and a comprehensive cost –benefit assessment
2, rather than being 
addressed within the limited scope of the Digital Omnibus call for evidence. 
In any event , the Digital Omnibus should not restrict its scope to incident reporting alone. Broader 
aspects of DORA also present opportunities for simplification. In particular, the consistent application 
of the principle of proportionality should be reinforced, so that smaller and less complex entities 
are not subject to the same obligations as systemically critical players. Likewise, obligations stemming 
from the oversight regime of critical ICT third- party providers should be streamlined as those 
are excessively burdensome.

Requiring Asset Managers to maintain detailed records of their ICT service providers (and also of the 
subcontractors) imposes an undue regulatory burden that is misaligned with the principle of 
proportionality. For firms without material systemic risk the effort needed to collect, verify, and 
continuously update this information might be  disproportionate to the risks involved and, in many 
cases, practically impossible to fulfil. For example, it is disproportionate and duplicative to require Asset 
Managers to exercise oversight over third party ICT service providers that are themselves directly 
subject to the same regulato ry framework, such as financial institutions under DORA or critical ICT 
providers already governed by DORA. This results in an obligation that risks becoming a formal 
compliance exercise with little supervisory value, diverting resources away from core res ponsibilities 
and ultimately reducing the efficiency of the regulatory framework.  
Finally, one additional point to consider would be the introduction of a regulatory and enforceable set 
of standard contractual conditions between financial entities and ICT providers, aimed at rebalancing 
the negotiating power that large technology providers currently hold over regulated financial clients. 
Addressing these elements alongside incident reporting would allow the Digital Omnibus to achieve  
genuine regulatory simplification while fully preserving resilience and supervisory objectives. 
 
3. Interaction between the AI, GDPR and DORA 
Several provisions of the AI Act duplicate or overlap with obligations already established under the 
GDPR and DORA: 
• Article 9 (Risk management system): mirrors GDPR Article 35 (DPIA) but lacks scope and 
methodology, creating uncertainty. 
• Article 10 (Data and data governance): overlaps with GDPR Article 5 on data quality and 
accuracy. 
• Article 14 (Human oversight): duplicates GDPR Article 22 on automated decision -making 
safeguards. 
• Article 26 (Deployers’ obligations): coincides with duties under GDPR and DORA, leading to 
overlapping compliance requirements. 
• Article 73 (Notification of incidents): replicates GDPR Articles 33 and 34 and overlaps with 
DORA reporting obligations. 
• Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (FRIA): substantially overlaps with GDPR Article 35 
(DPIA), raising the question of whether the two can be integrated into a single process. 
These overlaps, if left unresolved, risk creating redundant and conflicting obligations. What is 
needed are either targeted amendments to the Level I text of the AI Act, possibly complemented 
by technical adjustments to GDPR and DORA, or supervisory guidance clarifying how compliance 
under one framework can be recognised under the other. Without such clarification, firms are left 
with duplicative procedures, inconsistent expectations and unnecessary administrative 
burden. 
Beyond regulatory overlap, the absence of harmonised guidance on lawful data processing further 
hinders AI development in the Asset Management  industry. Divergent national interpretations  (for 
example in relation to biometric authentication, fraud prevention systems and creditworthiness 
assessments) generate uncertainty about the appropriate legal basis for data use. This undermines 
innovation while offering no additional protection for fundamental rights.

The successful application of the AI Act requires not only the resolution of overlaps with existing 
legislation, but also the creation of supportive conditions for innovation. Asset M anagers need clarity 
on supervisory competences: whether obligations such as the FRIA will be overseen by data protection 
authorities, AI supervisors or a coordinated structure. Without this, firms face conflicting interpretations 
and uncertainty as to compliance expectations. 
Simplification should also foster responsible innovation. It could be beneficial for the EU to consider 
establishing pan-European regulatory sandboxes using synthetic datasets and cross -border pilot 
facilities, where firms can test AI applications under regulatory oversight.  Likewise, the accreditation 
of independent algorithm auditors at EU level could provide a harmonised ‘AI -fit’ certification 
recognised across the Single Marke. Finally, the creation of dedicated training hubs under Erasmus+ 
or ESF+ would be a valuable way to address the skills gap among compliance, risk and portfolio teams, 
helping ensure the workforce is equipped to deploy AI responsibly and in line with EU values. 
Conclusion 
The Spanish Asset Management  industry supports the Digital Omnibus as a vehicle to achieve real 
simplification. However, we call on the Commission to resolve regulatory overlaps within the data 
acquis, to provide harmonised guidance on lawful data processing,  to strengthen the principle of 
proportionality and to reject duplicative reporting structures to ensure that the initiative delivers on its 
objectives. In doing so, the Digital Omnibus can serve as a practical step towards a more coherent and 
innovation-friendly digital framework, aligned with the Commission’s broader simplification agenda.

The Confederation of Finnish Industries (EK) input on the Digital 
Omnibus 
The Confederation of Finnish Industries (EK) represents 15,000 companies (96% SMEs) 
employing around 900,000 people. 
The European Commission has indicated that the Digital Omnibus will particularly address: 
• data legislation, including rules on cookies and other tracking technologies, 
• cybersecurity incident reporting, and 
• targeted adjustments to the AI Act to ensure its effective application. 
EK welcomes this comprehensive approach. However, the review should also include a 
streamlining of the GDPR, which would be most effective if conducted alongside the revision 
of other data-related legislation within the same process. The joint Nordic Business 
Organizations’ messages on GDPR simpliﬁcation are attached to this consultation response. 
GDPR 
Data has become a key enabler of advanced products and digital services. High-quality data 
is essential for developing competitive AI models and solutions. Pseudonymised data often 
suffices for innovation and carries minimal or no risk to individuals. However, the broad 
deﬁnition of personal data, combined with strict requirements for legal bases and purpose 
limitation, signiﬁcantly restricts data collection, sharing, and use for innovation and 
technology development in the EU. 
We propose that data should not be treated as personal data when processing is temporary 
and unrelated to identifying a natural person. 
We further propose that low-risk processing for R&D, AI training, and deployment should 
generally be considered lawful, even when special category data is involved, as this is crucial 
for developing unbiased services and products. 
More detailed GDPR simpliﬁcation proposals are attached. 
AI Development and Use in the EU 
While the AI Act and the GDPR govern different areas, they are closely interlinked and must 
be applied consistently. For high-risk AI systems, alignment between the two is essential to 
make compliance feasible. Parallel application currently creates overlaps: some provisions 
prioritize the GDPR, others align with it, and some add new requirements. To foster trust and 
innovation, compliance must be simpliﬁed.

For example, Article 59 of the AI Act allows deviation from the original GDPR purpose 
limitation in sandbox environments, e.g. for public health, public security, or the green 
transition. This exemption should be expanded to cover companies’ own AI development 
needs. 
Moreover, the connection between the GDPR’s restrictions on proﬁling (Article 22) and the 
AI Act’s high-risk use cases (Annex III) should be clariﬁed to reduce legal uncertainty and 
support AI pilots in both the public and private sectors. 
The implementation of the high-risk provisions of the AI Act should be postponed until 
standardized frameworks are available, giving companies sufficient time to integrate them. 
Roles and responsibilities across providers, deployers, and downstream operators in complex 
supply chains also require clariﬁcation. 
The provision allowing market surveillance authorities to access the source code of AI 
systems (Article 74(13)) should be repealed. To ensure effective supervision, we propose 
that the competent supervisory authority be empowered to prohibit the use of the AI system 
until the operator has reliably demonstrated the system’s technical compliance with the 
regulation. However, effective supervision does not require access to the source code. 
The AI Act should be implemented, including through codes of practice and guidelines, with 
a strong focus on the EU’s strategic goal of fostering AI adoption before introducing any new 
legislative changes. Implementation should be coherent, avoid regulatory overlaps, and 
remain grounded in the AI Act’s legal basis. 
Data and Data Sharing 
The EU has adopted numerous legislative measures on data sharing in recent years, leading 
to uncertainty, overlapping requirements, and administrative burdens. The EU must urgently 
address overregulation and fragmented implementation of the digital regulatory 
framework. 
Voluntary data sharing should be promoted through the forthcoming Data Union Strategy, 
while also safeguarding security and trade secrets under the Data Act and the Data 
Governance Act. The Commission should reassess data-sharing obligations beyond the 
EEA in light of evolving geopolitical circumstances and reinforce the protection of European 
companies’ data-driven expertise.

Facilitating data ﬂows and digital trade remains crucial. Adequacy decisions are valuable, 
but implementation could be simpliﬁed, for example by better recognizing intra-company 
data transfers. Alignment with international standards should be ensured in technical 
speciﬁcations and implementing acts. 
Manufacturers’ rights to access data from IoT products and related services must be 
strengthened for quality control, innovation, failure prediction, and product safety under the 
Data Act. 
The Commission should also establish a single compliance framework, similar to the GDPR. 
Currently, IoT products may face overlapping enforcement across multiple Member States. 
Greater clarity is also needed regarding the Data Act’s contractual obligations, especially in 
complex supply chains. Articles 32 and 28(1)(b) should be removed due to redundancy with 
GDPR Chapter V. 
The European Data Union Strategy should focus on effective implementation of existing 
rules rather than introducing new ones. 
ePrivacy 
Current cookie rules are outdated and require urgent reform. The ePrivacy Regulation 
proposed in 2017 was intended to replace parts of the ePrivacy Directive and harmonize 
cookie practices across the EU. However, after years of delay, the Commission withdrew the 
proposal in August 2025. As a result, the ePrivacy Directive continues to be applied 
inconsistently across Member States and overlaps with the GDPR. 
The ePrivacy framework should be revised and aligned with the GDPR, adopting a risk-
based approach and the same legal bases for data processing. Article 5(3) on cookie consent 
should be repealed and integrated into a GDPR-based solution, since cookies typically 
qualify as personal data. This would enhance legal certainty and reduce divergent 
interpretations across Member States. 
Cookies serving editorial or audience measurement purposes should be exempt from 
separate consent. In general, a more user-friendly approach is needed, allowing functional, 
security-related audience measurement and editorial cookies without prior consent. 
The current classiﬁcation of cookies as “necessary” or “non-necessary” no longer adequately 
reﬂects legitimate interests. Editorial cookies used for personalizing content or analytics in  
journalistic contexts should not require separate consent, as they are low-risk and essential 
for modern media services.

Cybersecurity 
Deﬁnitions of cybersecurity vary signiﬁcantly across EU legislation (e.g. Machinery 
Regulation, CRA, RED, NIS2), creating confusion for businesses. The Commission should 
harmonize and centralize these deﬁnitions to ensure consistent requirements across 
frameworks and provide cross-references where legislation overlaps. 
Duplicate reporting obligations should be eliminated by applying a “once-only” principle. A 
single national entry point for incident and cybersecurity reporting (all EU-regulations, 
including GDPR-related notiﬁcations) should be established, alongside common digital 
platforms for reportees integrating necessary information and where necessary, 
reporting templates for the same purpose and broader stakeholder participation in 
certiﬁcation processes. 
Coherence Across Strategies and Inclusion of Key Regulations 
The Data Union Strategy, Apply AI Strategy, and Union of Skills should be closely linked to 
ensure coherence. In practice, data capabilities and AI deployment develop hand in hand. 
Companies should not face uncertainty about regulatory compliance. Currently, data 
innovation and AI adoption are hindered by a fragmented and overly complex regulatory 
environment. Therefore, all relevant regulations should be included within the Digital 
Omnibus. 
EK calls for the EU to streamline existing regulations, postpone the application of new 
rules until necessary standards are available, and avoid introducing new digital and data 
economy legislation (e.g. AI in the Workplace), so that companies have sufficient time to 
adapt and remain competitive in the global market.

Background 
The focus on regaining the EU's competitiveness with the Omnibus packages is a necessary help for 
companies that need to balance between increased EU compliance complexity and competitiveness. Not 
least since companies experience that data protection has become disproportionate, overemphasizing 
protection where little is needed, hindering innovation where speed and ingenuity matter, and applying 
high administrative burdens where costs define competitiveness.  
 
The EU's current SMC Omnibus Proposal introduces  changes to the GDPR,  by extending the exemption 
for documentation requirements of low and medium risk processing activities. Although we welcome the 
changes, we acknowledge the strong need to further simplify the regulation. 
 
Introduction  
The reality of European businesses is characterized by increased competition from global players and a 
deterioration in competitiveness. Excessive regulation is one of the biggest hurdles for the digital 
economy’s competitiveness and growth in EU.1  
 
The general principles of the GDPR safeguard fundamental rights and ensure customer’s trust in digital 
services and new technology. When done in a proportionate and risk-based manner, innovation and 
competitiveness can be strengthened.  
 
The GDPR represents a hindrance, a limitation, and a burden on businesses in mainly two ways: 
 
Challenge # 1 Innovation and technology development  
The GDPR represents a severe hindrance to innovation and technology by setting absolute 
limitations for use of data and restricting the use of data for the purposes of developing new 
products and technology, such as AI models.    
 
Challenge # 2 Administrative burden and cost of compliance  
The GDPR puts an enormous administrative and cost burden in the form of documentation and 
technical infrastructure cost – affecting the general competitiveness of businesses.  
  
According to Draghi’s report, the GDPR has slowed down the growth of data-driven businesses in the EU by 
as much as 15–20 %, specifically in sectors such as the pharma and health technology. In addition, the 
costs of GDPR compliance may amount to as much as EUR 500,000 for a SME, and up to EUR 10 million for 
larger organizations.2

A new research report from Finland, states that the GDPR has cut pharma and biotech R&D spending by up 
to 39 % within four years. The effect is sharpest for firms operating solely under the strict EU regime, while 
multinationals soften the blow by shifting data-heavy work abroad. Smaller firms bear the brunt, as

are visible, as 61% of Danish companies in a report from 2025 found GDPR to be a burden4.   
  
EU needs to ensure that European businesses are given the necessary room to develop new innovations 
and new technologies, be competitive and take part in Europe's transformation to become a leading AI 
continent.  
  
If we are to regain some of the lost ground, costs and administrative burdens need to be kept as low as 
possible to get EU-made innovation and competitiveness fit for a global market. In the words of von der 
Leyen, we need less paperwork, less overlaps, less complex rules.5  This would also strengthen legal 
certainty, compliance and a level playing field. 
 
Challenge #1, Innovation and technology development  
More than ever before, data is the key component in creating advanced products and digital services, and 
high-quality data is essential for developing competitive AI models and solutions. To strengthen European 
tech capacity, the EU would benefit from a regulatory framework that does not hinder the development 
of AI-solutions, IoT-products, cloud storage solutions and AI models.6 They cannot be developed and 
improved without access to comprehensive and high-quality training data. De-identified or pseudonymised 
data is often sufficient for innovation and can be used with minimal or no risk to individuals. 
 
But the combination of a broad definition of personal data (also including highly de-identified data) and the 
requirements for legal bases and the purpose limitation, significantly restrict collection, sharing, and use 
of data for innovation and technology development (AI included).  
  
If European businesses are to develop technology such as AI-systems and -models, it must be clarified 
how (not if) they may process the data they need to develop digital products. Accessibility to high quality 
data is a prerequisite for EU- competitiveness.  
  
As a solution to the Challenge #1, we propose  
1) that data should not be treated as personal data if the processing is temporary and if the purpose 
of the processing is unrelated to identifying the data subject as a natural person.7 
2) processing both regular and special categories (e.g. sensitive) of personal data should not be 
disproportionally restrictive.8 We propose that low-risk processing for R&D purposes, training and 
use of AI should be deemed lawful as a main rule. Even if such data is special category data since 
this is crucial to achieve non-biased services and products. 
3) the balancing test required for legitimate interests should be unnecessary to document in many 
cases, and this should be explicitly stated in either the regulation or preambles.

Challenge #2: Administrative burden and cost of compliance  
The sum of all required GDPR documentation is comprehensive for nearly all businesses.  To this adds that 
authorities issuing guidance are often unable to clarify what is "enough", while also introducing new 
documentation requirements beyond those that are explicitly mentioned in the GDPR. Examples of this are 
requirements to conduct Transfer Impact Assessments (TIAs) before any third country transfer and 
Legitimate Interest Impact Assessments (LIIAs) when using legitimate interest as legal basis. 
 
There is a real risk that the documentation requirements will keep expanding in the coming years. This 
unfortunately increases the already present chilling effect companies exhibit and calls for more pragmatic 
decision making from EDPB and national authorities.   
 
Documentation requirements that create administrative burden without improving data protection, 
sometimes serving mainly auditing purposes, are of kind that would benefit from simplification. GDPR’s 
accountability and documentation duties should be more risk-based to reduce paperwork and focus on 
real risks.  
 
An example of the excessive administrative burden are the provisions and guidelines regarding third 
country transfers, which lead to irrational accumulative workload and bureaucracy for each company. 
Another area is data subject’s rights. They are important to keep but they need to be proportionate, less 
complex and less administrative burdensome. 
 
As a solution to the Challenge #2, we propose  
1) simplification of the documentation requirements in the GDPR and a real risk-based approach to 
reduce unnecessary paperwork, further improving on the SMC omnibus proposals. 
2) to limit administrative burdens related to the data subject’s rights by making them clear, limited 
and proportionate. 
3) to ease the burden on companies through additional adequacy decisions and clear 
recommendations on data transfers to third countries.

Art 6, 
Lawfulness of 
processing 
Processing of personal data for product and 
technology development (AI included), is currently too 
restricted. In practice, it is rarely feasible to base such 
processing on consent. At the same time, it is often 
uncertain whether other, more appropriate, legal bases 
such as legitimate interest (the balancing of interests)

assessment on a project-by-project basis cannot be 
completely removed. However, from a broader

processing personal data. Legitimate interest and 
contract are the most important and widely used legal 
bases for companies’ data processing. Their application 
should be straightforward in ordinary, low-risk 
situations. For processing of pseudonymised data for 
technology and AI development, the main rules should 
be that a company’s interest shall prevail. The possibility 
to rely on contractual processing should be broadened. 
The balancing test required for legitimate interests 
should, in most cases, be unnecessary, and this should 
be explicitly stated in the regulation.

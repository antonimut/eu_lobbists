About Seznam.cz
Founded in 1996, Seznam.cz remains the largest Czech online platform and news publisher, continues to
rank among the top five media companies in the Czech Republic. The company operates a comprehensive
portfolio of services, including news portals such as Seznam Zprávy and Novinky.cz, entertainment through
Stream.cz and the terrestrial Seznam TV, internet search, online advertising via the Sklik ad network, free
email under Email.cz, mapping and navigation services through Mapy.cz, price comparison tools such as
Zboží.cz,  and  industry-oriented  platforms  including  Firmy.cz  and  Sauto.cz.  Seznam.cz  also  owns  two
regional radio stations, Expres FM and Classic Praha.
With over 1,500 employees, Seznam.cz reaches more than 90 percent of the Czech internet population,
representing approximately eight million users.
In 2023, Seznam.cz generated revenues of CZK 6 billion and achieved a profit before tax above CZK
1 billion. The company continues to invest in infrastructure and innovation. In March 2023, it opened its
second  proprietary  data  centre,  Nagoya  in  Czech  Republic.  It  is  also  developing  artificial  intelligence
technologies, including a generative language model and improved fact-checking tools for comment sections
to combat misleading content.
Digital Omnibus (Call for Evidence)
We would like to begin by expressing our sincere appreciation to the European Commission for launching the
Digital Omnibus initiative and opening the consultation process to stakeholders across the digital sector. This
open and inclusive approach provides a valuable opportunity to share practical experience and constructive
proposals aimed at improving the regulatory environment for digital services in the European Union. In this
context, we welcome the chance to contribute our perspectives on key areas such as the ePrivacy Directive,
the AI Act, the NIS 2 Directive, and the Digital Services Act.
I) ePrivacy Directive
1) Cookies
When considering the regulatory framework for cookies under the ePrivacy Directive and related digital
legislation, it is important to distinguish between different types of cookies based on their function and
impact on user privacy. While technical cookies are already recognized as essential and exempt from
consent requirements, the current regime often places analytical cookies under the same obligations

as marketing cookies, despite their fundamentally different purposes. We believe that, under clearly
defined conditions, analytical cookies should be treated in the same way as technical cookies and not
require user consent. The following arguments support this position and highlight the benefits of such
an approach for both users and website operators.
Essential for Website Operation and Service Quality
Analytical cookies that are used solely for measuring website traffic, detecting errors, and optimizing
website performance are crucial for website operators to ensure security, stability, and quality of
services. Without such data, it is not possible to efficiently manage the website, detect technical issues,
or plan capacity. If the data is anonymized and not used for user identification or marketing, the impact
on privacy is minimal.
Limited Impact on Privacy
Unlike marketing cookies, which track user behaviour across websites and create profiles for targeted
advertising, analytical cookies (in their basic form) only aggregate statistical data about website usage.
If implemented in a way that does not allow identification of individual users (e.g., anonymizing IP
addresses, not using unique identifiers), the privacy risk is negligible.
Reducing User Fatigue from Consent Banners
The current practice, where users are constantly required to provide consent for various types of
cookies, leads to so-called “cookie fatigue.” Users often accept without reading the details, which
paradoxically reduces the level of real awareness and privacy protection. Exempting analytical cookies
from consent would better protect users from unnecessary overload and allow them to focus on
genuinely sensitive types of cookies.
Consistency with ePrivacy Objectives – User Friendliness
The European Commission, in its draft ePrivacy Regulation, itself proposed that “non-intrusive”
cookies used to improve user experience or measure website traffic should not require consent. The
aim is to simplify the rules and reduce administrative burdens for both operators and users, without
compromising privacy protection.
Clear Legislative Definition is Possible
It is possible to precisely define by law which analytical cookies may be used without consent – for
example, only those that do not allow user identification, are not shared with third parties, and are
used exclusively for aggregated measurement of website traffic and operation. Such conditions would
ensure privacy protection while enabling effective website management.
2) Consent or Pay
We support the introduction of the “consent or pay” principle as a modern and pragmatic approach to
balancing user privacy, business sustainability, and regulatory compliance in the digital environment.
Under this model, users are offered a genuine choice: they may either consent to the processing of
their personal data (for example, for personalized advertising) in exchange for free access to digital
services or opt for a paid version of the service that does not involve such data processing. This
approach empowers users to make informed decisions about their privacy and supports the continued
availability of high-quality, free online content and services.

transparency, respect user autonomy, and reduce administrative burdens for both consumers and
businesses. To ensure fairness and compliance with EU data protection law, it is essential that:
Consent is freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous, in line with the requirements of the
GDPR and ePrivacy Directive.
The  paid  alternative  is  genuinely  equivalent to  the  free  service,  except  for  the  absence  of
personalized advertising or tracking, and is offered at a reasonable price.
Users are  not subject to undue pressure, coercion, or negative consequences for refusing
consent, and the choice between consent and payment is presented in a clear and accessible
manner.
Special attention is paid to vulnerable groups, ensuring that privacy does not become a privilege only
available to those who can afford it.
The model is accompanied by robust transparency measures, including clear information about data
processing practices and the implications of each choice.
II) AI Act
We understand the concerns of EU and US companies regarding the scope of the recently approved
AI Act, which is, in some cases, very demanding. It is therefore understandable that the European
Commission is seeking to ease some of its provisions or interpretations. As representatives of online
content creators of text, audio, and video, we wish to voice our opposition to any simplification of the
already vague copyright protections in Art. 53 of the AI Act. On the contrary, the obligations set out
should be expanded and specified further to reflect current best practice. At the same time, we believe
that the EU should contribute much more actively to finding a reasonable balance between the creative
sector and AI developers. Compromises and practical solutions need to be sought that would protect
the interests of content creators without overly restricting the development of AI in the EU.
III) NIS 2 Directive
Based on practical experience, we would like to highlight the following areas where there is room for
improvement:
1. Simplification and harmonization of incident reporting obligations
Currently,  organizations  must  report  incidents  under  several  regulations—for  example,  GDPR
(personal data breaches), NIS2 (cyber incidents), DORA (financial sector), or other specific rules. Each
regime requires different data, different deadlines, and different recipients. This leads to duplication,
ambiguity, and errors.
We propose that a single report could fulfil multiple legal obligations at the same time. The European
Commission should support the principle of single reporting, either through a central European portal
or through a single interface where data would be entered once and then automatically distributed to
the relevant authorities (data protection supervisory authorities, national CSIRTs, sectoral regulators,
etc.). This approach would significantly reduce the administrative burden and increase the efficiency of
the entire process.
2. Reducing duplication between EU and national regulations

The fragmentation of obligations is also evident between the EU and Member State levels. Some
countries, including the Czech Republic, require additional reporting or use their own formats and
deadlines, which leads to legal uncertainty and often to inconsistent results.
We therefore consider it essential that the revised framework enshrines the principle of "one report,
multiple uses", which will be binding not only for European regulations but also for their national
implementation. Common data models, harmonized deadlines, and clearly defined thresholds should
be set out in a binding manner so that businesses know exactly when and how to proceed.
3. Linkages with other areas of digital legislation
Cybersecurity incidents often have implications for other areas of digital regulation. For example,
failures or attacks can affect the functioning of digital identity (eIDAS2) or the operation of artificial
intelligence systems (AI Act) . If legal frameworks are not interoperable, there may be overlaps,
duplicate obligations, or even situations where individual regulations contradict each other.
We therefore propose that the review also focus on linking cybersecurity with other digital regulations
and that clear cross-references and methodological guidelines be developed. These guidelines should
explain how businesses should proceed if an incident falls under multiple legal regimes at the same
time.
4. Role of regulators and supporting materials
The role of national regulators is a very important factor in successful implementation. They should not
only act as supervisory and sanctioning authorities but should also have sufficient capacity to perform
an advisory function—that is, to provide methodological support, issue practical guides, and explain
the overlaps between individual regulations.
Experience shows that the lack of supporting materials is a major problem for businesses. In some
cases,  the  publication  of  promised  methodological  documents  is  repeatedly  postponed,  which
increases uncertainty and complicates the proper implementation of requirements. Timely and high-
quality methodological  support  is  essential  for  organizations  to  be  able  to  fulfil their obligations
effectively and in accordance with the law.
IV)  Digital Services Act
Unfortunately, the Digital Services Act (DSA) is not among the digital regulations identified by the
Commission for review. We have identified several areas where administrative costs place an
unfair  burden  on  local  European  businesses  without  providing  any  meaningful  benefits  to
European citizens. Such changes would align with the goals of the Digital Omnibus, and we would
therefore like to suggest including them in the upcoming proposal. These suggestions can be
divided into the following sections:
1)  Clarify the definition of “online platform”
Compared to other types of services, the DSA establishes a substantial number of obligations for
online platforms. To prepare for compliance with the regulation, service providers must be able to
correctly identify the type of service they are providing. Issues with identification can be illustrated by
the recent Zalando case decide d by CJEU . To increase legal certainty and enable innovation, we
suggest clarifying that services classifiable as online platforms should contribute at least 50% of the
provider's total turnover for the service to be identified as such. Additionally, the Commission should
clarify that providing space for discussions or blogs in connection with editorial or news content does
not lead to identification as an online platform.
Some argue that providing space for online advertisements constitutes hosting. This is a dangerous
idea because online advertising is the main source of revenue for most websites, which would lead to
hosting obligations for the whole web.  Under this interpretation, any website displaying advertising

2) Base exemptions on the platform´s reach, not size of the provider
While  the  DSA already  provides  exemptions  for small  and  micro  providers  in  certain  areas  of
regulation, this owner-based approach has several drawbacks. These categories are based on the
size of the provider without considering that such an online platform may be only a secondary asset
for the provider and have only a minor impact on EU citizens. The user base of these platforms and
their contribution to the total turnover of the parent company (used for calculating potential sanctions)
is, however, minimal. This leads to a disproportionate burden and has already forced some of these
platforms to cease trading. Therefore, we suggest adopting the risk-based approach used in other EU
regulations  and  introducing  a  new  set  of  exemptions  from  administrative  obligations  for  online
platforms with fewer than 5 million users (10% of the VLOP threshold). All online platforms must
already publicly declare this number in accordance with Art. 24(2), so it would be easy for the DSCs
to monitor potential circumvention. The following exemptions should be established for these minor
online platforms:
● Art. 20 – For smaller platforms, the system for handling complaints appears to be underused, yet
providers still need to maintain the system architecture and employ staff to handle complaints. To
maintain the variety of smaller online services, the article should only be mandatory for larger
platforms.
● Art. 21 (5) – We understand the intention to protect users from expensive lawsuits that they would
not be able to afford, unlike the very large online platforms. However, for smaller platforms, the
obligation to pay all the recipient's expenses in the event of losing the dispute would pose
significant financial issues. Therefore, this obligation should apply only to large or very large online
platforms, or be omitted altogether. We find it unjust and an excessive financial risk for smaller
online platforms that in case the out-of-court dispute settlement body decides in favour of the
provider, the provider has no right to be reimbursed for the expenses of the out-of-court dispute
by the recipient unless the recipient manifestly acted in bad faith. Even more so since it is out of
the provider’s control how many out-of-court dispute settlements will be initiated by recipients. We
propose deleting this provision, unless the whole Art. 21      would be considered for deletion.
● Art.  24 (5)  – The  data on restrictions imposed by the provider are contained in the publicly
accessible transparency report pursuant to Art. 15.       In view of that, as well as in view of
administrative burden on the side of the providers to establish the API or send the individual
statements of reasons (after excluding personal data) via the web interface manually,       the
added      value of the DSA Transparency Database  by smaller online platforms      is rather
minimal, so importing the      data into the Commission's database can be omitted. Alternatively,
the requirement to report even minor content changes (e.g. , changing a sign in a comment ,
correction of typos, reformatting of posts to comply with technical requirements, etc.     should be
excluded.

Committed to Europe 
Orange Group’s position on the European Commission consultation on the 
Digital Omnibus 
 
Executive Summary  
We warmly welcome the EC willingness to simplify the digital rule book. Achieving such a 
simplification is of paramount importance to ease operators’ compliance. This is one of the key 
aspects of strengthening EU competitiveness.  
We call the EC to adopt an ambitious Digital Omnibus proposal and ensure the same approach 
will apply to the future Digital Network Act. The current European Electronic Communications 
Code also deserves a substantial simplification exercise notably in terms of consumer protection 
rules, since some of them have become obsolete or redundant with EU horizontal laws.  
The main simplifications that should be included in the Digital Omnibus, and further detailed 
below, are the following:   
AI Act  
▪ The implementation of the rules on high-risk AI should be postponed to one year after the 
standardisation process has been finalised.  
▪ We call for setting up one single certification for all harmonised standards.  
▪ The Act should ensure that a single point of contact is available at national level covering the 
various public authorities in charge of monitoring the AI Act implementation.    
ePrivacy Directive  
▪ We call for the repeal of the provisions on metadata (art.6) to ensure operators can innovate 
and manage data under the same rules (GDPR) as other digital entities, while inserting the 
principle of confidentiality of communications in another EU law (ex: DNA, GDPR).  
Data Act  
▪ The Commission should clarify the scope of the Act as definitions of “Connected Product” 
and “Connected Service” create legal uncertainty, particularly regarding whether servers, 
routers, and set-top boxes fall under mandatory data-sharing rules. The Data Act should 
explicitly exclude some products such as rented or leased devices.  
▪ Overlaps between the Data Act and the GDPR risk conflicting data rights and hindering lawful 
innovation – guidance is necessary. 
Cybersecurity Regulations (NIS2, CER, CRA, DORA)  
▪ Reporting requirements should be simplified and streamlined across all Regulations, notably in 
terms of what should be reported, their thresholds, timelines and procedures of reporting.  
▪ Establishing a single-entry point for reporting in Member States. 
▪ For cross border incidents or those affecting multiple entities within a group, clarification and 
consistency between the various texts is paramount regarding the entity responsible for 
reporting the incidents and the concept of ‘main establishment’.  
▪ Specifically related to DORA: we call for the EC to publish standard contractual clauses to 
clarify customer-supplier and supplier-supplier relations. Provisions on the definitions 
(‘ongoing basis’ and ‘financial performance’) and on audit should be clarified. The EU should 
establish a presumption of conformity that NIS2 providers are compliant with DORA.

Detailed remarks 
AI Act  
1. Standardisation and Implementation date of the high-risk AI provisions 
The standards on high-risk AI rules are not ready yet, which creates uncertainties for the AI 
Implementation. We call for a postponement of the entry into force of those provisions, which 
should apply one year after such standards are available.  
 
We also believe it would help implementation to set up one single certification for all harmonised 
standards under definition and ensure a total alignment between the various standards under 
definition.  
 
2. Setting up a single point of contact at national level  
Member States are currently organising themselves and allocating competences to various 
national authorities to monitor the implementation of the AI Act. In certain countries it will lead to a 
patchwork of national entities in charge of some parts of the provisions of the AI Act.  
 
We call for a simplified regime whereby entities providing or deploying AI use cases would have to 
liaise with one single national point of contact. This single point of contact would then be in 
charge of contacting/liaising with any other national authority in charge of the topic. For European 
international companies, dealing with several entities in the various Member State they are 
operating in would not simplify AI adoption or improve business efficiency. 
 
We also call for improving harmonisation in the implementation of different legislations at national 
level, for instance between GDPR or ePrivacy and the AI Act.  
 
3. Single Platform gathering all compliance information on GPAI 
It would help downstream providers to have a single platform at EU level where to find all relevant 
information on GPAI models and GPAI models with systemic risks, including the list of entities 
qualified as such, the list of open source GPAI models, and the summary of data used for training.  
 
4. Clarification of biometrics definition  
The AI Act should be clarified regarding the definition of biometric and emotional AI based tools, 
especially in the context of GPAI, to better understand the scope of the AI Act rules.  
 
We therefore ask the Commission to: 
▪ Postpone for one year the implementation of provisions on high-risk AI following the 
finalization of standardization works.  
▪ Set up one single certification for all harmonised standards.  
▪ Ensure that a single point of contact is available at national level covering the various 
public authorities in charge of monitoring the AI Act implementation.

ePrivacy Directive  
While acknowledging the Commission’s efforts to review the ePrivacy Directive with regard to 
cookies, we believe this review should be extended to the provisions on confidentiality of 
communications and metadata (articles 5 and 6).  
The rules on metadata (article 6) adopted more than a decade ago have become obsolete and put 
the European operators under more stringent rules than big tech notably hindering their capacity 
to handle those data and innovate under the same rules.  
We call for the repeal of this article 6, as the metadata can already be addressed under the GDPR 
while the principle of confidentiality (article 5) could be inserted in an EU horizontal law (ex: DNA, 
GDPR). 
We therefore ask the Commission to: 
▪ Repeal article 6 of the ePrivacy Directive  
▪ Insert the provisions on confidentiality of communications in an EU horizontal law (ex: 
DNA, GDPR).  
 
The Data Act 
1. B2B and B2C data sharing obligations 
a) Definition of a “Connected Product.” 
First, it should be remembered that the Data Act was designed in response of the rapid IoT sector 
growth, to correct the imbalance between manufacturers controlling product data and users 
generating it, and to ensure data access and re -use to drive downstream innovation. Clearly, 
telecom devices form part of the essential infrastructure that enables connectivity, not the 
downstream IoT ecosys tem where data lock -in occurs. Likewise, connectivity services were 
specifically left outside the scope of the Data Act. 
Article 2(5) of the Regulation defines a Connected Product as: “ an item that obtains, generates or 
collects data concerning its use or environment and that is able to communicate product data via 
an electronic communications service, physical connection or on -device access, and whose 
primary function is not the storing, processing or transmission of data on behalf of any party other 
than the user” .  
The FAQ of the Commission p.9 introduces a precision in relation to Connected Product that is not 
in the definition of Article 2(5) : Products which primarily fulfil the function of storing, processing, or 
transmitting data (e.g., servers and routers) are outside the scope of the mandatory data -sharing 
obligations under Chapter II of the Data Act, unless they are owned, rented, or leased by the user." 
Applied literally, this addition could undermine the definition in Article 2(5) itself, nullifying the 
exception it contains . Routers, modems or set -top-boxes belong to equipment which primary 
function is “ operating for a party other than the User ”. Any device that performs interpersonal 
communication or internet access function operates on behalf of a party other than the user: it 
allows communications to be carried one way and the other way round, including at least operating 
for one party other than the user. As such they should be excluded from the definition of “connected 
product” in the Regulation.  
4 
However, this qualification  seems different under the FAQ, as in the electronic communications 
sector, it is common for internet service providers (ISPs) to rent to their subscribers ’ gateways or 
other equipment used for the processing or transmission of data.  
Clarification is needed to ensure predictable compliance boundaries  and to considerably simplify 
the potential obligations in terms of access to data and reporting for ISPs.  
We therefore ask the Commission: 
▪ To introduce a list of excluded devices directly in the text of the regulation , as was partly the 
case in the original proposal of the Data Act (Recital 15). This list should explicitly extend to 
servers, routers and set-top-boxes. 
▪ To remove the carveout “unless they are owned, rented, or leased by the user” from the FAQ 
page 9, as it creates legal uncertainty and undermines the regulation’s purpose. 
 
 
b) Definition of a “Connected Service” 
The current Data Act FAQ1 states: “The service must affect the connected product's functions, 
behaviour, or operation. Determining the ‘functions’ of a connected product is an ongoing and 
evolving task. Practice and courts’ interpretation will play an essential role in further delineating 
whether a digital service is a related service.” 
While this guidance acknowledges the evolving nature of digital services, it does not provide 
sufficient clarity for businesses. 
We therefore ask the Commission: 
▪ To provide clarification on the concept of “functions”. It is for instance unclear whether it refers 
to hardware operation, software-based usage, or both, and how content delivery impacts 
device functionality. 
 
c) Overlaps between Data Act and GDPR 
The relationship between the GDPR and the Data Act raises several questions of overlap and 
potential conflict. The Data Act imposes significant restrictions on data holders’ ability to use or 
share data, which may contradict rights recognized under the GDPR. 
Recognized rights to use personal data 
Under the GDPR, data controllers may rely on lawful bases for processing personal data, such as 
the performance of a contract (Article 6(1)(b)) or legitimate interest (Article 6(1)(f)). However, Article 
8(4) of the Data Act prevents data holders from making data available to third parties except at the 
explicit request of the user. This provision risks creating “locked datasets,” limiting legitimate data 
uses otherwise permitted under the GDPR.  
The resulting uncertainty may hinder European industry’s ability to make lawful and beneficial use 
of data derived from customers or business systems, particularly for the development of artificial 
intelligence.

Restrictions of capabilities to use non-personal data 
Article 4(13) of the Data Act provides that a data holder may only use non-personal data on the 
basis of a contract with the user. This raises definitional and operational issues. If such data are 
“non-personal,” they cannot be tied to identifiable users, making the concept of “user’s non-
personal data” incoherent. Conversely, if the data can be indirectly linked to individuals through 
additional identifiers, they constitute pseudonymized data and should be treated as personal data 
under the GDPR, rendering the term “non-personal data” inaccurate. 
Moreover, the Data Act acknowledges in Recital (34) that personal and non-personal data are 
often inextricably linked, echoing Article 2(2) of Regulation (EU) 2018/1807 on the free flow of non-
personal data. In such cases, GDPR rules prevail, since mixed datasets cannot be effectively 
divided into separate regulatory categories. The terminology of “non-personal data” therefore 
becomes misleading. 
Where the user is a legal person rather than a natural person, “non-personal data” largely 
corresponds to confidential business information, such as trade secrets, intellectual property, or 
security data. These are already governed by confidentiality agreements and Directive (EU) 
2016/943 on the protection of trade secrets. Treating such information under the Data Act’s data-
sharing regime introduces unnecessary constraints and could effectively prohibit normal data 
uses not explicitly foreseen in contractual terms. 
We therefore ask the Commission: 
▪ Revise provisions such as Article 8(4) to remain compatible with GDPR lawful bases (Articles 
6(1)(b) and 6(1)(f)), to safeguard legitimate, GDPR-compliant data processing for innovation 
and AI. 
▪ Ensure that EU trade secrets remain protected under Directive 2016/943 and provide EU-
level guidance on how explicitly how the GDPR and Data Act interact, specifically how GDPR 
rules take precedence where datasets overlap. 
 
 
Cybersecurity Texts  
The adoption in the previous mandate of a multitude of cybersecurity legislations (NIS2, Critical 
Entities Directive (CER) Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), DORA) with non-aligned provisions leads to 
complexities and overlaps for multi-country Groups such as Orange.  
 
1. Lack of consistency across the various texts 
There is an urgent need for simplification notably as differences in reporting obligations, timelines, 
procedures and tools/designated authorities, reporting for Groups of multiple entities, lead to a 
complexity and substantial additional administrative burden from an organisational perspective 
when implementing procedures to be compliant with the different texts.  
 
Some examples of what, when and how reporting is required: 
▪ What is to be reported in terms of incidents:  
o Under NIS2, Reporting of an incident that has a ‘significant impact’ with a subsequent 
definition of what is considered significant. 
o Under CER, Reporting of a ‘critical incident’ that means an event that causes or risks 
causing a ‘significant disruption’ to the functions of critical entities that is then further 
detailed. 
6 
o Under CRA, Reporting of an ‘actively exploited vulnerability’ and/or ‘severe incident’ 
affecting ICT products or services 
▪ Timelines for reporting:  
o Under NIS2, ‘Without undue delay," and in any case, within 24 hours of becoming 
aware of the incident.  Follow up within 72 hours with a final report 1 month after.   
o Under CER, no later than 24 hours after becoming aware of the incident. or as per 
national procedures followed by a detailed report 1 month after. 
o Under CRA, within 24 hours an early warning, following by a notification within 72 
hours, and a final report within 14 days (for actively exploited vulnerabilities) and 1 
month (for severe incidents unless already provided within 14 days.) 
▪ Authorities to whom reporting incidents are to be addressed:  
o Under NIS2, Entities must notify their national CSIRT or competent authority.    
o Under CER, Notify the designated national competent authority. 
o Under CRA, Reporting to national authorities designated for market surveillance or 
cybersecurity oversight and simultaneously to ENISA via a Single Reporting Platform. 
o Reporting of cross border incidents or those affecting multiple entities within a group: 
clarification is required as the legislation does not clearly specify the manner in which 
reporting is required in function of the text, specifically concerning NIS2.  For instance: 
▪ Under the Cyber Resilience Act, section 7 of article 14 of regulation (EU) 
2024/2847 the text states that the reporting shall be submitted to the CSIRT of 
the Member State where the manufacturers have their main establishment in 
the Union.  
▪ Under NIS2, Article 26 of Directive 2022/2555 states that the essential and 
important entities shall be considered to fall under the jurisdiction of the 
Member State in which they have their main establishment in the Union, where 
Article 23 does not specify which entity in a Group shall submit the reporting.   
▪ Clarity on the notion of ‘main establishment’ is also necessary 
 
We therefore ask the Commission: 
▪ Reporting requirements afore mentioned should be simplified and streamlined across 
Regulations, notably in terms of what should be reported, their thresholds, timelines and 
procedures of reporting to a unique national appropriate authority.  
▪ Establishing a single-entry point for reporting in Member States would allow a one-time 
submission, automatically distributing the information to all relevant authorities. 
▪ For cross border incidents or those affecting multiple entities within a group, 
clarification is paramount. The entity responsible for reporting the incidents should be clearly 
defined in all texts, in a similar way, specifically in NIS2. Where there are multiple entities 
within a Group, the entity reporting should be that of a single-entry point of the most relevant 
jurisdiction, i.e. where the Group of entities has its main establishment in the Union. Finally, 
enhanced clarity on the notion of ‘main establishment’ is also a prerequisite. 
 
2. Specific issues at stake concerning DORA and proposed solutions 
 
While several aspects of DORA raise uncertainties or complexities and should be simplified, 
notably on training/awareness programs, the scope of the RTS on subcontracting, the concept of 
resellers, the scope of the supply chain mapping, we list below the main issues that should be 
addressed as a priority: 
7 
▪ Publication of Standard Clauses 
o Issue: Lack of standardised contractual clauses that align with regulation, risking 
inconsistency. 
o Solution: Publish standard contractual clauses by the European Commission that 
reflect the regulation, similar to GDPR, to ensure supply chain security for customer-
supplier and supplier-supplier relations. 
▪ Audit Rights 
o Issue: Unlimited audit rights pose risks related to foreign interference, confidentiality, 
and trade secrets. 
o Solution: Limit audit rights to what is necessary and proportionate, ensuring they do 
not exceed the scope mandated by DORA. 
▪ Definitions of ICT and CTPP 
o Issue: The term "ongoing basis" may be vague, leading to inconsistent interpretations. 
The concept of "financial performance" is too broad and misunderstood 
o Solution: Clarify that ICT services must be delivered on an ‘ongoing basis’, either 
through explicit regulation language or guidelines from ESAs. Restrict the scope to the 
"impacts on the client's information system (IS)," focusing on significant effects related 
to DORA. 
▪ Legal Presumption of Compliance for NIS2-Subject Providers 
o Issue: Uncertainty about whether providers under NIS2 are presumed compliant with 
DORA. 
o Solution: Establish a legal presumption that NIS2 providers are compliant with DORA 
for overlapping scopes and obligations, simplifying compliance processes. 
▪ Complexity in the Implementation of Flow-Down Terms to Subcontractors 
o Issue: Clients often demand exact replication of contractual provisions, including 
notification periods and audit conditions, which is impractical given the global and 
multi-client nature of subcontractors. Only mandatory DORA provisions should be 
flowed down, avoiding overly restrictive or unnecessary clauses. 
o Solution: Clarify that only the contractual provisions mandated by DORA need to be 
flowed down to subcontractors, and that additional or more restrictive terms are not 
required. This ensures consistency and practicality across multiple engagements.

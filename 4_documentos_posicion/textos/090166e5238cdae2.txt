The European Publishers Council (EPC), representing leading news media and 
publishing organisations across Europe, strongly supports the need for pragmatic 
reform of the ePrivacy Directive’s outdated rules on cookies and tracking 
technologies. The current consent -based framework generates severe “consent 
fatigue” among users, distorts the digital advertising market, and limits publishers’ 
ability to monetise quality journalism responsibly. Furthermore, these outdated 
rules are stifling innovation in privacy-enhancing technologies, as companies focus 
on navigating complex consent requirements rather than investing in new privacy-
by-design solutions such as secure data clean rooms or contextual targeting 
models that could offer both high privacy standards and sustainable business 
models.  
 
Pluralistic, free and responsible journalism is one of the strongest pillars of 
democracy. It is important that independent journalism is given an opportunity to 
be run as a sustainable business. Regulation must recognise the unique role of 
trustworthy journalism in the digital information landscape and ensure its viability 
in online environments.  
 
To strike a fair balance between privacy protection and sustainable media business 
models, EPC calls for: 
 
• Legal clarity on the overlap between ePrivacy and GDPR  to remove 
inconsistencies and the cumulative application of both texts and provide 
publishers with predictable rules on user consent and processing. 
• Recognition of the legitimate interest as a lawful basis for the processing 
of personal data, introducing a risk based approach with its accompanying 
safeguards.

Review of article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive 
 
The EPC welcomes the work of the European Commission to consider updating the 
rules on cookies and other tracking technologies. We believe that a solution can be 
found to better align the rules of the ePrivacy Directive with the risk based approach 
of the GDPR in a manner that recognises the operational level of the digital world, 
while continuing to maintain the strong principles of privacy and data protection, 
and offer publishers a more flexible environment which can support sustainable 
business models which are essential for Europe’s media plurality.  
 
 
1. Importance of access to data for publishers: 
As audiences move online and traditional print revenues continue to decline, 
European publishers depend increasingly on data to understand audiences, keep 
readers engaged and to finance quality journalism through subscriptions and 
digital advertising. 
Advertising is the main source of revenues for European publishers, followed by 
subscriptions. However, currently most of the subscription revenue originate from 
print subscriptions, which is a heavily declining stream, while publishers also 
observe a decr easing in users willingness to pay for digital news (especially 
younger audiences). Therefore, the share of publishers' revenue that will depend 
on advertising will grow and the survival of European media plurality will depend 
heavily on the ability to effectively refinance digital content through advertising1.

industry outlook, 2025”

Advertising accounts for between 50% and, in some cases, up to 100% of total 
revenue for most publishers. The vast majority of display advertising relies on 
various forms of data -driven targeting, which are essential for maintaining 
commercial viability. B y contrast, contextual advertising represents only a small 
share of publishers’ advertising revenue, primarily due to limited demand from 
advertisers for this type of inventory.  
Data, therefore, plays an important role to enable publishers to monetise their 
content, through the placement of advertising. Data supports many functionalities, 
from ad serving and performance measurement, including frequency capping 
functionality, first -party audience measurement, storage of privacy/consent 
choices; support for monetisation models as well as security and fraud prevention.   
Data plays a vital role in publishers’ ability to innovate and develop new products 
that respond to audience interests and attract new readers. Cookies and other 
tracking technologies are not solely linked to advertising; they are also essential for 
measuring and understanding user engagement, for example through website 
analytics and audience behaviour tools that enable publishers to improve content, 
functionality, and user experience. These measurements help publishers 
understand the value and attractiven ess of their content, as well as support their 
effort in developing innovative content, and increase their relevance towards their 
users. Personalisation is also an essential element for publishers, allowing them to 
develop and adjust their sites functions, features and content to increase relevance 
to their users.  
Publishers also face unique challenges in sustaining their businesses, particularly 
as they must compete with very large platforms whose share of advertising 
revenues far outweighs that of the press, and which capture not only the largest 
portion of the ma rket but virtually all recent growth in digital advertising income. 
As a result, publishers’ share is remarkably low by comparison, which directly 
undermines their ability to monetise content and fund independent journalistic 
work. Publishers also handle d ata processing in ways that differ significantly from 
large platforms.  Media companies handle personal data on a much smaller scale 
than large platforms. Their processing is fair, expected by users, and typically not 
cross-service. The purpose of this dat a is directly linked to serving readers and 
sustaining quality journalism, contributing pluralistic, free and responsible 
journalism, as one of the strongest pillars of democracy.  
 
 
2. A confusing overlap between 5(3) and personal data 
 
Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive (ePD), adopted in 2009, provides a rigid legal 
framework which requires the user’s consent (as defined in the later adopted GDPR) 
for any storing of information or gaining access to information stored on a user 
device.  
 
 
 4 
As lex specialis to the GDPR, the ePD takes precedence in regulating electronic 
communications, including the use of cookies, trackers, and software development 
kits. In practice, the consent requirement for electronic communications under the 
ePD applies before and independently of GDPR's legal bases for processing 
personal data. 
Yet, this interplay between the ePrivacy Directive and the GDPR causes significant 
legal uncertainty. 
First, the broad interpretation of the scope of article 5(3) by the EDPB has led to its 
application to almost all online interactions, creating legal uncertainty for widely 
accepted use cases such as caching, custom fonts, DNS lookups, CDN requests, 
responsive images, video buffering, automatic updates, security handshakes, tag 
management. 
Second, authorities, scholars and courts disagree on what constitutes personal data 
in online interactions.  
The Article 29 Working Party, the predecessor of the EPDB, considers a person 
identified when they are distinguished from all other members of a group. This 
interpretation means the GDPR applies whenever controllers reach you as a specific 
individual throu gh cookies, IP addresses, or other ways of distinguishing one 
device from another. 
The Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) has taken a more narrow  stance 
in two critical decisions. In Breyer v. Germany (2016), the CJEU held dynamic IP 
addresses may be personal data if the controller has legal means to identify 
individuals. However, an IP address doesn't directly reveal an identity or relate to a 
natural person2. In EDPS v. SRB (2025), the Court added an additional requirement 
by ruling pseudonymised data isn't personal data unless the controller has realistic 
and lawful means to re -identify subjects. This represents a decisive move from 
"theoretically identifiable" to "practically identified" with reasonable technical 
means as a new threshold. 
Therefore, a broad interpretation of personal data and the technical scope of 
ePrivacy means both legislations apply cumulatively for most online 
communications.  
The conflation of the scope of article 5(3) together with the notion of personal data, 
triggers the   cumulative application of the two legislations without enabling 
publishers to benefit from the flexibility offered by the GDPR.

3. Main challenges for publishers 
 
• Impact on publishers financial sustainability 
The broad applicability of art 5 (3), with very limited exemptions, has made it very 
hard to be compensated for journalistic content if users refuse consent choices. 
This has led many publishers to implement so -called consent or pay models. The 
model offers users the choice to either pay for accessing publishers' content, or to 
consent to the different data processing purposes to provide publishers with 
sufficient advertising revenues to make its content accessible without monetary 
compensation. However, th e consent -or-pay model is currently under close 
scrutiny by some regulators and courts, who question whether the “pay” alternative 
is sufficient to ensure that user consent is genuinely freely given. Some authorities 
argue that users should be able to prov ide granular consent for each specific 
purpose of data processing, without regard for what is necessary or proportionate 
to the publisher’s business model. Such an approach significantly increases the 
number of choices presented to users, thereby aggravati ng consent fatigue and 
undermining the goal of providing clear and meaningful user control. 
 
• Legal uncertainty around common use cases 
More generally, the legal uncertainty which persists about common use cases 
represents a huge challenge for publishers which miss out on  further opportunities 
of monetisation through different advertising models. As mentioned above, the 
consent or pay mod el remains under significant scrutiny preventing publishers 
from fully embracing this model which offers users choices, while ensuring 
publishers generate revenues for the access to their content. Contextual targeting 
also remains uncertain. While this adv ertising model has been presented as a 
solution to the consent fatigue problem in certain forums, it falls under both article 
5(3) and GDPR cumulatively. Contextual advertising as a unique solution bears a 
number of additional challenges for publishers (see section below).  
 
Common advertising use cases for publishers face legal uncertainty. Contextual 
advertisements based on an IP address alone may require consent based on the 
EDPB’s interpretation of the scope of ePrivacy. Session identifier for impression 
measurement may or may not constitute personal data. Direct marketing banners 
using account data under a legitimate interest require a separate consent per 
website or application. This can lead the same user to express different preferences 
for the GDPR versus ePrivacy choice for this single use case. 
 
• High cost of maintenance 
The cost of implementing a consent banner solution on a publisher site (i.e. 
partnering with a Consent Management Platform CMP SaaS (Software as a Service), 
implementing in house solutions, etc…)   vary greatly from publisher to publisher, 
based on country, market size and other criteria, but can range from €100K up to 
€700K for a larger publisher per year.  
 
 
 
 6 
Most pieces of code on a media website or app run conditional on consent choices. 
Indirect costs derived from dealing with the complexity of consent, such as legal 
and technical expertise, management of technical suppliers, etc.  
The technical expenses associated with the strain that Consent Management 
Platforms (CMPs) impose on publishers' websites and apps, including their 
development and server -side capabilities (such as Data Management Platform, 
Customer Data Platform, and first-party analytics), arise because all data flows must 
pass through intricate consent choices, where user preferences can be revisited 
and altered at any time.  
 
• Limitation on innovation in Privacy Enhancing Technologies 
Privacy Enhancing Technologies can be useful to publishers to reduce the risks 
associated with some of their data processing activities. However, the legal 
framework in place which requires consent for almost all interactions creates zero 
incentive to inve st in PETs that could balance performance and privacy. In most 
cases, Publishers would have to continue to seek a user’s consent, and bear the 
associated cost of collecting and managing this consent, regardless of the 
safeguards that the PETs could bring forward, therefore limiting the added value of 
the PETs altogether.  
 
 
4. Updating the legal framework 
 
• A necessary media stress test 
Pluralistic, free and responsible journalism is one of the strongest pillars of 
democracy. It is important that independent journalism is given an opportunity to 
be run as a sustainable business. Regulation must recognise the unique role of 
trustworthy journalism in the digital information landscape and ensure its viability 
in online environments. 
 
Policies are often made with one objective in mind - e.g. consumer protection, or 
data protection, which often creates unintended consequences for editorial media 
and undermines their financial sustainability. For this reason, it is essential that the 
European Commission evaluates specifically the impact that changes brought to 
the ePrivacy Directive would have on media viability, ensuring that policies do not 
accidentally undermine publishers financial sustainability, but instead actively help 
the sector prosper. Making a media sustainability impact assessment would ensure 
that EU policy is coherent, future -proof and supports financial sustainability of 
editorial media, and democracy. 
 
The implementation of new, or amended legislation relies on interpretations, 
including interpretations by regulators. The legal uncertainty that publishers 
continue to face today, such as around the topic of consent or pay, should be 
avoided at all cost. Providing clarity on the intention of the legislator in the law itself 
would highly contribute to any legal uncertainty, or overly restrictive interpretation.

• Clarifying the scope of article 5(3) 
There is a need to better clarify the scope of article 5(3), as well as its interplay with 
the GDPR.  
 
The notion of “storing of information” and of “gaining of access to information 
already stored” needs to be reviewed in order to limit the scope of application of 
5(3), and avoid an undistinctive application to all online interactions. Clarifying the 
scope of the article would have the benefits of formalising the reality of today’s 
internet, taking into consideration user’s online experience and expectation.  
As an example, IP addresses are transmitted automatically and merely processed 
to serve information from a server rather than being stored for further processing. 
Also, the interpretation of “storage” has been broadly interpreted by including any 
memory and compute, and “gaining access”, by including the passive receipt of 
HTTP metadata such as an IP address. 
 
• Introducing a risk based approach  
EPC is suggesting a better alignment between the ePrivacy and the GDPR in order 
to depart from the current very rigid legal framework while recognising the GDPR's 
multiple legal bases when personal data is processed. The objective is to adopt a 
framework which provides the possibility to adjust the level of safeguards to the 
level of risk attached to the data processing for the user. The ePrivacy would only 
apply residually to non-personal data or to the confidentiality of communications.  
 
This approach would ensure that where the processing results in higher risk, the 
assessments under GDPR would anyway result in the need to rely on the user’s 
consent, therefore preserving user rights, while offering the possibility for lower risk 
processing to be performed on the basis of legitimate interest.  
 
In order to bring clarity to article 5(3) and the interplay with GDPR, where personal 
data processing is covered by GDPR and legitimate interest applies, this should 
qualify and be recognised as an exception to the consent requirement under Article 
5(3) ePrivacy.  
 
Practically, with this approach, users would be presented with three different 
situations: 
 
i. opt-in (consent) for data processing which generate higher risks; 
 
ii. opt-out (legitimate interest) for lower risk personal data processing; and 
 
iii. exemptions (legitimate interest and performance of a contract). 
 
Aligning article 5(3) with GDPR ensures online communications with personal data 
benefit from the corresponding principles. Different data use cases benefit from 
 
 
 8 
the more sophisticated principles (e.g., data minimisation and proportionality), 
legal bases (contract, legitimate interest) and controls (e.g. DPIA, ROPA, privacy by 
design, data subject rights, transparency, vendor agreements, data breach 
procedures) related to the legal bases of GDPR art. 6. 
 
Conducting an assessment of the risk offers the opportunity to take into 
consideration many criteria, including the level of identifiability, the level of insight, 
the purpose of the processing, but also the context of the processing as well as the 
expectation of the user and the relation between the user and the service provider. 
The balance of power between the user and the service provider should also be 
considered.  
 
The risk based approach is better adapted to the diversity of online advertising 
practices and their associated data processing. Instead of potentially considering 
one specific advertising model as low risk and another as high risk, the risk based 
approach requires an assessment adapted to the specificities of the model. It would 
support the opportunity for publishers to explore advertising models that involve 
processing of less personal data, and how to incentivise innovation in privacy 
solutions which wou ld maintain publishers’ ability to generate revenues, while 
departing from the costs associated with consent collection.  
 
As an example, advertising, based on i) a session cookie, ii) a persistent cookie, or 
iii) a user profile built over time with different data sources do not bear the same 
level of identifiability and insight therefore leading to different risk assessment.  
 
Similarly, this approach would help distinguish between 1st party and third party. 
While a first party might be in a position to demonstrate a legitimate interest, a  third 
party with no direct contact with the user might not. This distinction is crucial f or 
sustaining independent journalism and ensuring media pluralism in the EU. 
 
It is important to restate here that relying on an opt -out mechanism, using the 
legitimate interest as a legal basis, would trigger the safeguards embedded in the 
GDPR, including:  
 
• Publishers have to conduct a three-part balancing impact assessment; 
 
• Publishers have to assess whether their interest to process personal data are 
not overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedom of the 
users;  
 
• In the case where publishers can rely on their legitimate interest, they have 
a transparency obligation towards the user; and 
• In the case where publishers can rely on their legitimate interest, they have 
an obligation to offer users the right to object to the processing of their 
data.

• Benefits of a risk based approach 
Supporting editorial media: recognising publishers' legitimate interest to process 
data in a more flexible legal framework would support publishers in implementing 
editorial decisions and curating content in line with journalistic standards and user 
expectations, through the delivery of high-quality media services.  
 
Enhancing the development of PETs : Simplifying consent requirement and 
introducing a risk based approach with low risk processing exempted from consent 
requirement would incentivise publishers   as well as other adtech partners to 
engage in low risk data processing, in an effort to limit t heir reliance on user’s 
consent. This evolution is likely to further develop new low risk solutions which 
could also lead to some changes in the functioning of the adtech ecosystem. 
 
Incentivise the reliance on lower risk advertising models: Enabling publishers 
to rely on their legitimate interests is also likely to incentivise the exploration and 
development of advertising models with lower risks for people’s privacy, such as 
models based on first party data while maintaining a viable adverti sing business 
model. This approach would incentivise publishers to engage in low risk data 
processing, as publishers should be able to rely on their legitimate interest for such 
processing. 
 
Limit consumer consent fatigue but improve their risk awareness: the proposal 
would limit   users’ consent fatigue by no longer exacerbating their concerns 
regarding operational activities which are inherent to the architecture and the 
functioning of the internet but which bear no privacy intrusion. In addition, this 
approach is likely to strengthen their protection, by increasing their awareness of 
practices with higher risk for which consent will continue to be required. Until now, 
the overreliance on consent has not created an improved level of trust from users, 
confronted throughout their online experience with consent banners. This new 
approach will contribute to strengthening user’s trust online.  
 
• Exceptions in article 5(3) 
Aligning the ePrivacy and the GDPR is the best approach to ensure a future proof, 
flexible approach while preserving user’s privacy. However, in the case where the 
European Commission would solely consider extending article 5(3) list of 
exceptions, minor adjustments to the existing exemptions will not be sufficient to 
address the challenges identified by the European Commission, and would fail to 
meet the objective of simplification intended by the Digital Omnibus.  
 
Exemptions to the consent requirement in article 5(3) would need to consider a 
more general approach and enable publishers to cover first -party data collection 
online (including declared user data), to support sustainable business models that 
do not pose h igh-privacy risks. A mere exemption for a specific business model 
such as contextual advertising is not a sustainable alternative (see section below on

contextual advertising). Publishers need to (a) both understand who their 
audiences are (in particular how younger audiences consume / do not consume 
news) in order to keep news products engaging, increase subscription numbers 
and compete with social media , and (b) be able to sell sufficiently attractive 
advertising to maintain advertising revenue which implies at the very least some 
kind of "interest" -based advertising. The latter can, as a safeguard, be limited to 
first parties and avoid extensive data sh aring with third party data controllers and 
the so-called "broadcasting" of data to the adtech ecosystem.  
 
Therefore, EPC would encourage the European Commission to consider further 
exemptions in article 5(3) beyond “technical storage or access” and   “strictly 
necessary” for the following functionalities: 
 
• Core service delivery and access management, including user 
authentication/session management/customisation;  digital rights 
management, technical updates. 
 
• Personalisation, including editorial cookies, that are aimed at analysing the 
use of editorial content and organising of editorial content, providing   
recommendations, based on user-declared and first-party behavioural data. 
Editorial cookies, used for content personalisation and editorial analytics, are 
essential for implementing editorial decisions and curating content in line 
with journalistic standards and user expectations.  
 
• Analytics and product development, including audience measurement, 
optimisation. Accurate audience data is vital for the media economy and 
should reflect actual user numbers, not just those who have given consent. 
This aligns with Article 24 of the Europea n Media Freedom Act, which 
promotes transparent and fair audience measurement practices. This 
audience measurement helps publishers understand the value and 
attractiveness of their content, as well as support their effort in developing 
innovative content, and increase relevance towards their users. Controllers 
should be able to perform cross-site and cross-device analytics for first-party 
domains3. Reliable figures on actual audiences are vital for advertising sales 
of editorial media. 
 
• Ad serving and performance measurement, including frequency capping 
functionality, metadata at impression level, including campaign ID, and 
technical measurement (e.g. Performance data such as ad impressions); first-
party audience measurement (reach, viewi ng time, programme 
performance); contextual advertising; storage of privacy/consent choices; 
support for monetisation models (e.g., paywalls, ad-based tiers).

5. Contextual advertising 
In the debates around digital advertising and privacy, contextual advertising has 
been presented often as a solution. EPC would like to recall that contextual 
advertising as a stand alone model is not a sustainable solution for publishers for 
several reasons: 
 
• It is important to understand that each publisher relies on its own specific 
combination of revenue sources, including subscription (print and digital), 
tracking based advertising and potentially other advertising models. The 
suggestion that all tracking b ased advertising filled inventories on a 
publisher's website can be replaced by contextual advertising negates the 
complexity of publishers advertising revenue structure. Contextual 
advertising is far from being ideal and bears a substantial lack of scalability.  
 
• In addition, it is important to understand that the decline of print 
subscriptions, combined with a decreasing willingness to pay for digital 
news from users (especially younger audiences) means that publishers' 
revenue share that will depend on advertisin g will grow. Contextual 
advertising alone cannot fill these gaps. 
 
• Not all pages have content for which an ad can be relevant, and not all ads 
can find a page with relevant content. Thus, contextual ads would primarily 
impact a publisher’s capacity to monetise general categories pages, such as 
news, politics and current affairs.  
 
• Furthermore, contextual advertising also requires a number of data 
processing actions and access to the user's device, such as for technical 
delivery, frequency capping, preventing invalid traffic, invoicing, etc, which 
requires the user consent under the ePrivacy Directive and the latest EDPB 
guidelines 2/2023 on Technical Scope of Art. 5(3) of ePrivacy Directive. 
Therefore, the burden related to consent management remains the same for 
publishers, generating little incentive to engage in this approach. 
 
 
 
 12 
• Finally, Digital advertising is an entire ecosystem with many different actors. 
Advertising models used by publishers are also highly dependent on the 
interest of advertisers and agencies to spend towards these different 
models. Advertisers are not necessa rily willing to have their campaign only 
placed on publishers' sites on a contextual basis. Publishers assess carefully 
any changes to their advertising model, considering the risk that an even 
higher amount of ad spend may be rerouted towards large online platforms. 
 
 
6. Centralised consent tools  
Part of the reflection around updating article 5(3) has been to reduce user consent 
fatigue through the development of global/centralised choice mechanisms. 
However, this approach raises a number of concerns particularly   for publishers, 
including: 
• Any centralised/global approach would de facto create a new intermediary 
between a publisher and their audience, setting the path towards new 
gatekeepers and cannot be dissociated from a real reflection on the issue of 
its governance. 
• Any centralised/global approach would prevent publishers from monetising 
their services, maximising their revenues or developing their business model 
by limiting their ability to directly engage with their audience, providing 
tailored languages and solutio ns. In addition, the development of a 
centralised consent tool would encroach on publishers’ fundamental right of 
freedom to conduct a business and choose their business model and the 
kind of advertising model they wish to offer within the limit of the law.  
• Consent given at such a centralised level will not be specific enough to 
comply with GDPR and ePrivacy requirements, thus would not replace the 
obligation for each publisher to continue presenting users with cookie pop 
ups on each visit. 
• A centralised model may create data protection and privacy issues, through 
the concentration of users data in one single location.  
Alternatively, we feel that consent fatigue can substantially be reduced by the 
introduction of the risk based approach described in the sections above. In 
addition, further regulatory and technical solutions should be explored, enabling 
them to make choices for a different scope of actors or services. For example: 
• Solutions to ensure choices expressed by users are persistent over time on 
the same domain; 
• Solutions to ensure choices expressed by users are applicable cross devices; 
and 
• Solutions to offer users with choices for a group of publishers

Implementation of the AI Act  
1. Copyright 
EPC would welcome the inclusion of measures to support the smooth application 
of the AI Act. Any simplification should facilitate compliance and enforcement, 
bridging the gap between regulatory intent and practical implementation, while 
ensuring that no existing obligations are weakened. 
The Digital Omnibus should facilitate: 
a) Clear guidance on the relationship between the AI Act and the Copyright and 
CDSM Directives, ensuring that content used for training general -purpose AI 
systems complies with EU copyright law. Where rights have been reserved by 
publishers under the TDM excepti on, AI developers must seek the necessary 
permission before using publishers’ content, and 
b) Effective compliance tools, particularly for model transparency templates, which 
must be adapted to the needs of the publishing sector. If insufficient detail is 
provided for publishers to enforce their rights, these templates fail to meet legal 
requirements. The European Commission should, at a minimum, establish a 
procedure enabling rightsholders to request further details from AI developers 
to determine whether their content has been used. 
Such measures would enhance legal predictability and reinforce Europe’s 
leadership in trustworthy, rights-respecting AI. 
We should not forget that publishers play a dual role: as users of AI systems, and, 
importantly, as rightsholders whose content is used extensively by AI developers. 
The Commission should therefore revise the GPAI Code of Practice, the obligations 
relating to the training data summary template, and the GPAI guidelines to ensure 
effective enforcement of Article 53, strengthen legal certainty, and align them with 
the EU’s wider digital framework, while properly reflecting the concerns of 
publishers that were largely overlooked in earlier discussions. 
 
2.  Transparency and Accountability 
In the context of the Digital Omnibus, measures should prioritise simplification, 
coherence, and proportionality across the digital regulatory framework. The goal 
must be to ensure that transparency rules reinforce trust and accountability without 
duplicating obligations, creating inconsistencies, or imposing unnecessary 
burdens on publishers and other media actors. 
a) Editorial Transparency and Independence: Transparency rules under the 
AI Act must clearly distinguish between AI outputs created under human 
editorial control and fully autonomous AI -generated content. Publishers 
already operate within robust editorial responsibility and legal liability 
 
 
 14 
frameworks and must not be subject to additional transparency or oversight 
obligations that would interfere with editorial freedom protected under the 
European Media Freedom Act. 
b) Transparency for Pure AI Outputs:  For AI systems that generate content 
autonomously, strong and consistent transparency standards are essential 
so that users can recognise synthetic material and understand that it is not 
subject to editorial or legal accountability. Labelling should be vis ible, 
persistent, and interoperable across platforms. Transparency obligations 
should also be linked to provenance and training data disclosure under 
Article 53, allowing rightsholders to identify whether their works have been 
used unlawfully. 
c) Transparency in Advertising:  Transparency measures must be 
proportionate and aligned with existing consumer protection and 
advertising law. Overly broad labelling of all AI -assisted content in 
advertising would create confusion, add compliance burdens, and risk 
undermining legitimate freedom of commercial communication. The Unfair 
Commercial Practices Directive already prohibits misleading or deceptive 
advertising, including AI-generated material, and should remain the relevant 
safeguard.

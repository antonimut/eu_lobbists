  Key  proposals:  
●  One  streamlined  template :  To  make  the  system  more  effective,  the  EU  should  move  towards  a  single,  harmonized,  standardized,  machine-readable  reporting  template  across  all  legislation,  with  one  streamlined  format,  clear  thresholds,  and  a  simplified  process,  supported  by  stronger  coordination  and  resources  for  ENISA.    ●  Establish  National  Single  Reporting  Entry  Points:  Each  Member  State  should  establish  a  single  national  portal  for  all  cybersecurity-related  incident  reporting (under  NIS2,  CRA,  DORA,  GDPR,  ePrivacy).  This  portal  would  then  securely route  information  to  the  correct  authorities.   ●  Align  reporting  deadlines  and  thresholds:

o  Deadlines :  NIS2  requires  entities  to  notify  the  CSIRT  or  competent  authority  within  24  hours  of  a  ‘significant  incident’  and  report  it  within  72  hours,  yet  some  Member  States,  such  as  Hungary  and  Croatia,  only  mandate  reporting  ‘without  delay’,  creating  divergence  that  undermines  harmonisation.  To  ensure  consistency,  national  CSIRTs  should  be  the  primary  notification  points  across  EU  legislation,  aligning  with  the  CRA  and  strengthening  trust  and  information-sharing.   o  Thresholds :  Further  discrepancies  arise  from  reporting  thresholds,  such  as  the  Dutch  draft  law’s  use  of  ‘may’  instead  of  NIS2’s  ‘likely  to’,  which  broadens  the  scope  of  incidents,  adds  uncertainty,  and  complicates  compliance  for  cross-border  providers.  Harmonised  rules  are  therefore  essential  to  prevent  unnecessary  compliance  costs  without  delivering  proportional  security  benefits.

Key  Proposals  
●  Align  Definitions:  To  improve  legal  certainty,  key  definitions  must  be  harmonized  across  frameworks.  This  includes:  o  Main  Establishment:  Aligning  the  CRA  definition  and  criteria  for  the  "main  establishment"  principle  with  the  definition  used  in  NIS2.   o  Concept  of  “Becoming  Aware”:  The  concept  of  when  an  entity  “becomes  aware”  of  an  incident  must  be  consistent  across  all  cybersecurity  regulations.  Alignment  should  be  ensured  with  the  EDPB’s  guidelines  on  personal  data  breach  notifications  and  the  NIS2  Implementing  Regulation  2024/269,  which  define  awareness  as  the  point  at  which  an  entity  has  a  reasonable  degree  of  certainty  that  a  security  incident  has  occurred.  o  Develop  a  Common  Taxonomy :  At  present,  the  NIS2  Directive  refers  to  “significant  incidents”,  whereas  the  CRA  uses  the  term  “severe  incidents”.  ENISA  should  take  the  lead  in  developing  a  common  incident  taxonomy  to  ensure  consistency  across  legislative  frameworks,  increase  legal  certainty  for  industry,  and  reduce  regulatory  overlap.    ●  Resolve  Overlap:  Clarify  and  resolve  overlap  between  regulations,  such  as  the  scope  of  "remote  data  processing  services"  under  the  CRA  versus  cloud services  under  NIS2.   ●  Introduce  Reciprocity:  Where  requirements  overlap,  compliance  with  one  regulation  should  be  recognized  for  another. Key  proposals:   
●  Establish  a  Cyber  Readiness  Framework:  The  prevailing  trend  is  that  cyberattacks  are  escalating  in  speed  and  effectiveness,  creating  a  mismatch  between  the  attack  speed  and  the  speed  of  effective  cyber  defense.  We  propose  a  new  comprehensive,  outcome-oriented  approach  built  on  a  Cyber  Readiness  Framework  to  efficiently  and  effectively  implement  existing  regulations  like  NIS2,  DORA,  and  the  CRA.  This  framework  aligns  the  policy  lifecycle  with  the  evolving  threat  landscape  by:  ○  Continuously  monitoring  and  analysing  the  cyber  threat  landscape  and  overall  cybersecurity  posture.  ○  Promoting  state-of-the-art  AI-enabled  cyber  defenses  for  real-time  resolution.  ○  Measuring  the  cybersecurity  outcomes  and  identifying  potential  gaps.  ○  Setting  the  right  conditions,  such  as  the  real-time  sharing  of  security  data .   ●  Address  Implementation  Gaps:  Timelines  for  implementation,  especially  for  new  rules  like  the  AI  Act  and  the  CRA,  must  be  realistic,  allowing  sufficient  transition  times  and  ensuring  guidelines  and  harmonised  standards  are  available  well  ahead  of  deadlines.  For  stronger  implementation,  guidance  on  requirements  should  be  released  simultaneously  with  regulations  like  NIS2.

Key  proposals:  
●  Establish  a  "Security  Data"  Sub-Category:  We  propose  the  establishment  of  a  sub-category  of  data,  called  "Security/Systems  Data,"  and  ensure  the  free  flow  of  such  data  in  real  time.  It  is  essential  that  regulatory  frameworks  do  not  restrict  the  processing  of  this  data  for  cybersecurity  purposes  as  it  is  critical  for  identifying  both  known  and  unknown  threats,  vulnerabilities,  preventing  and  responding  to  cyber  incidents,   cyber  threat  intelligence,  and  enhancing  overall  network  security.  All  these  activities  are  foundational  to  advancing  the  overall  privacy  of  EU  citizens  and  safeguarding  their  data  against  increasingly  sophisticated  cyber  threats.  Security/Systems  Data  encompasses  various  types  of  information  generated  or  collected  through  the  use  and  operation  of  cybersecurity  products  and  services.   
○  This  category  of  data  can  include  logs,  telemetry  data,  threat  intelligence,  and  others,  which  are  crucial  for  real-time  threat  detection  and  mitigation.  When  identifiable  personal  information  is  identified  within  collected  systems  /security  data,  it  is  strictly  for  the  purpose  of  ensuring  and  enhancing  network  and

information  system  security,  which  is  in  compliance  with  existing  legitimate  interests  in  most  privacy  regulations  across  the  globe.  The  ability  to  process  Security/Systems  Data  in  a  manner  to  address  a  dynamic  and  growing  cyber  threat  landscape  is  imperative  for  cybersecurity  providers  to  protect  European  entities  effectively. ○  A  balance  between  privacy  rights  and  cybersecurity  needs  will  help  ensure  the  continued  protection  and  resilience  of  European  digital  infrastructure  against  cyber  threats.

  Key  suggestions:   ●  Enhancing  the  European  Cybersecurity  Certification  Framework:  The  framework’s  potential  depends  on  a  clear,  efficient,  and  globally  interoperable  approach.  We  offer  the  following  recommendations:

●  Focus  on  Technical  and  Objective  Criteria:  Certification  must  be  technical,  risk-based,  technology-neutral,  and  outcome-focused.  Non-technical  “sovereignty”  requirements  (e.g.  data  localisation,  ownership,  immunity  from  non-EU  laws)  weaken  security,  raise  costs,  reduce  choice,  and  conflict  with  EU/international  rules.  Such  debates  should  be  handled  outside  the  CSA  review.  ●  Ensure  Harmonisation,  Avoid  Fragmentation:  The  CSA  should  guarantee  that  EU  schemes  override  national  ones,  with  sunset  clauses  and  clear  transition  paths  (mutual  recognition/grandfathering)  for  existing  national  certifications.  ●  Use  International  Standards  &  Mutual  Recognition:  Align  with  recognised  global  standards  (e.g.  ISO/IEC)  and  support  mutual  recognition  of  equivalent  schemes  and  CABs  from  trusted  third  countries.  ●  Improve  Scheme  Development:  o  Set  predictable  deadlines  for  scheme  adoption.  o  Clarify  and  strengthen  the  roles  of  the  European  Cybersecurity  Certification  Group  (ECCG)  and  the  Stakeholder  Cybersecurity  Certification  Group  (SCCG),  with  the  SCCG  empowered  to  issue  opinions  and  engage  regularly  with  the  ECCG.  o  Keep  ENISA’s  role  purely  technical  and  independent,  with  political  choices  made  via  legislation  before  technical  mandates.  ●  Ensure  Proportionality  &  Flexibility:  o  Certification  should  remain  voluntary  by  default,  with  mandatory  schemes  only  for  high-risk  cases  after  impact  assessment.  o  Allow  vendor  self-assessment  and  declarations  of  conformity  at  Basic/Substantial  levels,  given  the  shortage  of  CABs.

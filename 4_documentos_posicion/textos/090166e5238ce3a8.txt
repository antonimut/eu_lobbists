 
PUBLIC SAP Point of View  EU Digital Omnibus SAP has long engaged constructively in policy discussions shaping the future of EU digital regulation. Historically, SAP has supported the overarching objectives outlined in European Commission proposals, while contributing additional perspectives from its position as a global B2B cloud technology provider and offering constructive critique when warranted. However, the legislative wave adopted between 2019 and 2024 has reached a scale that has become extremely burdensome for businesses. This challenge is compounded by the rapidly evolving global context. The EU must match the pace of technological and economic transformation by delivering less bureaucracy, greater regulatory agility, and deeper collaboration with companies operating at the forefront of global change. The upcoming Digital Omnibus initiative presents a strategic opportunity to recalibrate Europe’s regulatory framework to better foster growth, innovation, and competitiveness. With this instrument, we urge the EU to take the approach “simple as possible, strict as necessary.” From SAP’s perspective, three legislative areas merit particular attention within this reform process: • Data Act It is critical that the Omnibus includes a substantive review of the Data Act, with particular attention to provisions that negatively impact cloud service providers. As currently structured, the Data Act imposes considerable administrative burdens, regulatory overlaps, and compliance costs, while generating more risk and uncertainty than other data governance instruments mentioned in the Call for Evidence. A meaningful reduction of the regulatory burden in EU data governance requires the inclusion and revision of the Data Act. • AI Act and Cyber Resilience Act SAP recommends delaying the application of the AI Act with respect to high-risk AI systems, as well as postponing the implementation of the Cyber Resilience Act, until clear and practical compliance guidance and European harmonised standards for presumption of conformity are available for both laws. Without such clarity, companies face significant uncertainty that hinders innovation, readiness, and in most severe cases – market access. SAP is concerned by the absence of any explicit reference to the Data Act or the AI Act or CRA delay in the Call for Evidence linked to this consultation. Excluding these three laws from the scope of the Omnibus – despite strong calls from EU industry for revision, clarification, or phased application – would represent a missed opportunity. Addressing them directly aligns with the recommendations of the Draghi report and would enable the Commission to strengthen its role as a champion of European economic resilience in an era of systemic global change. Overarching Principles in EU Digital Framework • Clarity before the law takes effect: Recent experience shows that EU laws often become enforceable before oversight bodies and compliance guidance or secondary acts are in place. Alternatively, the necessary clarity in the form of guidance, implementing/delegated acts, or national transposition comes shortly before the compliance deadline. This creates legal uncertainty for businesses: for both compliance teams and technical experts who need clear instructions in development phase. Companies often need to start preparing to compliance working on assumptions, only to change the course of action when clarity is provided. The European Commission and Member States should coordinate to ensure regulatory readiness and sequence the implementation of new rules so that guidance is available before they take effect, safeguarding business continuity and avoiding unnecessary burdens. • Regulatory timelines should reflect feasibility rather than political ambitions: It is essential to allow sufficient transition periods to minimize business disruption and ensure regulators are fully prepared, including for guidelines, secondary acts, model contracts, and harmonized standards. Thorough impact assessments should be completed well before public consultations, which themselves should be extended to give stakeholders ample time to provide informed feedback. This approach fosters smoother implementation and greater regulatory effectiveness. • Coordination in oversight and interpretation of the law: The rapid expansion of the EU’s digital acquis has increased the number of national and EU-level regulators with narrow, uncoordinated mandates, leading to fragmentation when authorities interpret and enforce rules differently, and inconsistencies when overlapping frameworks are applied in divergent ways. To address this, the EU should promote structured dialogue and coordination between regulators, encourage Member State authorities to collaborate on intersecting regulations, and enable EU-level bodies to form ad-hoc fora for consistent interpretation of laws such as the AI Act, GDPR, and cybersecurity legislation (NIS2 vs DORA vs CRA). An imposed statutory duty to consult and cooperate across competent authorities for digital laws would help prevent conflicts between authorities, strengthen Single Market cohesion, and ensure alignment of policies and legal interpretations. These efforts should be guided by open consultation with regulated companies and stakeholders, applying transparency and proportionality principles. • Recognition of international standards and third-country Conformity Assessment Bodies to accelerate compliance and market access: Global standards provide a clear, consensus-based way to demonstrate compliance, streamline the EU’s regulatory framework, and support companies in expanding internationally. Increasing regionalization risks duplicating and fragmenting standards, undermining their global value, and stretching the scarce skills base. The EU should enhance mutual recognition of both internationally accepted standards and Conformity Assessment Bodies (CABs) in third countries to simplify compliance and reduce burdens in the Single Market. Where EU harmonized standards are delayed, such as under the CRA or AI Act, as it the appointment of national notified bodies, recognition of global standards and trusted CABs would provide practical, efficient routes to compliance, avoid duplicative testing, and accelerate market access. EU Data Framework (1) Challenges on the Data Act: The Data Act is a large and complex legal text with ambitious goals, including removing barriers to the transfer of data in the EU Single Market and achieving a more competitive market, in particular by lowering barriers to provider switching. However, in the current form, the Data Act poses challenges due to unclear definitions of in-scope products, services, and data, complex requirements, overlaps, and uncertain deliverables like guidance and standards. Its complex obligations generate high implementation costs and compliance burdens, discouraging investment in data innovation:  • Notably, the uncertainty persists around Data Processing Services definition. To determine if a cloud service qualifies, companies must fully understand the criteria in Article 2(8), which are cumulative and complex. However, the Act lacks sufficient guidance and clarity on many elements of this definition. Notably, the criteria seem better suited to Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS) than Software as a Service (SaaS) offerings, adding to the uncertainty.  • The Commission’s interpretation to apply Chapter VI of the Data Act retroactively to existing contracts is detrimental to EU companies with a large customer base in Europe, as the law does not explicitly allow it. Applying new obligations to existing contracts risks violating the EU’s fundamental right to conduct business freely. This retroactivity poses significant commercial risks, exemplified by SAP’s need to renegotiate over 50,000 contracts quickly, disrupting operations and financial stability. • B2B cloud contracts, and in particular SaaS contracts, are negotiated on equal footing by sophisticated parties. Parties should be free to agree and clearly define in contracts how such rights and obligations apply in their business relationship. • Also, Data Act’s Chapter VIII push for compulsory technical specifications is particularly problematic. Unlike the European Standardization System’s process, the Data Act mandates compulsory technical specifications which, once adopted, require providers to ensure all their solutions comply within 12 months. It is not guaranteed that these requirements are properly elaborated harmonized standards, and could be coming from specific actors, not reflecting the current state of the art. This approach will hinder innovation and necessitate costly redevelopment, potentially leading to market distortions if privately developed open interoperability specifications from one company are imposed on its competitors.

Recommendations: • Reopen and deeply modify the Data Act cloud switching provisions under the Digital Omnibus. If a modification of the scope to exclude SaaS could not be considered, the way the Data Act applies should be modified. It must be made clear that the cloud switching rights cannot be invoked without contractual language (no direct or automatic rights). Direct rights are typically created where there is a clear imbalance of power, as in data protection or consumer law. In the Data Act, parties are sophisticated and negotiate on equal footing – they should be free to agree and clearly define in contracts how such rights and obligations apply in their business relationship. • Clarification is necessary with regards to how the term “computing resources” should be understood for SaaS, what is “rapid provisioning and release,” what constitutes “minimal management effort” and “service provider interaction”, and how to interpret the term “ubiquitous.”  (2) Challenge on the ePrivacy Directive: The current framework has become increasingly outdated and burdensome, particularly due to rigid cookie consent rules that contribute to widespread banner fatigue among users. Its consent-based regime remains incoherent with the GDPR and newer regulatory instruments such as the Data Act, creating legal uncertainty for businesses and citizens alike. Enforcement is further fragmented across multiple authorities, including data protection and telecommunications regulators, undermining consistency and effectiveness. Moreover, overlapping and at times conflicting provisions with the GDPR and other EU digital laws generate unnecessary compliance complexity. Finally, overly rigid or absolutist interpretations of certain provisions have constrained technological innovation and hindered the development of the Internet of Things (IoT) ecosystem. Recommendations: • Modernize cookie rules by incorporating GDPR’s full legal bases or disapplying ePrivacy Directive 5(3) where GDPR applies. More concretely, insert ePrivacy Directive’s cookie provisions into GDPR to centralize regulation under Data Protection Authorities. • Create consent exemptions for low-risk use cases, privacy-enhancing technologies, and data uses permitted under other laws. For instance, exempt analytics, fraud prevention, and employment-related data from consent. • Align traffic data provisions with GDPR to allow broader legal bases including legitimate interest. • Empower the European Commission to adopt delegated acts for future-proofing. • Harmonize enforcement with centralized interpretation and unified breach reporting.  (3) Position on Data Access: SAP manages security risks through robust technical and organizational measures, including encryption and strict access controls. The risks often cited in policy debates (e.g. reputational risks, or threats to EU Member States national security, data misuse by third country governments or unauthorized entities) is very low and effectively mitigated by these measures. Additional requirements against unauthorized access by countries, and stricter conditions weighing on cross border transfers of sensitive data would not be needed: The Data Act's Chapter VII already provides sufficient guardrails against unauthorized access; new provisions would create marginal benefits at a high cost.  (4) Challenge on the free flow of non-personal data: As a global leader serving a global customer base, international data transfers are essential for SAP’s operations. Strict data localisation

Recommendation: Easing international data flows is undoubtedly beneficial. (5) Position on Data Sharing: Regarding data sharing, SAP is an active participant in European data spaces and sees immense value in sharing, especially for AI development. Our experience confirms that a voluntary, contract-driven approach is the most effective way to manage data ownership, security and quality and to ensure interoperability while respecting competition, copyright, trade secret and data protection. While we welcome standardization efforts for data models and protocols, they must be developed through recognized international or European standard bodies to ensure they are technologically neutral and globally relevant. Their recognized and well-established consultation and elaboration procedures ensure that any candidate standard would be relevant and could apply agnostically to a wide array of technological options. We would welcome standards on identification of legal entities, data models for specific domains, data sharing agreements and data access protocols. For these reasons – among others, as we explained above – the Data Act should undergo a fundamentally revision. 
EU Artificial Intelligence Framework  (1) Challenge on the implementation of the AI Act: A pro-innovation implementation of the AI Act is crucial to fostering an ecosystem of AI excellence and trust in Europe. However, current uncertainties due to lack of guidance, delays in standardization, delays in national governance setting, and regulatory overlaps create significant challenges for AI innovators, especially ahead of the Act’s full application on 2 August 2026. To prevent market fragmentation and enhance legal certainty, stronger efforts are needed to ensure consistent application of AI rules across Member States. Competent authorities must be equipped to balance fundamental rights, competition, innovation, privacy, and security effectively. Recommendations: • Longer transition timelines are needed for AI Act requirements dependent on European harmonized standards and legislation not yet available or delayed. Specifically, more time is essential to comply with high-risk AI obligations and the transparency requirements of Article 50. • Ensure secondary legislation, guidance, and compliance tools like Codes of Practice are targeted, proportionate, practical, timely, and aligned with EU simplification goals, with robust industry consultation to reflect technical realities. Additional time can help especially in this goal. • Strengthen regulators’ expertise by investing in ongoing training and fostering close engagement with industry to effectively oversee AI regulation.

(2) Challenge on enforcement and collaboration with authorities: The AI Act’s enforcement system creates complexity for companies operating across the EU. Unlike GDPR, which allows companies to work primarily with a single national authority, the AI Act requires engagement with multiple national Market Surveillance Authorities for compliance in different Member States and oversight from the European Commission’s AI Office for general-purpose AI. This fragmented approach forces companies to navigate varied regulatory interactions, leading to inconsistent enforcement of similar AI use cases and substantial financial and administrative burdens, with high compliance costs potentially reaching per high-risk AI system. Though coordination mechanisms exist, fragmentation within jurisdictions risks legal uncertainty and hampers smooth market operations. Recommendations:  • Simplify the AI Act enforcement and collaboration with authorities by allowing companies to primarily work with a single national authority, following the country-of-origin principle, to boost efficiency and legal certainty while preventing market fragmentation. • Create a mutual recognition system among national Market Surveillance Authorities to enable seamless EU-wide operations without country-specific product changes. • Empower the AI Board to ensure consistent, coordinated, and innovation-friendly AI Act application across Member States, with transparent and open procedures. • Enhance coordination among authorities responsible for product-related EU tech rules (especially AI Act, CRA, sectoral legislation) to support companies’ compliance and align enforcement for a cohesive Single Market approach.  (3) Challenge on provisions of transparency (Art. 50 of the AI Act): The AI Act’s Article 50 transparency provisions take effect on 2 August 2026, with key technical details in the Code of Practice due only weeks before. Compliance will require significant technical effort, such as marking generative AI outputs as artificially generated. Companies face a tight timeline to implement these requirements without grandfathering protections, creating challenges in readiness and risk of non-compliance. Recommendation: As recommended in previously, allow for a longer implementing timelines, with a minimum of six months after finalizing the Code of Practice for companies to adapt effectively. (4) Challenge on the AI Act and GDPR overlaps: The GDPR requires detailed accountability like Register of Processing Activities (ROPA) and under specific circumstances Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) for AI systems processing personal data. Similarly, the AI Act mandates technical documentation and Fundamental Rights Impact Assessments (FRIAs). However, due to missing FRIA implementation guidelines, companies are unable to start working on potential synergies with DPIAs.  Recommendation: Commission should work to release implementation guidelines for FRIA as soon as possible allowing companies to not only establish existing synergies with DPIA, but also establish necessary additional controls.  (5) Challenge on data governance: The AI Act requires assessing and mitigating biases in datasets for high-risk AI (Article 10) and addressing systemic risks in General Purpose AI (Article 55), which may involve processing so-called “sensitive data” (Art. 9 GDPR) restricted under the GDPR. GDPR restrictions on sensitive data complicate compliance, as AI needs such data to ensure fairness and accuracy. (6) Challenge on accuracy and representativeness vs data minimization: The AI Act requires ensuring that training, validation, and testing datasets are representative, accurate, and robust, necessitating access to large datasets, including personal data. However, the GDPR’s data minimization principle and purpose limitation principle limits the use of personal data, creating a conflict. This puts developers in a difficult position amid differing interpretations by Data Protection and AI authorities, leading to uncertainty. While data minimization safeguards like responsible collection and synthetic data can help, proportionality is essential, as AI systems often need large, diverse data to train effectively and mitigate bias. The same is true for the purpose limitation principle as we need an interpretation that can hold up to the technology developments, Recommendations: • The European Commission should clarify the suitable GDPR legal basis for AI training like the legitimate interest and the required guardrails – but with a practical feasible approach. The privilege for academic research under Art. 89 GDPR could be extended to industry research including training AI models. • The European Commission should initiate a broader political debate on AI and GDPR including engagement with the EDPB to ensure a unified and modern approach and interpretation of the GDPR. Notably, the European Commission together with the EDPB should establish best practice guidelines (that honour the technological developments and a unified risk-based approach) e.g.  to define when personal data can be considered anonymized (example: synthetic data for AI). 
EU Cybersecurity Framework  (1) Challenge on the Cyber Resilience Act’s (CRA) deadlines: • Lack of robust harmonized standards for presumption of conformity: Imposing ambitious deadlines for the development of numerous standards for various categories risks compromising their quality. Consensus-building must take precedence over speed to ensure that resulting standards reflect best practices. As an interim measure, self-assessment with widely recognized

international standards should be permitted across all product categories until comprehensive harmonized standards are finalized. • Lack of sensible guidance for scope definition: The lack of clear guidance on scope definition leaves cloud service providers without legal certainty regarding the extent to which SaaS offerings need to comply with CRA provisions. Without this clarification – often followed by lengthy internal assessments of the portfolio – companies are unable to confidently invest in compliance preparations or allocate resources effectively. Clear and timely guidance is essential to enable informed planning and support early compliance readiness. Recommendation: Delay in CRA’s application – for at least 2 years after the guidance is available. The additional months provided should be dedicated to reaching agreement on the most urgent harmonized standards and developing clear, practical interpretations of the CRA’s requirements. This will ensure the rules are workable for companies and allow sufficient time for proper implementation. It is also essential to use this period to deliver on commitments to the promised simplification package. (2) Challenge regarding the CRA: The CRA’s reference to cloud services in the context of remote data processing solutions (RDPS) for products with digital elements (PDEs) has created significant confusion for manufacturers-cloud service providers, both legally and technically. While standalone cloud services are generally not considered products, ambiguity remains regarding SaaS offerings and their potential inclusion under CRA provisions, complicating both legal interpretations and DevOps processes. Efforts to refine the scope of remote data processing are critical to clarify the CRA’s scope of application. Recommendation: Include in the CRA scope only those cloud-based RDPS that are specifically designed and developed for the PDEs, and exclude general-purpose cloud services – also SaaS services –, as they are covered by fitting cybersecurity measures mandated by NIS2. Alternatively, providers of cloud solutions that are in scope of NIS2 should be explicitly and fully excluded from the CRA, to avoid duplicative requirements.  (3) Challenge on the CRA’s scope: Key aspects like scope and product definitions under the CRA remain unclear. Complying will be resource-intensive for digital providers, requiring in-depth understanding of requirements, supply chain review, operational adjustments, and testing of compliance strategies. Adequate preparation time and clear, actionable guidance are essential for effective compliance. Recommendation: Clarifying obligations for self-managed software with optional remote features is essential. The CRA defines “remote data processing” as processing “the absence of which would prevent the product with digital elements from performing one of its functions,” but does not address self-managed or on-premise software that functions independently but offers optional cloud-based features like license checks, telemetry, or updates. Clear guidance is needed to distinguish software requiring remote processing for core functions from software that operates independently with optional connectivity. Without this clarity, vendors cannot determine applicable compliance obligations, complicating product design and update delivery, since update servers may be classified as remote data processing solutions requiring separate conformity assessments.

 (4) Challenge on overlaps between the CRA, NIS2 Directive and Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) for Business-to-Business ICT service providers: IT solutions for different use cases or contexts often rely on shared technologies such as infrastructure, databases, AI models, communication tools, and user interfaces. Implementing separate requirements and processes for each context or sector increases redundancy, multiply workload, and reduce competitiveness. However, multiple EU regulations set distinct security requirements and compliance processes for organizations and products, often overlapping yet differing in scope. The lack of cross-referencing, mutual recognition, and alignment between the operational requirements for cloud operations of NIS2 and DORA (and between the two) and the product-level obligations of the CRA can lead to overlapping demands in risk management, audits, incident reporting, and technical documentation. Disparate definitions of service and product (topped with different kinds of products), combined with divergent regulatory approaches, increase complexity, administrative burden, and compliance costs, ultimately hampering innovation. This creates complexity in tracking obligations, integrating requirements into a unified internal security program, and managing audits and documentation with customers and regulators. Even companies following the strictest rule face redundant audits and processes for each regulation, diverting resources from effective security and innovation to inefficient compliance activities. Recommendations: • A coherent, layered cybersecurity framework across EU cybersecurity laws should define baseline and tiered requirements (e.g., basic, medium, high) based on sensitivity and sector (critical, non-critical), with standardized core definitions to ensure consistent terminology across regulations, such as incident severity definitions (“severe”, “important”, “significant”, “becoming aware”).  • Allow critical ICT third-party service providers under DORA to streamline compliance by minimizing repetitive demonstrations of the same controls in multiple audits. Establishing a mutual recognition framework for certifications and audit results across supervisory authorities and customers would simplify oversight, eliminate duplication, and improve regulatory efficiency without compromising oversight quality. Recognition of internationally recognized industry standards will help achieve these outcomes. To this end, the EU should: o Recognize audits performed under one regulation for others, including certifications like ISO 27001 and SOC 2. o Align security control requirements to avoid overlaps in EU security legislation, enabling compliance reciprocity. o Accept security testing (e.g., vulnerability scans, penetration tests) across regulations. o Create a single, streamlined process for compliance documentation and reporting that could be automated. o Harmonize subcontractor risk management with mutual recognition of compliance. o Avoid duplicative product certification under CRA and NIS2 (Art. 24(2)) by accepting CRA CE marking as sufficient for NIS2 delegated acts on ICT product certification.  (5) Challenge on incident reporting: NIS2, DORA, and CRA each mandate incident reporting but differ in criteria and scope, causing duplicated reporting and increased operational complexity. Concurrently, reportable incidents under these frameworks may also be reportable under the GDPR

or the AI Act. Overlapping requirements lead to heavy administrative burdens, especially given tight reporting deadlines (sometimes as short as 24 hours) and dispersed authorities involved. Varied reporting formats and jurisdictional rules further complicate reporting, sometimes requiring notification of the same incident to dozens of bodies across multiple member states. This fragmentation raises costs without improving cybersecurity outcomes. Recommendations:  • Establish national single reporting entry points covering NIS2, CRA, DORA, AI Act and GDPR to reduce fragmentation. These entry points should forward reports to relevant authorities and allow single reporting in one language within statutory deadlines. ENISA’s single reporting platform could support this system, while preserving national access while avoiding single points of failure. • Clarify inter-framework exemptions, allowing reports under one framework (e.g., NIS2) to satisfy obligations under others (e.g., CRA) when incident thresholds overlap. • Enhance coordination by developing common incident taxonomy, standardized reporting templates, and secure centralized communication between ENISA, CSIRTs, and authorities to avoid duplicated follow-ups and inconsistencies. • Leverage digital tools and AI to automate reporting processes; enable machine-readable formats, standardized data, to reduce administrative burdens. • Include liability protections in the reporting framework to encourage timely and complete incident disclosures with legal clarity, especially for actively exploited vulnerability reporting. • Harmonize establishment rules and extend the country-of-origin principle to simplify oversight, reducing legal and operational complexity. • Target reporting thresholds to focus on relevant incidents with clear operational impact to avoid overreporting and enhance the quality of cybersecurity responses.

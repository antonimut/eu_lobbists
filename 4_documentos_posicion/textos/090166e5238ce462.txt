Executive Summary  
CEN and CENELEC recommends that the EU use existing standards to ensure harmonisation and 
reduce burdens on businesses. Standards can provide a common language and uniform processes 
that strengthen compliance and reduce fragmentation. In the area of cybersecurity, CEN and 
CENELEC recommends that reporting requirements be based on existing standards for incident 
management and information security. Regarding the AI (Artificial Intelligence) Act, CEN and 
CENELEC emphasises that standardization is a key tool for implementation and should be carried 
out in broad cooperation between businesses, authorities, SMEs and civil society. CEN and 
CENELEC and its national members support the development of guidelines and resources for 
stakeholders to prepare for the coming AI standards. To alleviate burdens concerning electronic 
identity and trust services, the EC should seek to ensure synergy with the standards supporting the 
eIDAS Regulation.  
 
CEN and CENELEC wishes to address the following three challenges listed in the Commission's call 
for evidence: 
1. Cybersecurity-related incident reporting obligations 
2. Application of the rules of the AI Regulation 
3. Electronic identity and trust services, including the EU Business Wallet 
 
 
Avoiding Fragmentation in the EU Digital Rulebook: The Case for European 
Standards: 
Standards are indispensable for translating legal obligations into practical and verifiable 
solutions. Harmonized European and international standards ensure clarity, 
interoperability, and global recognition. The Omnibus should reaffirm the central role of 
voluntary, consensus-based standards developed by CEN, CENELEC, ETSI, ISO, and IEC 
as the backbone of a coherent and flexible compliance framework.

Commission to adopt common specifications through implementing acts if standards are 
not finalised or deemed adequate— raises important considerations. these specifications, 
developed without the involvement of the European Standardization Organisations (CEN, 
CENELEC, ETSI), would operate outside the consensus-based process established under

Regulation (EU) No 1025/2012 on European standardization. 
 
Under this regulation only Harmonized standards, once cited in the Official Journal of the 
European Union (OJEU), confer a presumption of conformity with essential legal 
requirements. This system, rooted in New Legislative Framework, guarantees 
transparency, inclusiveness, and international alignment. Introducing common 
specifications could alter  this architecture by creating parallel, prescriptive rules outside 
the standardization framework, which may result in duplication, fragmentation, and 
greater complexity for businesses. 
 
Cybersecurity-related incident reporting obligations: 
Standards play a central role in how companies handle cybersecurity incidents and 
reporting obligations. For example, the ISO/IEC 27035 series (Information security 
incident management) is used as a reference for incident management. More widely used 
is ISO/IEC 27001, which supports the establishment of information security management 
systems that include reporting processes. 
 
By building on existing standards, the EU can ensure harmonisation and reduce the 
burden on businesses. The standards provide a common language and processes that both 
national authorities and businesses can use. This will reduce fragmentation and contribute 
to more uniform compliance across Member States. 
 
Application of the rules of the AI Regulation: 
 
Standardization is recognised in the AI Act as a key tool for ensuring its effective 
implementation. Within CEN and CENELEC, the joint technical committee JTC 21 is 
working intensively to develop AI-related standards based on consensus among 
companies, public authorities, consumer organisations, and NGOs. Considerable efforts 
have been made to involve SMEs, academia, and civil society, ensuring broad 
representation, legitimacy, and the practical usability of the resulting standards. The time 
and effort invested by JTC 21 to achieve this inclusiveness reflect its commitment to 
developing safe, trustworthy, and widely accepted standards. 
 
Many stakeholders are already preparing for the forthcoming AI standards. By joining their 
national standardization committee, stakeholders can follow and contribute to the work as 
it evolves. In addition, public consultations provide an open opportunity to review draft 
standards and submit comments. Several European and international standards are 
already available for reference, and national organisations have published supporting 
guidelines to help stakeholders prepare for implementation. For example, the standard 
ISO/IEC 42001 establishes requirements for AI management systems and can serve as a 
preparatory tool for organisations to comply with the AI Act. It is however not able to give 
presumption of conformity for compliance with the AI Act requirements. 
 
A specific area where greater clarity would be beneficial concerns general-purpose AI 
(GPAI) and the European Commission’s initiative on Codes of Practice. While these codes 
could offer providers a pathway to demonstrate compliance under the AI Act, they raise 
important questions. By allowing the Commission and the AI Office to both draft and 
validate these codes, the approach risks conflating distinct roles—regulator, specifier, and 
 
assessor. This concentration of responsibilities could create uncertainty and reduce trust in 
the process. 
 
 
Electronic identity and trust services, including the EU Business Wallet 
 
Several international standards already exist in the field of electronic identity and trust 
services. The ISO/IEC JTC 1 SC 17 (Cards and security devices for personal identification) 
and ISO/IEC JTC 1 SC 27 (Information security, cybersecurity and privacy protection) 
groups have developed standards covering digital identity, authentication and security 
aspects (e.g. the ISO/IEC 24760 series on identity management). 
 
At European level, CEN and CENELEC contributes to the standardization of interoperability 
and user-friendliness in connection with electronic identity systems. There are many 
groups within CEN and CENELEC working on EU Digital Identity (EUDI) wallet and Trusted 
Services. These committees include CEN/TC 224 ‘Personal identification and related 
personal devices with secure elements, systems, operations and privacy in a multi-
sectorial environment’, CEN-CLC/JTC 19  ‘Blockchain and Distributed Ledger Technologies’, 
and ‘CEN/TC 468 ‘Preservation of digital information’.  
.  
This work will be relevant for the upcoming EU Business Wallet. The European Commission 
(EC) should aim to ensure synergy between the work already carried out and utilise 
established standards where possible. In this way, standards can promote interoperability, 
security and innovation, while reducing implementation costs in line with the ‘one in, one 
out’ principle of the EC. 
 
CEN and CENELEC closely follows the above three topics and the connected 
standardization work. We are available for dialogue on how international and European 
standards can best support political ambitions and the implementation of adopted 
legislation.

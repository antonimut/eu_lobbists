In the light of the upcoming simplification omnibus of the digital package, ESBG would 
like to highlight the following recommendations and areas of uncertainty. 
 
Data legislation 
 
We welcomed the 2020 European strategy for data aimed at creating a single data 
market. The establishment of a common framework prevents fragmentation and enables 
companies to leverage economies of scale, thereby strengthening Europe's 
competitiveness in k ey areas such as artificial intelligence . In addition, in the context of 
the upcoming FiDA framework, we would like to stress that any potential standards 
developed by the European Standardisation Organisations (ESOs), as proposed by the 
Commission, should remain voluntary. This would allow industry participants to continue 
developing solutions that effectively address key challenges such as privacy, security, 
and fraud, while ensuring sufficient flexibility for innovation. 
 
Since then, the Commission has gradually built a comprehensive horizontal framework 
with laws such as the Data Governance Act (DGA), the Open Data Directive, and the Data 
Act, as well as promoting the emergence of common European data spaces in strategic 
economic sectors. The DGA, with its ambition to establish a European model for data 
sharing through trusted neutral intermediaries for B2B data sharing, was particularly 
promising as it aimed to facilitate interoperability within as well as between sectors.  This 
latter point is crucial as the maximum value is created by cross -referencing data from 
multiple sectors. As Mario Draghi highlighted in his report, the acceleration of AI 
integration, especially generative AI, in European industry requires coordination and data 
sharing across sectors. The European Data Innovation Board (EDIB) was created to assist 
the Commission in supporting this data sharing across sectors by defining common 
standards. 
 
However, in addition to this horizontal framework, it is envisaged to add specific sectoral 
rules for certain sectors, such as the financial sector. This leads, as emphasised in the note 
accompanying the call for evidence on the European Data Union Strategy, to a regulatory 
landscape marked by a complex mix of general and sector -specific laws. Companies 
struggle to understand the interaction between these various rules, as s ome appear to 
lack coherence with the overall framework.  
 
For instance, the proposed FIDA regulation, which will establish the operational 
framework for the European financial data space, risks isolating it from other sectors, 
thereby hindering the reali sation of a single market, which is contingent upon the 
effective interconnection of sectoral spaces. While FIDA has the potential to foster 
standards within the financial sector, it does not address the cross -sector dialogue that 
EDIB is tasked with facilitating. 
 
Furthermore, one might question whether the data-sharing schemes envisioned by FIDA 
do not duplicate the neutral data -sharing intermediaries established by the DGA, which 
already aim to bridge data holders and users, ensure interoperability within and between 
different sector and define sharing conditions. This duplication will generate  increased 
costs for the financial sector, which, moreover, will invest while isolating itself from other 
sectors. 
 
If Europe wishes to achieve its ambitions for a single data space and global leadership in 
AI, it must pay the utmost attention to two major objectives of its strategy.  
 
First, streamlining existing rules must be an absolute priority, conducted with 
determination both horizontally and within individual sectors  with an awareness of the 
       
 
 
 
 
 
 
3 
economic stakes. The competitiveness of European companies depends on the 
operability and coherence of the legal framework.  
 
Secondly, strengthening the European data ecosystem cannot be envisioned through the 
chaotic multiplication of sharing ecosystems. It is important to assess the risk of 
imbalance that the imposition of autonomous sectoral regulatory models, such as the 
FIDA schemes, represents . Especially when the market has already developed its own 
models (BaaP, BaaS), complemented by a voluntary intermediation model for all sectors 
established by the DGA.  
 
Finally, FIDA must not become an example of what to avoid in promoting EU 
competitiveness. We therefore believe that it displays an ambition that is out of 
proportion, given the weaknesses of the impact assessment and the absence of 
demonstrated market demand. Indeed, the  lack of  its schemes and permission 
dashboard’s operational feasibility, the uncertainty in terms of demand, and the risk of 
favouring non-European players should be carefully discussed and adjusted before final 
adoption. In that way our key recommendations are the following: 
 
- Narrow down the scope of FiDA, both as to the type of customers (focus only on 
retail natural persons), and data categories (choose a very limited number of data 
categories  based on evidenced needs of the market) 
- Ensure that the development of schemes is  driven by genuine market demand, 
while also rolled-out with a gradual approach and realistic timelines , especially 
given the lack of evidence supporting widespread interest in financial data sharing. 
- Exclude gatekeepers and require third-country FISPs to have an establishment in 
the EU  to avoid the exploitation of sensitive data held by Europe’s financial 
institutions and to strengthen any dominant position and customer lock -in, 
contradicting the Commission’s focus on competitiveness and sovereignty. 
- Properly design the permission dashboard , the first tangible test of customer 
trust. While a welcome tool, its implementation as currently envisaged presents 
significant operational, legal, and technical challenges. 
Avoid excessive reliance on new Regulatory Technical  Standards or Guidelines which 
could complicate compliance 
 
 
ePrivacy Directive 
A reassessment of the rules governing cookies and similar tracking tools under the 
ePrivacy Directive is necessary, particularly in light of  growing consent fatigue among 
users. 
Greater alignment with the GDPR is essential. The Directive’s exclusive reliance on 
consent contrasts with the GDPR’s broader legal bases and creates legal uncertainty and 
operational inefficiencies. 
To modernize the framework and improve user experience, it is recommended to simplify 
consent mechanisms and allow alternative legal grounds for processing, as permitted  
under the GDPR. This would enhance legal clarity and reduce compliance burdens.  
 
Cyber Resilience Act

A clarification regarding the (non-)applicability of the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) to Fis 
would be highly appreciated. We urge the EC to scope FS industry out of the CRA due 
to overlapping and more stringent requirements already set under the DORA to avoid 
unnecessary double implementation efforts and reporting. DORA already cover s the 
entire lifecycle, from development to decommissioning, and includes risk -based 
management, incident handling, vulnerability management, and customer 
communication strategies. 
 
Major incident notification process: 
 
Financial entities are required to report major incidents in accordance with: (i) the DORA 
Regulation, (ii) the Cyber Resilience Regulation, and (iii) the NIS2 Directive. Although the 
reporting deadlines and the number of required reports are consistent ac ross the 
applicable regulations, the coexistence of multiple regulatory frameworks leads to 
overlaps in reporting obligations and discrepancies in the definition of what constitutes a 
major incident. 
 
As a result of these regulations, financial entities are subject to a wide range of reporting 
obligations to various competent authorities, including the Computer Security Incident 
Response Team (CSIRT), the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA),  the 
CNMV (Spanish Securities Market Commission), the Bank of Spain (BdE), and the 
Directorate-General for Insurance and Pension Funds (DGSFP), among others. 
Additionally, until recently, certain entities were also required to report major incidents 
to the European Central Bank (ECB), a requirement currently under review due to the 
direct application of the DORA Regulation.  
 
As a result, in the event of a major incident (especially one affecting personal data) 
entities are forced to make parallel notifications to different authorities, depending on the 
nature of the incident and the relevant sectoral authority. For example, under regulations 
related to cyber resilience and data protection, an incident affecting a financial entity’s 
mobile application might need to be reported simultaneously to the AEPD (Spanish Data 
Protection Agency), due to the impact on personal data, the CSIRT, if mobile applications 
or remotely connected devices are affected, the CNMV or the BdE, depending on the 
type of financial entity involved.  This scenario creates significant regulatory overlap , 
resulting in duplicated administrative bur dens, operational difficulties, and potential 
inefficiencies in incident management. We therefore suggest to: 
 
- Expand the exception in the Cyber Resilience Regulation regarding the obligation 
to report major ICT -related incidents, given that the DORA Regulation serves as 
a lex specialis  for the financial sector and should therefore prevail as the sector -
specific regulation. 
- Unify the concept of “major incident”  across the DORA Regulation, the Cyber 
Resilience Regulation, and the NIS2 Directive to avoid regulatory overlaps and 
bring coherence to the regulatory framework.  
- Establish a single -window mechanism or mutual recognition system for 
notifications, serving as a centralized point for receiving incident reports in the 
financial sector. This system should be managed by a single competent authority 
specifically designated for this purpose, which would coordinate directly and 
automatically with the other relevant bodies (CNMV, BdE, CSIRT, ENISA, etc.).

The goal is to ensure that a single notification made by a financial entity is automatically 
distributed to all relevant authorities based on the nature of the incident and the 
competencies of each body. This would reduce administrative burden, eliminate 
duplication, and improve coherence in incident management and oversight. 
 
Setting up a single portal for reporting should go hand in hand with harmonising legal 
framework on incident reporting covering aspects like harmonised templates, thresholds, 
deadlines, etc. Therefore, considering the complexity of setting up such an EU le vel 
system, an in -depth analysis and discussion, based on impact assessment, addressing 
aspects like costs, liability, best technical solution, etc. must be held first.  
 
 
 
AI ACT 
 
As the digital transformation drives EU growth and productivity, we welcome the 
European Commission’s commitment to accelerate AI adoption and drive innovation with 
the ultimate goal to make Europe a global leader in Artificial Intelligence.  
 
In this global race for leadership in AI, Europe wants to defend its own distinctive 
approach to AI by  fostering a responsible artificial intelligence development and 
deployment in the EU. To achieve this choice, Europe has adopted an ambitious 
regulation, the AI Act, establishing the first comprehensive horizontal legal framework for 
the regulation of AI systems across the EU. 
 
Now, the EU must ensure that its regulatory framework enables companies to compete 
effectively and contribute to European growth and innovation. As acknowledged in the 
AI Continent Action Plan, the AI Act’s success will primarily depend on how workable its 
rules are in practice and on its successful implementation. Ensuring that horizontal 
workstreams arising from the AI Act are functional for the banking sector is  therefore 
vital. 
 
Banks are actively preparing for implementation, mobili sing significant resources across 
multiple business lines. However, we face uncertainties surrounding the interpretation of 
the AI Act , as the guidelines produced on its practical implementation do not take into 
account all the sectoral specificities. This uncertainty inevitably hinders our a bility to 
innovate. 
 
Furthermore, the potential delivery delays by the CEN and CENELEC, tasked to develop 
European standards including standards on risk management systems for AI systems, 
could also complicate banks' compliance efforts which will  have to complement and 
adjust their current procedures and practices on a very short timeline. 
 
A dedicated task force involving several banking sector supervisors, including the EBA, 
ESMA, the SSM, and national banking supervisors is currently conducting a gap analysis 
between the existing banking regulatory framework (encompassing MCD, CCD, CRR, 
CRD, relevant EBA guidelines, DORA) and the AI Act requirements. It is important that 
the results of this work, expected by the end of 2025, are made available to the financial 
sector as early as possible as a timely publication would contribute to a smoothe r and 
more efficient compliance process.

Furthermore, clarifying the interplay between the AI Act and the GDPR is of utmost 
importance to support consistent and harmoni sed implementation. We welcome the 
statement of the EDPB underlining the need to clarify the substantive and regulatory 
enforcement interplay between the application of the GDPR and other EU legislation as 
we believe it would be highly valuable to provide further guidance or clarification on this 
matter. Clearer delineation between the two frameworks would enhance legal certainty 
for all stakeholders involved. A lot of unnecessary efforts could be avoided and invested 
in competitiveness boosting actions if guidance regarding important new legal provisions 
was provided by the legislator at an early implementation stage. 
 
Concerning the AI system definition under the AI Act - we call on the EC to not scope in 
logical regression, especially when employed on a stand -alone basis, considering that it 
does not transcend “basis data processing” and, therefore, cannot be considered an AI 
system in the meaning of the AI Act. We urge the EC to provide an expli cit statement 
covering this, in addition to the recent guideline on AI system definition.  
 
Moreover, g iven the comprehensive incident reporting framework already in place for 
financial entities under the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) and its 
implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/302, which ensures structured, timely, and 
harmonized communication of  ICT-related incidents and cyber threats, it is both 
reasonable and necessary to limit any additional obligations under the AI Act. When AI 
systems used by financial institutions fall within the scope of Annex III, points 5(b) and 
(c), the only justified e xtension of reporting duties should concern breaches of 
fundamental rights. This approach avoids regulatory duplication, respects the sector -
specific safeguards already in force, and maintains legal clarity for institutions operating 
in a highly regulated environment. 
 
Last but not least, once the AI Act is implemented, the complexity of supervision by 
multiple national and EU-level supervisors may present additional challenges. The risk of 
multiple, potentially contradictory injunctions could stifle innovation. Therefor e, we 
appreciate the Commission's encouragement for Member States and itself, including its 
AI Office, to facilitate a smooth and predictable application of the AI Act. The anticipated 
launch of the AI Act Service Desk is welcomed, and we hope that it will provide accessible, 
tailored responses to sector-specific inquiries. 
 
As outlined in the Commission's work program for 2025, all institutions should embrace a 
robust implementation and simplification agenda, aiming to address gold plating, simplify 
rules, and implement policies more effectively. We rely on the commitment of each EU 
and national institution to support a seamless implementation of the AI Act, fostering an 
environment where innovation thrives alongside regulatory compliance. 
 
 
European Digital Identity Framework 
 
The implementation of the EUDIW presents numerous challenges to be addressed in a 
very short timeframe due to areas of uncertainty: 
 
- Legally, the scope of acceptance of the EUDIW depends on the interpretation of 
the interaction between eIDAS2 and other regulations such as PSR and AMLR. To 
overcome legal uncertainty regarding European Digital Identity Wallet and the 
payments use case – we recommend that the European Commission provide s a 
clarification concerning the legislator’s intent in this regard 
- SCA and SUA are not corresponding concepts, which creates uncertainty 
regarding the responsibilities of Payment Service Providers (PSPs) and digital 
       
 
 
 
 
 
 
7 
wallet providers . This lack of alignment raises concerns about compliance 
obligations and the interoperability of authentication frameworks across regulatory 
domains. 
- The IT developments for the acceptance of the EUDIW involve technical choices 
that are not yet stabilised (implementing acts, ARF, and ongoing Large scale 
pilots) and require close coordination with the  Member States, whose  digital 
identity wallet developments are ongoing. 
Furthermore, we are also concerned about the unclear relation between the PSR and the 
European Digital Identity Wallet (EUDIW) implemented under Regulation (EU) No 
910/2014. This jeopardises the acceptance of the EUDIW and its potential for European 
digital sovereignty. 
 
In general, the payment service provider must decide which payment instruments and 
products they will integrate into the EUDIW (no mandatory usage). 
 
Where payment service providers accept the EUDIW for the purpose of payments, the 
relation to the relevant PSR provisions has not yet been adequately defined. The 
acceptance of EUDIW in accordance with Art. 5f paragraph 2 of Regulation (EU) No 
910/2014 pos es an incalculable risk for payment service providers in cases of 
unauthorised transactions or malfunctions within the sphere of the EUDIW provider. The 
following key aspects must be regulated by the PSR: 
- Clarification that no contractual arrangements or outsourcing agreement towards 
the EUDI wallet operator are needed. 
- Clear definition and distinction between obligations for EUDIW providers and 
payment service providers, e.g. in conjunction with Articles 51, 55 and 56 (as 
regards payment instruments and strong customer authentication), and Article 83 
(transaction monitoring). 
- In particular, the authorization of a payment transaction shall be rebuttably 
presumed, if the payer has used the EUDIW for the purpose of authentication. 
- Furthermore, regulatory provisions need to be clear on how banks can reject a 
particular EUDIW if security vulnerabilities are known. This requires, among other 
things, that the technical parameters and the provider of the EUDIW used by the 
payment services user can be identified by the bank at all times. 
- Clear allocation of liability for EUDIW providers (in cases of unauthorised payments 
or where legitimate payments cannot be executed due to malfunctioning of the 
EUDIW). 
- Access to relevant evidence that PSPs may require in civil liability proceedings.

Subject: Urgent need to address FiDA’s incompatibility with EU goals 
 
Dear Ambassador, 
 
On behalf of the European financial services sector , we are writing to reiterate our 
deep concern regarding the proposed Financial Data Access (FiDA) Regulation and its 
current negotiations. Despite recent suggestions for simplification, the proposal remains 
flawed in its design and incompatible with broader EU strategic objectives. Against the 
backdrop of a changing and unstable geopolitical environment , and in the context of  EU 
competitiveness, we are also concerned that FiDA will lead to undue bureaucratic burdens 
and jeopardize the level playing field for European companies. 
 
It has been suggested that FiDA is important for the Savings and Investments Union (SIU). 
While we fully support the goal of mobilising private savings for investment and 
empowering retail investors,  we do not see  FiDA as the appropriate instrument  to 
achieve the SIU. In the context of the many challenges to be resolved first to create a 
real SIU, it is noted that access to financial data does not automatically translate into the 
necessary higher levels of financial literacy, investment or better outcomes for consumers. 
On the contrary, the risks created by FiDA including data privacy erosion, fraud and cyber-
attacks may in fact undermine consumer trust and limit rather than expand financial 
participation. 
 
We are also sceptical about the ability of FiDA to assist the establishment of a Data Union, 
which above all requires sharing of large, pooled data sets and cross -industry sharing of 
data. FiDA does not fit with these objectives. It also seems premature to establish FiDA, 
while the overarching European Strategy for Data is under review. 
 
We therefore urge the Council to consider calling for the withdrawal of the FiDA 
proposal, unless the issues outlined below are addressed in a meaningful and substantial 
way. 
 
• Need for a market-driven, customer-demand approach 
 
At the heart of a successful data economy lies real, demonstrable customer demand. 
The FiDA proposal, as it stands,  assumes rather than proves customer appetite for 
widespread financial data sharing. 
 
Experience from international initiatives, such as Australia’s open banking framework, 
clearly shows that massive investment without strong demand leads to low uptake and 
wasted resources. In the Australian case, after more than €1.5 billion in costs, customer  
 
 
 
 
 
usage of open data remained below 0.3%  and more than 50% of data sharing 
arrangements have been discontinued or allowed to lapse 1. 
 
We strongly advocate for a gradual, demand-driven, and market -led rollout of any 
data access scheme  and for an adequate extension of implementation timelines  in 
order to allow data holders to adequately set up and test the technical infrastructure and 
IT solutions (security included) for data sharing. 
 
• Scope too broad: still the main driver of complexity and cost 
 
The scope of data categories envisaged under FiDA remains extremely broad. As proposed, 
FiDA would require the creation of hundreds of APIs and multiple access schemes to 
accommodate the large volume and diversity of financial products included. The 
Commission estimated that implementation of PSD2 open banking obligations alone cost 
above € 2 billion 2. We expect implementation costs for FiDA to be at least three times 
higher3. If left unaddressed, this overreach risks displacing resources from core innovation 
investments required to implement the European roadmap for competitiveness for key 
strategic sectors, technological innovation such as AI, and digital transformation. 
 
We reiterate the need to limit FiDA to  a very small number of truly relevant data 
categories4, based on demonstrable customer demand. 
 
It should also be clarified that only raw data in a digital and structured form are in scope 
of FiDA. In addition, while the introduction of a time limit on historical data is positive, we 
find that even a 7 year-limit and its scope are still disproportionate and requiring further 
discussion. In terms of customers, some adjustments have been suggested in the ongoing 
discussions to exclude large corporates. However, more should be done to make FiDA truly 
mass retail customer-oriented. 
 
• Gatekeepers and third-country FISPs: risks should be addressed 
 
We reiterate our support for the complete exclusion of gatekeepers and their 
subsidiaries from the FiDA framework . The proposed licensing regime is insufficient 
to mitigate the risks of data exploitation and more extensive cornering of the data market 
by dominant global tech-players. 
 
In addition, third-country entities should be required to have an establishment in 
the EU in order to become FISPs , subject to EU jurisdiction , supervision  and 
enforcement mechanisms. Anything less undermines the EU’s ambition for technological 
sovereignty and leaves European consumers and firms exposed.

• Privacy, security and fraud concerns should not be underestimated 
 
Beyond operational burdens, FiDA as proposed multiplies the risk of fraud, privacy 
breaches and cyberattacks by forcing open access to a wide array of sensitive financial 
information. The attack surface expands dramatically under such a framework, and 
consumer authorisation processes will be increasingly complex to verify and protect.  
 
These risks must be central in any evaluation of the proposal’s costs and benefits, together 
with Europe’s security and resilience objectives. We urge the Council to put data security 
and trust at the core of future data -sharing initiatives , not as a secondary 
consideration. 
 
Finally, we would like to underline the importance of considering the broader regulatory 
landscape. The implementation of FiDA would add more complexity and strains to already 
scarce resources needed to implement recent and upcoming EU legislation such as DORA, 
Digital Identity, the AI Act, and PSD3/PSR, rather than contributing to simplification. 
 
The upcoming trilogue negotiations represent the  final stage when the  above-
mentioned concerns can be addressed comprehensively. Withdrawal of the FiDA 
proposal should remain the only realistic path forward, if the issues mentioned 
above are not resolved. 
 
We continue to be  fully committed to working constructively with the institutions to 
develop a future-proof, proportionate and consumer-oriented data-sharing framework that 
can benefit European businesses and citizens alike.

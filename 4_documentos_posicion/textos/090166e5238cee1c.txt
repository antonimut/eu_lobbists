Digital Simplification Package – E.ON Consultation Response 
E.ON appreciates the opportunity to provide  feedback to the European Commission ’s 
consultation process in view of the upcoming Digital Simplification Package. 
In general, E.ON  welcomes the Commission's  ambition for  simplification and has repeatedly 
advocated for the reduction of bureaucracy and the streamlining and simplification of legislation 
at both national and European levels. 
Reducing bureaucracy is a key prerequisite for effectively advancing the energy transition and 
leveraging the great potential of digital isation in the EU. There exist  opportunities for 
simplification, improvements and/or clarification s inter alia within the following legislative 
frameworks, outlined in greater detail below: AI Act, Data Act, GDPR, NIS2 and CER Directive. 
The ambiguity of precedence of the Data Act regarding sectorial law, different definitions used in 
different legal acts, divergent enforcement regimes, overlapping of certain legal acts with others 
all clearly points to an existing need for coherence. 
 
AI Act 
E.ON advocates for a workable legislative framework that keeps a practicable balance between 
protecting the safety and fundamental rights of people and fostering European innovation and 
competitiveness. 
Regulatory sufficiency 
E.ON strongly advises against additional regulation and bureaucratic obligations. E.ON advocates 
for not adding more sector specific regulations to the general (horizontal) law but rather clarifying 
the AI Act and reducing bureaucratic burdens. 
More and more timely clarity from the European Commission is needed related to the 
implementation of the AI Act obligations and the measures to be taken by developers  and 
deployers; such as practical guidance on high -risk AI systems and safety components . While 
keeping the bureaucratic burden on companies at an appropriate and reasonable level, measures 
should be provided in a timewise coordinated fashion to ensure sufficient time is left for 
businesses to implement specific statutory obligations before they enter into effect at reasonable 
cost and use of company resources. 
Safety Components 
AI systems used as safety components in the management and operation of critical infrastructure 
could be classified as high -risk AI. Hence, the definition of safety components must be clear, 
unambiguous, and restrictive. 
• Legally secure clarification of the term ‘safety component’ in accordance with Art. 6 (2) in 
conjunction with Annex III of the AI Act. 
• The AI Regulation itself does not contain any provisions defining the magnitude or the 
conditions under which the materiality threshold (“at large scale”/ “appreciable disruptions”/ 
“significant”) is deemed to be reached or exceeded in the event of a disru ption. However, it 
can be assumed that this means a disruption or system failure that has a significant impact 
on the provision of an essential service by a critical entity within the meaning of the Resilience 
of Critical Entity (CER) Directive, of which the AI system in question is a component.

➔ E.ON proposes to introduce material threshold levels in the application of the AI Act with 
regard to the consequences of a disruption of AI safety components. E.ON therefore 
suggests that the thresholds of the CER Directive, Art. 7, shall also be applied in the area 
of AI to determine the extent of a disruption or failure of critical infrastructure and its 
duration, i.e. a failure should be deemed to lead to an appreciable disruption only if it 
affects a minimum number of x individuals for a minimum duration of x hours. An AI  
system would therefore only qualify as a safety component if its failure or malfunction 
may affect such number of people for such period of time. 
• Clarification is needed: 
- regarding “large scale” and "appreciable disruptions” , which E.ON interprets as a 
“materiality threshold” , which includes the elements of “dimension/ extent” and also a 
“temporal/ duration” aspect, 
- regarding the nature of the (direct) connection between the system failure or malfunction 
and the risk for the physical integrity of critical infrastructure, 
- regarding whether or not it is possible to refer to other risk-mitigating measures (switching 
measures, system redundancy, multiple layers of safety systems etc. in critical 
infrastructure), which may be used in parallel when assessing the “safety componen t” 
characteristic and actual risk or is it always to be looked at in isolation, 
- regarding whether or not AI systems that enable optimization compared to the status quo 
are generally excluded. 
High-risk AI systems 
In conjunction with that, E.ON argues for more timely clarity from the Commission especially 
about the definition and interpretation of high-risk AI, at the same time taking a sensible approach 
on high-risk AI that is mindful of the risk that is being caused. An AI managing a single substation 
poses a very different risk compared to an AI potentially overseeing a large high -voltage grid or 
even a broader power market. 
• A restrictive understanding of the concept of high -risk AI systems in the area of critical 
infrastructure. The rules for high -risk AI systems used in the operation and management of 
critical infrastructure (i.e., the energy grid ) must make the most use of the possible 
exemptions that were formulated in Article 6 (3) and also considers the individual real -life 
circumstances of the AI’s deployment (i.e., if the risk is limited to the functioning of a 
subsystem (e.g., one substation and its feeders)); in such case, harm to the health, safety and 
fundamental rights of persons still must to be avoided, but it is clearly different from a case 
where the AI system malfunctioning or even just its normal functioning would create a more 
systemic or even system-wide risk).  
 
Data Act 
E.ON appreciates further clarifications by the European Commission regarding the Data Act. The 
foremost priority is to ensure legal clarity.  The lack of harmonised definitions creates legal 
uncertainty as does a lack of coherence between various legal acts (see below “terminology 
inconsistencies”).  
With regard to specific provisions of the legislation, E.ON proposes the following: 
Clarification and coherence of sector-specific data access and use rights and their interplay 
with the Data Act 
• Applicability of the Data Act to Smart Meters: 
- Ownership ambiguity: Consumers typically do not own smart meters , nor lease  / rent 
them, depending on Member States‘  approach, complicating the definition of “user” 
under the Data Act. (Art. 2.12). It is thus unlikely that Smart Meters do fall under the Data 
Act, still this is subject to ongoing discussions and unclarity remains at this point. 
- Even if Smart Meters were considered or will be considered a "connected product" under 
the Data Act, there must be limitations to user rights as the usage of metering data that is 
required for various purposes, including, but not limited to, billing, observability, system 
stability, and flexibility cannot be dependent on consent by the user. 
- The question of limitations of user rights under the Data Act is generally an important one. 
The right cannot be unlimited as the rights of the data subjects under GDPR, too, do face 
a number of specific limitations e.g. in social security systems etc. Do flexibility services 
qualify as “related services” under the Data Act, especially for Smart Meters? 
• Divergent Treatment of Smart Meters and Dedicated Measurement Devices (DMD): DMDs are 
more likely to meet Data Act criteria in terms of ownership, contractual use, or integration in 
appliances. This could lead to unequal consumer rights depending on the device used. 
• Terminology inconsistencies: “Eligible parties” (Electricity Directive) vs. “third parties” (Data 
Act) vs. “relevant market participants” (Electricity Regulation).  It seems, “eligible parties” 
implies some sort of limitation regarding to which 3 rd party the data can be made available 
under IR 2023/1162; and the IR takes precedence over the Data Act.  Lack of harmoni sed 
definitions create legal uncertainty as does a lack of coherence between various legal acts. 
• Coherence: It seems that Art. 44 (2) of the Data Act leaves room for its applicability even in 
cases where sector-specific data access and use rights exist. Again, if the Smart Meter was a 
“connected product” in the sense of the Data Act, the user would primarily have the rights 
under IR 2023/1162. Still, outside the scope of IR 2023/1162, the Data Act might be applicable 
to Smart Meter data and it remains unclear what this would entail exactly. 
Targeted recalibration of use rights under the Data Act 
• The ability of the data holder to gain value from data generated by the use of a product and/or 
related services it provides to the user is too limited and entails the risk of lack of incentives 
for the data holder to innovate. 
• Usage of data for defined purposes of the data holder without prior consent by the user shall 
therefore remain possible . A representative example : the usage of  metering and energy 
consumption data e.g. for billing purposes cannot be dependent on consent by the User (see 
above). 
• Regarding trade secrets, the limitation of data holders’ rights is too far-reaching, at minimum 
regarding data holders’ right to define technical means of protection of data holders’ trade 
secrets to be applied by the user/data recipient. Otherwise, this might jeopardize trade 
secrets of the data holder. This would also be more in line with the principles set forth in 
Directive (EU) 2016/943. In the current  legislation, the safeguards for intellectual property 
rights of the data holder, including trade secrets, seem far too limited still. 
• The Data Act contains too limited financial incentives and protection of data holders’ 
investments in data-driven products and services. After all, it remains true that the making of 
such products and related services requires the investment of considerable human, technical 
as well as financial resources. This continues to justify the recognition of certain rights of use 
of the data holder to the data as well as an overall better recognition of the legitimate interests 
of the data holder. 
Simplification potential 
• With regard to personal data in product or related service data and where the user is not the 
data subject, the data holder shall be in a position to rely on authorisation from the data holder 
(who must be responsible for any breach of data protection law), unless the d ata holder is 
itself a controller of such personal data. 
• Statutory permission should be introduced to the Data Act for any transfer of personal data 
from the data holder to the data user and/or any third party. 
 
General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) 
E.ON proposes the following recommendations in order to further improve GDPR.  
• Processing of pseudonymised personal data shall be permitted whenever a strong 
pseudonymisation method is applied, not only for predefined purposes (Art. 6, section 1) but 
also for other purposes such as developing new products. Consequently, binding 
recommendations of strong pseudonymisation concepts shall be provided by competent 
bodies. Alternatively, it would be beneficial to receive binding recommendations when 
personal data is considered anonymised.  
In the energy sector, there is a need to process personal data, which is already available to 
grid operators, to observe and analyse network usage to develop “smart” grid operations. The 
restrictions of Art. 6 place a burden, since developing new products may not be based on one 
of the legal bases. Alternatively, recommendations for anonymisation of personal data are of 
practical relevance, since this would offer the opportunity to process personal data already 
available to grid operators. 
• A more practical approach is needed as to when processing of personal data can be based 
on legitimate interest / statutory permission. 
Hands-on guidance for specific sectors - like the energy sector - could eliminate legal 
uncertainty when legitimate interest for a controller exists. Examples in the energy sector 
include grid operation and congestion management, flexibility, grid expansio n and other 
processes that have demonstrable direct climate benefits. 
• It is needed to extend the time period to notify data breaches up to five working days. 
The existing 72 -hours window is challenging due to complex infrastructures of involved 
applications, providers and entities, since facts of alleged data breaches often take time to 
investigate. 
• Establishing clear rules is needed, whenever the transfer of personal data is based on SCC; 
conducting a TIA should follow a risk-based approach. 
A TIA should be conducted whenever there is a real risk to the rights and freedoms of a natural 
person. There is often the mere possibility of access to a very limited data set from a third 
country for a second -level support. Such possibility of access to a limited data set alone 
stands in no proportion to conduct a burdensome TIA. Processing of personal data with minor 
risks should thus not require a TIA. 
• Ensure a more consistent approach to the application of the GDPR. 
Even in a single Member State there are often conflicting opinions from numerous data 
protection authorities. As a multi -national company, it is challenging and expensive to 
implement standardised processes across the group, when there are differing regulatory 
opinions across the EU. It also creates considerable uncertainties that entail a potentially 
high financial risk. 
• Assess the intricacies between GDPR and Data Act for data generated by connected products 
in energy critical infrastructures. 
The Data Act is "without prejudice" to the GDPR. The  Data Act goes yet beyond some GDPR 
requirements, like for users’ rights to share data directly with third parties comprising also 
non-personal generated data. The possibility and impacts of inextricably linked datasets from 
connected devices in critical infrastructures should also be thoroughly assessed. In the 
interest of legal certainty and, more importantly, of security of energy supply, the relationship 
(e.g. between Data Act, GDPR, Implementing Acts on Data Access and Interoperability) and 
attached risks should be carefully assessed by the relevant expert groups in that area. 
 
NIS2 Directive 
E.ON puts forward a number of recommendations to enhance and simplify the NIS2 Directive. 
Usage of components (Articles 18, 21): 
• “State of the art” is defined by technical standards rather than by political wishes. It must be 
clarified that expert communities can quickly and specifically define appropriate standards, 
and detailed technical specifications should not be lost in bureaucratic processes of 
authorities. Otherwise overlapping and contradictions might occur. This is also why there 
should be no obligation for certification of any ICT products. This would narrow down markets 
and lead itself to additional risks.  
ICT product and service supply chain (Articles 5, 18): 
• “Security and Privacy by Design” includes the accountability for OEMs and solution providers 
of ICT/OT products for security standards. These parties have full overview of data processing 
and storage, have all capabilities including direct access and should be obliged to provide 
also full security services for all operators.  
Information sharing and cybersecurity reporting obligations (Article 20): 
• E.ON supports the obligation to report significant IT security incidents.  However, reporting 
obligations should remain limited to previously defined, significant IT security incidents . A 
warning within 24 hours cannot provide value due to missing essential information. Same, if a 
threat cannot be specified. 
• Multiple and competing reporting obligations and responsibilities must be avoided (especially 
regarding the CER Directive). It must be ensured that reports from entities to national CSIRTs 
are shared with Member States and then spread again to all potentially affected entities. Also, 
the handling of 0-day vulnerabilities should be explicitly covered in the Directive. 
 
Resilience of Critical Entity Directive 
E.ON proposes some suggestions to further improve and simplify the CER Directive.  
• Overlapping regulations must be avoided, and regulation must be consistent on both 
European and national level. There should be a uniform scheme to identify critical operators: 
Only companies with cross -sectoral or cross -border impact should be covered by the CER 
Directive. Otherwise, the threshold for critical operators of national regulation should apply. 
• The task of maintaining the resilience of critical IT- infrastructures should also consider cross-
sectoral aspects in the Directive. National authorities only have the task to define risk 
scenarios and the potential impact of individual operators on society. 
• Compliance with resilience requirements based on international standards should be 
sufficient, additional inspections should be avoided. There must be no additional legal 
requirements or bureaucracy for support services offered by the EU. This should be th e 
accountability of Member States. 
• Information reported to the national authority should be forwarded to all other national 
authorities within the EU and from there back again to all operators of critical infrastructures.

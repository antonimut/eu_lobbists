 
 
 
 
Position on the Digital Omnibus (cybersecurity 
simplification) 
 
Introduction 
 
Trellix welcomes the European Commission's Digital Omnibus initiative as a timely and 
essential step towards ensuring the EU’s digital rulebook is both effective and efficient. As a 
leading global cybersecurity company dedicated to protecting European enterprises and 
public institutions, we strongly support the stated goal of reducing administrative burdens 
without compromising the Union's high security and privacy standards. 
 
This position paper offers specific, actionable recommendations focused on the 
cybersecurity aspects of the simplification package. We believe that by focusing on four 
core pillars, the Commission can achieve its simplification goals while strengthening 
Europe's overall security posture: 
1. Streamlining incident reporting: harmonising rules, implementing a ‘report once, 
inform all’ system and ensure legal clarity thanks to liability clause. 
2. Harmonizing legal definitions: Creating a common lexicon across regulations to 
provide legal clarity and reduce confusion. 
3. Promoting compliance efficiency: Establishing mechanisms for the mutual 
recognition of compliance efforts to eliminate duplicative work. 
4. Ensuring a Pragmatic Implementation of New Rules: Adjusting the timeline for the 
Cyber Resilience Act to align with the development of necessary technical standards. 
 
 
1. Streamlining incident reporting 
 
Timely incident reporting is vital for digital resilience, but the current EU framework 
complicates this with overlapping and conflicting requirements that draw resources away 
from response. We support harmonizing reporting rules, implementing a "report once, 
inform all" system, and ensuring legal certainty through a liability clause to encourage 
transparency. 
 
Harmonize reporting obligations rules across EU framework 
Organisations operating in Europe today face a patchwork of cybersecurity reporting 
requirements across multiple laws including NIS2, the Cyber Resilience Act, DORA, and the 
GDPR. Each of these instruments establishes distinct incident notification processes 
with different triggers, timelines, competent authorities, and information 
requirements. This proliferation of overlapping obligations creates significant administrative 
burdens without a commensurate security benefit. 
 
For example, a single cyber incident might require notification under NIS2 (to national 
CSIRTs or authorities within hours), under the CRA (to ENISA’s platform within 24 hours for 
actively exploited vulnerabilities), under DORA (to financial supervisors), and possibly under 
GDPR if personal data is involved each with different content and format. Trellix strongly 
supports efforts in the digital omnibus to simplify and harmonise cybersecurity reporting 
obligations across the EU’s regulatory landscape.

A “Report once, inform all” mechanism should be created 
A key improvement would be to implement a “report once, inform all” principle via a single 
reporting platform managed by ENISA. Organisations should be able to submit a single 
comprehensive incident report that satisfies multiple legal requirements, rather than 
duplicating work, while ensuring that the relevant reports are transmitted to the right 
authority.  
 
Moreover, due to the fast implementation of cybersecurity laws, while the Commission and 
ENISA work on single European reporting platforms, national reporting should be simplified 
with the creation of national single reporting entry points. 
 
Increase legal clarity with liability clause 
To foster this transparency, it is vital to integrate liability clauses within the reporting 
framework. Incorporating liability protections into the reporting structure offers legal 
clarity and assurance for organisations. This increased certainty is likely to promote more 
prompt and comprehensive reporting, thereby strengthening situational awareness and 
response capabilities throughout the EU. 
 
 
2. Harmonize key definitions and threshold for legal clarity 
 
A major obstacle to unified cybersecurity reporting is inconsistent terminology across 
regulations, which causes confusion and administrative burden. For example, terms like 
"incident," "significant incident," and "becomes aware" have varying definitions in directives 
such as NIS2 and GDPR, requiring organizations to create separate assessment and 
reporting processes. Definitions of "main establishment" also differ, complicating cross-
border compliance. 
 
Another example is that determining the exact moment of "awareness" is challenging, as 
initial investigative findings are often incomplete. Premature reporting may lead to confusion 
and reputational damage without benefiting authorities. 
 
To solve this, the EU should adopt harmonized definitions for key cybersecurity terms 
through an omnibus, either by creating a shared glossary or amending existing legislation. 
Essential terms for alignment include: 
• Cybersecurity incident 
• Significant incident 
• Become aware of an incident 
• Main establishment 
 
Consistent terminology would clarify obligations and streamline incident reporting for 
organizations. 
 
Furthermore, Trellix is pleased with the development of the EU Vulnerability Database 
(EU-VD) and strongly supports ongoing efforts to align its methodology with the US National 
Vulnerability Database (NVD). We also welcome the adoption of the CVSS score within the 
EU-VD, as it promotes consistent and effective vulnerability assessment across jurisdictions. 
 
3. Reciprocity and mutual recognition of compliance efforts 
 
Beyond incident reporting, the broader compliance workload could be eased by recognizing 
equivalence between different regimes. For example, risk assessments, audits, and 
penetration testing conducted for one regulatory framework (say DORA in finance) could 
also satisfy similar requirements in another (such as NIS2) to the extent the objectives 

 
 
overlap. The Omnibus should encourage the use of reciprocity mechanisms so that 
entities are not double-audited or required to maintain duplicate documentation for 
similar cybersecurity controls. 
 
We recommend the Commission work with ENISA to map overlaps and issue guidance or 
amendments enabling legislative cross-recognition. This would maintain high cybersecurity 
standards while eliminating duplicative efforts. In summary, greater harmonisation of terms 
and consolidation of processes will reduce the regulatory maze that companies navigate, 
freeing up resources to focus on actual security improvements. 
 
4. Improving implementation timeline of the Cyber Resilience Act 
 
The Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) is set to introduce landmark cybersecurity requirements for 
products with digital elements. Trellix supports the CRA’s objective, but we caution that a 
rushed implementation will create unnecessary legal uncertainty and compliance 
burdens, undermining the very principle of this simplification package. 
 
Currently, the CRA’s timeline appears overly ambitious considering the preparatory work still 
needed. The harmonised European standards that manufacturers will rely on for CRA 
compliance are not expected to be ready until late 2027 for many product categories. In 
other words, the CRA will begin to apply and mandate compliance before the detailed 
technical standards (developed CEN/CENELEC or ETSI) are in place. It is well known that 
standardisation processes often face delays, and indeed key product security 
certification and standards (including those aligning CRA requirements with the forthcoming 
EUCC certification scheme) are envisaged but not planned yet. Imposing hard CRA 
compliance deadlines on industry without available standards or approved certification 
schemes would risk a situation where manufacturers are unsure how to demonstrate 
conformity. 
 
Therefore, we recommend that the Digital Omnibus explicitly adjusts the CRA’s 
implementation timeline to account for these realities. This could mean providing 
additional transitional periods or a phased enforcement that kicks in only after corresponding 
standards are published. 
 
Additionally, Mutual Recognition Agreements (MRAs) could substantially accelerate the 
implementation process, enabling the timely and efficient recognition of equivalent 
international standards. Such agreements would alleviate bottlenecks associated with 
standardisation delays, reduce compliance uncertainty for manufacturers, and expedite the 
overall deployment of secure products across Europe. 
 
Conclusion 
 
The forthcoming Digital Omnibus is a crucial opportunity to bolster Europe’s competitiveness 
by reducing unnecessary complexity for businesses and ensuring its digital rulebook is fit for 
purpose. By focusing on pragmatic reforms in reporting, legal harmonisation, and 
implementation, the Commission can reinforce the EU’s cyber resilience in a balanced and 
effective manner. 
 
Specifically, we urge the Commission to: 
1. Establish a “report once, inform all” system via a single EU entry point, supported 
by national platforms in the interim, to allow companies to devote more resources to 
active defence and recovery. 
2. Harmonize key legal definitions across all cybersecurity legislation to eliminate 
ambiguity and ensure consistent interpretation of obligations. 
 
 
3. Introduce reciprocity mechanisms to allow compliance with one regulatory 
framework to satisfy similar requirements in another, eliminating duplicative audits 
and documentation. 
4. Adjust the implementation timeline of the Cyber Resilience Act to ensure its 
requirements are realistic, workable, and supported by available technical standards 
before they become mandatory. 
 
These reforms require strong coordination and commitment. We agree with the Commission 
that simplification efforts must not compromise security effectiveness; rather, they should 
eliminate redundant bureaucracy that diverts resources from actual cyber defence. Trellix is 
grateful for the opportunity to contribute to this consultation and remains committed to 
supporting a secure and resilient Europe through constructive public-private collaboration.

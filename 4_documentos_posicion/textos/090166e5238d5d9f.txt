Cigref’s response to the call for evidence on the Digital Package                  
and Omnibus initiative 
 
 
Cigref, representing a network of 150 leading French companies and public administrations, 
welcomes the Digital Package and Omnibus initiative. 
As major users of digital technologies, our members face the daily operational and strategic 
consequences of fragmented, overlapping, and sometimes inconsistent EU digital regulations.  
We first wish to re-emphasise our support for robust EU  tech rules as a mean  of shielding 
European businesses from unfair practices by actors from third countries. At the same time, we 
recognise that the lack of clarity in the digital regulatory framework may inadvertently serve their 
interests. Therefore, we believe the priority should be to remove overlaps and contradictions that 
generate legal uncertainty and administrative burdens, ultimately weakening business 
competitiveness. 
We outline below several concrete examples and offer recommendations to ensure the Digital 
Omnibus answer these concerns. 
 
1. Addressing gaps in the EU Data Governance landscape 
Cigref closely monitored the implementation of the Data Act and wishes to raise several concerns, 
particularly regarding the lack of harmonised definitions and the unclear treatment of trade 
secrets. 
• Harmonizing terminology to ensure legal consistency 
Cigref calls on the Commission to adopt a consistent and harmonised interpretation of key 
definitions in the Data Act. Clear and aligned definitions especially in relation to other EU 
legislations such as the GDPR, Data Governance Act or AI Act, are essential to prevent confusion 
and legal uncertainty. 
Several provisions currently lack coherence and create contradictions, notably regarding: 
• The timing and recipients of data access obligations 
• The status and categorisation of users under parallel frameworks (e.g. CRA, AI Act) 
• The term “data spaces,” which is inconsistently used across the Data Governance Act, the 
Data Act, and FIDA 
A unified approach is needed to ensure legal predictability and smooth implementation across 
regimes. 
• Clarifying trade secret exceptions under the Data Act 
Article 4 of the Data Act introduces rules on the mandatory sharing of trade secrets, provided that 
all necessary technical and organisational safeguards have been implemented to protect their 
confidentiality. In cases, where the owner can demonstrate a risk of serious economic harm, they 
may refuse access to the data, though this decision can be contested by the data user.

However, the scope and practical application of these provisions remain unclear. It is currently 
difficult to determine what qualifies as a valid exception, and the burden of proof placed on the 
trade secret holder is heavy and complex, especially when responding to multiple requests.  
Cigref calls for clearer procedures to ensure legal certainty and reduce administrative strain. We 
urge the Commission to provide clear guidelines on how to apply this provision. 
 
2. Updating ePrivacy Directive and targeting GDPR complexity 
Cigref calls for an update of the ePrivacy Directive, which it considers outdated and redundant 
with the GDPR , particularly regarding cookie management. It advocates for a streamlined 
implementation process to reduce complexity. 
In parallel, Cigref recommends a targeted simplification of the GDPR to enable a more balanced 
application between data protection and innovation. This includes clarifying the definitions of 
personal, pseudonymized, and anonymized data, and promoting a ris k-based approach with 
harmonized enforcement across the EU. 
3. Towards coherent cybersecurity reporting obligations 
To ensure that cybersecurity regulation supports rather than hinders operational resilience, Cigref 
highlights several areas where simplification and harmonization are urgently needed. From 
clarifying entity responsibilities to aligning reporting timelines and audit procedures, these 
adjustments would reduce administrative burden, enhance legal certainty, and make compliance 
more practical for companies operating across the EU. 
• Clarifying entity scope to enable efficient group-level compliance 
Under NIS2, many companies , especially those operating as corporate groups , face persistent 
uncertainty about which entity is responsible for incident reporting. Subsidiaries and parent 
companies alike struggle to determine who holds the reporting obligation, leading to confusion, 
duplicated efforts, and unnecessary administrative burden. 
Cigref urges policymakers to establish clear, harmonized rules that define oversight 
responsibilities within group structures. To ensure legal certainty and reduce complexity, 
reporting mechanisms should allow for a single, consolidated notification per group, to a single-
entry point that automatically redistributes notifications to relevant authorities . This shared 
notification format would fulfill cybersecurity requirements and could also be used for broader 
reporting duties, including those under GDPR and the CRA. 
• Aligning reporting requirements with operational realities 
In light of the rigid and misaligned nature of current incident reporting obligations, particularly the 
impractical requirement for weekend notifications when no meaningful action can be taken , we 
also stress the need for a clear definition of when an incident is considered to be effectively known 
by the company at the appropriate internal level.  
Without such clarification, firms face uncertainty about their reporting  obligation, compounding 
the administrative burden. 
• Aligning incident reporting timelines between NIS2 and DORA 
There is a clear misalignment in incident reporting timelines between NIS2 and DORA, creating 
unnecessary burdens for ICT service providers in the financial sector. This leads to duplicated 
efforts and operational inefficiencies.

Notification deadlines, ranging from 24 to 72 hours , often fall while technical investigations are 
still underway, forcing companies to submit rushed, partial, or inaccurate reports. 
The burden is further amplified by the need to notify multiple authorities under different 
frameworks. Without clearer guidance, firms face ongoing uncertainty and a disproportionate 
administrative load. 
Cigref calls on the Commission to harmonize incident reporting obligations under NIS2 and DORA, 
enabling affected entities to report through a unified and synchronized procedure. 
• Harmonizing reference frameworks and audit requirements  
A major challenge in the EU cybersecurity landscape is the lack of harmonization in conformity 
assessment procedures across legislations such as NIS2, DORA, and the CRA  and countries. This 
fragmentation imposes administrative burdens on businesses operating across multiple Member 
States.  
To address this, Cigref recommends establishing a centralized conformity assessment framework. 
All cybersecurity legislation should follow uniform methodologies, with mutual recognition of 
certifications across Member States. 
 
4. Clarifying key concepts and responsibilities under the AI Act 
 
• Refine key definitions to ensure legal clarity 
Cigref calls on the Commission to refine and clarify key definitions within the AI Act to ensure legal 
certainty and practical implementation.  
The definition of “AI system” remains ambiguous, making it difficult for stakeholders to understand 
their obligations. To address this, Cigref recommends that the Commission provide concrete 
examples and use cases that illustrate how the concept applies in practice.  
Besides, the definition of “substantial modification” requires greater precision. As currently 
drafted, it leaves providers and developers uncertain about when changes to an AI system may 
trigger new compliance requirements. A more operational and fine-tuned definition is essential to 
avoid disproportionate burdens and support consistent application across the ecosystem. 
• Clarify the burden of proof and contractual responsibilities 
Cigref calls for clearer guidance on the allocation of responsibilities between deployers and 
providers, particularly regarding high-risk AI systems. For example, it should be specified whether 
proof of CE marking, completed or in progress, is sufficient.  
Additionally, Cigref recommends the development of standard contractual clauses to help the 
contractual relations between providers and deployers. 
 
5. Enhancing trust and simplification in EU digital identity governance 
Cigref welcomes the future EU Business Wallet initiative, recognizing its potential as a key enabler 
of regulatory simplification. By offering companies a secure, unified interface to manage national 
and cross-border compliance, notifications, and administrative procedures, the Business Wallet 
promises to reduce manual processing, cut costs, and improve operational efficiency. 
Cigref reiterates that trust, reliability, and sovereignty must remain core principles in the design 
of the future wallet . Digital identity is a strategic capability, and its infrastructure must reflect 
Europe’s commitment to autonomy and data protection. Given the sensitive nature of the 
information handled, it would be difficult to justify a Business Wallet built on non-EU technologies.

Conclusion  
 
Cigref’s recommendations aim to support the Commission in delivering concrete simplification 
across cybersecurity, data governance, and digital identity. Fragmented reporting obligations, 
unclear definitions, and overlapping frameworks are creating real op erational burdens for 
European companies. 
By clarifying responsibilities, harmonizing procedures, and ensuring sovereign digital 
infrastructure, the EU can reduce compliance costs for companies without lowering standards. 
Cigref remains committed to supporting these efforts and contributing to a more agile, secure, 
and sovereign European digital ecosystem , one that serves both operational realities and 
strategic ambitions.

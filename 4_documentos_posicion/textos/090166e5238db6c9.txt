Danish Industry 's contribution to a less complex and 
stronger digital Europe  
Europe has long been at the forefront of regulation and requirements in the digital sector  
with the aim of creating a safe and secure environment for the use of digital 
technologies, as well as strengthening digital security against  growing threat s. While the 
intention is both right and necessary, the volume and complexity of the legislation has 
increasingly led to uncertainty and burdens for European businesses.  
 
This has left the European continent with  deteriorati ng digital competitiveness , and as 
geopolitical tensions increase, the need for digital sovereignty is growing. Regulatory 
simplification is therefore not only a means to reduce the burden on business es and 
strengthen the economy – it is a strategic necessity. Strengthening Europe's position in 
the global technological race requires simpler and more targeted regulation that 
supports innovation, protects value chains and strengthens resilience. Ultimately , it is 
about ensuring Europe's competitiveness, security and independence.  
 
The need for a digital omnibus package that brings together and simplifies existing 
regulation is therefore urgent. Regulation remains a necessary tool to strengthen our 
common resilience, but action is needed that not only reduces administrative burdens 
for businesses  but also strengthens trust in the EU regulatory process and makes the 
European market more attractive for investment  while  contributing to the goal of more 
digital sovereignty. Only in this way can we create a framework that both protects and 
promotes innovation and competitiveness.  
 
At D anish Industr y (DI) we see the digital omnibus as an opportunity to revise current 
and future legislation with the aim of creating simplification and harmonisation , 
proportionate and risk -based regulation , postponement or phasing in where the 
legislation is provisional  or not ready for implementation, as well as  uniform 
enforcement across the board . 
 
In this paper, DI presents the Danish business community's contribution to how we can 
create a set of rules that support rather than inhibit innovation, investments and 
competitiveness.  
 
The paper focuses on the general issues and recommendations that cut across the 
digital regulatory areas and also makes specific proposals for concrete  regulatory 
changes within the EU regulation of artificial intelligence , cybersecurity , data and

We hope that the paper can support the further work on the digital omnibus and

competitiveness  and digital sovereignty.

Overall position and recommendations  
In the following, a number of general issues and recommendations are highlighted that 
cut across legislative areas in the digital area, and which DI considers particularly 
crucial to address. In addition, DI concludes by presenting five principles for EU 
legislation that will contribute to more effective and future -proof regulation.  
 
1. Simplification, harmonisation and elimination of double regulation  
EU digital legislation has become complex and fragmented, creating unnecessary 
burdens and uncertainty for businesses. Overlapping requirements, unclear definitions 
and lack of coordination between EU legislation lead to duplication of work and 
confusion, where the same incident must be reported several times to different 
authorities, for example. Companies are thus affected by overlapping requirements 
from, for example, the AI Act, the Data Act, NIS2, CRA, DSA, GDPR, etc. Uniform 
definitions, common reporting platforms and " once-only" principles are necessary to 
reduce burdens.  
 
Recommendations:  
• Introduce a "once -only" principle for reporting, so that companies only have to report 
once per incident, regardless of whether it is under NIS2, CRA, GDPR or other 
relevant  pieces of  legislation . 
• Create common, harmonised reporting forms and platforms that automatically 
forward reports to relevant authorities.  
• Conduct a systematic review and adjustment of definitions and requirements across 
legislation.  
• Remove or align  requirements that cover the same area, such as reporting, impact 
assessments, and documentation.  
 
2. Proportionate and risk -based regulation  
Businesses  find that digital regulation is not proportionate to  the actual  risk posed by a 
product. This may be due to unclear definitions making it difficult to clarify what level of 
risk a product or service is attributed  to. This  leads to unnecessary requirements and 
documentation burdens – especially for SMEs.  
 
There is a need for rules  and documentation to be adapted to the actual risk and 
complexity, and for regulation to be proportionate, clear and predictable, so that 
companies can focus on real risks rather than redundant processes . 
 
  
 
 
 5 
Recommendations:  
• Risk classification in legislation should be clear, proportionate and based on real risk 
– not broad or unclear definitions that may give rise to different interpretations.  
• Maintain and strengthen the principle of proportionality in the implementation of 
legislation, including the possibility to carry out self -assessment of products and 
services if there is no evidence of increased risk. If a third party is to be involved in 
the conformity assessment, it should be done on the basis of documented evidence 
showing the increased risk of the product or service.  
• Ensure that reporting and compliance requirements are proportionate to the size of 
businesses and do not impose unnecessary burdens,  particularly on SMEs.  
 
3. Postponement and phasing -in of claims  
A lack of consistency between the entry into force of legislation and the availability of 
harmonised standards makes it difficult for companies to comply with the rules. Thus, 
the entry into force of n ew rules often takes place without  regard to whether relevant 
standards and guidelines are available. Short and uncoordinated transition periods 
create uncertainty and the risk that products will have to be withdrawn from the market. 
Realistic and flexible transitional arrangements are nee ded that take into account the 
time taken to develop standards, guidance and the adaptability of businesses.  
 
Recommendations :  
• Delay the entry -into-force of new requirements until relevant standards are 
published.  
• Introduce "stop -the-clock" mechanisms so that companies are not imposed 
requirements they cannot meet. For example, for the AI Act and the Data Act.  
• Introduce flexible and realistic transition periods that consider  the adaptability of 
businesses.  
• Allow temporary use of existing international standards until EU standards are in 
place and involve the industry systematically in the standardisation process to 
ensure practicality.  
 
4. Harmonised  enforcement  
Inconsistent interpretations and inadequate enforcement of EU law lead to  an uneven 
playing field across  member states and cause  significant problems for businesses, 
especially SMEs. Furthermore , many larger  companies operate in multiple  member 
states and sectors, and it is therefore crucial that rules are interpreted in the same way. 
Therefore, the Commission should develop clear guid elines  that harmonise  the many 
interpretation s of requirements and practices . 
 
Recommendations : 
 
 
 6 
• Ensure uniform interpretation of legislation through the development of central 
guidelines and coordinated enforcement of EU rules, so that companies are met by 
similar requirements across member states.  
• Apply consistent common interpretation of key concepts and classification of 
products and services.  
• Prepare user -oriented guides with clear examples of not only illegal , but also 
permitted actions and solutions ( white  lists), so that companies can navigate the 
rules more easily , without slowing down innovation and the use of emerging 
technolog ies. 
• Strengthen enforcement cooperation between national authorities to avoid 
fragmentation and an uneven playing field across the EU.  
 
Key principles for more effective EU legislation  
To avoid the challenges mentioned above repeating themselves in the future, DI 
recommends five key principles for EU legislation , both in the digital sector  and more 
generally:  
 
1. Early clarification of overlap with existing rules  
New legislative initiatives should take into account  existing EU laws from the outset 
to avoid duplication of regulation, conflicting requirements and unnecessary 
complexity for businesses and authorities.  
2. Refrain from  develop ing new unnecessary burdens  
Strengthen the use of "Better Regulation" principles in the development of new 
legislation with a focus on minimising  unnecessary burdens.  
3. Innovation as an integrated principle  in legislation  
If the EU is to keep up with technological developments and global competition, 
innovation must be given the same priority as security and privacy considerations. In 
addition to protecting fundamental rights and EU values, legislation should also 
support development an d innovation, not hamper it.  
4. Standards and guidelines must be ready before entry into force  
New requirements should not enter into force until relevant standards and guid ance 
are available. Mechanisms should be put in place that enable  a postponement of 
entry into force  if the basis for implementation is not yet in place.  
5. Timely involvement of industry experts  
Practitioners, experts and professionals from  relevant  industr ies should be closely 
involved early and systematically in the legislative process, including in the 
preparation of delegated acts, to ensure that the rules are applicable and reflect 
real -world needs and challenges.

Specific recommendations  
This section includes specific proposals for changes within the EU regulation of artificial 
intelligence, cybersecurity, data and product legislation . These proposals are  based on 
examples that Danish companies have experienced . 
 
1. Artificial Intelligence  
1.1 Extension of implementation deadlines ("stop -the-clock")  
The development of harmonised European standards is moving slowly, creating 
uncertainty and ambiguity for businesses . Without standards, businesses  risk having 
difficulty  comply ing with the AI Act, and enforcement  will becom e uneven and 
fragmented. Even when the standards are ready, it takes time and resources to 
implement them. For businesses this process  involves, for example, adapting existing 
systems, employee training and changing internal proce dures . This is particularly 
troubling since  the AI Act is the first comprehensive regulation  that focuses on AI 
technology specifically .  
 
Recommendations : 
• Extend implementation deadlines in the AI Act so that AI requirements are only 
applied when appropriate harmonised standards are in place and available.  
• It is recommended that an automatic rule be introduced so that requirements that 
depend on harmonised standards do not apply until 12 months after the relevant 
standard has been published .   
  
1.2 Legal clarity and clarification of interaction with other legislation  
There is a need for legal clarity on the interaction between the AI Act and other EU 
legislation such as GDPR, copyright and sector -specific rules. Unclear interfaces and 
overlapping requirements create uncertainty and administrative burdens for businesses . 
 
Recommendations :  
• The interaction between the AI Act and other EU legislation must be clarified and 
followed by guidance.  
• Map out which interactions between the AI Act and other EU legislation are 
particularly a source of doubt and legal ambiguity for businesses  with the aim of 
prioritising the EU’s guidance efforts .   
• In order to avoid legal ambiguity, it should be clarified which specific parts  of the AI 
Act only appl y once the sector -specific legislation has been updated.   
 
Specific proposals for changes : 
 
 
 8 
• Article 10  on data and data management: The documentation requirements in the 
article can be very burdensome, especially for smaller companies. Likewise, there is 
unclear overlap with the GDPR in relation to the use of personal data.  
• The requirements for data documentation and governance must be made 
proportionate and risk based . There is also a need for the AI Act's data requirements 
not to create overlap or conflict with the GDPR, including clear guidelines in relation 
to, among other things, anonymisation and the use of personal data.  
• Article 53 on obligations for providers of AI models for general use: It is unclear when 
an actor adapting or using a GPAI model will be subject to the obligations.  
• It should be clarified that downstream actors (e.g. companies fine -tuning or using 
GPAI models) only become GPAI providers if they make "significant changes" that 
create a new general -purpose  model. At the same time, "significant changes" must 
be described in more detail.  
 
1.3 Removing unnecessary and overlapping requirements  
Duplication of requirements and double regulation hamper the innovation and 
competitiveness of businesses, creating uncertainty, additional costs and 
administrative burdens for businesses, especially SMEs. Effective regulatory 
simplifications should theref ore be introduced  in areas where administrative burdens 
are disproportionate or where there are unnecessary overlaps between the AI Act and 
other EU legislation .  
 
Specific proposals for changes : 
• Article 6  on classification rules for high -risk AI systems: The article is interpreted 
differently across sector -specific legislation. Therefore, it should be clarified that 
only safety components can be classified as high -risk. At the same time, the extent 
to which the use of third parties in other sector -specific  legislation is relevant for the 
definition of a security component as high -risk should be reassessed.  
• Categorisation of products should be based on evidence  to ensure proportionality in 
requirements, and a risk -based approach should be used , so that only AI systems 
that actually pose a high risk should be classified as high -risk.  
• Article 43  on conformity assessment: The use of 3rd parties should be evidence -
based. The use of 3rd parties due to a lack of harmonised standards should be 
reassessed as it increases burdens without improving compliance. The lack of third  
parties also poses a risk that compliant products cannot be placed on the market.  
• Article 82  on AI systems  which  are compliant  but present a risk : The article 
undermines legal certainty and predictability for providers because a compliant AI 
system may still face additional requirements or restrictions from national 
authorities. This can lead to fragmentation of the internal market, as different 
member states can introduce different additional requirements.  
 
 
 9 
• The article should be removed as compliance with the AI Act should be sufficient to 
be able to use and market AI systems across the EU without the risk of additional 
national requirements.  
 
Case example:  
The requirements for high -risk AI will enter into force in August 2026, and for products 
such as hearing aids (covered by Annex I) in August 2027. However, the harmonised 
standards needed to prove compliance will not be ready until mid -2026 or later. It 
takes several years to develop a hearing aid. Without ready -made standards, 
companies cannot prepare properly.  
 
 
2. Cybersecurity  
 
2.1 Alignment of rules and requirements for reporting security incidents (NIS2, CRA, 
GDPR, DORA)  
Companies in the EU are currently covered by several different reporting mechanisms 
for security incidents in NIS2, CRA, GDPR and DORA, respectively. For example, 
different thresholds for security incidents are operated in the CRA, DORA and NIS2, 
respectiv ely, and in addition, different reports must be prepared for the incidents due to 
divergent requirements in the different legal acts . The deadline s for submittin g report s 
are also uneven.  
 
Case example : 
Company X , a Danish company,  discovers a cyberattack on their systems. They must 
now consider whether the incident meets the threshold for security incidents in either 
the CRA , NIS2 or both, as the thresholds are different  but not mutually exclusive . They 
must now inform the supervisory authority for the CRA and NIS2 (the Danish Civil 
Protection Agency) about the incident within 24 hours. As it is also a data breach with 
personal data, Company X must also inform the supervisory authority for the GDPR  
(the Danish Data Protection Agency) about the data breach, but only within 72 hours.  
  
Company X must also prepare three different reports for the incident due to different 
content requirements in the CRA, NIS2 and GDPR, respectively. According to the CRA, 
the report must be ready within 14 days, but according to NIS2, Company X has one 
month.  
 
 
Recommendations:  
 
 
 10 
• There is a need to streamline the rules across the different legal acts, and therefore it 
is recommended to align the rules/requirements for reporting data breaches in the 
GDPR with reporting of security incidents for the other areas.  
• In the standardization, consideration should be given to where the greatest risks lie 
in relation to assessing the right time frame for reporting security incidents, for 
example:  
o One common threshold for action requirements  
o One common timeframe for informing regulators of a security incident  
o One common reporting template for NIS2, CRA and GDPR.  
 
2.2. Simplify the implementation of cybersecurity requirements for products  
EU product regulation introduces requirements for cybersecurity on an ongoing basis. 
This applies to the Radio Equipment Directives (RED  DA), the Machinery Regulation (MR), 
the General Product Safety  Regulation (GPSR) and the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA). As 
many products are subject to several different legislations, meaning that companies 
must  deal with different requirements with different deadlines for implementation. This 
is happening at the same time as companies have already been in the process of 
strengthening the cybersecurity of their products for a number of years, typically using 
international standards. At the same t ime, the Commission has announced that it will 
withdraw the cybersecurity requirements of the Radio Equipment Directive (RED DA) 
when the CRA applies.  
 
Recommendations:  
• Compliance with CRA ahead of time should result in automatic compliance with RED 
DA, just as compliance with CRA should be equated with compliance with RED DA 
during a transitional period.  
• Withdraw RED DA for B2B products. The purpose of RED DA was to secure consumer 
products. In addition, the companies have worked to secure B2B products in 
connection with NIS1 and now NIS2.  
• Postpone the implementation of the CRA reporting requirements until the other 
requirements apply (by 15 months) . 
• Reassess the date of application of the CRA in relation to when the harmonized 
standards are ready. Provide the opportunity to apply international standards to 
obtain the right of presumption.  
• Re-evaluate products that are subject to the use by third  parties in the CRA. Self -
assessment (Module A) should be possible if there is no evidence of increased risk.  
 
 
 11 
 
2.3 Harmonisation of definitions of cyber security (Machinery Regulation, CRA, RED, 
NIS2)  
Within the different pieces of legislation, there is a big difference in how cybersecurity is 
defined, which creates confusion for companies that typically have to deal with the 
security aspect across the different types of regulation.  
2.4 Harmonisation of rules across the EU (NIS2)  
Strengthened and fair competition depends on rules working the same for companies 
across EU countries. Unfortunately, the NIS2 Directive leaves room for many different 
interpretations across the Member States, and it is not appropriate for such central 
legislation for both society and companies to be implemented in very different ways. At 
the same time, the Draghi report advocates easing administrative burdens for 
businesses in order to ensure improved European competitiveness, and regulation 
should therefo re be made according to the minimum requirements set out in NIS2.  
 
Recommendations:  
• Future negotiations of the NIS should focus on strengthening harmonisation and 
standardisation of interpretations across Member States, as well as with minimum 
requirements as a starting point for legislation. In addition, it is recommended to 
cross -refere nce when legislation is in conflict. This applies not only to cybersecurity, 
but to a number of areas of legislation where there are inconsistencies in 
terminology.  
 
3. Data  
 
3.1 Postpone entry into force of the Data Act  by 24 months  
There is a broad desire across several EU member states as well as the European 
business community to slow down the implementation of the Data Act based on the 
following:  
• Legal uncertainty  such as overlaps with other EU legislation (see proposal 3.2).  
• The Regulation imposes requirements for data sharing even in areas where there is 
no concrete market need or where European companies risk giving up a competitive 
advantage (see proposal 3.3).  
• Unclear scope, for example, the definition of data processing services, where 
regulation targeted at larger cloud providers ends up having noticeable 
consequences for smaller providers with specialized solutions.  
• Parts of the  Data Act will apply  retroactively, which will place  major burdens on 
companies with existing (and completely well -functioning) contracts for data sharing 
and processing. In addition, the rules are likel y to enter into force before relevant 
standards are  published .   
 
 
 12 
 
Recommendations:  
• It is recommended to postpone the entry into force of the new requirements of the 
Data Act by 24 months.  
 
3.2 Address ambiguities in data sharing rules for mixed datasets ( Data Act, GDPR)  
The Data Act places great demands on the sharing and availab ility  of data , includ ing 
datasets that partially contain  personal data , which is  covered by the GDPR. This 
applies, for example, in cases where a company owns  a dataset that another company 
has requested access to, in which  dataset contains both  personal and non -personal 
data . A subset of the total data set is therefore covered by the rules of the GDPR, 
including the principles of "privacy by default" and data minimisation, while another 
subset is not covered (a so -called "mixed data set"). In these cases, a disproportionate 
burden is placed on the companies responsible for  having to  make the right legal 
assessments about whether the dataset can be shared and which parts of the dataset 
are covered by which rules .  
 
Recommendations:  
• The Data Act and GDPR should be harmonised to prevent conflicting requirements, 
especially for mixed datasets containing personal data and non -personal data, so 
that businesses are not subject to disproportionate burdens.  
• A joint guide on mixed datasets prepared by the supervisory authorities for the Data 
Act and GDPR (in Denmark, respectively, the Agency for Digital Government  and the 
Danish Data Protection Agency).  
 
3.3 Prioritise the protection of trade secrets ( Data Act) 
DI is a strong advocate of data sharing to promote innovation, growth and strengthened 
competition. At the same time, it is essential for companies to continue being able to 
properly  protect their critical trade secrets  and know -how.  
 
The Data Act states that as a data holder, you must be able to prove exactly where in the 
requested dataset that  trade secrets can be  found before you can reject a data sharing 
request for a potential competitor. This  process is a highly  resource -intensive task for a 
business , and it should therefore be easier to preemp tively reject the data sharing 
request .  
 
Case example : 
Company X receives a request to share user data for one of the company's best -selling 
products. The request comes from Company Y, which is a competitor, and therefore it 
may have competitive consequences to share  over user data. In order to process the 
 
 
 13 
request, according to the Data Act , Company X must state where in the requested data 
there are instances of  trade secrets or know -how, with the purpose of entering into an 
agreement with Company Y that they will protect this information after  data is shared.  
However, the  problem  for Company X is not  just that trade secrets could be exposed to 
a third party, but that they are shared with Company Y  in the first place .  
 
Only in "exceptional cases" can the request be refused, requiring both thorough 
documentation and reporting to the national supervisory authority. Company X does 
not want to risk sharing trade secrets with a competitor, and must therefore allocate 
signific ant resources to uncover and document this across all requested data.  
 
The entire above process requires both financial, technical and legal resources that 
only a few companies possess  whilst  plac ing the burden entirely on the data holder  
 
Recommendations:  
• Recogni se the protection of trade secrets and know -how as a fully legitimate basis  
for rejecting data sharing  requests in the Data Act. 
• Shift the burden of challenging the rejection  to the request ing party , instead of 
imposing resource -intensive documentation requirements on the data holder.  
• Extend the transition period for the mapping of  trade  secret s from 20 to 36 months to 
reflect the heavy burden placed on businesses and ensure that proper guidance  is in 
place .  
 
3.4 Limit  contractual obligations in the Data Act with a focus on freedom of contract  
The Data Act determines  several  pre-contractual obligations, meaning  that suppliers 
must make various information about data formats, data structures, system setup  etc. 
available  in advance  and before there is a specific need or request . It should be 
sufficient to simply be obliged to disclose this  information  at the request of potential 
customers.  
 
In addition, the Data Act contains provisions that dictate mandatory contractual terms 
for data processing services that diverge  from the principle  of freedom of contract that 
characterize other EU legislation . In addition, the rules are currently so broadly defined 
that they could void  both future and existing contracts , even for  small and medium -sized 
suppliers with specialised SaaS -solutions, even though the purpose is to regulate the 
terms of contracts with the largest companies.  
 
Recommendations:  
• Limit the Data Act's pre -contractual obligations, including:  
o Requirement s for sharing  data  and accompanying information  by default , so 
that it is only shared  at a user’s request . 
 
 
 14 
o Requirements for contract ual terms for data processing services in the Data 
Act, so that the parties can agree on whether the supplier is entitled to charge 
separately for exit assistance. This could be achieved  by narrowing the 
definition of data processing services so that suppliers of smaller, specialised 
solutions are not covered by the requirements – in accordance with the 
Regulation's purpose of regulating the large cloud solutions that see  broad 
use.  
 
3.5 Eliminate the many overlaps in rules on data transfers to third countries (DGA, Data 
Act, GDPR)  
Data transfers to countries outside the EU (third -country transfers) are regulated by 
three different EU legal acts, respectively the DGA, the Data Act and the GDPR. It is 
unnecessary to regulate on top of existing rules and it increases the complexity of the 
conclusion of new contracts for third -country transfers enormously. It also creates 
uncertainty as to whether the EU's standard contracts (SCCs) for  third -country transfers 
can be used in practice if they do not cover the additions in the DGA and the Data Act, 
respectively.  
 
Recommendations:  
• Eliminate the provisions on third -country transfers in Chapter VII of the DGA and the 
Data Act. Instead, both regulations should refer to the applicable rules of the GDPR.  
 
3.6 GDPR  
The GDPR has been in place for over seven years and has been developing rapidly 
throughout that time. Many guide s and rulings  have made companies wiser about the 
actual  requirements of the GDPR , and there are quite a few.  
 
Data protection is a fundamental right and is an essential part of the EU's DNA. 
Unfortunately, the balanced approach taken by the regulation, in which data protection 
had to be seen in the context of other fundamental rights, has been overshadowed by a 
disproportionate focus on the protection of individuals.  
 
The problem is twofold: Too much uncertainty about legal activities and uneven 
interpretation of the rules slow down companies' innovation and technological 
development, and unsustainable administrative burdens increase the pressure on 
earnings. This has l ed to a shift in the competitiveness of European companies, which 
must be restored if the EU is to continue to be a standard -bearer for digital products and 
services that also ensure the best protection of our personal data.  
 
The specific challenges can be found both in the GDPR's broad definition of personal 
data, in uncertainties about legal basis and in expanding  interpretations of the 
 
 
 15 
regulation's articles. For the latter, the worst examples are two new assessments: 'TIA' 
for third -country transfers and 'LIIA' for balancing of interests, which are not stated in 
the GDPR, but are interpreted through  the Regulation's general  requirement of 
accountability.  
 
Attached is a separate position paper regarding GDPR, which elaborates on DI's 
recommendations in the area.  
 
Recommendations:  
• Proportionality in data protection rules must be recognised as necessary.  
• It must be easier to find a legal basis for product development in new technologies, 
such as AI.  
• Make the GDPR more risk -oriented, especially with regard to documentation 
requirements. There should be fewer requirements for low - and medium -risk 
processing , and one should revisit the requirements for, for example, Article 30  on 
records  of processing activities , LIIA, Article 13 & 14 on duty to provide  information to 
the data subject  and Article 15 on right of access.  
• Greater focus on the rules being interpreted uniformly across EU member states, so 
that companies only have to go through one compliance review across EU countries. 
The GDPR became a regulation to ensure harmonisation, but the differences are still 
too gre at. If necessary, introduce a Once -only principle.  
• Simplify supplier screening and supervision processes and/or simplify rules for third -
country transfers, in particular TIAs.  
• Facilitate the creation of Codes of Conduct that have the potential to streamline data 
protection.  
 
4. Standardisation and EU product legislation  
Companies use standards to show that their products comply with legal requirements. 
They are developed by standardisation organisations commissioned by the Commission. 
Experience shows that standards  used to comply with legal requirements regarding 
product cybersecurity and the use of AI are delayed. This means that companies do not 
know how best to meet legal requirements and they  instead  have to act blindly. When 
the standards are not ready, legislation sometimes requires the use of 3rd parties, which 
makes compliance more expensive and increases complexity and speed to market.  
 
As a replacement for standards, the European Commission proposes to develop 
common specifications for companies to use. However, this is not an appropriate 
solution, as these are not based on the international standards that companies already 
use globally,  and they do not risk building on the state -of-the-art solutions that are in the 
market or are in line with future standards. The lack of progress in the existing 
standardisation system is linked to disproportionately strict requirements for the 
 
 
 16 
structure and content of the standards, combined with a lack of experts who can 
develop standards at the speed necessary to meet the application dates of relevant 
legislation. This is a complex of problems that appl ies across all product legislation, but 
especially affect s the digital area, where standardisation is more immature.  
 
Recommendations : 
• The Commission should reassess its approach to the approval of standards to be 
proportionate to the proper functioning of the internal market.  
• Dates of application of the legislation should be revised in relation to when it is 
realistic that the standards are ready.  
• It must be possible to use international standards to obtain a presumption of 
compliance with EU product legislation.  
• Common specifications are an emergency solution that should only be used when all 
other options have been exhausted.  
 
4.1 Other recommendations for EU product legislation  
In addition, DI has prepared a number of recommendations on how EU product

GDPR simplification – Nordic position paper 
October 2025 
 
The Nordic national cross-sectorial employer’s federations; Danish Industry (DI),  
Confederations of Finnish Industries (EK), Norwegian Enterprise (NHO) and Swedish Enterprise (SN) thank 
the Commission for its work with simplification and stress the importance of this effort for EU 
competitiveness. 
 
In this position paper we present our common view of businesses’ experience related to the GDPR and 
propose some ideas about how to reduce burdens and strengthen compliance. Our detailed proposals are 
set out in Annex I to this document. 
 
Background 
The focus on regaining the EU's competitiveness with the Omnibus packages is a necessary help for 
companies that need to balance between increased EU compliance complexity and competitiveness. Not 
least since companies experience that data protection has become disproportionate, overemphasizing 
protection where little is needed, hindering innovation where speed and ingenuity matter, and applying 
high administrative burdens where costs define competitiveness.  
 
The EU's current SMC Omnibus Proposal introduces  changes to the GDPR,  by extending the exemption 
for documentation requirements of low and medium risk processing activities. Although we welcome the 
changes, we acknowledge the strong need to further simplify the regulation. 
 
Introduction  
The reality of European businesses is characterized by increased competition from global players and a 
deterioration in competitiveness. Excessive regulation is one of the biggest hurdles for the digital 
economy’s competitiveness and growth in EU.1  
 
The general principles of the GDPR safeguard fundamental rights and ensure customer’s trust in digital 
services and new technology. When done in a proportionate and risk-based manner, innovation and 
competitiveness can be strengthened.  
 
The GDPR represents a hindrance, a limitation, and a burden on businesses in mainly two ways: 
 
Challenge # 1 Innovation and technology development  
The GDPR represents a severe hindrance to innovation and technology by setting absolute 
limitations for use of data and restricting the use of data for the purposes of developing new 
products and technology, such as AI models.    
 
Challenge # 2 Administrative burden and cost of compliance  
The GDPR puts an enormous administrative and cost burden in the form of documentation and 
technical infrastructure cost – affecting the general competitiveness of businesses.  
  
According to Draghi’s report, the GDPR has slowed down the growth of data-driven businesses in the EU by 
as much as 15–20 %, specifically in sectors such as the pharma and health technology. In addition, the 
costs of GDPR compliance may amount to as much as EUR 500,000 for a SME, and up to EUR 10 million for 
larger organizations.2

A new research report from Finland, states that the GDPR has cut pharma and biotech R&D spending by up 
to 39 % within four years. The effect is sharpest for firms operating solely under the strict EU regime, while 
multinationals soften the blow by shifting data-heavy work abroad. Smaller firms bear the brunt, as 
compliance costs hit harder relative to scale, widening gaps across companies and regions.3 The effects 
are visible, as 61% of Danish companies in a report from 2025 found GDPR to be a burden4.   
  
EU needs to ensure that European businesses are given the necessary room to develop new innovations 
and new technologies, be competitive and take part in Europe's transformation to become a leading AI 
continent.  
  
If we are to regain some of the lost ground, costs and administrative burdens need to be kept as low as 
possible to get EU-made innovation and competitiveness fit for a global market. In the words of von der 
Leyen, we need less paperwork, less overlaps, less complex rules.5  This would also strengthen legal 
certainty, compliance and a level playing field. 
 
Challenge #1, Innovation and technology development  
More than ever before, data is the key component in creating advanced products and digital services, and 
high-quality data is essential for developing competitive AI models and solutions. To strengthen European 
tech capacity, the EU would benefit from a regulatory framework that does not hinder the development 
of AI-solutions, IoT-products, cloud storage solutions and AI models.6 They cannot be developed and 
improved without access to comprehensive and high-quality training data. De-identified or pseudonymised 
data is often sufficient for innovation and can be used with minimal or no risk to individuals. 
 
But the combination of a broad definition of personal data (also including highly de-identified data) and the 
requirements for legal bases and the purpose limitation, significantly restrict collection, sharing, and use 
of data for innovation and technology development (AI included).  
  
If European businesses are to develop technology such as AI-systems and -models, it must be clarified 
how (not if) they may process the data they need to develop digital products. Accessibility to high quality 
data is a prerequisite for EU- competitiveness.  
  
As a solution to the Challenge #1, we propose  
1) that data should not be treated as personal data if the processing is temporary and if the purpose 
of the processing is unrelated to identifying the data subject as a natural person.7 
2) processing both regular and special categories (e.g. sensitive) of personal data should not be 
disproportionally restrictive.8 We propose that low-risk processing for R&D purposes, training and 
use of AI should be deemed lawful as a main rule. Even if such data is special category data since 
this is crucial to achieve non-biased services and products. 
3) the balancing test required for legitimate interests should be unnecessary to document in many 
cases, and this should be explicitly stated in either the regulation or preambles.

Challenge #2: Administrative burden and cost of compliance  
The sum of all required GDPR documentation is comprehensive for nearly all businesses.  To this adds that 
authorities issuing guidance are often unable to clarify what is "enough", while also introducing new 
documentation requirements beyond those that are explicitly mentioned in the GDPR. Examples of this are 
requirements to conduct Transfer Impact Assessments (TIAs) before any third country transfer and 
Legitimate Interest Impact Assessments (LIIAs) when using legitimate interest as legal basis. 
 
There is a real risk that the documentation requirements will keep expanding in the coming years. This 
unfortunately increases the already present chilling effect companies exhibit and calls for more pragmatic 
decision making from EDPB and national authorities.   
 
Documentation requirements that create administrative burden without improving data protection, 
sometimes serving mainly auditing purposes, are of kind that would benefit from simplification. GDPR’s 
accountability and documentation duties should be more risk-based to reduce paperwork and focus on 
real risks.  
 
An example of the excessive administrative burden are the provisions and guidelines regarding third 
country transfers, which lead to irrational accumulative workload and bureaucracy for each company. 
Another area is data subject’s rights. They are important to keep but they need to be proportionate, less 
complex and less administrative burdensome. 
 
As a solution to the Challenge #2, we propose  
1) simplification of the documentation requirements in the GDPR and a real risk-based approach to 
reduce unnecessary paperwork, further improving on the SMC omnibus proposals. 
2) to limit administrative burdens related to the data subject’s rights by making them clear, limited 
and proportionate. 
3) to ease the burden on companies through additional adequacy decisions and clear 
recommendations on data transfers to third countries.

please see Annex I to this document.

Art 6, 
Lawfulness of 
processing 
Processing of personal data for product and 
technology development (AI included), is currently too 
restricted. In practice, it is rarely feasible to base such 
processing on consent. At the same time, it is often 
uncertain whether other, more appropriate, legal bases 
such as legitimate interest (the balancing of interests) 
would apply. Of course, the need to carry out a legal 
assessment on a project-by-project basis cannot be 
completely removed. However, from a broader 
perspective, the current legal situation appears overly 
uncertain and restrictive to foster innovation. 
 
As a solution, we propose clarifying the legal grounds for 
processing personal data. Legitimate interest and 
contract are the most important and widely used legal 
bases for companies’ data processing. Their application 
should be straightforward in ordinary, low-risk 
situations. For processing of pseudonymised data for 
technology and AI development, the main rules should 
be that a company’s interest shall prevail. The possibility 
to rely on contractual processing should be broadened. 
The balancing test required for legitimate interests 
should, in most cases, be unnecessary, and this should 
be explicitly stated in the regulation.

risk of transfer. This risk-based approach should be 
reflected in the provisions or the preamble of the 
regulation.  
 
The Commission should take more decisions on the 
adequate level of data protection and assess the legality 
of data transfers by large players rather than individual 
companies.

GDPR and the 
AI Act 
The AI Act and the GDPR are different sets of rules, but 
they have many similarities and need to be applied 
together in their own areas. For high-risk AI systems, it is 
especially important that the two are aligned to make 
compliance possible. Applying them in parallel creates 
overlaps: some rules confirm that the GDPR comes first, 
some aim to match the AI Act with it, and others add to its 
rules.2 
 
It is crucial that the GDPR does not disproportionately 
hinder the EU's goal of becoming a global leader in AI 
development. To ensure that the EU regulation fosters 
trust and innovation, it must be refined into a more risk-
based, proportionate framework that is future-proof and 
eases compliance.  
 
For example, Art 59 of the AI Act defines when it is 
possible to deviate from the original purpose of personal 
data processing under the GDPR in a sandbox-
environment during AI development. This is allowed if 
the AI model is being developed, for instance, to 
promote public health, public security, critical 
infrastructure, or the green transition. The scope of these 
exemptions should be clarified and expanded to also 
cover companies’ own AI development needs.

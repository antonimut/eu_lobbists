Introduction 
Founded in 2000, Namirial Group operates in over 85 countries with approximately 1,400 
employees. Thanks to an international network of over 1,000 strategic partners, it serves 
more than three million customers worldwide, processing millions of transaction s every 
day. 
Namirial is the largest European qualified trust service provider under EU Regulation 
910/2014 eIDAS and is actively engaged in the evolution of the EU Digital Identity 
Framework and the new trust services defined in EU Regulation 2024/1183. 
1. General Remarks  
Europe's digital regulatory framework has reached a critical point. The proliferation of 
horizontal and sectoral acts – including the NIS 2 Directive, Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), 
Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act), revised eIDAS Regulation, Data Act, and  GDPR – has 
created a complex ecosystem that absorbs resources, slows innovation, and limits the 
competitiveness of European digital service providers.  
Qualified Trust Service Providers (QTSPs), as the cornerstone of digital trust, face 
particularly acute challenges. The cumulative effect of multiple compliance regimes (NIS, 
GDPR, DORA, eIDAS supervision) and overlapping audit obligations has become 
unsustainable. European companies are increasingly required to dedicate 
disproportionate resources to compliance and reporting, rather than to innovation and 
service delivery. At the same time, while Europe decides and regulates increasingly 
complex systems, gl obal Big Tech players continue to establish de facto standards 
through simple, userfriendly solutions rapidly adopted by European citizens. Once these 
solutions achieve significant market share —such as web browsers, operating systems, 
and now payment walle ts—they become entrenched and nearly impossible to displace, 
further weakening the competitiveness of European digital services.

2. Key Challenges  
2.1 Fragmentation and Excessive Audits  
QTSPs are subject to recurring audits from different authorities – supervisory bodies, 
conformity assessment bodies (CABs), cybersecurity authorities under NIS 2, and data 
protection authorities under GDPR. The result is a fragmented compliance landscape with 
divergent requirements and duplicative inspections.  
Proposed modification:  Introduce a single-audit framework applicable to QTSPs and 
other critical digital service providers. This unified process should be recognised by all 
competent authorities (supervisory, NIS 2, GDPR, and DORA). The European Commission 
should formalise a  mutual recognition principle  under which one comprehensive audit 
report, performed by an accredited body, satisfies all overlapping obligations.  
 
2.2 Misaligned Timelines and Missing Standards  
The regulatory implementation calendars across digital acts are inconsistent. For 
instance, the AI Act and CRA impose compliance dates before the publication of 
harmonised standards, leaving industry without clear benchmarks. The EUDI Wallet 
depends on mor e than 190 standards still under development and without a common 
certification schemas, while the deadline for implementation is still ad end of 2026 and 
the key goal was interoperability in Europe avoiding National silos. Such misalignment 
creates legal uncertainty and discourages investment at European level.  
Proposed modification:  Introduce a  “stop-the-clock” clause  across all digital 
regulations, ensuring that obligations become enforceable only  36 months after  the 
relevant harmonised standards are officially published in the  Official Journal of the 
European Union. This principle should be enshrined in the Digital Omnibus as a horizontal 
simplification rule, implicitly forcing the simplification of standards and encouraging a 
freer, innovation-driven approach rather than one centred on regulation.

comprehensive reality check on Levels of Assurance (LoA)  should be performed, 
quantifying the actualised costs an d benefits of different assurance levels. Based on its 
findings, the Commission could decide to extend the existing “stop -the-clock” provisions 
for additional time to ensure proportionality and real-world feasibility and adoption.  
 
2.4 Complexity and Unsustainable Compliance Costs for QTSPs  
Operating as a QTSP under eIDAS has become disproportionately expensive. Each service 
(QES, QeRDS, QEAA, eArchiving) requires individual conformity assessments, incident 
reports, and continuous supervision. Combined with NIS 2 obligations, these processes 
multiply administrative and financial burdens.

Proposed modification:  
• Establish a Trust Service Simplification Track, exempting QTSPs from redundant 
conformity assessments when compliance with NIS 2 or CRA standards can 
already demonstrate equivalent security guarantees.  
• Define a  proportional documentation regime  for low -risk services, allowing 
micro- and small -enterprise QTSPs to use simplified templates and risk -based 
reporting, as recommended in the BDI simplification framework.  
 
2.5 The EUDI Wallet: Excessive Standardisation and Delayed Benefits  
The implementation of the European Digital Identity Wallet involves nearly 190 technical 
standards and certification dependencies. Several Member States have postponed 
deployment due to technical and financial infeasibility. The lack of a sustainable business 
model for Wallet providers further threatens long -term adoption, especially where 
mature national eID schemes (SPID, itsme, BankID, Identité Numerique) already exist.  
Proposed modification:  
• Apply a modular compliance model under eIDAS 2, allowing Wallet Providers to 
demonstrate conformity through existing QTSP certifications (QES, QEAA, QeRDS) 
without duplicative audits.  
• Introduce a  Sustainability Assessment  requirement for Member States, 
ensuring that public investments in Wallet infrastructures are backed by cost -
benefit analyses and realistic adoption metrics, leaving space for market 
operators to build new business models with added value and without defin ing 
state monopolists instead of competitive market operators. Namirial is already 
developing the ETSI TR 119 479 -2 to create new opportunities for Qualified 
Electronic Attribute Authorities that should be supported by  wallet providers to 
create a new sustainable business for private entities and new use cases for the 
users.  
• Recognise LoA Substantial as sufficient for business and cross -border scenarios 
until at least 2028, specifically for the application of Articles 24 and 51 of eIDAS, to 
ensure the practical adoption of LoA High eIDs and Wallets for issuing signature 
certificates without undermining real-world usability or creating disproportionate 
burdens for negligible benefit.

2.6 The Future Business Wallet (EUBW)  
The forthcoming European Business Wallet must be conceived as a market -driven 
initiative, not a public monopoly. It should reflect the logic of legal representation and 
enable interoperability with existing trust services.  
Proposed modification:  
• Separate the EUBW legal framework from the EUDI Wallet to reflect the distinct 
nature of business identities and representation models.  
• Mandate that EUBW solutions be  operated or delegated to private QTSPs , 
ensuring competition, innovation, and sustainability.  
• Anchor the EUBW on existing  qualified trust services  (QES, QeRDS, QEAA, 
eArchiving) to avoid duplication.  
• Permit fee-based issuance and maintenance  to guarantee viable economic 
models, as business identities serve commercial, not fundamental -rights, 
purposes.  
• Standardise the use of  LoA Substantial  for business operations, aligning with 
AMLR and DPP requirements, while allowing optional LoA High only for high risk 
scenarios.

2.8 Recognition of Existing Industry Standards  
Many industries already apply internationally recognised standards (ISO 27001, EMVCo, 
GSMA eSA). Forcing re -certification under EU -specific schemes creates duplication and 
delays.  
Proposed modification:  
• Grant automatic  CRA and NIS 2 compliance equivalence  to providers certified 
under established international standards.  
• Allow a transitional period of  36 months  after publication of new European 
standards before deprecating existing ones.

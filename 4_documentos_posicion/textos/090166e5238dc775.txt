Mastercard’s Response to the Public Consultation on the Digital Omnibus Package 
Introduction 
As a global payments and digital infrastructure provider with a strong presence in the 
EU for more than 45 years, Mastercard welcomes the opportunity to contribute to the 
European Commission’s consultation on the Digital Omnibus package. 
We fully support the EU’s objectives of simplification and fostering the Digital Single 
Market by reducing duplicative obligations, creating legal certainty, and promoting a 
balanced regulatory approach. Such a framework is essential to enhance consumer 
trust online, foster fairness in platform -to-business relations, and improve security 
and innovation in digital services.  As the Commission approaches review of the Digital 
Omnibus, we would like to reiterate the importance of adhering to a few high -level 
principles for governance of the digital ecosystem, namely: 
1. Proportionality & Flexibility 
2. Technology-Neutrality 
3. Clarity & Legal Certainty 
4. Public-Private Coordinated Enforcement 
In addition to these General Principles, in the following paper we provide some 
additional input on: 
1. Ensuring Better Cybersecurity Collaboration 
2. Advancing Trustworthy AI Through Proportionate Regulation 
3. Building Trust and Usability into Cross-Border Digital Identity 
4. Harmonizing ePrivacy Rules for Smarter, Safer Communications 
5. Enabling Responsible Data Sharing 
General Principles 
1. Proportionality & Flexibility  
Regulatory obligations should be proportionate to risks and scale of operations, 
carefully calibrated to minimize unintended burdens on microbusinesses, 
fintech innovators, or nascent technologies. 
2. Technology-Neutrality 
Rules should remain technology -agnostic wherever possible, avoiding overly 
prescriptive requirements that could constrain future developments and 
innovation. 
3. Clarity & Legal Certainty  
       Ensure that rules are clear, future -proof, and supported by straight  forward 
reporting and enforcement mechanisms, enabling consistent and efficient 
application across borders within the digital ecosystem. 
4. Public-Private Coordinated Enforcement  
Effective cooperation between European authorities and the private sector is 
critical. Interoperability, data portability, and simplified access to the EU 
market require strong coordination and mutual trust. Close collaboration on AI,

1. Ensuring better Cybersecurity Collaboration  
The scale and sophistication of cyber threats are growing rapidly. For payment and 
digital identity systems, robust cybersecurity is essential to maintaining consumer 
trust. Fragmentation across Member States introduces avoidable costs and heightens 
vulnerabilities. 
 
More specifically, the complexity of reporting obligations across NIS2, the GDPR, SIPS, 
PSD2, DORA, nation specific agreements, and  more diverts resources from incident 
response to compliance paperwork, weakening the EU’s cyber resilience and 
competitiveness at large. A harmonized approach is essential to streamline 
cybersecurity incident reporting, ensuring swift action whilst reducing administrative 
overhead. We would encourage that this approach follows the endorsed principle of 
once-only reporting for entities, who would report to a single designated authority to 
ensure that national competent authorities remain the primary recipients of incident 
reports, while ENISA coordinates and supports harmonization and analysis at the EU 
level. Such an approach should also ensure that definitions are aligned across 
legislations and Member States, as well as harmonize reporting criteria, timelines, 
data fields and templates as much as possible.  
 
While the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) and the Cyber Resilience Act 
(CRA) were not explicitly mentioned in the Call for Evidence, these legislative 
frameworks contribute to the broader framework on cybersecurity in the EU. We have 
also identified areas in these frameworks that may create unnecessary burdens and 
duplications. Under DORA, for example, systemically important payment systems 
(SIPS) should remain out of scope of the direct applicability of DORA, as they  are 
already regulated under existing frameworks and subject to cyber risk and operational 
resilience requirements under it SIPS designation , in particular Article 21 of SIPS 
Regulation on Cyber Risk and the  Cyber Resilience Oversight Expectations 
framework. For the same reason,  Core processing services should not be considered 
ICT services under DORA as they are already covered by the same requirements on 
operational resilience and cyber risk management under the SIPS oversight. 
 
In regard to CRA, we would like to avoid duplication and confirm compliance with the 
rules using the existing evidence for Smart Payment Cards. We see a risk that the 
implementation of the CRA may impose substantial costs without providing 
corresponding improvements in end -user security for Smart Payment Cards. The 
current payments industry security practices have proven successful in minimizing 
fraud rates over the past three decades to as low as 0.031% of the total value of card 
payments in the EU. The in troduction of a new process via the CRA could potentially 
lead to certification duplication, complicate planning, and potentially cause delays in 
the delivery of new Payment Cards. This redundancy may increase complexity, 
contradicting the goal of simplifying security measures for the end users. An ideal 
solution would be for the CRA to provide a mechanism to confirm compliance with the 
existing industry practices that have proven successful.  
 
Proposals: 
• Implement a harmonized approach for reporting obligations across all 
cybersecurity legislation that applies once-only reporting principle to a single 
entity, keeps definitions of incidents aligned across legislation, and harmonizes 
reporting criteria, timelines, data fields and templates as much as possible.  
• Avoid duplication and respect existing legislative frameworks by leaving SIPS 
and Core processing services out of the scope of DORA, both directly and 
indirectly as an ICT service, and by providing a mechanism under CRA to 
confirm compliance with existing industry practices that have proven to be 
successful, such as for Smart Payment Cards. 
• Promote public-private partnerships for real-time threat intelligence sharing. 
• Encourage outcome -based rather than prescriptive requirements to allow 
innovation while maintaining high security. 
• Ensure EU certification focuses on meaningful improvements, with 
transparent, multistakeholder processes.   Any new frameworks should take 
into account existing rules and standards to avoid duplications and 
unnecessary administrative burden.  
• Conduct thorough impact assessments before introducing new requirements. 
• Strengthen and resource the EU Cybersecurity Agency to ensure consistent 
implementation. 
 
2. Advancing Trustworthy AI Through Proportionate Regulation  
AI is central to fraud detection, risk management, and innovation in payments. 
Mastercard supports the EU’s goal of building trust in AI, while requirements are 
calibrated to avoid unintended constraints on beneficial , low risk  use cases.  
Unfortunately, there appear to be areas of friction in the interplay between the EU AI 
Act and existing legislation, such as GDPR.  In particular, Article 10(5) of the EU AI Act 
provides an exception to Article 9 GDPR by establishing a legal basis for processing 
special categories of personal data for the purpose of ensuring bias detection and 
correction in relation to “high-risk” AI systems. Further clarification on the scope and 
conditions for this exception to apply would be welcome. We believe that a legal gap 
remains for bias testing of AI systems that are not considered as “high-risk” under the 
AI Act. There is legal uncertainty as to which legal basis companies can rely on to 
process special category data for bias testing in non -high-risk AI systems, such as 
systems used for fraud detection and prevention. 
 
Furthermore, the legal basis for processing personal data to train AI models remains 
a critical issue for developers as there is significant legal uncertainty around this issue. 
In the case of using AI for fraud prevention, consent is not optimal. In fact, under 
GDPR, fraud prevention is explicitly stated as a legitimate interest for processing 
personal data. As such, data controllers processing personal data to train AI for fraud 
prevention purposes should also be allowed to rely on legitimate interest as a legal 
basis.  
 
The enforcement landscape under the AI Act is also quite complex, as over 200 
competent authorities are expected to be designated. This is likely to result in 
fragmentation and uncertainty for companies in their compliance efforts. We see this 
issue particularly evident in the reporting of serious incidents, where businesses must 
navigate coordination across different jurisdictions and different legal frameworks (AI 
Act, GDPR, NIS2, etc.), each potentially applying different templates and procedural 
approaches. We would strongly advocate for the designation of a single regulatory 
point of contact per Member State to streamline interactions between businesses and 
regulators.  
 
Proposals: 
• Allow controllers processing personal data to train AI to rely on all available 
legal bases under the GDPR, including legitimate interest for fraud prevention.  
• Provide clarity on the scope and conditions of the legal basis for processing 
special categories of personal data for the purpose of ensuring bias detection 
and correction for “high -risk” AI systems under Article 10(5) of the AI Act , as 
well as what legal basis is available for bias testing for non-high-risk use cases. 
• Designate a single regulatory point of contact per Member State to streamline 
interactions between businesses and regulators. 
 
3. Building Trust and Usability into Cross-Border Digital Identity  
Digital identity and trust services form the cornerstone of a secure and inclusive digital 
economy. The development of the European Digital Identity and the proposed 
European Business Wallet (EBW) should place usability, interoperability, and trust at 
the heart of their design and implementation. 
From our perspective, the EBW could be particularly useful in helping financial 
institutions better onboard their business customers and conduct the necessary Know 
Your Business procedures and AML compliance. We understand that onboarding in 
particular SMEs can still be a very cumbersome and time-consuming exercise, leading 
to many inefficiencies. An EU -wide business identity wallet could facilitate those 
procedures and reduce cost and friction. Further, enhanced KYB procedures could help 
reducing fraud and  scams by ensuring an earlier identification of fraudulent 
merchants. So, when developing the EBW, it is important to thoroughly assess liability 
implication and ensure those rules are clear and aligned with existing frameworks, as 
a lack of clarity on this point has been a concern in the implementation of eIDAS2. The 
interaction between the EBW and eIDAS2 should also be made explicitly clear. The 
EBW would also benefit from relying on existing industry standards to be make 
solutions more easily compatible and scalable.  
 
SCA delegation and outsourcing 
In the payments ecosystem, there exist  a broad range of authentication and 
authorization solutions which do not constitute outsourcing —for example, 
authentication services provided by card schemes and wallet providers, and 
authorization solutions provided by schemes. Treating these solutions as outsourcing 
as currently proposed by the EC as part of the revision of the PSD2 would significantly 
restrict PSPs’ ability to make use of them as card issuers would need to conclude 
dozens, if not hundreds, of outsourcing agreements with all possible pro viders of 
authentication and authorization solutions. Such an interpretation would also 
undermine the roll -out of the European Business Wallet (EBW) and the EU Digital 
Identity Wallet (EUDIW) introduced by Regulation (EU) 2024/1183 (the “eIDAS2 
Regulation”). In order for the EBW and EUDIW to be used for the authentication of 
payments, PSPs would be required to enter into outsourcing agreements with each 
EBW/EUDIW provider, creating unnecessary friction and regulatory burden and 
potentially delaying consumer adoption of these strategic EU initiatives. 
 
Proposals: 
• Establish clear liability rules for EBW, aligned with existing frameworks, to 
avoid legal uncertainty. 
• Ensure coherence with eIDAS2 and avoid duplicating business identity 
provisions. 
• Enable linkage between individual and business wallets (e.g., payments 
authorization, contract signing). 
• Leverage industry standards and open APIs to promote interoperability and 
scalability. 
• Allow design flexibility , including for payments,  so private sector actors can 
build innovative, value-added services on top of EBW infrastructure. 
• Promote global interoperability of business identity to reflect cross -border 
operations. 
• Apply the highest cybersecurity standards, given the sensitivity of stored 
credentials. 
• Ensure harmonized implementation of eIDAS2 across Member States. 
• Ensure harmonized simplification revision of the PSD2, in particular on the 
treatment of SCA delegation and outsourcing 
• Facilitate adoption by SMEs through simplified onboarding and integration 
support. 
 
4. Harmonizing ePrivacy Rules for Smarter, Safer Communications  
The European Commission’s decision to withdraw the long -stalled ePrivacy 
Regulation proposal appears justified, given that negotiations had been deadlocked 
for several years and the text no longer reflected the realities of today’s digital 
communications landscape.  
In the meantime, the existing ePrivacy  Directive has allowed Member States wide 
discretion in implementation, resulting in divergent national rules —particularly 
regarding direct marketing to individual business email addresses. This fragmentation 
has created legal uncertainty and increased com pliance burdens for cross -border 
business communications. Any future legislative initiative aimed at harmonizing these 
rules should ensure a clear and practical framework, including a straightforward opt-
out mechanism for B2B marketing, proportionate conse nt requirements, and 
coverage of emerging communication channels not explicitly addressed under the 
current Directive.  
 
Proposals: 
• Ensure that ePrivacy  rules are applied consistently across Member States, 
addressing national discrepancies and divergent interpretations—such as in the 
case of B2B marketing—while also adapting to today’s digital communications 
landscape by covering emerging channels not exp licitly addressed under the 
current rules. 
• Ensure that ePrivacy rules are aligned with the relevant financial sector 
regulation, including legal obligations to prevent fraud , and that they 
accurately classify payment service providers (PSPs) as end users of electronic 
communication network, not providers.   
 
5. Enabling Responsible Data Sharing 
The EU Data Act introduces a new horizontal framework for data access and use, with 
significant implications for payment services and digital infrastructure. As supervisory 
authorities take on expanded roles in overseeing non -personal data, clarity on 
governance structures and coordination mechanisms becomes essential—particularly 
in complex ecosystems like payments, where data flows span jurisdictions and involve 
mixed datasets. 
 
Operationalizing the Data Act requires clearer guidance on how its provisions interact 
with existing frameworks such as the GDPR, including how obligations apply to 
products and services that combine personal and non -personal data. For example, 
further clarification is needed on whether and which paym ent tools or services are 
excluded from the Data Act’s scope in line with the ECB’s 2022 guidance. 
Proposals: 
• Provide practical examples of how Data Act obligations could be applied in 
practice—such as in relation to data sharing, access rights, and transfers— and 
help clarify which types of products and services fall within scope. Additional 
guidance on how these obligations apply where products involve both personal 
and non -personal data, and how this aligns with existing regulatory 
interpretations (e.g., from the ECB), would further support consistent 
implementation. 
• Clarify the role of supervisory authorities   under the Data Act and better 
explain the governance structures, coordination mechanisms, and the 
interaction the existing data protection authorities , especially in scenarios 
involving mixed datasets. 
• Provide clear and practical guidance on how the Data Act interacts with the 
GDPR, particularly on: 
o How to reconcile GDPR principles with the data sharing obligations 
introduced under the Data Act;  
o Governance structures that can help prevent conflicting instructions or 
duplicative compliance burdens; 
o How to handle mixed data sets for international data flows; and  
o Sector-specific examples on how to handle GDPR obligations while 
fulfilling Data Act requirements. 
 
Conclusion 
Mastercard supports the objectives of the Digital Omnibus package to simplify rules, 
reduce duplication, and strengthen the EU Digital Single Market. A regulatory 
framework built on proportionality, flexibility, and cooperation will protect consumers 
and businesses while enabling innovation and global competitiveness. 
 
At the same time, it is important to take into account the varying levels of 
development of digital payments infrastructure across EU Member States. Common 
rules should be both practical and adaptable to national conditions to ensure equal 
access to digital opportunities and fair growth of the ecosystem. 
 
We stand ready to work with the European Commission, Member States, and other 
stakeholders to help deliver a balanced and future -proof digital regulatory 
environment for Europe.

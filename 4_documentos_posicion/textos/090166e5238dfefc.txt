As the leading trade body for digital advertising and marketing in France, Alliance Digitale respectfully recommends to the Commission to: 1.  Alliance Digitale welcomes the European Commission’s ambitious simplification agenda for the EU rulebook.  We strongly support those efforts to lower administrative costs for businesses while maintaining a high level of trust and protection in the online environment.  As a contribution to this discussion, we respectfully recommend to the Commission that this agenda should aim at  (1) taking a broader approach to include GDPR-related simplification measures,  (2) trading the current Article 5(3) binary interpretation for a risk-based approach, (3) streamlining existing rules to unlock AI innovation,  (4) reducing ePD and GDPR implementation fragmentation across Member States, and  (5) abandoning any centralized cookie management mechanism which would only create more risks both for users and businesses.   1. Taking a broader approach to include GDPR-related simplification measures  Context: As “a first step in ‘stress-testing’ our digital rules”, the Digital Package on Simplification will represent a very welcomed and immediate streamlining of existing rules. Among them, those on cookies and other tracking technologies laid down in the ePrivacy Directive (ePD) are mentioned in this Call for Evidence. One of the main issues encountered by the sector in applying those is that they co-exist with the GDPR, leading to interpretations which vary greatly between Member States, and are oftentimes even incoherent. Hence, the future Digital Omnibus should also seek to explore targeted simplification measures linked to the GDPR, especially when connected to privacy-related issues laid down in the ePD. This would provide direct benefits to businesses while maintaining regulatory objectives.  The GDPR implementation has placed a disproportionate burden on small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and startups. Obligations such as maintaining records of processing activities and responding to data subject access requests often require significant financial and human resources, even when these companies do not process sensitive data or when data processing is not their core activity. Furthermore, the absence of clear guidance regarding the scope of data subject rights, combined with legal uncertainty surrounding identity verification procedures, exposes companies to compliance risks while offering limited added value to individuals. Alliance Digitale therefore calls for a more proportionate application of GDPR obligations and the establishment of clear, standardised rules that both protect fundamental rights and support the competitiveness of the European digital economy. Trading the current Article 5(3) binary interpretation for a risk-based approach  Context: Implementation of Article 5(3) of the ePD poses great difficulties to companies, has allowed for changing interpretations, and contributes to consent fatigue while reducing the industry’s innovation capacities.  Article 5(3) of the ePD establishes a one-size-fits-all consent requirement for a vast range of technologies. Unlike the GDPR’s sophisticated, risk-based framework, this binary approach does not differentiate based on context, technology, or potential for harm. This creates several unintended consequences that undermine our shared policy goals: • It devalues consent: By requiring consent for nearly every interaction, from essential security functions to routine analytics, the current framework inadvertently turns a meaningful choice into a routine, low-engagement task for users. This over-reliance on a single mechanism erodes its value for the high-risk data processing where explicit user control is most critical; • It deters privacy innovation: The current rules provide no incentive for investment in and adoption of privacy-preserving technologies. A service deploying state-of-the-art, privacy-preserving APIs faces the exact same rigid consent requirement as one using older, more intrusive methods. This lack of regulatory distinction creates a chilling effect on innovation and discourages the very technological solutions that can better protect user data; • It creates legal uncertainty: The coexistence of two different legal regimes for data processing—one context-aware (GDPR) and one binary (ePrivacy)—results in fragmented interpretations across Member States, creating legal uncertainty for businesses and inconsistent protections for users.  A future-proof approach would align the rules for electronic communications data with the risk-based principles of the GDPR, creating a single, coherent, and predictable rulebook for all data processing.

 Alternatively, a significant step toward simplification would be to establish a clear "whitelist" or “allowlist” of specific, low-risk, and pro-user processing activities that do not require user consent under Article 5(3). This would include essential functions such as first-party audience measurement, frequency capping (to prevent users from seeing the same ad repeatedly), and anti-fraud measures. These are not secondary to the user experience; they are essential to the functioning of a safe and sustainable digital ecosystem. Recalibrating the framework to permit such low-risk activities to be pursued on the legal basis—an approach being considered at the moment in the United Kingdom—would reduce friction for users, restore the significance of consent for high-risk processing, and create positive incentives for privacy innovation. A more flexible approach by regulators should be pursued in this regard. It would also help incentivizing the development and use of privacy enhancing technologies (PETs) by allowing the processing of those under the legitimate interest basis.  Meanwhile, another option worth considering would be to use the approach adopted by the French DPA, the CNIL, to some analytic trackers. The CNIL allows for a possibility for certain analytics trackers to be exempted from traditional consent, subject to users being informed about their use and having capacity to object to such use. Only analytic trackers that meet criteria listed by the CNIL are considered by this approach.  The CNIL approach can be analyzed as follows: • Analytic trackers may have different impact on data subjects, some are less intrusive than other (risk-based approach); • When processing of data by analytic trackers is sufficiently inoffensive to data subjects, these trackers may benefit from the exemption of consent as this term is currently interpreted for use of trackers and consent-based processing; • Criteria that need to be met by analytic trackers to be considered as benefiting for consent exemption are defined by the CNIL (“whitelist” approach); • Use of such analytic trackers is not free of any constraints: a data subject needs to be informed and have a possibility to oppose to their use.  The approach adopted by the CNIL could also be applied to low tracking activities. When processing undertaken through the use of trackers represents low risk to data subjects but might not meet the Article 5(3) exemption criteria, the criteria used to determine whether consent validity conditions are met could be modified. Consent would be given if an end-user is informed about the trackers, their purposes, has a possibility to refuse and still continues using a service despite full disclosure. The GDPR consent validity criteria would still be met (consent needs to be freely given, be specific, informed and unambiguous) however the standards applied to verification of when these mandatory criteria are met would vary depending on the risks represented by trackers.

Applying gradual risk-based approach to appreciation of validity of consent corresponds to the GDPR risk-based approach. Modification of the interpretation of presence of consent criteria is an existing practice and represents universally accepted capacity of adapting the interpretation of law to the evolution of society. It is also a long-standing principle that actions can constitute consent as long as it is not difficult for users to distinguish that they constitute consent1.   Such risk-based approach to the interpretation of condition of presence of mandatory consent validity criteria could be introduced even before implementation of any changes of applicable laws (i.e. our proposal to delete Art. 5(3) of the ePD with continuous applicability of the GDPR risk-based approach of various possible legal basis). The innovative and equitable approach implemented by the CNIL could be used to address, in a rapid and efficient way, the obvious shortcoming of the current Article 5(3). Reducing ePD and GDPR implementation fragmentation across Member States  Context: The enforcement of EU digital rules, particularly the ePD and the GDPR, differs greatly from one Member State to another. This creates significant fragmentation and complicates compliance for businesses operating across the single market. The situation is exacerbated when national authorities responsible for the GDPR also oversee privacy-related topics under the ePD, leading to inconsistent interpretations of core concepts and legal principles.   In France, the CNIL’s dual competence over both GDPR and e-Privacy matters, combined with its public communication practices in enforcement procedures, generates regulatory instability and reputational risks for market actors.  Strengthening the role of the EDPB as an independent European authority, and ensuring the confidentiality of advisory exchanges with data protection authorities are essential to foster legal certainty and regulatory coherence. Furthermore, a return to a risk-based regulatory approach is necessary to avoid over-cautious practices that impede legitimate data processing, including international data transfers and the use of non-sensitive inferred data. Abandoning any centralized cookie management mechanism which would only create more risks both for users and businesses  Context: Centrally managed cookie preference mechanisms have long been proposed by the European Commission as a solution to reduce ‘consent fatigue’ experienced by users, while always proven to be short of addressing such concerns. Having to consent to an increasing number of data treatments is not a result of a strategy business pursued willingly. It is the consequence of the EDPB and national DPAs’ continuous push for consent as the paramount solution to strengthen users’ autonomy, especially by taking the ePD as their underlying reasoning instead of the GDPR and its risk-based approach. As laid down in the above section, simplification measures exist to address this concern.  Another option worth considering is to explicitly encourage and facilitate cross-domain consent. This would reduce how frequently users are requested to consent for certain data processings, provided that such domains share characteristics (e.g. being part of the same media group).  Furthermore, even where a GDPR-valid consent would be obtained, and thus would constitute the sole legal basis covering the relevant processing, a centralized cookie management would present numerous risks. For users, it would not allow for expressing granular and informed consent when navigating different types of digital services and websites – as well as refusing in the same conditions, as set out in the GDPR. For the media, this could put in jeopardy their business models with low consent rates. For other websites’ editors, it would mean giving up more market power to services which would allow for such centralization (e.g. browsers) in an already concentrated market.

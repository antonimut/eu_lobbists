Digital Simplification Package 
Schneider Electric position 
 
 
Schneider Electric welcomes the European Commission ’s willingness to simplify the 
European digital regulatory acquis, which has been complexified by overlapping 
legislations and standards to the detriment of an effective implementation and the 
development of a trusted environment.  
 
Targeted simplification will bring enhanced  regulatory stability  by addressing the most 
significant hurdles to European competitiveness . This s implification package should 
therefore not drastically modify the regulations currently or soon to be implemented. An 
oversimplification of the rules  could risk undermining legal certainty in the EU.   
 
Schneider Electric  believes simplification should benefit all stakeholders , independent 
of their size. We operate in an ecosystem. A differentiated approach to simplification would 
lead to ineffectiveness and could make it impossible for larger stakeholders to meet their 
own obligations .

In detail: Schneider Electric key recommendations  
I. Ensure transversal simplification through key principles 
 
1. Scope: define a clear scope and key terms 
 
Clearly defining the scope  and key terms of a regulation  is fundamental . Currently, 
essential concepts are too often not precisely defined or remain unclear. For instance:  
➢ The broad definition of the data in scope of the Data Act  poses significant 
interpretation challenges , especially considering the variety of sectors in scope .  
➢ The distinction between “ data processing services ” and “ related services ” is 
not always clear, particularly for hybrid or vertical solutions . N either is it clear 
whether a single service can belong to both categories . Practical examples on 
how to distinguish between the two would be helpful.  
➢ Ambiguity remains around the categorisation of AI systems as “ high-risk” under 
the AI Act . What constitutes a “safety component ” requires more precision. 
Specifically, we encourage the Commission to clarify the exceptions listed in article 
6.3. (“perform a narrow procedural task”, “improve the result of a previously completed 
human activity”, etc.). For this purpose, it should rely on examples from the industry.  
➢ The definition of an “event” needs to be further clarified: it should not be based on 
the notion of “occurrence”, it should not be equated to an “ incident”, and it should 
include all internal measurements when abnormal/out of range (for example IOT 
temperatures measurements) – thus it should not only focus on circumstances.  
 
Sector-specific guidance and  FAQs with examples, developed in collaboration with 
relevant stakeholders  should be the first step to facilitate compliance.  
 
In addition , the Commission should focus on  improving  consistency between 
regulations. Similar but different terms in different regulations hamper compliance , notably: 
➢ “Data processing services ” (Data Act) and “remote data processing solutions ” (CRA). 
➢ “Significant incident ” (NIS2) and “severe incident ” (CRA), “serious incident ” (AI Act).  
 
2. Applicability: publish important deliverables 12 months before applicability 
 
Secondary acts and important guidance should be published at least 12 months  
before the applicability of the related requirements. This has not always been the case. 
Guidelines on the definition of AI systems and prohibited AI practices were released the 
same week the corresponding AI Act requirements became applicable. The Commission 
should commit to strict delivery timelines for guidance on the concept of “ substantial 
modification”  under the CRA  and on the risk assessment under the AI Act.  
 
Most importantly, because standards  underpin a product’s ability to be placed on the EU 
market, their timely delivery is essential for manufacturers to adapt their processes. The 
delayed publication of RED DA standards created challenges that persist today.  This 
situation should  not be replicated for CRA standards.  Tight timelines and unpredictability 
are not conducive to a trusted business environment.  The availability of harmonised 
standards at least 12 months  in advance  should be a precondition to the entry into 
application o f any New Legislative Framework legislation , such as the CRA . 
Experience has shown that a few months are not enough neither for entities in scope, nor 
for notified bodies.

3. Reporting: streamline reporting with one platform, one format, one 
language 
 
In the current EU regulatory landscape,  cybersecurity incident reporting is 
fragmented and burdensome , especially when a single incident falls under multiple 
regulations. Under this complex framework,  CISOs and DPOs must report the same incident 
to multiple authorities  – up to four per country  – in each impacted country, each report in its 
own language and format. This creates inefficiencies, diver ts resources from incident 
response, and increases the  risk of non -compliance. Avoiding unnecessary redundancies  
within ecosystems  is also crucial : the same incident should not have to be reported  twice. 
Schneider Electric urges the Commission t o streamline the reporting process, with: 
➢ One definition:  Harmonise the definition of incident (see section I. i.)  and 
threshold for reporting across regulations and Memb er States.  
➢ One format:  Develop a standardi sed European reporting template that allows 
multi-regulation reporting in one submission.  
➢ One language:  Enable reporting in English with secure, automatic translation 
into official EU languages.  
➢ One platform:  Establish a single EU -wide platform for reporting cybersecurity 
incidents under all relevant regulations and re -routing notifications to relevant 
national authorities, building upon ENISA’s future CRA single reporting platform . 
➢ One authority:  Encourage Member States to designate a single  reporting 
authority at national level  (especially under CRA and NIS2) .  
 
4. International standards: leverage existing international standards 
 
Broadly adopted i nternational cybersecurity standards and frameworks such as the 
ISO/IEC 2700X series, ISA/IEC 62443, and the NIST Cybersecurity Framework should be 
recognised and leveraged as foundational tools for demonstrating compliance  with 
European regulations like the NIS2 directi ve, the CRA,  the Data Act,  and the GDPR , 
provided they align with European requirements.  
 
Such rationali sation is already happening in practice. In this respect, Schneider Electric 
commends Belgium’s incorporation of the NIST framework and ISO 2700X series into its 
national framework for compliance with the  NIS2 directive (CyFun Framework) , and 
Romania and Ireland’s endorsement of CyFun . The implementation of NIS2 would greatly 
benefit from a unified EU-wide framework grounded in these global standards  allowing 
Member States to add requirements tailored to their national context if necessary. 
 
5. Secondary legislation: avoid overinterpretation in secondary acts and 
guidance  
 
Secondary acts and guidance should remain strictly within the mandate and scope 
defined in the ir primary legislation . For instance, the draft guidance document EG RE 
(19) 21, which addressed the definition of “ safety component”  under the AI Act and proposed 
its harmoni sation with the Radio Equipment directive’s definition , effectively attempted to 
broaden the original scope of “ high-risk AI systems ”. By extending the definition from 
“safety” to include “security”, it risked overstepping the original intent  of the legislator . Such 
overinterpretation  only brings uncertainty and should not be replicated.

II. Clarify inconsistencies and overlaps between legislations 
 
1. Cybersecurity: facilitate the transition from the RED DA to the CRA 
 
Schneider Electric emphasises the importance to facilitate the transition from the  RED DA 
to the CRA.  The RED DA standards were cited only in February 2025, despite the act’s 
entry into application in August 2025. This left insufficient time for stakeholders to prepare, 
leading to bottlenecks at notified bodies and increased compliance costs.  It is essential that 
the Commission capitali se on the lessons learned from the RED DA  experience : 
➢ CRA essential requirements should only become applicable  at least 12 months 
after harmonised standards are published in the OJEU .  
➢ In case the  standards are not ready by December 202 6, we encourage the 
Commission to postpo ne the applicability of the requirements in order to 
preserve the possibility for stakeholders to fully benefit from all conformity 
assessment options  available under the CRA (third -party certification or self -
assessment).  
➢ Products deemed  compliant with  the RED DA  should not be  required to 
demonstrate compliance again for equivalent CRA requirements. Schneider 
Electric welcomes the  decision to repeal the RED DA  once the CRA becomes fully 
applicable. This will avoid overlapping obligations and redundant certifications . 
 
Attention should also be paid to the CRA’s interplay and potential overlaps with the 
revised Machinery directive  which will apply from January 2027. Consistency  and non-
duplication between regulations is fundamental.  
 
2. Cybersecurity: review the purpose of European certification schemes 
 
The purpose of certification schemes under the Cybersecurity Act  (CSA) should be clarified:  
➢ With the growing number of cybersecurity requirements  in the EU , the need for 
additional voluntary certification schemes , in a context of simplification , 
should be thoroughly reassessed . When defining the schemes to develop in 
priority, the European Commission should ensure they would be aligned with market 
needs and would effectively support compliance with  EU regulation.   
➢ If judged necessary, it is crucial that European certification schemes are based 
on existing international standards . This would greatly facilitate their adoption and 
ensure they bring added value .   
➢ Schneider Electric encourages the Commission to reaffirm the voluntary 
nature of European certification  schemes. It is presently unclear  whether such 
schemes could evolve into mandatory tools , as is currently expected under NIS2 and 
the CRA. Making their use mandatory would incur unreasonable costs on business 
and would be particularly redundant for global companies which are already subject 
to extensive legal cybersecurity requirements and invest heavily in strengthening 
their resilience. Member States should limit as much as possible the scope of ICT 
products, services, and processes subject to mandatory  certification under NIS2 , 
based on the risks they pose .  
 
3. Cybersecurity: harmonise NIS2’s implementation across Member States  
 
On top of the much -delayed transposition process , in its current form,  the NIS2 directive 
does not effectively create the conditions for enhanced cyber resilience in the EU and 
undermines the EU’s competitiveness . Imposing a flurry of diverging obligations  and 
redundant processes  does not serve the initial goal of ramping up cybersecurity.

Compliance with NIS2 across 27 Member States is especially challenging for 
companies  operating in multiple jurisdictions . Monitoring the transposition  process, 
registering entities and defining applications in scope  in each country, mapping national risk 
management frameworks , tracking audit obligations  etc. are extremely time -consuming 
tasks that divert significant resources from cybersecurity measures  deployment . Certain 
requirements are not adapted to the cross -border nature of cybersecurity management in 
international companies. For instance, the appointment of local cybersecurity manager s 
proves difficult in practice , considering  that these skills are scarce, and such tasks are often 
performed at the regional level .  
 
This complexity also creates obstacles for smaller companies to scale up  in the EU. 
Schneider Electric calls for more pragmatism in NIS2’s implementation. The Commission 
should introduce prioritisation in the NIS2 requirements , so that entities can identify the 
most critical ones and quickly reach a base level of cybersecurity. Such a pragmatic 
approach would allow incremental but consequential progress across entire ecosystems 
without overwhelming smaller actors.  
 
The Commission and the Member States, via the NIS Cooperation Group,  should rationali se 
the national implementation of NIS2  through: 
➢ The development of an EU -wide NIS2 risk management framework grounded 
in existing international standards  (see section I. iv.) . 
➢ The streamlining of reporting obligations  (see section I. iii.): the Commission 
should press Member States to  align on the threshold and the timeline to report an 
incident. National variations are counterproductive, and sometimes unrealistic.  
➢ The centralisation of compliance in a single country  for global entities  (as is  
already the case under the main establishment rule for certain categories of entities) : 
entities operating in multiple jurisdictions  should be able  to leverage their size  to 
drive compliance and achieve higher maturity . 
➢ The establishment of a n easy -to-access tracker which would also monitor  
secondary legislation  at national level .  
➢ The recognition of NIS2 audits across Member States : many applications and 
processes are global, thus their certification  in multiple countries is inefficient and  
unwarranted from a cybersecurity point of view. The Commission should require 
Member States to accept audits conducted under another Member’s transposition.  
 
To prevent these difficulties  in the future, NIS3 should be a regulation, not a directive .  
 
4. Data protection: conduct targeted GDPR simplification  
 
Schneider Electric strongly supports the principles of the General Data Protection 
Regulation (GDPR) and is committed to maintaining high standards in personal data 
protection. We believe that reopening the GDPR could introduce significant risks and 
undermine legal certainty. Simplification efforts should thus focus on very specific and 
targeted amendments , as well as on the ecosystem surrounding the GDPR, such as:  
➢ Streamlining cybersecurity and personal data breach reporting requirements  
(see section I. iii.), especially with new regulations like the CRA and the AI Act.  
➢ Publishing clearer guidance on risk -based assessments  for data controllers on 
when to notify Data Protection Authorities and data subjects.  
➢ Harmonising model contractual clauses across jurisdictions  such as the EU, 
the UK, and Switzerland, and updating guidance on impact assessments for 
third-party data transfers . Additionally, the identification of high -risk data

5. Artificial Intelligence: support the AI Act’s implementation 
 
Schneider Electric is committed to leading responsible industrial AI take-up under the EU 
AI Act. We believe a few modifications of the Act would ensure effective implementation and 
help maintain a healthy balance between innovation and regulation :  
➢ Attention should be paid to  the AI Act ’s interaction with other regulation s, such 
as the CRA, notably regarding  robustness and  incident reporting (see section I. iii.). 
➢ Schneider Electric strongly supports the risk -based approach adopted by the AI Act. 
However, we believe the European Commission should introduce more flexibility in 
its implementation . Indeed, the AI Act should leave more room for exceptions 
when they can be duly justified and when they can bring added value without 
increasing risks . A hypothetical example would be the introduction of an algorithm 
based on AI technology to detect arc faults and differentiate between dangerous arcs 
and false positives . Such application  would create a safer world by reducing fires 
and preventing users from removing devices due to false positives. However, it may 
not comply with  all the logging and supervision applicable to high -risk systems under 
the AI Act. Many similar examples could be gathered from other sectors. We 
encourage the Commission to engage with industry to identify use cases  of high-
risk AI systems which reduce the overall risk level  and could thus constitute 
exceptions. This would boost AI deployment and European competitiveness .  
➢ It is also essential that risks are assessed at system level, and not only at 
component level . AI systems are often used as component s in products . 
Safeguards can be introduced in a product to mitigate the risks posed by an AI 
system and make the final product secure.   
➢ Market surveillance bodies should be  staffed with skilled  and mature 
regulators with industry and product/system level  expertise covering the whole 
lifecycle of a high -risk AI s ystems (including a practical understanding of complete 
product architecture (AI and non -AI interactions),  design and development, as well 
as prelaunch phases ). The AI Office should introduce an EU-wide certification 
program to train authorities and stakeholders on the technical and procedural 
aspects of the AI Act, to foster  consistent compliance across the Union.  The 
Commission should also support initiatives to strengthen the maturity of all actors – 
from management, to marketing, quality, sales, and support – inside organi sations.

Call for Evidence on the Digital Omnibus 
noyb, The European Center for Digital Rights, is a non -profit organisation enforcing consumers' 
rights in cases concerning the right to personal data protection and privacy.  
 
1. General Remarks 
As a general observation noyb would like to underline that experience is still lacking in relation to 
the implementation, its application, enforcement and effectiveness of big parts of the digital aquis.  
The Commission’s simplification agenda sh ouldn’t lead to rushed proposals without proper 
consultation, evaluation and impact assessments. Rushed changes can easily lead to reduction of 
protection and further legal uncertainty – for business and consumers.  
The Digital Omnibus which is by definition a fast-track regulatory tool with less time for 
institutional deliberations does not seem to be the right path for amending provisions with a huge 
impact on the protection of fundamental rights such as the right to privacy and confidentiality.   
If, however, the Commission proposes changes, simplification must not be designed as a one-way 
street for business but should be pursued to benefit society as a whole and promote fundamental 
rights (including “simplification” for citizens). We make proposals below how to achieve this.  
 
2. Article 5(3) ePrivacy 
The Commission’s call for evidence for the Digital Omnibus mentions that the Commission plans 
to introduce changes to the rules on Article 5 (3) e-Privacy directive. Our comments below focus 
on this topic. 
2.1. Factual background on the status quo 
 
2.1.1. “Cookie fatigue” is mainly triggered by intentionally bad design (“dark patterns”) 
Identifying the real causes behind the symptom of the “cookie fatigue” should be an important 
objective for the call for evidence.  
In our work on cookie banners over the last years, it quickly became apparent that a root cause of 
“cookie fatigue” is the extensive use of dark patterns by controllers  – more to be found online 
under the industry euphemism “consent optimization”. 
In short, UI and UX design is used to bother Europeans into clicking “accept” with any trick in the 
book, even if depending on the study only 0.16% to 3%1 of Europeans want to be tracked online.

2.1.2. The lack of enforcement on “dark patterns” lead to wholly artificial consent rates 
Using “dark patterns” consent rates can be driven up to 50%, 70% or even 90% despite the GDPR 
requiring “fair” processing in Article 5(1)(a) and consent must be “genuine” and “freely given” 
under Article 4(11). The widespread use of dark patterns has led to wholly artificial consent rates 
of up to 90%2 the numbers of the genuine wishes of Europeans.  
In other areas of consumer law – let alone fundamental rights – it would be unacceptable if the 
majority of consumers would be forced into a legal declaration that is not their actual choice. Only 
when it comes to digital rights, such tactics seem to be accepted – and users or EU law is blamed 
under the narrative of “consent fatigue” or “disinterest” in protection oneself more. 
Furthermore, many national supervisory authorities across the EU do not enforce  baseline 
standards like having a “reject” option on the first page or having non-deceptive design. 
2.1.3. Consent is the legal basis for data flows from EU websites to Big Tech players 
While consent rates are usually debated by industry players under the narrative of “news media” 
(a tiny fraction of the internet ecosystem), the lack of meaningful data protection has in fact lead 
to massive benefits and therefore profit gains for Big Tech  – to the expense of traditional media 
that has largely lost advertisement revenue.  
The example of the US shows best, that with no regulation of personal data, traditional media lost 
almost all revenue, while the profits of Big Tech companies reach new highs every quarter:

Even on news pages, consent banners are the legal footing to allow data flows from news pages to 
the much larger advertisement partners – usually Google and alike. The consent banner on most 
websites do not give consent to advertisement of that website, but to allow third parties to profit 
from the website – while only giving a fraction of the revenue to the website publisher.

2.2. Legal background on the status quo 
 
2.2.1. Basis of Article 5(3) in Article 7 of the Charter 
Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive is a cornerstone of implementing Article 7 of the Charter in 
the field of telecommunications (see Recital 3 of Directive 2002/58/EC).  
While Article 7 and 8 of the Charter have largely overlapping applications, the scope and depth of 
protection is different, as Article 7 , for example , covers legal persons and interference with 
communication is typically subject to much higher protections than “common” personal data. 
Article 7 of the Charter protects people against arbitrary or disproportionate surveillance. Any 
changes to these rules and particularly a potential opening the legal basis Article 5(3) to the six 
legal bases in Article 6(1) GDPR could entail serious negative consequences on the fundamental 
right to privacy in communication. Such changes could even be subject to challenges before the 
CJEU, if the rights under Article 7 of the Charter get seriously limited for purposes that are not in 
the (immanent) public interest or beyond what is proportionate under Article 52 of the Charter. 
2.2.2. Combined GDPR and ePrivacy consent banners 
While the public debate largely focuses on “cookie banners”, in fact most banners collect consent 
for setting and reading cookies under Article 5(3) ePrivacy, but also other consents under 
Article 6(1)(a) GDPR for the processing of personal data for other purposes. Such banners usually 
also implement the right to withdrawal consent and even objections under Article 21 GDPR. 
To solve the “consent fatigue” problem, it must therefore be highlighted, that any solution must 
include consent, withdrawal and objections under Article 5(3), 6(1)(a), 7 and 21 GDPR. 
 
2.3. Suggested Path Forward 
Based on the outlined background, we suggest the following path forward: 
2.3.1. Merging Article 5(3) with the GDPR or not? 
We note that there is a discussion about moving Article 5(3) to the GDPR and thereby largely 
eliminate the lex specialis status.  
We note the Article 5(3 ) deliberately went beyond the baseline guarantees of the GDPR  and 
Directive 95/46 at the time , based on an understanding that communication data is especially 
protected under Article 7 of the Charter and e.g. also covers legal persons. 
Such a migration into the GDPR would typically also change the personal and material scope  of 
the provision and suddenly fall under the “market approach” of Article 3 (2) GDPR, which has  
large extraterritorial effects, especially when it concerns the “monitoring of behaviour”. 
Furthermore, such a change would in many jurisdictions also result in a change of the complaints 
process, enforcement and responsible authorities. In many jurisdictions the responsibility would 
shift from Tele communication Authorities to the GDPR Supervisory Authorities – who already 
suffer from very limited resources and extremely low enforcement rates. Equally the complaints 
procedure under Article 77 GDPR and the cooperation procedure under Article 56 and 60 to 66 
GDPR would then apply to “cookie cases” throughout the EU. 
While noyb would welcome some of these effects, we note that it must be given sufficient thought.

2.3.2. Keeping the clear “whitelist & consent” approach  
We note the re is a  push to open up all legal basis under Article 6(1)(a) GDPR to the storage or 
access to information stored in the terminal equipment. This would be a sweeping change that 
would massively go beyond the mere “cookie” matter , given that many more scenarios exist to 
“store” and “access” data on terminal equipment.  
If Article 5(3) ePrivacy would be merged with Article 6(1) GDPR, and given that a number of 
companies seem to argue that also merely seeking profits can be a “legitimate interest”  
(Article 6(1)(f) GDPR). For example, the following types of exploitations of terminal equipment s 
could be argued to be e.g. a “legitimate interest” by more questionable players: 
- Cryptomining in a browser – using the users’ processing power without consent3,4, 
- Access to data in terminal equipment beyond cookies in a browser (e.g. access to smartphone 
data, business hardware, photos, videos, messages), 
- Installation of any kind of software on a terminal equipment which later would allow for 
further access on the device. 
➔ We therefore urge the Commission to keep the current approach  in Article 5(3) ePrivacy -
Directive of having a clear whitelist of allowed operations and otherwise require user consent. 
2.3.3. Limiting the need for consent for unproblematic processing by expanding the 
“white list” in Article 5(3) ePrivacy 
One simple way to limit “consent fatigue” is by increasing the number of purposes that do not need 
consent by the user, because the impact is minimal or consent can be assumed. While identifying 
the areas that users would usually consent to would require qualitative data, noyb would expect 
that purposes previously discussed (see file 2017/0003(COD) , e.g. data security or strictly 
anonymous statistics) would qualify for such an expansion. 
Given that only 32.6%5 of EU businesses reported to have ever engage in online advertisement  
(which may include mere ads on Meta or Google, without own data processing) it can be expected 
that the vast number of businesses would not need a consent banner anymore , if processing 
activities like anonymous statistics or processing for security purposes are allowed by law. 
Limiting the consent requirement only to websites that do process data in a questionable way 
would also increase the “warning” function of a consent request. 
2.3.4. Technical solution for remaining consent requests 
Most consent to processing of personal data is automated (e.g. access to microphones, cameras or 
alike) and therefore uses a neutral interface by the relevant software (e.g. Browser pop -up). 
Browsers also signal many other preferences (e.g. screen size to get a “mobile” view, or the 
language setting to the right language page) automatically in the background. Users would have 
e.g. massive “language choice fatigue” if browsers and servers would not communicate  such 
preferences automatically in the background. 
Surprisingly, GDPR and ePrivacy choices are communicated in an old -fashioned analogue way , 
where thousands of websites ask the exact same question and users have to fill in the same

Article 21(5) GDPR already foresee a legal duty to accept an automated signal  for objections. 
Recital 66 of the ePrivacy Directive 2009/136/EC equally foresee such signals for consent, even if 
the Recital itself is not binding. Also, Article 9 (2) and 10 of Commission Proposal for an ePrivacy 
Regulation (see file 2017/0003(COD)), foresaw such an obvious approach. 
In the US, there was a first attempt to voluntarily implement a “Do Not Track” signal, which was 
sabotaged by industry players. Now California has implemented similar signal called “Global 
Privacy Control” that has come into force last week. 6 So even on the home turf of Big Tech, there 
is now a digital signal to communicate privacy choices. 
We think this Omnibus is a window of opportunity to finally implement Article 21(5) GDPR and 
Recital 66 of ePrivacy Directive and to ensure that the Omnibus includes a crucial simplification 
for businesses and users. 
Given that such a signal would not create new rights for users, it would be sufficient to have two 
provisions:  
 
First, a legal provision (ideally linked the Article 6(1)(a) of the GDPR , which ePrivacy refers to) 
that sets the basic rules for such a signal that would be compatible with EU law, such as: 
• Establishing an open-source standard, allowing open free-market competition of providers on 
the controller side (usually the existing “Consent Management Platforms”) and on the user 
side (usually browsers or third-party plugins) 
• Enabling specific choices, per domain and per purpose to comply with Article 6(1)(a) GDPR, 
but also allow users to “favour” certain trusted companies (e.g. local newspapers), while not 
providing personal data to others (e.g. big tech players, tracking companies). 
• Limiting the regulation merely to opening a new form of communication  (like HTTP header 
signals), while avoiding the US approach of having technically codified choice options (e.g. 
only objections) or technically codified processing purposes (e.g. “third party tracking”) 
• Either introducing a duty that browsers provide for the management in a fair way, or a duty 
to allow independent third-party plugins to manage consent locally  
• Minium standards against abuse (e.g. no dark patterns, no ‘favouring’ of associated companies, 
no “pay to play” arrangements) 
An example of the technical implementation of such an approach can be found in the “Advanced 
Data Protection Control” (ADPC) project between noyb and the University of Economics in Vienna 
at https://www.dataprotectioncontrol.org/adpc-spec/, with a fully function prototype.

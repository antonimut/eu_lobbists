1 INTRODUCTION 
Dedalus welcomes the opportunity to contribute evidence as a European-based manufacturer 
of medical software, regulated under most of the laws encompassed by the Digital Omnibus 
framework. These include the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the Medical Device 
Regulation (MDR), and the In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR), as well as the Network and 
Information Systems Directive (NIS2), the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), the AI Act, and others. 
Together, these have created a complex and often overlapping regulatory ecosystem. Our 
portfolio covers the full continuum of care - from primary care and hospital information 
systems to diagnostics, emergency care, and population health management. A significant 
share of our product range consists of clinical information systems that support healthcare 
professionals in managing clinical workflows and documentation, while providing evidence-
based tools to assist in clinical decision-making.

2.1  Tackling regulatory overlaps and bureaucratic burden in 
the EU digital landscape 
Organizations are increasingly confronted with overlapping and sometimes conflicting 
regulatory obligations, which pose significant operational and compliance challenges: 
• Redundant reporting and regulatory ambiguity 
Multiple reporting requirements often lead to duplication and confusion. Companies 
face difficulties in determining which rules apply and how to interpret them, especially

under tight deadlines. The complexity of navigating these overlapping frameworks 
increases the likelihood of non-compliance, as it becomes difficult to fully satisfy all 
applicable regulations simultaneously. This fragmented landscape not only exposes 
organizations to legal risks - such as fines and sanctions - but also discourages 
proactive incident reporting. The inconsistent transposition of EU directives, such as 
NIS2, across Member States further exacerbates the compliance burden. 
• Strained resources during critical moments 
Meeting regulatory reporting deadlines can divert essential resources away from 
incident response efforts. At a time when organizations should be focused on 
managing and mitigating the impact of a crisis, compliance obligations often force a 
reallocation of personnel and attention, undermining operational resilience. 
• Emerging obligations under the AI Act 
The regulatory landscape is further complicated by new requirements introduced by 
the AI Act. Article 73 mandates that providers of high-risk AI systems must report any 
“serious incident” or malfunction that breaches fundamental rights to the relevant 
market surveillance authorities. 
 
These new obligations overlap with existing regulatory frameworks, adding to the complexity 
and increasing the risk of inconsistent or delayed reporting. 
Recommendation: 
To reduce fragmentation and improve regulatory coherence, it is essential to establish a more 
streamlined and practical approach for instance to cyber incident reporting across the EU. 
• Create a centralized reporting mechanism for cyber incidents 
A unified “single point of entry” for cyber incident notifications should be implemented 
at the EU level. This could take the form of a harmonized portal - ENISA could be the 
appropriate coordinator - that serves as the central hub for receiving incident reports 
and routing them to the appropriate national and sectoral authorities. Such a system 
would simplify the reporting process, reduce duplication, and enhance coordination 
among regulatory bodies. 
• Introduce a standardized EU Cyber Incident Identifier 
To further facilitate cross-regulatory reporting, a unique EU-level Cyber Incident 
Identifier (EU Cyber Incident ID) should be assigned to each reported incident. This 
identifier would allow organizations to reference the same incident across multiple 
regulatory frameworks - such as NIS2, GDPR, and the AI Act - without having to 
resubmit the same information multiple times. For example, once an incident is 
reported under NIS2, the same ID could be used when fulfilling related obligations 
under GDPR, ensuring consistency and reducing administrative burden.

• Increase the AI Office’s strategic role 
The AI Office should be formally recognized as the central authority responsible for 
guiding the consistent application of the AI Act throughout the EU. Its leadership must 
be strengthened to ensure that secondary legislation, guidance documents, and 
enforcement practices remain aligned with the core principles and objectives of the 
Act. A strong coordination mechanism is essential to avoid fragmentation and ensure 
regulatory clarity across diverse sectors and Member States. To fulfill its broad 
mandate - including oversight, advisory functions, and support for innovation - the AI 
Office must be equipped with sufficient human and financial resources. This includes 
the capacity to develop practical tools that facilitate compliance, particularly for small 
and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). For example, a standardized self-assessment 
questionnaire could help companies evaluate their obligations under the AI Act in a 
simplified and accessible manner, reducing administrative burden and promoting 
proactive compliance. 
Together, these measures would significantly improve the efficiency, clarity, and 
responsiveness of cyber incident reporting across the EU, while supporting better regulatory 
alignment and reducing compliance risks for organizations. 
2.2 Strategic proposals to streamline EU digital legislation 
Data Act and GDPR 
A truly effective Data Union strategy requires the alignment of all major EU data -related 
legislation - including the Data Act, GDPR  - into a coherent, interoperable, and predictable 
regulatory framework. This involves: 
• Clarifying the scope and boundaries between regulations 
Clear delineation between the objectives and obligations of different legislative 
instruments - such as the Data Act and the GDPR - is essential to avoid overlaps and 
contradictions. This clarity will support legal certainty and facilitate compliance, 
especially for data-driven businesses operating across multiple sectors. 
• Eliminating redundant international Data Transfer Requirements 
To streamline compliance and reduce regulatory burden, international data transfer 
obligations that duplicate those already established under the GDPR should be 
removed. This would contribute to a more consistent and efficient cross-border data 
governance regime. 
• Aligning data portability provisions across frameworks 
Provisions related to data portability should be harmonized with existing rules under 
the GDPR. This alignment is crucial to prevent conflicting obligations and ensure a 
seamless experience for users and businesses navigating multiple regulatory regimes.

GDPR and AI Act 
• Ensure alignment between the AI Act and GDPR regarding special categories of 
personal data 
The AI Act’s provisions on processing sensitive data should be harmonized with 
existing GDPR rules, rather than introducing more restrictive or conflicting 
requirements. This alignment is essential to avoid legal uncertainty and ensure 
consistent data protection standards. 
• Provide clear guidance on areas of regulatory overlap 
Comprehensive guidance should be issued to clarify how the AI Act interacts with the 
GDPR, particularly in areas where principles may appear to conflict. For example, the 
GDPR’s emphasis on data minimization may be at odds with the AI Act’s focus on data 
accuracy, transparency, and bias mitigation. Clear interpretation frameworks are 
needed to help organizations navigate these tensions. 
• Resolve conflicts between data minimization and bias mitigation objectives 
Where the AI Act requires data processing to address bias, this should be permitted 
only when strictly necessary and when synthetic or anonymized data cannot achieve 
the same outcome. Such processing must always be subject to robust safeguards, in 
line with Article 10(5) of the AI Act. This approach would balance the need for fairness 
in AI systems with the fundamental principles of data protection. 
 
GDPR  
• Reduce complexity in documentation requirements 
Simplify the documentation obligations under accountability provisions to ease the 
administrative burden on organizations and support more efficient compliance. 
• Streamline the International Data Transfer Framework 
Rationalize the international data transfer regime by eliminating duplicative 
requirements, particularly those that overlap with the GDPR. This would help create a 
more consistent and manageable cross-border data governance structure. 
• Encourage broader use of PETs data 
Support the wider adoption of pseudonymized or of further privacy-preserving 
technical measure that enables data sharing while mitigating risk. Promoting its use 
can facilitate innovation and compliance simultaneously. 
• Develop a comprehensive guidance document on GDPR–AI Act interactions 
A detailed guide is needed to clarify how the GDPR and the AI Act intersect, helping 
stakeholders navigate overlapping provisions and avoid regulatory conflicts.

• Avoid ambiguous or accelerated implementation timelines 
To support effective compliance, regulatory rollouts should be accompanied by 
realistic timelines and clear guidance. The publication of detailed implementation 
guidelines would be highly beneficial in helping stakeholders prepare and align with 
new requirements. 
• Clarify cross-regulatory obligations for manufacturers and software developers 
It is essential to ensure that the responsibilities imposed on manufacturers and 
software developers under emerging regulations - such as the CRA - are clearly aligned 
with existing frameworks like the GDPR and NIS2. Without such alignment, 
organizations may face conflicting duties and legal uncertainty. Particular attention 
must be given to resolving potential jurisdictional overlaps between the CRA, NIS2, and 
the AI Act to prevent regulatory fragmentation and ensure coherent enforcement. 
NIS2 Directive (NIS2) 
• Align transposition timelines and compliance requirements across Member 
States 
To prevent legal fragmentation and ensure a unified approach to cybersecurity across 
the EU, it is essential to harmonize key regulatory elements - including transposition 
schedules, audit obligations, reporting thresholds, and deadlines. Divergent national 
implementations risk undermining the goal of achieving a high and consistent level of 
cybersecurity across the Union. 
• Ensuring uniform application of Standards 
Consistent enforcement and interpretation of technical and procedural standards 
across Member States is critical to maintain regulatory coherence and supporting 
effective cross-border compliance. 
 
Cybersecurity Act  
• Enhance ENISA’s mandate through the revision of the Cybersecurity Act 
The upcoming revision of the Cybersecurity Act should reinforce ENISA’s role by 
providing it with additional resources and operational capabilities. This would enable 
the agency to more effectively coordinate cybersecurity efforts and develop best 
practices, particularly in close collaboration with the EU AI Office and the European AI 
Committee. 
• Promote alignment with international Standards 
To ensure consistency and global interoperability, ENISA’s activities and frameworks 
should be closely aligned with internationally recognized cybersecurity standards. This 
will support harmonized implementation across Member States and strengthen the 
EU’s position in global cybersecurity governance.

AI Act and interplay with MDR/IVDR 
• Support for Moving Medical Devices under MDR in EU AI Act to Section B of 
Annex I  
Dedalus supports shifting regulated products under MDR from Section A to Section B, 
as this would prioritize sector-specific regulation (MDR/IVDR) over cross-cutting AI Act 
requirements, granting medical device manufacturers more flexibility and reducing 
redundant compliance efforts.   
• Alignment of Standards and Certification  
Dedalus advocates leveraging existing medical device standards (e.g., ISO 13485) and 
integrating AI Act requirements into these, to avoid duplicate certifications and audits. 
The company recognizes gaps in current standards (e.g., logging) and supports 
targeted development to address these.   
• Implementation Timeline Linked to Prerequisites  
Dedalus recommends tying the implementation timeline for new regulations to the 
designation of notified bodies and other prerequisites, rather than fixed dates, to 
prevent rushed compliance and ensure readiness.   
• Streamlined Reporting and Market Surveillance  
Dedalus calls for harmonized reporting mechanisms across regulations (AI Act, MDR, 
GDPR), favoring a single reporting form and process to reduce administrative burden 
and avoid fragmentation.   
• Protection of Legitimate Expectations and Grace Periods  
Dedalus highlights the need for clear rules on legacy systems and modifications, 
advocating against retrospective compliance for minor changes and supporting 
reasonable grace periods for existing products.   
• Anticipation of EHDS Technical Implementation  
Dedalus suggests referencing and aligning with existing medical device standards for 
the European Health Data Space (EHDS), and recommends proactive development of 
technical implementation rules to ensure future compatibility.   
• Intellectual Property and Data Act Considerations  
Dedalus acknowledges national differences (e.g., Italy’s strict IP laws) and recommends 
an analytic approach to IP exposure in AI systems, referencing the Data Act and 
awaiting further interpretive guidelines.   
• Phased Implementation and Governance

Dedalus supports an EU-wide roadmap and toolkits for phased implementation,

member states and sectors, calling for broad alignment and clear guidance.

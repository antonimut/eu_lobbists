Position on the Commission Proposal for a Regulation - COM(2025)726 
The Commission's draft "Regulation addressing the effects of global overcapacity on the Union steel 
market" has clearly shown that the downstream industry, with its thousands of small and medium-
sized enterprises that make up the largest part of the steel value chain, has not been taken into 
account by a single word. This sector alone provides more than 11.5 million direct jobs and, under 
the current draft, is being pushed out of competitiveness. If only 20% of the affected companies 
depend on imports, this inevitably means that the implementation could result in the loss of up to 
2.3 million jobs. 
Furthermore, due to an alleged urgency, the Commission did not carry out a full impact assessment. 
The expiry of the EU safeguard measure on certain steel products has, since its entry into force, been 
limited to a maximum duration of eight years. 
In addition, no SME test or SME check was considered or carried out. This already constitutes, in 
principle, a violation of the Interinstitutional Agreement of 13 April 20161 on Better Law-Making and 
thus also of Article 41 - Right to good administration - of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights2. 
The accompanying Staff Working Document is largely based on data that are neither public nor 
freely accessible. The fact that the vast body of data of the European statistical office EUROSTAT and 
other public sources within the EU Member States was not used calls into question the transparency 
and credibility of the entire data basis. 
For months, the EU has been working on several omnibus packages intended to reduce bureaucratic 
hurdles and extraordinary burdens on European companies. Competitiveness is one of the central 
themes in the political discourse in the EU. 
In a request from the European Parliament to the Commission from October 20253, it is stated, inter 
alia: 
"The EUROSTAT insolvency index is more than 20% above the 2019 level, and 66% higher than in 
2021. Additionally, European trade defence measures, tariffs and the EU Carbon Border Adjustment 
Mechanism (CBAM) are imposing a growing burden. For SMEs, even minor increases in raw material 
costs can make the difference between profitability and bankruptcy." 
This shows that many companies are already no longer able to afford the high price level in the EU 
and are being pushed into insolvency. If the CO₂ levy under CBAM and, at the same time, EU steel 
duties and tariff rate quotas are now imposed on the downstream industry with its thousands of 
SMEs, this will lead to exorbitant price increases. Since the currently proposed import quota of 
approximately 18.3 million tonnes per year roughly covers the imports of the large steel-importing 
companies, SMEs will be left empty-handed under the proposed first-come, first-served principle for 
quota allocation. Moreover, in some categories the import volume has been reduced by 60%. This 
will lead to a significant supply shortage in the area of flat-rolled stainless steel (categories 8, 9, and 
10).

Summary of Demands: 
 
1. Limitation of tariffs and introduction of realistic quotas 
• Maximum tariff of 25% instead of 50%. 
• Prohibition of "tariff stacking" (no cumulation with anti-dumping/anti-subsidy measures) 
in order to prevent unilateral tightening by the Commission. 
• WTO-compliant tariff rate quotas based on the most recent three-year period (2022-
2024, at least 25.5 million metric tonnes). 
• Imports of slabs into the EU are included in the measure and receive an additional TRQ 
of 3.6 million tonnes. 
• Stainless steel must be exempted from the measure due to existing undercapacities in 
the EU and the extensive trade defence instruments already in place. 
• Small and medium-sized enterprises are fully exempted from the measure. 
• Quota adjustments only on the basis of current data and a full impact assessment, no 
longer based on outdated years. 
• No silent concessions to WTO partners in other economic sectors without the 
involvement of Parliament and Council. 
2. Obligation to conduct impact assessments and market analyses 
• Before any change to quotas, rules of origin, or country allocations, the Commission 
must present a full economic, social, and trade policy assessment. 
• Urgency procedures must not be used without scrutiny and parliamentary approval. 
• Mandatory consultations with industry, importers, and in particular SMEs. 
3. Democratic oversight of bilateral safeguard measures 
• Bilateral safeguard measures may only be applied if they are explicitly provided for in the 
respective free trade agreement. 
• Approval by Parliament and Council is mandatory prior to entry into force. 
• No early application before the first mandatory review. 
4. Restriction of the Commission's delegated powers 
• Delegations may only be used for liberalisation or technical adjustments, not for new 
trade barriers. 
• Limitation of delegated powers to two years instead of indefinite application. 
• Every delegated act must include SME protection clauses. 
• Parliament and Council may suspend the urgency procedure if misuse is identified. 
5. Regular reviews only with the involvement of the legislators 
• Annual comprehensive reviews involving the entire value chain. 
• Mandatory effectiveness evaluations every two years, carried out jointly by the 
Commission and the Member States. 
• Only Parliament and Council decide on the continuation, amendment, or repeal of the 
regulation, not the Commission.

No economic impact assessment! 
Impact Assessment: Commission once again fails to carry out important impact 
assessments 
In the proposal for a REGULATION OF THE EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL to combat 
the negative impact of global overcapacity on the Union steel market 2025/0726 (COD),4 the 
Commission stated that it once again refrained from carrying out an important impact assessment 
of the presented measure, citing, among other things, the "urgency" with which a successor 
measure to the expiring EU Safeguard measure would now have to be introduced. 
Nothing at the end of the EU Safeguard measure on certain steel products comes as a surprise. It 
was already known when Safeguard was introduced that this measure would have to end after 8 
years at the latest. The WTO5 and EU rules6 are clearly formulated. Thus, the Commission would 
have had a long time to evaluate whether Safeguard was successful and what a successor measure 
could have in terms of impact on the European economy. 
Instead, the Commission is relying exclusively on the preparation of a Staff Working Document, 
which is considered sufficient within the framework of the European Commission's Better Regulation 
Guidelines and Toolkit (SWD(2021) 305 final) in a case of "urgency" and where an "exemption is 
granted". 
The results of this SWD are also corresponding. Apart from non-representative survey results and 
rough estimates of the impact on GDP in the EU, the document remains empty of content. 
There are no assumptions for production cost increases due to the new tariffs, nor information on 
how the simultaneous application of a Safeguard successor, CBAM and existing trade defence 
measures will affect the economy. 
The Commission also did not mention small and medium-sized enterprises at all in its SWD and 
once again broke its promise to focus on the interests of SMEs. Better Regulation looks different. 
An economic level playing field? It is only available for large corporations. In addition, the contents 
of the SWD cannot be independently verified. And the most important question: who has allowed 
the Commission to waive the full impact assessment?

Existing Safeguard Not Comparable to Steel Tariffs and Quotas 
While in 2018 a comprehensive safeguard procedure with more than 900 participants from all 
business sectors was carried out for the EU safeguard measure on certain steel products, the 
Commission conducted only a "Have Your Say"8 consultation in advance of the steel tariffs and 
quotas. With not even half the number of participants compared to the safeguard procedure, this 
consultation can only be described as barely representative or statistically meaningful. In terms of 
quality and evidentiary value, the Have Your Say procedure in no way corresponds to the EU 
safeguard procedure of March 2018. 
Comparing the safeguard measure with the Commission's current proposals is like comparing apples 
and oranges. Safeguard procedures are subject to a clearly defined legal framework. By contrast, the 
tariffs and quotas were simply decided upon. 
If one compares the two procedures and focuses solely on a basic numerical relationship, the 
following facts emerge: 
Import volume prior to the safeguard (2015-2017): 28.7 million tonnes of imported steel. 
Safeguard level set in the first full year after the introduction of the measure: 31.5 million tonnes of 
imported steel. This represents an increase of approximately 10% in the permitted import volume. 
The Commission's draft for the new quotas provides for a maximum possible import volume of 18.3 
million tonnes. Measured against the average import volume for the years 2020 to 2024 (with an 
average of 25.5 million tonnes), this constitutes a drastic reduction of 30%. 
When one compares the extensive fact-finding and consultation phase of the former safeguard 
procedure with the process currently being conducted at breakneck speed and without any form of 
assessment, no other conclusion can be reached-even if the Commission claims otherwise-that the 
two procedures cannot be compared given the massive difference in the degree of market 
intervention. It is therefore imperative that the impact assessment be carried out even more 
comprehensively than was already the case under the previous safeguard procedure. 
Import quotas of 25.5 million tonnes plus 3.6 million tonnes for slabs 
The import volume of steel products covered by the safeguard has remained almost constant over 
the past five years, averaging approximately 25.5 million tonnes per year, and thus about 24% below 
the safeguard quotas of 33.5 million tonnes. If the EU were to expand its quotas to include the still 
unaccounted imports of slabs and other semi-finished products (CN codes: 7206, 7207, 7218, 7224), 
which amount to roughly 8 million tonnes (average 2020-2024), an obvious solution would exist. By 
reducing the import quotas from the previous 33.5 million tonnes per year to 25.5 million tonnes per 
year and introducing a quota for slabs of 3.6 million tonnes, a fair compromise would be achieved 
that would benefit all European importers.

Tariff Rate Quotas: SMEs are left empty-handed 
Tariff rate quotas – in the end, only large enterprises get anything from the quota 
The Commission has proposed that the Tariff Rate Quotas (TRQs) should be reduced to 
approximately 18,3 million tonnes per year. The EU Safeguard TRQs most recently amounted to 
approx. 33,5 million tonnes. This corresponds to a reduction in TRQs of about 45%. 
SMEs are disadvantaged by the EU compared to large enterprises 
SMEs are prioritized by CBAM and Safeguard. According 
to a statistical analysis by EUROSTAT10 from October 
2024, the largest share of imports is attributable to 
large enterprises. Only 3% of all EU importers are large 
importers, but together they account for 57,3% of the 
total value of all extra-EU imports. On average, a large 
enterprise imports for about 35 million euros per year, 
according to EUROSTAT.  
97% of all other importers are thus to be classified as 
SMEs, even though this includes a share of 2,1% of 
unknown company sizes and whose average imports are 
only marginally higher than those of micro-enterprises. 
SMEs are thus responsible for just 42,7% of the total 
value of all extra-EU imports. A medium-sized company imports only for a little more than 4,6 
million euros, a small one for 1,1 million euros and a micro-company even for only 0,2 million euros 
per year. 
Large enterprises can take advantage of economies of scale, SMEs can't 
While it is well known that large enterprises also receive better purchasing conditions and prices 
through greater economies of scale, SMEs cannot benefit from such advantages. Large enterprises 
also replenish the Safeguard quotas with their imports much faster than SMEs could. Thus, SMEs are 
always at a disadvantage compared to large enterprises when it comes to imports. Delays due to 
Safeguard quotas being overflowed too quickly or costs for Safeguard tariffs can be absorbed by 
SMEs much more poorly than a large multinational corporation. If CBAM with its costs for CO2 
certificates and bureaucracy is added to this, the competitiveness of SMEs will be dramatically 
curtailed. 
This circumstance has been known for a long time. The European Commission had already stated in 
"The Single Market Review – Impact on Competition and Scale Effects – Econommies of Scale11" of 
1996 that SMEs are at a significant disadvantage compared to large enterprises. The economies of 
scale discussed in this report relate to the cost advantages that companies achieve by expanding 
their production volumes. As the production volume increases, the average unit cost decreases, 
which is due to various internal and external factors. Something that SMEs cannot implement as 
easily as large enterprises. Even then, the special needs of SMEs were emphasized – but hardly 
anyone has taken this into account since then.

Reduction of TRQs: There is a threat of a price diktat by EU steel producers  
We have already explained that SMEs are at a disadvantage compared to large enterprises in the 
economy of scale due to their significantly lower purchasing power. They simply cannot finance the 
purchase of large quantities in order to be able to impose volume effects and thus lower prices on 
steel producers, whether in the EU or outside. SMEs are therefore much more quickly subject to the 
price dictates of manufacturer oligopolies, as there is one in the EU for steel. 
If TRQs are reduced by 45%, this means that the few importing large enterprises will claim 97% of 
the proposed 18,3 million tonnes at worst. And while SME still had 15.800.000 tons of TRQs under 
the Safeguard measure, they are lucky enough to have 611.000 tons left. A level playing field looks 
different.  
Together with the proposed increase in tariffs from the current 25% to 50%, the price dictate of the 
domestic steel manufacturer oligopoly, which has so far been kept in check by the better-adjusted 
Safeguard TRQs, will become much more acute. By comparison, when Section 232 tariffs were raised 
from 25% to 50% in the spring of 2025, US stainless steel producers in particular immediately revised 
their prices upwards by more than 25 percentage points.

SMEs: dramatic impact on production costs and competitiveness 
As early as 2021, the Dutch Central Bank (De Nederlandsche Bank, DNB) had derived some 
important findings on the impact of a carbon tax and CBAM on the European economy with the DNB 
analysis "The impact of carbon pricing and a CBAM on EU competitiveness14 ". Based on the 
assumptions of the DNB and applied to the present case, it becomes clear what cascading effects the 
repeated interventions of the European Commission will have on small and medium-sized 
enterprises in particular.  
This is because: In a comparison between all EU member states, the DNB had found twice as high a 
burden of CBAM in the metals sector, and in the Central and Eastern European (CEE) countries of the 
EU, this burden was even 3,5 times as high.  
The Commission's plan to increase tariffs on steel and reduce TRQs cannot and must not be 
considered as a single measure. We have already impressively explained that almost no quantities 
remain of the planned TRQs for SMEs and that more than 97% of them will be used by large 
enterprises in the future. For all SMEs in the EU Metals Sector, this will lead to significant increases 
in production costs per tonne of more than 15%. The impact on the Central and Eastern European 
Countries (CEE) will be even more serious. In the CEE countries, production costs in the Metals 
Sector are expected to increase by as much as 19,5% per tonne.

Structural overcapacities in steel non-existent 
Overcapacities in steel: lie or mistake? 
For years, there has been talk of the alleged overcapacities that are said to exist worldwide. The fact 
that this overcapacity does not exist is made clear by a simple example. 
The entire technical complex for production from crude steel to finished sheet, bar or tube is highly 
mechanized. In some cases, temperatures of more than 2.000 degrees Celsius are reached in the 
blast furnace, and the processing demands enormous loads from man and machine. Where physical 
laws and chemical processes collide, something quickly breaks down and then the huge converters, 
continuous casting plants or rolling mills and galvanizing plants suddenly come to a standstill. 
Maintenance work must be carried out regularly in order to keep a steel mill running around the 
clock. There are constant shift changes, and accidents are not uncommon in this challenging 
environment. 
No steel mill can produce at 100% 
Simply put, no machine or human can always function 100% 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. 
Therefore, there is a benchmark factor in which a machine or even a steel mill runs at an optimal 
economically and technically justifiable level. In the best case, this is 85%. Otherwise, blast furnaces, 
converters and rolling mills will wear out too quickly, they will no longer have time for maintenance 
work and the more often technical failures, accidents or unplanned maintenance cycles will occur. 
Your investment will reach the end of its useful life faster than you can provide a replacement.  
So if you are planning a new steel mill and want to effectively produce 1 million tons of steel per 
year over the next 10 years, then you have to plan that in the best case you can only produce at a 
real capacity utilization of 85%. You must therefore design your blast furnace, your steel mill and 
also your rolling mills to be correspondingly larger – 15% larger or with a nominal capacity of 1,118 
million tons in order to be able to achieve the target of 1 million tons per year. 
Steel manufacturers are also only commercial enterprises 
And even below 80% capacity utilisation, systems can wear out more quickly or become 
economically unattractive.  
80% capacity utilisation: this mystical value that the Commission has set as a target and which was 
allegedly last achieved in 2013, before the overcapacity of more than 600 million tonnes, which 
allegedly still exists today, occurred. Worse still, there are even capacities under construction that 
are expected to increase overcapacity to 720 million tonnes. 
The figures from European steel producers show that this mystical capacity utilisation of 80% has not 
been seen for well over a decade. This is no wonder, since these figures are calculated on the 
nominal capacities of more than 200 million tons of crude steel per year of the domestic steel mills 
and not on the real capacities! 
If you transfer all this to global steel production, then it can be seen that it is not only the domestic 
steel producers who have been producing well over 80% of their capacity utilization for years. The

annual capacity installed. Theoretical capacity is not the same as actual available capacity. EU 
stainless steel manufacturers are aware of this. Due to technical limitations, Outokumpu, for

meet the EU's total stainless steel demand, EU manufacturers would need a theoretical total

restriction on import quotas (currently, stainless steel quotas are to be reduced by 60%)

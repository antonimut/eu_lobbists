EUROFER Comments on the Commission Proposal  
for a Highly Effective Trade Measure 
 
• EUROFER welcomes the European Commission’s proposal for a new trade measure addressing 
the negative spillover effects of global steel excess capacities. 
• Importantly, the Proposal foresees: 
o A TRQ system, rather than a blanket tariff, allowing steel users to source tariff-free volumes 
within the quota; 
o Quotas that drastically reduce 2024 import levels, in line with the Steel and Metals Action 
Plan’s objective of protecting and promoting EU industrial capacities by increasing capacity-
utilisation rates, which are currently at unviable levels. Maintaining quotas at current levels, 
as requested by some stakeholders, would run counter to the logic of the measure, whose 
purpose is to enable EU steel producers to increase production and regain market share; 
o A 50% out-of-quota tariff to prevent the redirection of trade flows following the introduction 
of blanket 50% tariffs in other jurisdictions; 
o A comprehensive geographical scope, reflecting the fact that steel overcapacity is a global 
phenomenon. Any country exclusion would create loopholes for circumvention. 
The measure does not target overcapacity per se, but rather its trade spillover effects, which 
can originate from countries that—while not necessarily at the source of overcapacity—are 
flooded with third-country exports and, lacking sufficient domestic demand, re-export steel 
to third markets, particularly the EU. The trade spillover can also generate from countries 
(like Japan and South Korea) that reduced exports to the saturated Asian market and steadily 
increased exports to the EU, which remains an attractive market; 
o The Melted & Poured principle, to identify the actual origin of steel and prevent 
circumvention; 
o The possibility to include additional steel-containing products in the scope, to prevent 
circumvention and preserve the whole steel value chain; 
o A flexibility mechanism allowing quotas to adjust to demand, both upwards and downwards;

o No carry-over of unused quotas, to avoid the stockpiling effects experienced under the 
current safeguard regime. Based on past experience, some large exporting countries can 
disappear from the EU market for several months only to re-enter when most tariff-free 
volumes become available. Thanks to the carry-over, a third country managed to export 35% 
of its total annual export volume to the EU in just one month. 
o Long-term protection subject to periodic review, with the first evaluation after five years, 
providing the stability necessary to support investment decisions. 
o Quarterly quota administration to avoid a situation similar to the one experience during the 
provisional safeguard measures when massive imports at the beginning of the year were 
detrimental to users that were not in a position to build up stocks and need to be supplied on 
a stable basis throughout the year.1 
• Of particular importance, as highlighted by several other submissions (including Bekaert, 
OWFA, Legi, the European Fittings Industry, EIFI, EUWA, Berlemann, Thüringer Agrartechnik & 
Maschinenbau, Rosenthal Zaunanlagen GmbH, among others), is the assessment of a possible 
scope expansion. 
This assessment should take place earlier than the proposed two-year deadline—ideally after 
six months—to determine whether conditions are met to extend the scope to downstream 
products or apply other forms of import protection. As repeatedly underlined by other 
stakeholders, failure to address downstream circumvention risks would undermine the 
effectiveness of the measure and the integrity of the entire steel value chain. 
• Regarding claims about limited availability of certain steel qualities, EUROFER stresses that 
there is confusion between shortages and willingness to pay the price. Indeed, the European 
steel industry operated at only 67% capacity utilisation in 2024, indicating substantial room to 
increase output and meet demand. EU producers are capable of supplying all required qualities 
and have invested in new production lines, for example for electrical steel.  
The fact that quotas are currently exhausted on the first day of the safeguard quarter is not an 
indication of shortage. Rather, it shows that importers are taking advantage of the so called 
“first day dilution” which allows them to pay an out of quota duty way lower than the nominal 
25%. A distinction must also be made between genuine shortages and speculative behaviour, 
whereby importers increase import volumes ahead of the measure’s entry into force to benefit 
from more generous tariff-free quotas under the current safeguard system. To be noted is that 
the Proposal is based on data analysis the Commission has been carrying out for 7 years,

Price developments must also be put into perspective: as a cyclical business, steel always had a 
natural volatility with prices. However post-covid, steel prices have remained persistently 
depressed fuelled by massive global excess capacity in a context of weak demand. Moderate 
price increases are necessary to restore profitability and support decarbonisation investments. 
In this context, the Commission anticipates a 3.25% price increase. 
Moreover, the new trade measure is structured as a tariff-free quota, not a blanket tariff 
imposing a direct cost on each imported ton like in the U.S. system. TRQs allow the market 
(supply-demand) to continue determining prices at the same time stabilizing imports at a level 
viable for domestic capacity in the importing market. 
Certain stakeholders claim that they have been bearing the costs of extraordinary safeguard 
measures on steel imports (25%) and the resulting consequences of price increases. However, 
on multiple occasions, the Commission found that the price increases were not linked to the 
safeguard measures. On the contrary, this was driven largely by international factors, such as 
raw-material costs, energy prices, and global demand cycles.2

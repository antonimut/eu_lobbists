 
 
DI Response to the Commission Adoption Consultation 
on the Digital Omnibus  
Confederation of Danish Industry 22 December 2025   
DI is grateful for the opportunity to submit a response to the adoption consultation. This 
response is divided into two parts: the first part addresses the omnibus on artificial 
intelligence, while the second part concerns the general digital omnibus on data, 
cybersecurity, and e-privacy. 
 
1) DI’s response to the COM proposal on the AI Omnibus 
Summary   
DI generally welcomes the Commission’s digital omnibus on artificial intelligence, but 
emphasizes that there is a need for further simplification and harmonization to strengthen 
Europe’s competitiveness and innovation. DI supports the postponement of requirements 
for high-risk AI systems, as relevant standards are not yet in place, and at the same time 
warns against uncertainty associated with the possibility of accelerating implementation. 
The extension of simplified requirements to SMCs and the reduction of registration burdens 
for certain AI systems are welcome. DI supports the promotion of AI skills through guidance 
and education rather than general obligations for all companies. DI recommends that clear 
frameworks are set for centralized supervision so companies avoid double regulation. DI is 
positive about the establishment of regulatory sandboxes, but underscores the need for 
smooth and transparent access. Furthermore, DI calls for clearer interaction and uniform 
interpretation between the AI Act and other EU legislation. 
 
General remarks   
The EU is in the midst of a global technological race with the world’s powers. In this light, 
regulatory simplification is not only a means of reducing administrative burdens – it is a 
strategic necessity to strengthen European competitiveness, innovation, and resilience.

Today, the EU’s digital legislation is complex and fragmented. There is a need to harmonize 
rules and enforcement across legislation and member states. Therefore, DI welcomes the 
Commission’s digital simplification package, including the digital omnibus on AI. 
However, further simplification efforts are needed to achieve the declared goal of at least 
25% reduction in burdens for companies and 35% for SMEs in line with the Commission’s 
simplification targets. For DI, it is therefore important that the proposed package does not 
stand alone and considers the package as the first of several necessary steps towards a 
simpler and more coherent digital regulation and a well-functioning digital internal market, 
which not only protects fundamental rights but is also competitive and innovation-friendly. 
DI also notes that the Commission, with the simplification package, has initiated the 
second phase of the simplification agenda in the form of a so-called “Digital Fitness 
Check” , which will assess the coherence and effect of the EU’s digital rules, including 
whether they strengthen competitiveness. 
DI looks forward to contributing to this work, but also emphasizes that it is not sufficient to 
look only at the existing rules in the digital area. To avoid the challenges of legal uncertainty, 
fragmented enforcement, and administrative burdens recurring in the future, there is also a 
need to adjust the approach to future EU regulation. There is a need for more clear, simple, 
and predictable rules going forward – with early clarification of overlaps, fewer burdens for 
companies, a clear prioritization of innovation as a driver of competitiveness, and better 
sector-specific guidance. This task should not be left solely to the national supervisory 
authorities. 
Below follow DI’s specific remarks to selected amendments contained in the 
Commission’s digital omnibus on artificial intelligence. 
 
Implementation schedule for high-risk requirements 
With the proposal, the Commission suggests that the implementation schedule for high-
risk rules is linked to the availability of standards and support tools. The requirements for 
high-risk AI systems will therefore only enter into force when harmonized standards, 
common specifications, or Commission guidelines are available. 
However, it is also proposed to set final dates for when the rules must apply at the latest. 
For the AI Act’s requirements for high-risk areas (Annex III), it is at the end of 2027. For the 
 
requirements for high-risk AI systems associated with products subject to sector-specific 
regulation (Annex I), it is at the end of August 2028. 
DI supports postponing AI requirements for high-risk systems, as relevant standards are 
not yet in place. Without standards, compliance and enforcement risk being uneven and 
fragmented. However, it is uncertain whether the AI omnibus package can be adopted 
before the high-risk requirements come into force in August 2026. DI therefore calls for this 
to be taken into account in the further legislative process, so the industry is not left in a 
regulatory vacuum where enforcement is possible without clear rules. 
DI also notes that the proposal allows the Commission to accelerate the implementation 
schedule for high-risk requirements at any time (by 6 months for Annex III and 12 months 
for Annex I) if the Commission decides that standards or “sufficient” support tools are 
ready. DI does not consider this appropriate, as the unclear criteria and the possibility of 
acceleration undermine predictability and create uncertainty for businesses. 
 
Expansion of simplified requirements for SMEs to include SMCs 
With the proposal, the Commission suggests that simplified requirements for SMEs be 
extended to include small mid-cap companies (SMCs). This means SMCs receive the same 
benefits as SMEs, such as simplified requirements for technical documentation and 
special considerations in calculation of fines. 
 
DI supports the proposal to extend the AI Act’s SME simplification regime to also cover 
SMCs. SMCs are a significant part of the European economy, but have so far received less 
attention at EU level. Tailored rules for SMCs alongside SMEs are a positive step forward. 
However, it is important that these initiatives supplement, and not replace, the existing 
SME provisions. DI further supports that voluntary support tools and guidelines under the 
AI Act consider both the needs and differences of SMEs and SMCs. 
 
Obligations on AI skills moved to the Commission and Member States 
With the proposal, the Commission intends that the Commission and Member States 
should promote AI skills through guidance, education, and best practice instead of 
 
imposing a general training obligation on all providers and users. It is also noted that the 
requirement for training is maintained for those using high-risk AI systems. 
DI supports that AI skills are promoted by the Commission and Member States via 
guidance and education rather than a broad, unclear obligation for all companies. This 
approach is more proportionate and potentially ensures more effective use of resources. At 
the same time, the business sector should be voluntarily involved in developing materials 
and best practice to ensure relevance and ownership. 
Reduction of registration burden for certain AI systems 
With the proposal, the Commission intends to reduce the registration burden for providers 
of AI systems that are used in high-risk areas, but which the provider has assessed not to 
be high-risk because they are only used for narrow or procedural tasks. 
DI supports that AI systems used only for narrow or procedural tasks and not classified as 
high-risk should be exempt from registration in the EU database. DI also points out that 
there is still a need for clearer delimitation of the classification rules for high-risk AI 
systems, as the rules are interpreted differently across sector-specific legislation. It is 
therefore important that classification of high-risk is based on evidence and actual risk, 
and that documentation requirements are clear and proportional. 
 
Centralization of supervision of certain AI systems 
With the proposal, the Commission intends that supervision of a large number of AI 
systems based on general-purpose AI models or embedded in very large online platforms 
and search engines should be centralized in the AI Office under the Commission. 
DI supports the proposal to the extent that it will actually support more uniform 
implementation of the regulation’s requirements across the EU and thus result in more 
harmonized enforcement, more effective processes, and fewer administrative burdens for 
business. DI points out that competences and responsibilities of centralized supervision 
must be clearly delineated in relation to national supervisory authorities, so companies 
avoid double regulation and parallel supervision. In addition, the powers of the centralized 
supervision should not be expanded via guidelines that in practice may create new 
obligations for businesses. DI also recommends that cooperation between the various 
supervisory authorities in the EU and at national level be strengthened to ensure uniform 
interpretations, less fragmentation, and more equal competition conditions across the EU. 
 
 
Facilitation of compliance with data protection legislation (GDPR) 
With the proposal, the Commission intends that providers and users of all AI systems and 
models may process special categories of personal data using appropriate security 
measures for the purpose of bias detection and correction. 
DI emphasizes the importance of reducing legal uncertainty regarding the use of new 
technologies such as AI, which is crucial for future competitiveness. DI supports creating 
clearer frameworks for processing sensitive personal data for bias detection, making it 
easier for companies to navigate the rules and develop AI systems without risking 
violations of GDPR. It is essential that the requirements for appropriate security measures 
are clear and proportionate, and that practical guidance is drawn up. 
 
Extended use of AI regulatory sandboxes and testing in real environments 
With the proposal, the Commission intends that the AI Office shall establish an EU-level AI 
regulatory sandbox from 2028, and that the use of real-world testing should be 
strengthened to benefit key industries. The sandboxes are highlighted as a central tool to 
support the development of reliable and compliant high-risk AI systems. 
 
DI is positive about the establishment of a regulatory sandbox and extended testing in real-
world environments, as close sparring with EU experts has the potential to reduce legal 
uncertainty and secure more harmonized interpretation of the AI Act. For the initiative to 
have broad impact, it is essential that the application process for the sandboxes is short, 
transparent, and smooth. Each sandbox project should conclude with a report that 
provides clear and specific answers to the legal questions raised during the project. DI also 
emphasizes that the sandboxes should be available to companies throughout the EU and 
support innovation without unnecessary administrative barriers. 
 
Clarification of interaction with other EU legislation 
 
With the proposal, the Commission intends, among other things, to clarify how the AI Act 
interacts with other relevant EU rules, such as data protection, cybersecurity, and 
machinery regulation. 
DI sees it as crucial that the interaction between the AI Act and other EU legislation (GDPR, 
cyber, sector-specific rules) is clarified and harmonized, including that the process for 
becoming a notifying body is simplified and streamlined across the EU. In addition, it is 
important to ensure uniform interpretation across legislation, so that different 
understandings of the interaction with the AI Act do not arise across, for example, sector-
specific rules concerning medical devices and toys. 
DI recommends that overlaps and uncertainties between the AI Act and other EU 
legislation are mapped and prioritized. For example, there is still a need for clarity regarding 
the regulation’s rules on risk classification to ensure that these are clear, proportional, and 
based on actual risk – and not broad or vague definitions, which can give rise to varying 
interpretations. This includes, among others, the following two articles in the AI Act: 
- Article 6 concerning classification rules for high-risk AI systems: The article is interpreted 
differently across sector-specific legislation. Therefore, it should be clarified that only 
safety components can be classified as high-risk. A reassessment should also be made of 
the extent to which the use of third parties in other sector-specific legislation is relevant for 
whether a safety component can be defined as high-risk. 
- Article 82 concerning AI systems that comply with the requirements but pose a risk: The 
article undermines legal certainty and predictability for providers, because an AI system 
that is compliant may still be subject to additional requirements or restrictions from 
national authorities. This can lead to fragmentation of the internal market, as different 
member states may introduce different extra requirements. The article should be removed, 
as compliance with the AI Act should be sufficient to market and use AI systems 
throughout the EU without risk of additional national requirements. 
Finally, DI wishes to point out that it is insufficient to primarily provide guidance regarding 
the many overlaps, as there is still a need for real simplification, increased clarity, and 
harmonization. A concrete example is in Article 53 of the AI Act concerning obligations for 
providers of general-purpose AI models. Here, it is unclear when an actor adapting or using 
a GPAI model becomes subject to the obligations. It should be clarified that downstream 
actors (e.g., companies that fine-tune or use GPAI models) only become GPAI providers if 
 
they make “significant changes” that create a new general-purpose model. “Significant 
changes” should also be detailed to increase clarity. 
 
1) DI’s response to the COM proposal on the General Digital Omnibus 
Summary 
DI generally welcomes the Commission's digital omnibus but emphasizes the need for 
further simplification and harmonization to strengthen Europe's competitiveness and 
innovation. DI supports many of the proposed amendments to the GDPR, including making 
the development and operation of AI models easier and strengthening harmonization 
within DPIA and breach notification. However, DI misses further initiatives in the area, such 
as a real change in practice concerning the definition of personal data, easing the 
documentation burden when using new suppliers, and broader exemptions from the duty 
to inform. For other data legislation, DI is positive about several of the proposals but calls 
for unambiguous protection of trade secrets in the Data Act. Regarding cybersecurity, DI 
partially supports the proposal for a consolidated reporting platform but points out that 
harmonization of templates, deadlines, and standards, as well as a missing link to the 
national reporting platform, are still outstanding. 
 
Below follow DI's specific comments on selected amendments contained in the 
Commission’s general digital omnibus, including in relation to GDPR, other data legislation, 
and cyber. 
 
Data Protection 
There is significant potential to simplify the GDPR without lowering the level of protection 
by providing more unambiguous legal frameworks for both the covered companies and 
authorities as well as the supervisory authorities, which today spend many resources on 
guiding, interpreting, and applying the rules. A pervasive challenge with the GDPR is thus 
the extensive, general, and judgment-based requirements that the regulation imposes. 
Many burdens arise as a result of differing interpretation and supplementation of the GDPR 
in the Member States. 
 
 
GDPR contains more than 50 references to Member States’ national law. Despite the fact 
that GDPR is a regulation, where part of the purpose was full harmonization, there are still 
considerable differences between Member States. This is a challenge for both large 
companies and SMEs. 
 
In addition to the burden associated with GDPR compliance itself, it is often not possible 
for companies to unequivocally – or with sufficient certainty – determine whether personal 
data may lawfully be used for a given purpose, when, and to what extent the rights of the 
data subject apply, etc. Several companies refrain from investing in or using new 
technology, as clarification of legality is either impossible or would be time-consuming and 
costly to obtain. 
 
With the proposal, the Commission intends that the EDPB must draw up a draft EU-wide 
list of processing activities that respectively require and do not require a data protection 
impact assessment (DPIA). DI supports the proposal, which concretizes the EDPB's 
intention to provide timely and concise guidance that is easy to understand and translate 
into practice. However, DI emphasizes that the lists should be prepared in close dialogue 
with the business community, as the real burden reduction depends on the EDPB – or the 
national data protection authorities – linking sufficiently concrete guidance to the 
individual processing activity on the lists, so the individual data controller can assess 
whether a DPIA should be prepared. 
 
The proposal also entails that EDPB must draft a common method and template for DPIAs, 
which the EU Commission can then adapt and issue as a delegated act. Thus, a legal act 
could be established which – in contrast to guidance from EDPB – has legally binding effect 
for the companies and authorities covered as well as the national data protection 
authorities. DI supports both the proposal and this regulatory approach in general, where 
the EU Commission lays down more specific, and possibly thematically limited, legal acts 
to fill out and concretize the requirements of the GDPR. Similar acts could thus be issued 
to fill out and concretize, for example, the principles in Article 5, the balancing of interests 
rule in Article 6(1)(f), Article 7, the data subject’s rights in Articles 12-22, privacy by design 
in Article 25 and the requirements for processing security in Article 32. At the same time, 
 
this approach does not involve lowering the level of protection of the GDPR, since the 
wording of the material provisions of the regulation is maintained. 
 
The Commission proposes that the definition of personal data be clarified so that it 
depends on whether a given actor can actually identify the person. DI is neutral to the 
proposal, which is seen as a clarification following the EU Court of Justice’s decision in the 
SRB case (C-413/23 P) and, in DI's view, is not a change to the current legal position. It is 
good if there can be greater clarity about the concept of personal data, but the 
Commission should be more ambitious, for example by following the Nordic interest 
groups' proposal to exempt processing that does not aim to identify an individual. 
 
The Commission also proposes to extend the deadline for reporting breaches of personal 
data security from 72 to 96 hours and that the notification can be made via ENISA’s new 
joint platform for incident reporting. Only breaches that are likely to involve a "high risk" 
must be reported to the supervisory authority, whereas breaches where it is unlikely that 
there is a risk to the rights and freedoms of data subjects are no longer subject to the 
notification obligation. DI supports the proposal. It is positive that reporting is only required 
in case of high risk – a change which DI has also requested. If reporting is to be done via 
ENISA in the future, it must be ensured that this does not cause new burdens for 
companies in Denmark, which today use Virk.dk for a wide range of reporting. It is 
important that any follow-up from the supervisory authority to the data controller is not 
affected by the fact that the reporting has been made to ENISA and not the national data 
protection authority. Furthermore, it is noted that the data controller's obligation to 
document the handling of a breach internally in accordance with GDPR Article 33(5) 
continues unchanged, and it would be appropriate if EDPB also provides guidance on 
exactly what information must be documented under Article 33(5). 
 
It is also proposed to introduce clearer guidelines and criteria for pseudonymization and 
anonymization, including the possibility to adopt implementing acts to establish 
techniques and assessment criteria. DI supports the proposal. The scope of the definition 
of personal data has been continuously expanded through a series of decisions by the EU 
Court of Justice to a point where the concept of anonymization nearly ceased to exist. With 
the proposed changes, the Commission will not only be able to ensure a clearer legal 
 
position but also adapt to technological development. The risk is that companies may face 
increased burdens if the emergence of new technology constantly entails that data shifts 
from being anonymous to personally referable or pseudonymous. This will create 
uncertainty and lead to a chilling effect similar to what we see in the current set of rules. 
The Commission should keep this risk in mind when practically implementing 
implementing acts under Article 41a. Consideration can also be given to adding an actual 
definition of "anonymized data" . 
The proposal also includes that the development and operation of AI models and systems 
can be carried out with legitimate interest as a basis for processing (except where special 
legislation requires consent). Among other things, an exception is proposed for processing 
special categories of personal data ("sensitive data") in AI development, if it is not 
intended, and data is only processed "residually" , under certain security measures. DI 
supports the proposal, as the Commission, with the amendment, emphasizes that GDPR 
also supports new technologies such as AI, which is necessary to strengthen Europe's 
competitiveness. The scope of the exception is, however, not immediately clear. It is thus 
uncertain to what extent the proposed exception will alleviate the challenge of processing, 
for example, health data in connection with training and use of AI. This particularly affects 
the development of AI solutions in the life science industry, where it is often unavoidable 
(and intentional) to process health data to develop the solution. The proposal could 
advantageously be combined with clarification (a whitelist) of which interests constitute 
legitimate interests as well as a simplified balancing model. While the proposal could 
improve the private sector’s development and use of AI, there are still challenges with 
public authorities’ use of AI in case processing, which under current rules require close to 
explicit authority in special legislation. The process of updating special legislation is 
cumbersome and time-consuming with significant risks that the legislator inadvertently 
restricts its possibilities, for example by referring to specific technology in the draft law 
instead of keeping the rules technology-neutral. This also hampers private suppliers to the 
public sector. 
A further exception to the duty to inform is proposed in cases where processing does not 
involve high risk, and it can reasonably be expected that the data subject already has the 
information (e.g., customer relationships in small businesses). DI supports the intention of 
the proposal, but the current proposal will, in DI’s view, rarely be usable in practice. The 
Commission could have advantageously chosen a line similar to the exemption for Article 
30 records and possibly extended it to all business sizes. This would save companies 
significant resources and ensure focus on processing with high risk. 
 
 
The Commission also proposes that consent for cookies is abolished for certain low-risk 
functions, e.g., technically necessary cookies, statistics, and certain measurements. It is 
proposed to establish a list of approved ("whitelisted") purposes for which consent is not 
required (e.g., for necessary services, security, and anonymous statistics). DI is neutral to 
the proposal. It could potentially be positive for the user experience with fewer and simpler 
cookie banners. The effect for companies is, however, unclear, as the requirement to store 
preferences for long periods may mean fewer users give consent. 
DI is concerned that the proposal does not address the significant practical challenges 
companies experience when using new data processors. The requirements for screening, 
including regarding third country transfers, are highly burdensome for companies and often 
ensure a very low level of data protection, as many suppliers only process general 
information, which will often already be publicly available. DI recommends that the 
documentation requirement for new suppliers becomes more risk-based, including by 
introducing a risk-based approach to third country transfers. 
 
Data 
With the proposal, the Commission intends that rules in the data area be consolidated 
under the Data Act, including the repeal of certain rules and various adjustments in the 
Free Flow of Non-personal Data Regulation (FFDR), Open Data Directive (ODD), and Data 
Governance Act (DGA). DI partially supports the proposal. It makes sense to merge the 
many regulations and directives in the data area, making it easier for companies to 
navigate the complex landscape of digital EU legislation. At the same time, it is uncertain 
whether the specific changes will have a significant effect on burden reduction, if much of 
the rules in FFDR, ODD, and DGA are maintained. 
 
The Commission also proposes a provision in the Data Act stating that data holders can 
refuse to grant access to trade secrets if there is a high risk of illegal disclosure to third 
countries with insufficient protection. DI supports the intentions of the proposal but 
believes there is still a need for unambiguous protection of trade secrets in the Data Act. 
Trade secrets are an independent intellectual property object, which under the EU’s Trade 
Secrets Directive (2016/943) can only be maintained if the holder has full control over 
 
access. Any disclosure involves a risk of loss of exclusivity and therefore a potentially 
irreversible loss of value. Once trade secrets are shared externally, they lose their value for 
the company. Trade secrets are, therefore, by definition always high-risk materials, and will 
always be risky for the company. The protection of European companies’ trade secrets 
should be an absolute top priority for the EU if European competitiveness is to be ensured. 
The proposal focuses on third countries, but the risk also arises in sharing within the EU, 
where the strength of the internal market is competition-based. In addition, the proposal is 
unclear on how insecure (and secure) third countries are defined. If companies themselves 
are to make this assessment, it will create massive legal uncertainty. Finally, the proposal 
imposes additional responsibility on the data holder to document the reason for rejecting 
data sharing requests, which creates a risk of misuse of the rules in the Data Act to create 
unnecessary administration for competing companies. 
The Commission also intends to relax the requirements for switching cloud providers in the 
Data Act, including for SMEs and tailored SaaS solutions. DI supports the proposal, which 
is expected to reduce administrative burdens, especially for SMEs. It is particularly positive 
that it narrows which cloud providers are covered, as well as that contracts do not have to 
be revised retroactively. As several of the rules in the Data Act have already entered into 
force, there is a need to quickly adopt the proposed changes to ensure legal clarity for the 
companies covered. 
In addition to the above proposals, DI wishes to point out further challenges with the Data 
Act, which is not currently part of the omnibus proposal: 
First, there is uncertainty regarding the sharing of mixed datasets. The Data Act places 
significant demands on sharing and making data available, including datasets containing 
personal data covered by the GDPR. In such cases, a disproportionately large burden is 
imposed on the companies responsible for making the correct legal assessments about 
whether the dataset can be shared and which parts of it are subject to which rules. Clear 
rules are needed about when a company can be exempt from the requirements of the Data 
Act in cases of mixed datasets. 
Second, there are overlapping rules on data transfers to countries outside the EU (third 
country transfers), currently regulated in three different EU instruments: the DGA, Data Act 
and GDPR. These overlaps significantly increase complexity and create legal uncertainty 
regarding the preparation of new contracts for third country transfers, including the EU’s 
standard contractual clauses (SCCs). DI therefore suggests removing rules on third country 
 
transfers in the DGA and Data Act Chapter VII. Instead, both regulations should refer to the 
applicable rules in the GDPR. 
Finally, certain types of legacy devices can advantageously be excluded from the data 
sharing requirements, as these requirements pose a significantly greater burden on 
businesses. Here, one can follow the approach in the General Product Safety Regulation 
(GPSR). 
Cyber 
With the proposal, the Commission intends to establish a single entry point for reporting 
security incidents (“Single Entry-Point”). The platform is to be developed and operated by 
ENISA as a digital gateway, where companies and organizations can submit reports, which 
are then automatically forwarded to the relevant authorities nationally and at the EU level. 
Companies must only report once, even if the incident triggers several different reporting 
obligations (e.g., NIS2, GDPR, DORA, CER, eIDAS, etc.). According to the proposal, the 
entry point must be interoperable with the European Business Wallet. 
 
DI partially supports the proposal. It is positive that the Commission harmonizes reporting 
across legislation and Member States. But to create a noticeable burden reduction, the 
joint platform should also be accompanied by joint templates, definitions, and deadlines 
across legislation, including NIS2, GDPR, DORA, CER, and CRA. In particular, the lack of 
harmonization (and extension) of the various deadlines will continue to make it more 
complex to assess what must be reported and when. In addition, Denmark already has a 
consolidated reporting platform at Virk.dk. To ensure a positive effect for businesses in 
Denmark, DI proposes that reporting of security incidents to Virk.dk is automatically 
forwarded to the EU's new platform, so complexity is not further increased in the Danish 
context. 
The above proposal is further suggested to be supplemented by the introduction of joint EU 
templates for incident reporting by EDPB under both NIS2 and GDPR (for data breaches), 
so reporting is streamlined across Member States and legislation. It is proposed that the 
Commission is given authority to lay down more specific requirements for form, structure, 
and content for reporting. In the longer term, there is the possibility for further 
harmonization across sector legislation (e.g., DORA, energy, aviation, etc.). DI partially 
supports the proposal. Joint reporting templates can reduce administration and make it 
easier for companies to act on security incidents, but it should be possible to create a joint 
 
template for DORA, CRA, and other relevant legislation. In addition, the same points as 
above regarding consistent definitions and deadlines apply. As the content requirements 
for reporting under NIS2, GDPR etc. are different, and since NIS2 requires multiple reports, 
it should be considered whether the content of the reports should also be standardized in 
the legislation itself. Furthermore, implementation of the NIS2 directive across the EU is 
fragmented, so that some Member States impose significantly stricter requirements for 
reporting time and content. 
The Commission also proposes a new principle for reporting security incidents, where 
reporting must only be done once for NIS2 and Cyber Resilience Act (CRA). DI partially 
supports the proposal, which helps reduce double regulation. However, the proposal may 
lead to a risk that software errors will not be detected if this does not have to be reported.

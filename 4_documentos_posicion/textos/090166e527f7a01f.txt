1. Introduction 
The EU`s Competitiveness Compass has identified the simplification of the regulatory 
environment as one of the horizontal enablers to improve competitiveness across all sectors. 
We fully share this view, as our companies increasingly perceive EU-Regulation as a burden 
that threatens their innovation capabilities and competitiveness.  
 
The European machinery and equipment manufacturers feel the regulatory burden even more 
than other sectors, as they must comply with many technology-specific and sectorial pieces of 
legislation and, as an export-oriented sector, compete with companies in other regions that have 
a lighter regulatory burden. In addition, the sector is dominated by SMEs or midcaps that 
innovate in small series and cannot spread compliance costs over large series and don’t have 
large resources to handle regulatory issues.  
 
As a digitalization of industry advances, almost all machinery provided by our sector is 
digitalized, connected and innovation is increasingly driven by digital technologies. Machinery 
and Manufacturing equipment is in scope of the EU´s acquis for digital regulation, which is, 
together with other regulatory areas, increasingly becoming a burden for innovation and 
competitiveness, outweighing the benefits in terms of legal certainty and harmonization.  
2. Summary      
VDMA welcomes in principle the Digital Omnibus proposals of the EU-Commission as the first 
small step in the right direction: It is positive that three of the most burdensome and complex 
pieces of legislation are addressed: the AI Act, the Data Act and the General Data Protection 
Regulation (GDPR). However, it is very disappointing that the Cyber Resilience Act was not 
addressed.   
 
The Digital Omnibus is not what is needed to strengthen Europe's digital sovereignty and to 
reduce the gap with the US and China in certain technology areas. The proposed changes to 
the EU digital rulebook are good, but not enough to keep the promises of the EU´s simplification 
agenda and to follow the recommendations of the Draghi-Report. The Digital Omnibus still 
leaves many open questions and a still considerable regulatory burden. A more substantial 
revision is necessary to reach a critical threshold of simplification.   
 
Therefore, VDMA has 4 requests for the EU legislators:   
 
• Maintain the simplifications proposed by the EU-Commission, particularly the 
simplifications regarding the GDPR. 
• Data Act: The proposed amendments to the Data Act are correct in themselves, but 
overall, they do not represent a fundamental relief and do not create any new 
 
 
 
 3 
entrepreneurial freedom. The opportunity to create legal certainty, e.g. by clarifying 
terminology, has not been used. We call for more substantial and clearer modifications, 
e.g. excluding B2B business agreements from the mandatory data sharing. 
Entrepreneurial freedom should be a key element of this legislation.    
• AI-Act: The AI-Omnibus-proposal contains purposeful clarifications and improvements, 
for example by linking the timeline of high-risk provisions to the availability of standards 
or by extending simplified rules to SMC´s. It is not addressing, however, simplification in 
the field of industrial and physical AI (Article 6 (1) and Annex I). This is disappointing as 
notably the products (incl. machines) regulated here pose low risks as they are already 
regulated in a technology-neutral way. This is an opportunity to simplify without 
increasing risk which must not be missed.  
• Cybersecurity: The Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) and Network and Information Systems 2 
(NIS 2) are important acts for increasing resilience and security. They both have, 
however, technical flaws. The CRA is causing substantial difficulties in supply chains, in 
particular regarding complex products such as machinery. Therefore, it is disappointing 
that the substance of CRA and NIS 2 has not been covered by Digital Omnibus. We call 
upon policymakers to urgently perform a reality check on the CRA and NIS 2.   
3.  Analysis and suggestions for amendments 
3.1 Digital Omnibus on AI Regulation Proposal 
(COM (2025) 836 final) 
Context: 
The AI-Omnibus proposal is not living up to the promise to be a major simplification package. 
While it contains a few limited elements that could be seen as incremental improvements or 
simplifications, it ultimately falls far short of the comprehensive simplification originally 
announced. As a result, fundamental problems remain unaddressed. 
The implementation of the AI Act continues to pose major challenges for companies and 
authorities. The main issue is the ambiguous “high-risk” classification, which creates major legal 
uncertainties. In particular, the interaction between the legal acts listed in Annex I (e.g. the 
Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230) and the AI Act creates uncertainty for companies and 
results in double regulation of products. 
In the interest of coherent, proportionate and user-friendly regulation, VDMA, therefore, 
maintains its core demand: the deletion of Article 6 (1) and Annex I (A) of the AI Act (for more 
details see here) 
 
 
 
 4 
Article 1 (3), (8) and (9) of the proposal COM (2025) 836 introduce definitions and 
simplifications for the categories of “SMC/SME” in the following Articles of (EU) 2024/1689:  
 
Art. 3: Definitions for “SMC/SME” 
Art. 11 (1): simplifying technical documentation for “SMC/SME”  
Art. 17 (2): proportionality of obligations regarding quality management systems  
 
Assessment: This is a sensible cut to bureaucratic obligations.   
 
Recommendation: These new provisions should be maintained. Considering the 
overall burden the AI Act places on companies, it should be extended to all company 
sizes.  
 
Article 1 (6) and (14) reduce “the registration obligations for AI Systems providers of Annex III 
AI systems for which the provider has concluded not to be high-risk "   
 
Article 6 (4): modification 
Art 49 (2): deletion  
 
Assessment: This is a sensible cut to bureaucratic obligations. 
 
Recommendation: These new provisions should be maintained.  
 
Article 1 (12) of the proposal introduces a new Annex XIV, which contains notification codes for 
the NANDO database. 
 
Art 30 (2): modification 
Assessment: the proposed approach does not simplify the framework but instead risks 
creating substantial new complexity. The introduction of highly granular NANDO codes 
for high-risk AI systems would make implementation significantly more difficult for 
authorities, notified bodies and companies. Two concrete examples are: AIH 0201 - “AI 
systems that process structured data” and AIH0205 – “AI systems that learn from the 
environment, excluding agentic AI.” These illustrate the kind of ambiguity and 
interpretative uncertainty the new granular approach introduces: key concepts are not 
clearly defined, and the boundaries between categories become even harder to 
determine.  
Recommendation: delete Art. 1 point (12). We do not support the introduction of 
narrowly tailored granular NANDO codes for the high-risk category under the AI Act. 
Instead of simplifying the framework, they would undermine regulatory coherence and

create new layers of uncertainty at a time when the Omnibus package was supposed to 
deliver the opposite.  
Article 1 (13), of the proposal adds to Art. 43 (3) a limit to the application timeframe to the 
streamlined notification procedure to 18 months after the application of this regulation.  
 
Art. 43 (3): Introduction of a sub-paragraph with a time limit  
 
Assessment: We consider it absurd to place an expiration date on the bureaucracy  
 decreasing measures. The temporal limitation should be removed.    
 
Recommendation: Deletion: “Without prejudice to Article 28, such notified bodies which 
have been notified under the Union harmonisation legislation in Section A of Annex I, 
shall apply for designation in accordance with Section 4 at the latest [18 months from 
the entry into application of this Regulation].” 
 
Article 1 (13) of the proposal adds a fourth sub-paragraph to Art. 43 (3) that removes potential 
duplicate obligations for high-risk AI listed in Section A of Annex I that also falls within one of the 
categories listed in Annex III. 
 
Art. 43 (3): “Where a high-risk AI system is both covered by the Union harmonisation  
legislation listed in Section A of Annex I and it falls within one of the categories listed in 
Annex III, the provider of the system shall follow the relevant conformity assessment 
procedure as required under the relevant Union harmonisation legislation listed in 
Section A of Annex I.’;” 
 
Assessment: This is a very sensible and clarifying cut to bureaucratic obligations. 
 
Recommendation: Maintain Article 1 Point (13) and the proposed sub-paragraph. 
 
Article 1 (31) of the proposal changes Art. 113 to allow for delaying the entry into force for high-
risk AI obligations based upon an EU-Commission decision.  
 
 Art. 113 (3), new point (d):  
 
Assessment: We welcome the option to postpone the entry into force of the obligations 
applicable to the high-risk category, as the additional time affords a necessary interval 
for the ongoing standardization activities and increases the likelihood that the 
corresponding work can advance meaningfully before the obligations take effect. 
Ensuring an appropriate alignment between regulatory implementation and the 
availability of harmonized standards remains essential for legal certainty and effective 
compliance. At the same time, it is not yet clear whether the duration of the proposed

3.2 Digital Omnibus Regulation Proposal 
(COM(2025) 837 final) 
3.2.1 Amendments to Regulation (EU) 2023/2854 – 
Data Act  
Context: 
The Data Act does not meet the needs of the industry and has created a high level of legal 
uncertainty and bureaucracy for industrial companies. The nature and complexity of industrial 
data processing procedures, particularly in the manufacturing industry, must be adequately 
taken into account if competitiveness of European companies is to be increased by data 
sharing. The proposed amendments in the Digital Omnibus are rather disappointing. Instead of 
the necessary changes to certain obligations, it only contains technicalities. We do support the 
goal of creating some kind of “Data law handbook” which includes all EU data legislation. 
However, integrating every data regulation into the Data Act doesn’t help with implementation of 
the Data Act itself. The opportunity to create legal certainty to support implementation of the 
Data Act and thus reducing bureaucratic burden for the industry was missed.    
 
Omnibus proposal COM (2025) 837 – Amendments to Regulation (EU) 2023/2854  
 
Article 1 paragraph 2 (b) amends the definition of “data holder” in Article 2 point (13) of the 
Data Act:  
 
‘(13) ‘data holder’ means a natural or legal person that has the right or obligation, in 
accordance with this Regulation, applicable Union law or national legislation adopted in 
accordance with Union law, to use or make available data, including, where contractually 
agreed, product data or related service data, which it has retrieved or generated during 
the provision of a related service; 
 
Assessment: The definition of the term "data holder" is only marginally adjusted. 
Instead of "using and providing data", it should read "using or providing data". This is 
disappointing, and the amendment does not bring any clarity to the term whatsoever and 
 
 
 
 7 
thus does not help in evaluating under what circumstances an enterprise becomes a 
data holder. The definition is still circular and differs from the definition the EU 
Commission refers to in their FAQ document.  
 
Recommendation: The definition should be adapted to at least match the definition the 
EU Commission lays down in their FAQ document.  
 
Article 1 paragraph 3 replaces Article 4 paragraph 8 of the Data Act with the following text:   
 
‘8. In exceptional circumstances, where the data holder who is a trade secret holder is 
able to demonstrate that, despite the technical and organisational measures taken by 
the user pursuant to paragraph 6 of this Article, it is highly likely to suffer serious 
economic damage from the disclosure of trade secrets or that the disclosure of trade 
secrets to the user poses a high risk of unlawful acquisition, use, or disclosure to third 
country entities, or entities established in the Union under the direct or indirect control of 
such entities, which are subject to jurisdictions offering weaker or non-equivalent 
protection compared to that under Union law, that data holder may refuse on a case-by-
case basis a request for access to the specific data in question. That demonstration shall 
be duly substantiated on the basis of objective elements, such as the enforceability of 
trade secrets protection in third countries, the nature and level of confidentiality of the 
data requested, and the uniqueness and novelty of the connected product. It shall be 
provided in writing to the user without undue delay. Where the data holder refuses to 
share data pursuant to this paragraph, it shall notify the competent authority designated 
pursuant to Article 37.’ 
 
Assessment: VDMA welcomes the efforts to protect trade secrets more 
comprehensively despite the obligation to provide data to users. However, the proposed 
amendment of Article 4 paragraph 8 is more of a technicality than further protection of 
trade secrets.  
 
Recommendation: Exclude trade secret from the scope of the Data Act entirely. This 
would lead to broader acceptance of the data sharing obligations and thus would support 
creating a real data economy.  
 
Article 1 paragraph 4 replaces Article 5 paragraph 11 of the Data Act with a similar text as 
Article 4 paragraph 8. Our recommendation remains the same: Exclude trade secrets from the 
scope of the Data Act entirely.  
 
Article 1 paragraph 5 replaces the title of Chapter 5 of the Data Act by the following:  
 
 
 
 
 8 
‘MAKING DATA AVAILABLE TO PUBLIC SECTOR BODIES, THE COMMISSION, THE 
EUROPEAN CENTRAL BANK AND UNION BODIES ON THE BASIS OF A PUBLIC 
EMERGENCY’ 
 
Assessment: VDMA welcomes that the rather vague term “exceptional need” is 
replaced by “public emergency”. This clarifies the scope of the capacity of public sector 
bodies to request data and therefore provides some more legal certainty. 
 
Recommendation: Maintain the new title of Chapter 5 and the corresponding changes 
to the Articles of Chapter 5 of the Data Act.  
 
Article 1 paragraph 15 adds paragraphs 1a in Article 31 of the Data Act:  
 
‘1a. The obligations laid down in Chapter VI, with the exception of Article 29, and in 
Article 34 shall not apply to data processing services other than those referred to in 
Article 30(1), where the majority of features and functionalities of the data processing 
service has been adapted by the provider to the specific needs of the customer, if the 
provision of such services is based on a contract concluded before or on 12 September 
2025. 
The provider of such data processing services shall not be required to renegotiate or 
amend a contract for the provision of those services before its expiry if that contract was 
concluded before or on 12 September 2025. Any contractual provision contained in that 
contract that is contrary to Article 29 (1), (2), or (3) shall be considered null and void. 
 
Assessment: VDMA welcomes the proposed exceptions to the provisions of the cloud 
switching regulations for data processing services that were specifically adapted to the 
needs of individual customers based on contracts concluded before 12 September 2025. 
 
Recommendation: Maintain this amendment.  
 
3.2. 2 Amendments to Regulation 2016/679/EU 
(GDPR) 
Context: 
The GDPR has a uniform data protection approach. The Regulation doesn’t differentiate 
between data processing activities but rather sets the same prerequisites for any processing of 
personal data, irrelevant of the risk for the data subject affected. This approach is appropriate 
for companies, whose business models rely on the processing of large volumes of personal 
data. In such cases data privacy should be practiced in a comprehensive way to ensure that 
natural persons and their personal data are protected. However, when it comes to situations 
 
 
 
 9 
where personal data is merely a byproduct and is not processed any further, there should be 
exemptions from certain provisions of the GDPR, which impose a huge organisational burden 
on companies, especially on small and medium enterprises. 
 
 
Comments on Amendments to Regulation 2016/679/EU on the GDPR: 
 
Article 3 (1) of the proposal COM (2025) 837 final adds the following sentences to Article 4 a 
point (1) of the GDPR: 
 
“Information relating to a natural person is not necessarily personal data for every other 
person or entity, merely because another entity can identify that natural person. Information 
shall not be personal for a given entity where that entity cannot identify the natural person to 
whom the information relates, taking into account the means reasonably likely to be used by 
that entity. Such information does not become personal for that entity merely because a 
potential subsequent recipient has means reasonably likely to be used to identify the natural 
person to whom the information relates.” 
 
Assessment: VDMA welcomes this addition, as it makes sure that information is not always 
personal data. This is also the case in the machinery sector where data is often machinery 
data and where the processing of personal data is of low-risk. VDMA would welcome here 
an explicit addition that the GDPR is not applicable when it does not concern personal data. 
 
Recommendation: Maintain Article 3 (1) and the addition to Article 4 (1) 
 
 
Article 3 (5) of the proposal replaces Article 13 (4) of the GDPR: 
 
“Paragraphs 1, 2 and 3 shall not apply where the personal data have been collected in the 
context of a clear and circumscribed relationship between data subjects and a controller 
exercising an activity that is not data-intensive and there are reasonable grounds to assume 
that the data subject already has the information referred to in points (a) and (c) of 
paragraph 1, unless the controller transmits the data to other recipients or categories of 
recipients, transfers the data to a third country, carries out automated decision-making, 
including profiling, referred to in Article 22(1), or the processing is likely to result in a high 
risk to the rights and freedoms of data subjects within the meaning of Article 35.” 
 
Assessment: VDMA welcomes this clarification, because the extensive information 
obligations require a huge organisational effort. The proposed amendment could reduce part 
of this organisational burden. 
 
 
 
 
 10 
Recommendation: Maintain Article 3 (5) of the proposal and replacement of Article 13 
(4) GDPR  
 
Art. 3(8) (a) of the proposal replaces Article 33 (1) of the GDPR: 
 
“In the case of a personal data breach that is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and 
freedoms of natural persons, the controller shall without undue delay and, where feasible, 
not later than 96 hours after having become aware of it, notify the personal data breach via 
the single-entry point established pursuant to Article 23a of Directive (EU) 2022/2555 to the 
supervisory authority competent in accordance with Article 55 and Article 56. Where the 
notification to the supervisory authority is not made within 96 hours, it shall be accompanied 
by reasons for the delay.” 
 
Assessment: VDMA is supportive of this statement. Not only because the notification 
obligation to inform supervisory authorities of data breaches has been extended from 72 to 
96 hours but also because of a limitation to high risks.  
 
Recommendation: Maintain Art. 3 (8) and the changes to Art. 33 (1) GDPR 
 
 
Article 3 (9) of the proposal replaces Article 35 (4), (5) and (6) of the GDPR:  
 
o “The Board shall prepare and transmit to the Commission a proposal for a list of the kind 
of processing operations which are subject to the requirement for a data protection 
impact assessment pursuant to paragraph 1. 
o The Board shall prepare and transmit to the Commission a proposal for a list of the kind 
of processing operations for which no data protection impact assessment is required.  
o The Board shall prepare and transmit to the Commission a proposal for a common 
template and a common methodology for conducting data protection impact 
assessments.” 
 
Assessment: VDMA is supportive of this amendment, as it will lead to clarity as to which 
activities need such an impact assessment. 
 
Recommendation: Maintain Article 3 (9) and the modifications of Article 35 (4), (5) and (6) 
of the GDPR 
 
Article 3 (10) adds a new Article (Art. 41a) to the GDPR 
 
“The Commission may adopt implementing acts to specify means and criteria to determine 
whether data resulting from pseudonymisation no longer constitutes personal data for 
certain entities.” 
 
 
 
 11 
 
Assessment: VDMA welcomes this amendment and the intention of the Commission to 
adopt implementing acts as pseudonymised data cannot be considered personal data in all 
cases. The GDPR is in principle not applicable to pseudonymised data, when the enterprise 
handling this data isn't able to identify a natural person to which the data relates. 
 
Recommendation: Maintain Art. 3 (10) and new Art. 41a

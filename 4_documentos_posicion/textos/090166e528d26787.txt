Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 - AI Act 
1. Availability of Standards 
 
Overall, a s acknowledged in the AI Continent Action Plan, the AI Act’s success will 
primarily depend on how workable its rules are in practice and on its successful 
implementation. Ensuring that horizontal workstreams arising from the AI Act are 
functional for the financial sector is vital.  
 
Several deliverables are key to successful implementation in the financial sector:  
 
- The European standards that CEN and CENELEC are tasked to develop are essential 
to allow banks to complement and adjust their current procedures and practices. 
The delay in their delivery complicates banks' compliance efforts. The possibility 
introduced through the AI Omnibus to ‘stop the clock’ for high -risk requirements is 
a very positive step. However, the uncertainty to reach a political agreement before 
the deadline for entry into application of the relevant high -risk obligations creates 
compliance uncertainty. It would be advisable to consider separating the ‘stop-the-
clock’ provision from the other proposed changes in the proposed Omnibus for AI 
to help reach a quick political agreement between the co-legislators.  
 
- Guidelines on the interplay between the AI Act and the Union legislation on financial 
services are crucial. The publication of the preliminary work by EIOPA and the EBA 
is a positive step, but the Commission’s delivery of comprehensive guidelines 
remains c ritical and should be expedited, as their current timeline suggests they 
would be issued quite late in the process.  
 
2. Definition of an AI system 
The AI Act applies only to AI applications that meet the legal definition of an AI system. 
However, the definition is vague, causing legal ambiguity about what qualifies as an AI 
system and how it differs from traditional software systems. This uncertainty leads to 
inconsistencies in application and potential legal challenges  across all sectors affected 
by the AI Act. 
While conventional IT systems without machine learning or self-optimization clearly do 
not meet the AI Act´s criteria of an AI system, the situation is less clear for 
fundamentally statistical methods  like linear and logistic regression , which have been 
widely used in the banking sector for many years, for instance, in the assessment of 
credit risks. These methods are fully transparent, have very limited learning capabilities 
and, therefore, do not pose the AI -specific risks the AI Act aims to address. The 
European Commission´s February 2025 Guidelines clarify that long -established 
regression models should not be considered AI systems. However, since these 
guidelines are not legally binding, some supervisory authorities in Member States 
continue to classify them as AI systems. This inconsistency undermines harmonized 
supervisory practice and creates legal uncertainty. 
To resolve this, we believe it would be prudent to include a direct clarification in the AI 
Act that regression models are excluded from the definition of AI systems, ensuring 
consistent interpretation across Member States and allowing the AI Act to focus on the 
risks associated with advanced AI technologies, rather than including decades -old 
statistical methods. 
3. Integration of AI Act and Financial Services Regulations 
The financial services sector is highly regulated at both national and EU levels. 
Accordingly, the European Parliament recognized in its November 2025 resolution that 
existing sectoral regulations for the financial services sector are largely sufficient to  
cover the deployment of AI. 
The AI Act takes the existing financial services law into account only selectively, 
allowing limited derogations for financial institutions where equivalent requirements 
exist in EU financial services law. In this context, Article 17(4) stands out as a rar e 
example of successful regulatory integration: it allows financial institutions to use 
governance systems under frameworks like CRD IV to meet the quality management 
requirements of the AI Act. 
However, this integration mechanism only applies to quality management systems, 
without providing guidance on aligning other obligations of the AI Act with sectoral 
frameworks. Articles 9 to 15 set out broad requirements that financial institutions must 
navigate without clear guidance on how they align with existing regulations, leading to 
redundant compliance structures. Most AI Act  obligations are already addressed by 
other frameworks (e.g., CRR/CRD, DORA, CCD, MCD, and PSD), as recently 
highlighted by the EBA in its gap analysis from November 2025. 
To improve regulatory clarity, it is crucial to extend the approach seen in Article 17(4) 
to other areas where overlaps between the AI Act and existing financial services 
regulations exist. By allowing financial institutions to rely on established sectoral 
frameworks for compliance with  other requirements of the AI Act —such as those 
related to risk management, data management, and transparency —the regulatory 
burden could be significantly reduced. This would not only streamline compliance 
processes but also ensure that the AI Act's implementation is in harmony with existing 
regulatory structures. 
We urge that similar provisions be included throughout the AI Act to mitigate 
complexity, reduce legal uncertainty and foster a n efficient regulatory environment , 
ultimately supporting AI adoption in the financial services sector.

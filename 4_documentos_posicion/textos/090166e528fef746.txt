Summary 
 
ZVEI welcomes the European Commission’s initiative to streamline and adjust the EU’s data, cyber security 
and data protection framework through the Omnibus VII. The Commission’s recognition of the need to en-
hance Europe’s competitiveness and to strengthen the conditions for data-driven innovation is both timely 
and necessary. 
At the same time, despite the broadly supported objectives underlying the proposed amendments, the cur-
rent proposal falls short of addressing the practical realities and needs of industrial data ecosystems  and 
even create more legal uncertainties.  
 
To avoid undermining the competitiveness of data-driven industries, mandatory B2B data-sharing obliga-
tions should be repealed wherever no demonstrable market failure exists, and at the very least, effective 
protection of trade secrets must be ensured for both B2B and B2C relationships. However, instead of 
tackling these structural shortcomings of the Data Act in a comprehensive and forward-looking manner or 
consolidating data-related legislation into a coherent regulatory framework, the proposal largely con fines it-
self to incremental adjustments.  
It is also regrettable that the Omnibus package does not meaningfully engage with the Cyber Resilience 
Act. In its current form, certain provisions of the CRA risk imposing disproportionate burdens on Euro-
pean companies and undermining their competitiveness in global markets. Addressing these issues should 
have been an integral part of a horizontal initiative aimed at improving the overall digital and data regulatory 
framework. 
 
By contrast, the proposed amendments in the area of data protection and the GDPR demonstrate a more 
constructive and targeted approach and point in the right direction. However, isolated improvements in indi-
vidual instruments are not sufficient to achieve the broader objective of a competitive, innovation-friendly reg-
ulatory environment. 
It is now incumbent upon the European Parliament and the Council to decisively address these shortcomings 
and establish a coherent, competitive legal framework aligned with Europe’s digital and industrial ambitions. 
The following recommendations set out concrete measures to better support industry and innovation. 
 
 
 
1 Data Act  
 
1.1 Exclusion of data sharing obligations in business-to-business relations 
 
ZVEI supports the objectives of the Data Act to strengthen the data economy and digitalization. However, the 
uniform application of Chapters II and III to both B2B and B2C contexts, combined with an overly simplified 
view of industrial data sharing, creates significant legal and technical uncertainty. The trade secret hand-
brake mechanism fails to provide effective protection, while the high threshold for refusing data access 
based on serious economic harm is impractical and risks incentivising data minimisation . The proposed ex-
ception for entities from third countries addressed in the Commission’s proposal is, in principle, welcome; 
however, it is incomplete and does not constitute a substantive novelty and fails to adequately address the 
insufficient protection of trade-secret-relevant data. The severe risk of sharing sensitive information or re-
verse engineering is not exclusive to third countries but equally applies to malicious actors within Europe.  
Consequently, companies may be incentivized to design their products in a more data-efficient manner, 
thereby undermining the very objectives the Data Act aims to achieve. In light of these concerns, if Europe 
does not repeal the Data Act entirely, we strongly recommend the repeal of any data sharing obligations

1.2 Trade Secret Protection  
 
Trade secrets are one of the most valuable strategic assets of companies and a key factor in sustaining 
European competitiveness. Mandatory data-sharing obligations that compel companies to disclose business-
critical information without ensuring robust and enforceable protection of trade secrets are fundamentally at 
odds with the EU Trade Secrets Directive. By normalising the forced sharing of commercially sensitive 
data even in the absence of demonstrable market failure, the current approach risks hollowing out a core pil-
lar of Europe’s innovation and competitiveness framework. A regulatory regime that weakens trade secret 
protection through broad and insufficiently safeguarded disclosure obligations undermines legal certainty, 
discourages investment, and contradicts the EU’s own commitment to effective intellectual property protec-
tion. Moreover, effective safeguards for trade secrets protection are particularly important in a B2C context 
where the trade secrets holder usually deals with largely unknown private actors with limited enfor cement 
potential. Proving serious economic damage and irreparable loss, even ex -ante, is almost impossible as the 
risk of disclosure is not apparent before sharing sensitive data. It should be made clear that mandatory data 
sharing under the Data Act must not be construed as conferring trade secret ownership on any other 
party than the trade secret holder. Even where such data access is made subject to the agreement of ap-
propriate technical and organisational measures pursuant to Article 4(6) and Article 5(9), the user’s entitle-
ment to data access does not create any status as a holder or co-holder of trade secrets within the meaning 
of Directive (EU) 2016/943, nor should it be treated as doing so. It must therefore be expressly stated that 
the data holder is and remains the sole holder of the trade secrets concerned, and that the user’s access to 
data neither establishes nor alters this legal position.

1.3 Limit cloud switching rules to Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS) Provider 
 
Under a potential interpretation of the Data Act’s definition of “data processing services,” the regulation might 
be seen as applying indiscriminately to all cloud service models, despite substantial differences between 
them. ZVEI generally welcomes the EU Commission’s proposal to exempt “custom-made” services and “data 
processing services that are not off-the-shelf” from the switching rules for contracts signed before 12 Sept. 
2025 (“Legacy Contracts”). Interoperability and switching might be technically and economically feasible for 
Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS). However, this is not the case for Platform as a Service (PaaS) and Soft-
ware as a Service (SaaS) in business to business context, since those services are highly customised, 
deeply integrated into business processes and development lifecycles, and rely on tightly coupled architec-
tures that are not easily portable or interoperable and lack rapid-provisioning capabilities. The EU Commis-
sion rightly reconfirms that Chapter VI applies only to data processing services, where the digital ser-
vice itself, incl. SaaS, can be rapidly provisioned or released with minimal management effort/service 
provider interaction (see FAQ 58a). However, in contrary to the Commission’s proposal, a genuine advance 
in legal certainty and innovation-friendly regulation would require exempting PaaS and SaaS from the 
scope of Chapter VI for all companies, regardless of their size and to extend the exemptions in Arti-
cles 31 (1a and 1b) for contracts signed on or after 12 September 2025.

2 GDPR 
 
The European Commission’s Digital Omnibus proposal also proposes targeted amendments to the GDPR 
with the aim to update and fine-tune the GDPR with necessary adjustments. The most relevant topics are as 
follows:  
 
 
2.1 Definition of "Personal Data"  
 
The Digital Omnibus refines what constitutes "personal data" through the concept of "relative identifiability," 
with clear criteria to be established under the new Article 41a empowerment. Specifically, data is only consid-
ered personal if the company processing it can reasonably identify the individual.  
We ask for upholding the "relative identifiability" concept, update and maintaining the proposed em-
powerment to establish clear criteria for "relative identifiability," enabling clearer guidelines on when industrial 
data qualifies as anonymous versus personal data. This approach would provide clearer guidelines for when 
such industrial data qualifies as anonymous versus personal data, enabling more confident data utilization 
for analytics, AI development, and operational optimization while maintaining robust privacy protection where 
identification is actually possible.  
 
 
2.2 New cookie rules with complications  
 
The Digital Omnibus aims to streamline cookie consent processes by integrating the rules into GDPR 
through Article 88a. The objective is to reduce consent banner fatigue and create clearer consent mecha-
nisms.  
However, the challenge is that the new rules establish two separate regulatory frameworks - Article 88a 
GDPR for personal data and Article 5(3) ePrivacy Directive for non-personal data. This means companies 
must categorize each type of data processed, which is often complex to determine. This dual approach in-
creases operational complexity rather than achieving the intended simplification.  
We ask for adjustment for planned cookie framework and simplifying the proposed dual regulatory 
approach under Article 88a GDPR and Article 5(3) ePrivacy Directive to create a unified framework that 
genuinely reduces operational complexity rather than increasing it.  
 
 
 
9 
 
2.3 Protection against misused personal data requests  
 
The Digital Omnibus provides companies with enhanced mechanisms under Article 12(5) GDPR to address 
data access requests (Article 15) that are manifestly unfounded, excessive, or being used for purposes unre-
lated to data protection - such as tactical leverage in legal disputes or automated mass submissions.

Companies face data access requests that are not made for genuine data privacy compliance purposes, but 
rather as discovery tactics in court proceedings or compliance investigations to obtain evidence that compa-
nies possess. These requests consume significant compliance resources while serving purposes unrelated 
to data protection rights. The enhanced provisions would provide clearer grounds to refuse requests that are 
clearly being used as alternative discovery mechanisms rather than legitimate exercises of data subject 
rights, allowing companies to focus compliance resources on genuine privacy concerns.  
 
We ask for upholding the new measures to reduce abusive or excessive data subject requests and pre-
serving the enhanced mechanisms under Article 12(5) GDPR to address data access requests that 
are manifestly unfounded, excessive, or used for purposes unrelated to data protection ri ghts.  
 
 
 
3 Cyber Resilience Act  
 
It is very unfortunate that the European Commission did not use the Digital Omnibus as an oppor-
tunity to undertake the necessary revisions of the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA). If the legal framework 
remains unchanged, there is a risk of serious supply chain disruptions, particularly in the field of mi-
croelectronics. This would result in production interruptions, product discontinuations and a potential reloca-
tion of production facilities. To decrease the chances for such scenarios and to ease the burden on European 
manufacturers, we propose the following measures. 
 
 
3.1 Exemption of "non-critical products" without cybersecurity risk from the CRA  
 
Many connected products – such as DAB radios, bike computers, radio clocks, barcode scanners, analog -to-
digital converters or integrated microchips – do not pose a relevant cybersecurity risk. Although they transmit 
data and are therefore covered by the CRA, this data is exclusively trivial and often processed within a single 
device. If, for example, only audio data and simple operating and status information are transmitted without 
access to other IT systems or security-relevant functions, this does not create a relevant attack surface in 
cyberspace. The same applies to most microelectronic components. Many integrated microchips only trans-
mit control and status information within a device (e.g. on/off, speed, temperature, fill level), usually via sim-
ple internal interfaces. Due to their installation situation and limited capabilities, they simply do not pose a 
cybersecurity risk.  
 
Manufacturers have developed these non-critical products and components without taking CRA into account 
and would now have to adapt their entire portfolio. A complete redesign by 2027 is practically impossible 
for many companies. This would result in product discontinuations, particularly in the semiconductor indus-
try, with serious consequences for supply chains.  
 
Practical example: A large international semiconductor manufacturer states that its product portfolio con-
sists of around 80,000 models; a complete redesign is not realistic. Exceptions for "non -critical" products with 
digital elements would significantly ease the burden here: according to estimates, around 60,000 models in 
this portfolio – i.e. 75 percent – could fall under this category.  
Removing these non-critical products from the scope of the CRA would significantly reduce the risk of bottle-
necks for important components such as microchips while maintaining a high level of security. At the same 
time, it would also make it significantly easier to place end products such as simple DAB radios or radio 
clocks on the market.  
 
A blueprint for such an approach can be found in the Electromagnetic Compatibility (EMC) Directive (Recital 
12), which stipulates that it “…should not regulate equipment which is inherently benign in terms of electro-
magnetic compatibility". A similar reference to products with digital elements that "are inherently benign in 
terms of cybersecurity risks" would be appropriate and sensible in the context of the CRA.  
10 
 
3.2 Simplified technical documentation for products in the "basic category" (without in-
creased cybersecurity risk)  
 
Under the CRA, manufacturers must, regardless of the CRA-category of the product, comprehensively de-
scribe in their technical documentation what function the product fulfils, how it is technically constructed, 
what cybersecurity risks may exist and what measures they use to control these risks. Article 33(5) of the 
CRA allows micro and small enterprises to submit this technical documentation in a simplified format. This 
simplification should be extended to all manufacturers for basic category products (not listed in An-
nexes III & IV). This category covers the majority of the CRA's scope. Especially digital consumer prod-
ucts (connected coffee machines, smart light bulbs, etc.) would benefit from this. According to a recent ZVEI 
survey, this would significantly facilitate the placing on the market of around 65% of the connected products 
of our member companies. 
 
 
3.3 Clarification that CRA compliance is possible for fully developed product models  
 
The CRA requires manufacturers to design and manufacture products with digital elements in such a way 
that they ensure an appropriate level of cybersecurity in view of the risks (Annex I, Part I, Number 1). This 
requirement must be interpreted in such a way that it is possible to re-qualify previously developed product 
models. Models of products with digital elements that are already available on the market must continue to 
be allowed to be placed on the market if it can be shown that they meet the essentia l technical cybersecurity 
requirements, even if no process taking into account the CRA legal text was applied during their original de-
velopment. There are product models on the market, for example in the semiconductor and railway indus-
tries, that have been developed over decades. They are needed by society and cannot be redesigned by De-
cember 2027 for purely technical reasons. It must therefore be clarified and stated that CRA compliance 
is possible for fully developed product models as long as they guarantee an appropriate level of cy-
bersecurity. This clarification can be provided, for example, in guidelines. 
 
 
3.4 Realistic, technically feasible timelines for the development of harmonised European 
standards  
 
Manufacturers must have sufficient time to incorporate the legal requirements specified in the standards into 
their development and production processes. There should be at least 36 months between the publica-
tion of the relevant harmonised standards in the Official Journal of the EU and the end of the CRA 
transition period – especially in the case of vertical, product-specific standards that trigger a pre-
sumption of conformity. If these standards are not available in time, so-called "important" class I products 
– such as routers, operating systems or microprocessors with security-related functionalities – would have to 
be certified by an external conformity assessment body. This affects around 21% of digital products in the 
electrical and digital industry. If an extension of the deadlines is not feasible, manufacturers of this product 
category should, in the interim, be able to demonstrate the conformity of their products with the CRA on their 
own responsibility (NLF Module A of internal production control), until the vertical standards are available. 
Once a vertical standard is available, an adequate transition period should be specified before it is applied. 
Otherwise, there is a risk of massive bottlenecks in placing products on the market, as was previously the 
case with the Medical Devices Regulation. 
 
 
3.5 Separation between physical and digital lifetimes when determining the support pe-
riod in the B2B sector  
 
During the support period for their products with digital elements, manufacturers are required to provide se-
curity updates free of charge. The length of this period is generally determined by the manufacturer itself, 
although it must take other relevant EU legislation into account. This leads to considerable challenges. Regu-
lations such as the Ecodesign Regulation require manufacturers to define the expected lifetime of products. 
Many industrial products have a physical lifespan of more than ten years, while their digital components are 
subject to much shorter innovation and support cycles. The CRA requirement could mean that the support 
period must not deviate significantly from the lifespan predicted under other regulations and that manufactur-
ers must provide free security updates until the end of a product's physical usability. This is a massive inter-
vention in common practice in the B2B sector. In order to enable risk-based and economically viable support 
11 
obligations, we therefore propose a clear regulatory distinction between the physical and digital life-
times of products with digital elements within the framework of the CRA .

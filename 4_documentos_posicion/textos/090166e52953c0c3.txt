EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
1. Proposal for a Regulation amending the Data Acquis. 
Telefónica supports the core objective of the Digital Omnibus: simplifying 
Europe’s digital regulatory environment. To achieve this ambition, the company 
urges the co -legislators to refine key provisions of the Data Act, clarify critical 
aspects of the GDPR, clarify some aspects of the functioning of the Single Point 
of Entry and fully repeal the outdated ePrivacy Directive. Ensuring harmonised, 
proportionate, and techn ically feasible rules will be essential for delivering a 
coherent, innovation -friendly framework that strengthens Europe’s digital 
leadership, accelerates investment, and enhances the resilience of its digital 
infrastructure. 
What Telefónica believes should be improved: 
1. Retain simplification as the guiding principle 
Telefónica warns that the current draft: 
• broadens obligations unnecessarily, 
• increases administrative burden, 
• and fails to address outdated or inconsistent provisions—particularly ePrivacy. 
2. Data Act – Key Concerns 
Data holder definition 
• The proposed redefinition (“use or make available data”) is:  
o unjustified, 
o operationally unworkable, 
o and creates legal uncertainty, especially for telecom operators. 
 
B2G data sharing 
• Requests:  
o clearer boundaries for metadata disclosure, 
o compensation for responding to emergency requests and 
anonymisation, 
o reinstatement of the option for public authorities to acquire 
non-personal data at market rates. 
 
Trade secrets framework 
• Calls for clear EU -level criteria to define “third countries with lower 
protection” . 
B2B exceptions & pre-contractual requirements

1.1. Amendments to Regulation (EU) 2023/ 2854 – the Data 
Act. 
• Data holder concept (Article 2. (b). 13 Data Act).  
Telefónica is concerned that the Digital Omnibus proposal to redefine the concept 
of data holder, from entities that “ use and make available data ” to those that “use 
or make available data” , significantly broadens the scope of the Data Act in a way 
that is neither justified nor operationally viable . We understand this change is 
due to the integration of other legislative texts. However, t his amendment risks 
classifying as data holder organisations that merely use data internally, without 
engaging in any form of data-sharing activity, thereby imposing obligations that were 
never intended for infrastructure operators or service providers whose functions are 
limited to proc essing data on behalf of users. Such an expansion creates legal 
uncertainty, particularly for telecommunications providers, whose internal 
operations, including network management, security and fraud detection, 
could be wrongly interpreted as activities triggering data -holder duties. 
Additionally, this proposal increases intervention and fails to simplify the legal 
framework as intended. 
• Business-to-Government Requests (B2G) (Article 15 Data Act). 
Telefónica welcomes the Commission’s decision to refine the Business -to-
Government (B2G) data -access regime by limiting mandatory disclosures under 
Chapter V of the Data Act strictly to public emergencies, thereby eliminating the 
overly broad and ambiguous notion of “exceptional need” . Telefónica also supports 
the proposal  to prioritize the sharing of non -personal data in such 
circumstances, recognizing that this approach appropriately  minimizes regulatory, 
operational and confidentiality risks.  
However, we do not support the shift in the nature of the article. The transition from 
Article 15 to Article 15a transforms an exceptional, safeguard-driven framework 
into a direct and enforceable obligation for data holders . This change reduces 
legal certainty and erodes the careful balance originally  established between the 
public interest and the protection of data holders’ legitimate interests.  
Additionally, several elements of the revised B2G framework remain problematic 
and require further reconsideration:  
- The obligation to  provide metadata  raises substantial concerns, particularly 
where such metadata may expose commercially sensitive information, 
network intelligence or operational vulnerabilities. The concept of metadata 
under the Data Act is broad and not clearly delimited , which may lead to

The Commission has rightly recognised that the ICT sector, generating EUR 791

Union’s overall economic competitiveness. However, from an industry perspective 
- especially the manufacturing side - a counterproductive trend is emerging:  in order 
to comply with the increasingly complex and often indeterminate obligations, 
manufacturers are deliberately limiting the amount of operational and usage data 
collected by their devices. S uch a reduction in data generation cannot be in the  
interest of the market , as it directly undermines the objective of strengthening 
Europe’s data-driven economy and risks widening the technological gap between 
the European Union and its global competitors.  
Against this background, we believe that narrowly tailored exemptions for B2B 
constellations should be introduced in the legislative process to ensure that the 
regulatory framework supports - rather than constrains – and simplifies, the

sectors. It is therefore essential that the provisions of chapter II and III of the D ata

relationships, where contractual  freedom and well -established commercial

exchange.  
• Pre-contractual information requirements (Article 3.2, 3 Data Act). 
Regarding pre-contractual information obligations, we believe a substantial

• Use of non-personal data. Art. 4 (13 y 14). 
A comprehensive possibility for consent-based use of non-personal data should be 
permitted without the imposition of additional purpose limitations ; and 
paragraphs 13 and 14 should be consolidated into a single, clearly structured 
provision. Such an approach would simplify the regulatory framework, enhance 
legal certainty, and more accurately reflect the operational realities of modern 
digital business models. 
 
• Possibility of imposing higher fees and specific conditions on very 
large enterprises. 
Further clarification is required with respect to (i) ensuring uniform application 
across Member States, (ii) establishing objective and transparent criteria for 
differentiated fees and conditions, and (iii) delineating the relationship between 
these measures and existing competition law instruments. 
Moreover, we emphasize that differentiated fee models for the re-use of public-
sector data by very large enterprises may have significant implications for 
Europe’s attractiveness and capacity for innovation . Companies that are 
research-intensive or heavily dependent on data for AI development rely critically 
on access to comprehensive public datasets. Increased fees in this context may 
operate as a de facto innovation surcharge, with the potential to influen ce 
investment decisions to the detriment of the Eu ropean digital ecosystem. Against 
this background, we encourage the Commission to carefully consider the broader 
effects of these rules on international R&D investment patterns and on the global 
competitiveness of Europe as a digital and technological hub. 
•  Definition “data processing service” 
We call for a clear and unambiguous definition in the Data Act specifying that a 
digital service, in and of itself, must cumulatively fulfil all characteristics set out in 
the definition in order to qualify as a data processing service. The clarification 
provided by the European Commission in  its FAQ should be explicitly reflected in 
the legal text. At a minimum, the recitals should be amended accordingly in order to 
eliminate this significant legal ambiguity and to close a critical loophole for the 
industry. 
 
• Cloud Switching 
The targeted exemption for certain customer-specific individual developments is, in 
principle, understandable. However, further regulatory clarification is required, in 
particular with regard to the proposed distinction between “custom-built” services 
and the newly introduced category of “custom -made services” pursuant to new 
Article 31(1a) DA -Draft, the interaction of this provision with existing contractual 
arrangements, and the practical scope of the remaining obligations under Chapter 
VI, such as interoperability requirements.

With respect to new Article 31(1a) DA -Draft, while the underlying legislative 
intention is acknowledged, the provision is fundamentally flawed and should

of clear rules o n retroactive application of Chapter VI, the current drafting

general, proportionate and legally coherent manner, for example by introducing

Data Act. Second, it introduces a new category of “custom -made services” that 
conflicts with the definition of “data processing services” in Article 2(8) DA. As 
currently formulated, this category woul d effectively neutralize the requirement of

coherence of the data processing services framework. If the genuine objective is to

the preferred option, or alternatively to introduce a proportionate transitional regime

with the established jurisprudence of the European Court of Justice on retroactivity 
and would preserve the integrity of the concept of data processing services.

1.2.1.  GDPR provisions:  
 
• Definition of “Personal Data” (Article 4.1 GDPR). 
Telefónica strongly welcomes the Commission’s proposal to revise Article 4(1) 
GDPR and introduce a new Recital 27, finally incorporating the CJEU’s nuanced, 
contextual approach to identifiability. The clarification that information  does not 
constitute personal data for a controller who lacks  reasonably available  means 
of identification and does not become personal data merely because another actor 
may have such means,  represents a decisive step towa rd restoring legal certainty 
and proportionality. Anchoring  the definition in  Breyer, Nowak and 
the Scania/Endemol line of jurisprudence will significantly reduce 
interpretative divergences across Member States and strengthen the role 
of pseudonymisation as a foundational Privacy -Enhancing Technology, enabling 
responsible data reuse, AI development, network  optimisation and cybersecurity 
analytics while maintaining high protection standards.  
However, to ensure that this reform delivers consistent and operational results, 
Telefónica encourages the co -legislators to clarify several points 
that remain uncertain in practice:  
- Specify how the revised definition applies depending on the role of the recipient, 
particularly distinguishing between controllers and processors . Regarding 
such roles and relations between parties, Telefonica considers that when a data 
controller makes available previously de-identified and/or pseudonymous data 
to third parties not having reasonable like means to reidentify a data subject, 
such sharing should not be considered as a sharing of personal data, neither 
from the perspective of the originator no r from the recipient. Therefore, this 
clarification should be introduced in the GDPR to avoid inconsistent 
interpretation in the EU. 
- Clarify how the “ reasonable expectations of the data subject ” should be 
balanced against the controller’s actual ability to  identify an individual .

• Use of Special Categories of Personal Data in AI Development 
(Article 9.2 k) and Article 9.5 GDPR). 
Telefónica welcomes the Commission’s proposal to introduce Articles 9.2 k), and 
Article 9.5 GDPR, establishing a narrowly defined derogation that allows the limited 
processing of special -category data when necessary for the development and 
operation of AI systems and models under Article 3.1 of the AI Act. Modern AI 
systems, particularly large-scale models, must be trained and tested on broad and 
representative datasets to avoid bias, ensure fairness and prevent discriminatory 
outputs. In many cases, this requires the ability to detect, control for, or 
algorithmically mitigate the presence of sensitive attributes. Telefónica therefore 
considers it appropriate that the incidental appearance of special -category 
data, despite the controller’s adoption of effective technical and organisational 
measures, should not automatically prohibit processing , provided that such 
data are removed or rendered ineffective as soon as identified.  
It should also be minded that the processing of special categories of data in the 
context of artificial intelligence systems is specifically regulated under the AI Act 
proposal. It is therefore essential to ensure proper coordination between both 
legal frameworks and to avoid situations in which the same conduct could be 
subject to parallel enforcement or sanctions under both the AI Act and the 
GDPR, in accordance with the principles of legal certainty, proportionality and ne 
bis in idem.

 
 
EPC position on the Proposal for a Regulation as regards the 
simplification of the digital legislative framework  
(Digital Omnibus) 
Transparency Register: 4456380381-30 
 
 
 
The European Publishers Council (EPC), representing leading news media and 
publishing organisations across Europe, strongly supports the simplification 
objectives of the European Commission aimed at supporting growth and 
competitiveness.   
 
We welcome the objectives of the Commission for a pragmatic reform of the 
ePrivacy Directive’s outdated rules on cookies and tracking technologies. The 
current consent -based framework generates severe “consent fatigue” among 
users, distorts the digital adv ertising market, and limits publishers’ ability to 
monetise quality journalism responsibly. Furthermore, these outdated rules are 
stifling innovation in privacy -enhancing technologies, as companies focus on 
navigating complex consent requirements rather than investing in new privacy-by-
design solutions such as secure data clean rooms that could offer both high privacy 
standards and sustainable business models.  
 
However, the proposed Digital Omnibus fails to meet these objectives. On the 
contrary, publishers are highly concerned that the provisions regarding processing 
of personal data in the terminal equipment of natural persons will in effect worsen 
the situation they find themselves in, while failing to improve the level of protection 
of online users’ privacy and their online experience.  
 
At a moment in time where media plurality and journalistic work has never played 
a more critical role in our European societies, this proposal will continue to benefit 
large online platforms at the expense of European publishers. It directly contradicts 
the European Commission’s message expressed by President Von der Leyen

Supporting the sustainability of free, independent and pluralistic news media 
Pluralistic, free and responsible journalism is one of the strongest pillars of 
democracy. It is important that independent journalism is given an opportunity to 
be run as a sustainable business. Regulation must recognise the unique role of 
trustworthy journalism in the digital information landscape and ensure its viability 
in online environments. 
 
It is essential that the specific impact that changes brought to the ePrivacy Directive 
would have on media viability be assessed, in order to ensure that policies do not 
accidentally undermine publishers' financial sustainability, but instead actively help 
the sector prosper. Making a media sustainability impact assessment would ensure 
that EU policy is coherent, future -proof and supports financial sustainability of 
editorial media, and democracy. 
 
This approach would be in line with the  Presidency Conclusions  on access to 
reliable news as part of the European Democracy Shield: “ Explore how to secure 
the best possible conditions for a free, independent and pluralistic news media 
environment within a competitive market so that media service providers can 
establish sustainable business models or make the necessary adjustments. This is 
especially relevant given that Big Tech platforms have gained an increasingly larger 
share of advertising revenue and AI technologies and AI -generated content could 
threaten existing business models as they benefit from news content from European 
media services without contributing to the creation of such content”.

Data plays a vital role in publishers’ ability to innovate and develop new products 
that respond to audience interests and attract new readers. Cookies and other 
tracking technologies, often relying on pseudonymisation technologies, are not 
solely linked t o advertising; they are also essential for measuring and 
understanding user engagement, for example through website analytics and 
audience behaviour tools that enable publishers to improve content, functionality, 
and user experience. These measurements hel p publishers understand the value 
and attractiveness of their content, as well as support their effort in developing 
innovative content, and increase their relevance towards their users. Content 
personalisation and recommendation is an essential element fo r publishers, 
allowing them to develop and adjust their sites functions, features and content to 
increase relevance to their users.  
 
Advertising is the main source of revenues for European publishers, followed by 
subscriptions. However, currently most of the subscription revenue originate from 
print subscriptions, which is a heavily declining stream, while publishers also 
observe a decr easing in users willingness to pay for digital news (especially 
younger audiences). Therefore, the share of publishers' revenue that will depend 
on advertising will grow and the survival of European media plurality will depend 
heavily on the ability to effectively refinance digital content through advertising4.  
 
Advertising accounts for between 50% and, in some cases, up to 100% of total 
revenue for most publishers. The vast majority of display advertising relies on 
various forms of data -driven targeting, which are essential for maintaining 
commercial viability. B y contrast, contextual advertising represents only a small 
share of publishers’ advertising revenue, primarily due to limited demand from 
advertisers for this type of inventory.  
 
Data, therefore, plays an important role to enable publishers to monetise their 
content, through the placement of advertising. Data supports many functionalities, 
from ad serving and performance measurement, including frequency capping 
functionality, first -party audience measurement, storage of privacy/consent 
choices; support for monetisation models as well as security and fraud prevention.   
 
Publishers also face unique challenges in sustaining their businesses, particularly 
as they must compete with very large platforms whose share of advertising 
revenues far outweighs that of the press, and which capture not only the largest 
portion of the ma rket but virtually all recent growth in digital advertising income. 
As a result, publishers’ share is remarkably low by comparison, which directly

Article 88a – Processing of personal data in the terminal equipment of natural 
persons 
The EPC welcomes the attempt of the European Commission to update the rules 
on cookies and other tracking technologies, and align the ePrivacy Directive rules 
for the storing and gaining access of information from the terminal equipment of a 
natural person with the GDPR. 
 
However, the proposed article 88a does not allow for the full application of article 
6 of the GDPR to cookies and other data access, failing to introduce the risk based 
approach of the GDPR to personal data collected from users’ terminal equipment. 
The pro posal is maintaining a very restrictive regime for publishers, further 
conditioning any data processing from a user’s terminal equipment to users 
consent. 
 
Paradoxically, the proposal simultaneously allows a more liberal regime for data 
processing needed for the development and operation of an AI system, or an AI 
model which can be pursued for legitimate interest within the meaning of Article 
6(1)f of the GDPR, further enabling AI systems to access publishers content in most 
cases outside of the limitations set under copyright laws.   
 
EPC continues to believe that the right framework can maintain the strong 
principles of privacy and data protection, and offer publishers an adequate legal 
environment which can support sustainable business models for media service 
providers, essential for  Europe’s media plurality, through the incentivization of 
investment in privacy protective models. It would respond to the  Presidency 
conclusions to Explore ways to foster and highlight advertising practices that 
contribute to the economic sustainability of the news sector in full respect of editorial 
independence. 
 
The application of the GDPR legal framework, including its risk based approach to 
personal data in the terminal equipment of natural persons, would provide both 
the flexibility and protection of the data protection regime currently in place for 
both publishers and their readers. This approach would ensure that, where data 
processing results in higher risk, the assessments under GDPR would result in the

need to rely on the user’s consent, therefore preserving user rights. The approach 
would also incentivise the use of models which integrate low risk data processing, 
integrating safeguards such as pseudonymisation, and the development and use 
of privacy en hancing technology solutions, offering the possibility for lower risk 
processing to be performed on the basis of legitimate interest. (Amendment option

Alternatively, this level of flexibility could apply to a more restricted scope, where 
only first parties, that is controllers directly engaging with users, would be allowed 
to rely on the GDPR entire framework. Any data sharing with third parties could only 
be performed with the user’s consent. This approach would encourage publishers 
and other first parties to develop in -house data solutions, and to limit the 
dissemination of personal data across the ad ecosystem (Amendment option 2). 
 
As another option, the GDPR flexibility of relying on article 6, could be afforded 
solely to media service providers within their own ecosystem (Amendment option 
3). This approach builds on the rationale developed to support independent 
journalism and medi a service providers economic basis in article 88b and recital 
465. 
 
In any case, EPC strongly believes that the flexibility afforded by article 6 of the 
GDPR should not apply to controllers designated as gatekeeper under the Digital 
Markets Act. 
 
EPC’s proposed approach would offer several added values to both publishers and 
online users. 
• Supporting editorial media: recognising publishers' legitimate interest to 
process data in a more flexible legal framework would support publishers in 
implementing editorial decisions and curating content in line with journalistic 
standards and user expectations, through the delivery  of high -quality media 
services.  
• Enhancing the development of PETs : Simplifying consent requirement and 
introducing a risk based approach with low risk processing, such as anonymous 
ad performance measurement, frequency capping, being exempted from 
consent requirement would incentivise publishers as well as other adtech 
partners to engage in low risk data processing, in an effort to limit their reliance 
on user’s consent. This evolution is likely to further develop new low risk solutions 
which could also lead to some changes in the functioning of the adtech 
ecosystem. 
• Incentivise the reliance on lower risk advertising models: Enabling 
publishers to rely on their legitimate interests is also likely to incentivise the

• Limit consumer consent fatigue but improve their risk awareness : the

Article 88a, paragraph 4: Consent collection standards: 
EPC welcomes the European Commission’s understanding that the current 
framework based on user consent has led to consent fatigue, and its effort to 
reduce the number of cookie banners presented to users while facilitating the 
expression of their choices. 
 
However, the proposal focused on setting rules on how often consent can be 
presented to users, and their design, is a failed attempt to mitigate the 
consequences of a situation rather than tackling the origin of the problem: the over 
reliance on user’s consent (see previous section). 
 
The proposal for “a single click button or equivalent means” for users to refuse 
consent is superfluous as the GDPR already has a full article dedicated to the 
conditions for consent to be valid (Article 7). Furthermore, under the current 
regime, consent as defined by GDPR already applies to consent under the ePrivacy 
Directive, as specified in the Directive, and by court of justice. The Digital Omnibus, 
as proposed restricts the way consent should be asked in a manner that would lead 
to a pure “yes or no” choice for consumers, going much further than the 
requirement set up in article 7. Many different consent banners models have been 
reviewed by supervisory authorities across Member States and deemed lawful 
without having to rely on a “yes or no” choice. 
 
Forcing such a model prevents users from meaningfully accessing information as 
to the advantages and disadvantages related to their choice, while having a high 
impact on publishers ability to collect consent and will ultimately benefit the walled 
gardens, who have a high logged -in reach and do not rely on cookie consent for 
their data access. 
 
As is, the Digital Omnibus proposal would simply force publishers across Europe 
to make their content available in an unsustainable financial manner, consequently 
leading to a rise in subscription costs for all. At the very least, it is essential that this 
provision is without prejudice to the ability of   publishers to define their business 
model and the conditions of access to its content.

Besides its impact on publishers' ability to access data and generate revenues from 
advertising, the proposed digital Omnibus will also not offer any financial benefits 
in terms of cost saving.   
 
Whether the consent banner is presented to the user or not on their first visit to a 
site, the technology must continue to run in the background, log in user’s consent 
choice, even when expressed at browser level and able to resurface a banner at 
any moment to enable users to modify their choice. 
 
The cost of implementing a consent banner solution on a publisher site (i.e. 
partnering with a Consent Management Platform CMP SaaS (Software as a Service), 
implementing in house solutions, etc…)   vary greatly from publisher to publisher, 
based on country, market size and other criteria, but can range from €100K up to 
€700K for a larger publisher per year. Most pieces of code on a media website or 
app run conditional on consent choices. Indirect costs derived from dealing with 
the complexity of consent, suc h as legal and technical expertise, management of 
technical suppliers, etc.  
 
The technical expenses associated with the strain that Consent Management 
Platforms (CMPs) impose on publishers' websites and apps, including their 
development and server -side capabilities (such as Data Management Platform, 
Customer Data Platform, and first-party analytics), arise because all data flows must 
pass through intricate consent choices, where user preferences can be revisited 
and altered at any time. Costs are also driven by the complexity of the choices 
presented to users. Any meaningful simplif ication and cost cutting exercise would 
have to take into consideration the possibility of steering away from such detailed 
choices.

Article 88b Automated machine readable indication of the data subject’s 
choice 
EPC welcomes the rationale behind the proposed exception for media service 
providers in article 88b, which is to avoid undermining the economic basis for 
independent journalism, as spelled out in recital 46. However, as proposed, the 
article would have the likely effect of creating negative reactions for users engaging 
with media service providers, when confronted with a consent banner. Our goal is 
to support user’s trust in publishers through the use of responsible data and privacy 
practices within their o wn ecosystem, which would not require consent under 
article 6 of the GDPR. 
 
Overall, EPC is concerned that a global browser -based consent signal does not 
seem viable for a number of technical considerations: 
• The proposal would trigger massive information requirements aggregated 
across thousands of controllers, with overwhelming impact on technology, and 
the accompanying risk of data centralisation;  
• A global standard would fail to capture the diversity of purposes controllers 
define within their specific context. A functional purpose for one controller may 
involve different processing steps from another. 
• The total number of vendor choices will be unmanageable. Different controllers 
will also use the same vendor for different purposes. 
• Such a structure would desincentivise controllers from engaging privacy friendly 
alternatives, such as through the use of PETs. 
 
 
 
 11 
Furthermore, the development of standards enabling consent to be expressed in a 
centralised manner, is likely to be expressed via users' internet browser, or mobile 
operating system, both services covered under the Digital Markets Act as core 
essential services for gatekeeper. The proposal in article 88b is likely to strengthen 
the position of companies already in a market dominant position, providing them 
control over how consent requests are to be presented, thus furthering publishers' 
dependency on these  companies. The French competition decision against 
Apple’s Ad Tracking Transparency tool in March 2025 demonstrates how a consent 
mechanism can be used to adversely affect publishers.  
 
In order to support independent journalism, the Digital Omnibus should allow 
media service providers to rely on the GDPR risk based approach to access the 
necessary data to support a sustainable business model. 
 
With these considerations in mind, the EPC believes that article 88b should be 
deleted and encourages the legislator to look for solutions to address the 
issue of consumer fatigue through the GDPR risk based approach which 
triggers incentives for low risk data processing.

I. Consumer relevance 
Digital services dominate consumers’ everyday lives and shape key areas such as communication, 
purchasing and access to information. Consumers make decisions about the use of these services 
under conditions of significant information asymmetry. According to the European Commission’s 
Consumer Conditions Scoreboard 2023, 70 percent of consumers stated that they were concerned 
about how their personal data is used and shared. In this context, 38 percent reported a decline in 
their confidence in electronic commerce.1 Other studies likewise show that concerns regarding 
data protection is one of the main reasons why consumers avoid certain digital services.2 
By contrast, a high level of data protection means simplification and debureaucratisation for 
consumers. Empirical data thus emphasises that consumer confidence is an essential requirement 
for the use of digital services and for strong brand loyalty. In a recent survey conducted by the 
Federation of German Consumer Organisations (Verbraucherzentrale Bundesverband e.V – vzbv)3 
63 percent of the surveyed consumers said that they trust companies complying with the General 
Data Protection Regulations (GDPR) significantly or somewhat more when it comes to handling 
their data. At the same time, 87 percent of the people surveyed consider it very or somewhat 
important to be able to trust companies with the handling of their personal data before using their 
services. 
High data protection standards can therefore directly influence the willingness to use certain digital 
services and create a location advantage for European companies whose offers are based on a 
legally guaranteed level of protection. A coherent and reliable data protection framework therefore 
remains a prerequisite for functional markets, strong consumer rights, and a sustainable digital 
economy. 
II. Summary 
A large part of the objectives of the Digital Omnibus cannot be achieved with the current proposals. 
Regarding the reform of the GDPR, the proposals do not result in simplification, but instead 
introduce new vague technical terms, a complex list of exceptions and additional assessment 
requirements. Legal certainty is weakened rather than strengthened, and diverging interpretations 
between Member States and courts, accompanied by lengthy proceedings, are to be expected. No 
tangible relief is provided for small and medium-sized enterprises. Instead, new risks arise due to 
unclear applicability thresholds and additional compliance uncertainty. The coherence of European

vzbv therefore recommends: 
• A reform of the European digital legislature must be based on clear problem definitions, 
transparent consultation, robust evidence and a comprehensive impact assessment in line with 
the European Commission’s Better Regulation standards. 
• The proposed new definition of personal data must be removed. Any definition compatible 
with European law may only build on the jurisprudence of the Court of Justice of the European 
Union (CJEU) and the existing objective criterion of identifiability of the GDPR and must not go 
beyond them. 
• The proposed definition of scientific research must be removed. Simplifications for research 
serving the common good require a clearly defined methodological and scientific concept of 
research that does not weaken purpose limitation, transparency or the safeguards of Article 89 
GDPR. What is needed are more precisely operationalised guarantees, not blanket exemptions. 
• The limitation of the right of access must be removed. Rather than creating legal clarity, the 
proposal would undermine established CJEU case law, create new interpretative uncertainty and 
likely necessitate renewed clarification proceedings. The right of access must remain exercisable 
without an examination of motives and without additional requirements. 
• The weakening of information obligations must be removed. Transparency requirements must 
be based on objective criteria and must not be replaced by contextual assumptions. A legal 
fiction of knowledge is incompatible with the transparency principle and with Article 8 of the 
Charter of Fundamental Rights (CFR) of the European Union. Reasonable simplifications are 
possible without material changes to the GDPR, for example through standardised short notices 
and pictograms, that support compliance while improving comprehensibility for consumers.

protection from Article 5 (3) of the ePrivacy Directive should be kept. However, the planned

ban on further processing, and – for measuring reach – a right to object that can be exercised at 
any time. The security exemption must be precisely formulated and limited to the necessary

III. Introduction 
With their proposal for a Digital Omnibus Regulation5 from November 2025, the European 
Commission wants to optimise the application of the European digital regulatory framework.6 The 
planned changes are intended to reduce the burdens for companies and citizens and to strengthen 
competitiveness. They are intended to ensure that compliance with the rules is achieved at lower 
cost, while delivering the same regulatory objectives, and creating a competitive advantage for 
responsible businesses. 
A simplification of European digital law is reasonable in principle.7 The existing regulatory 
framework is spread across many legal acts, partially inconsistent and difficult to understand for 
both consumers and companies. Even experts can hardly maintain an overview of the reciprocal 
effects of the various legal acts. A simplification, consolidation and improved coherence would 
therefore be desirable. However, the Omnibus Reform currently proposed does not fulfil these 
objectives. Instead, it systematically shifts the protection scope of the GDPR, by, for example, 
changing definitions and legal bases. 
Significant deficiencies in the legislative process are particularly problematic. The reform proposals 
were presented without robust evidence, without adequate public consultation, and without an 
impact assessment. GDPR changes were not the subject of the call for evidence in September 
20258, although the reform proposal must have already existed eight weeks before it was published. 
The GDPR Multistakeholder Expert Group of the European Commission, composed of equal 
numbers of representatives from all sides, was not involved, despite having been created precisely 
for these cases. Reality checks carried out in parallel were unbalanced and influenced by economic 
interests.9 A procedure with the intention to make substantial changes to fundamental rights 
should however not be built up on informal formats with unbalanced participation. This is contrary 
to the European Commission’s own Better Regulation Guidelines10, which provides for 
consultations, transparency and a robust impact assessment. Despite redefining key structures of 
the GDPR, the proposals were declared as “technical adjustments” and thus exempted from the 
regular legislative procedure. 
The political context intensifies this criticism. In vzbv’s view, it is misguided to simply present data 
protection as an obstacle to competitiveness and technological innovation. There is a lack of robust

1.1 On the new definition of personal data  
Addition to Article 4 (1) GDPR / new Article 41a GDPR

information is only to be considered personal if a given processing entity has the means to identify 
the data subject (Art. 3 (1)(a) of the Digital Omnibus Draft (in the following: DO-D)). Data would no 
longer be considered personal where that entity cannot identify the natural person to whom the 
information relates, even where potential subsequent recipients are able to identify that person. In 
addition, the European Commission is to be allowed to determine, by means of an implementing

Article 4 (1) GDPR-D is not just a technical clarification but fundamentally changes the dogmatic 
structure of the GDPR and the relevant CJEU jurisprudence instead. Judgment C-413/23 P SRB17, 
which the proposal purports to implement, is selectively interpreted and significantly 
overstretched. Instead of an abstract entity logic, the CJEU has confirmed a case-by-case 
assessment (paragraphs 86, 100) in the judgement. This case-by-case assessment should be 
based on costs, time, technical possibilities and the means of third parties who are likely to be used 
for identification purposes. Thus, for recipients, pseudonymised data are only not personal in 
limited exceptional cases. For controllers, they remain personal data and become personal data 
once again – contrary to the proposals of the European Commission – upon transmission to 
subsequent recipients with the ability to identify the data subject (paragraphs 81, 84, 85). 
The change contradicts the still applicable definition of personal data and thus weakens the key 
rights and freedoms of data subjects. The GDPR covers online identifiers and cases of singling out, 
i.e. the clear recognisability of a person without clear data.18 The concept is a key element of the 
present CJEU’s approach.19 Abandoning this dimension would privilege tracking IDs, hashed values 
and segmenting categories that in essence function as direct identifiers.20 Companies could 
additionally, by means of organisational separations or the transfer of hashed values, create 
structures that claim formal non-identifiability without actually preventing re-identification. 
The function of pseudonymisation is systematically underestimated. While it primarily provides a 
technical and organisational measure of protection,21 it is not an anonymisation of data. A 
prohibition against re-identification does not replace technical protection and does not prevent 
third parties – for example in data broker chains, via technological progress or outside of Europe – 
from re-identifying data subjects. The proposal also presents false incentives: Companies would 
have fewer incentives to invest in robust anonymisation procedures if weak pseudonymisation is 
sufficient to exempt them from the application of the GDPR. 
It is also questionable if the rules for processing personal data by a processor or those on joint 
controllership will reliably apply in situations where pseudonymised data are no longer to be 
considered personal for the recipient. 
Overall, supervisory authorities would in fact be unable to verify whether identifiability can be ruled 
out. The simultaneous planned reduction of companies’ documentation obligations 
(2025/0130/COD) would further complicate these checks.

transfer the power to define the scope of protection of a fundamental right to the executive level, is 
particularly critical. The question as to when data is no longer personal affects the core of the 
protection scope of Article 8 CFR, which has historically been based on the systematics of Data

1.2 On the definition of scientific research 
New Article 4 (38) GDPR / New version of Article 5 (1)(b) GDPR / Addition to Article 13 (5) GDPR 
The draft significantly expands the definition of scientific research. In future, the definition would 
cover “any research which can also support innovation”, including the use of “existing knowledge in 
novel ways” to “commercial interest” (Article 3 (1)(b) DO-D). In addition, further processing for 
research purposes would always be considered as compatible with the original purpose (Art. 3 (2) 
DO-D). Provision of information to data subjects can be omitted if it is impossible, requires a 
disproportionate effort or would pose a risk to research (Article 3 (6) DO-D). Research in Recital 32 
DO-D is declared to be a legitimate interest within the meaning of Article 6 (1)(f) GDPR. 
Alleviations for common-good research would be generally reasonable, yet the proposals 
fundamentally shift the research definition. Research changes from a methodical-scientific 
concept to a broadly defined innovation category that includes almost all data-intensive activities. 
The definition avoids methodical minimum standards and uses vague phrasing such as “can 
support innovation”, “aim of contributing to growth of society’s knowledge and wellbeing” or “apply 
existing knowledge in novel ways”.22 It thus opens up the potential for misuse as companies can

2. Extensive changes to the rights of data subjects and 
transparency obligations 
2.1 On the limitation of the right to access 
New version of Article 12 (5) GDPR 
The proposal fundamentally changes the right to access (Article 3 (4) DO-D). Alongside existing 
reasons for rejection, a new category of “abuse of the right of access” (via information requests “for 
purposes other than the protection of their data”) is to be introduced, with the addendum of a 
reduced standard of proof (“reasonable grounds to believe”). At the same time, data subjects 
would be required to be more precise in their requests (Recital 35 DO-D: “the data subject should 
be as specific as possible”). Broad or undifferentiated requests could be rejected as “excessive”. 
Article 12 (5) GDPR-D also does not present a technical amendment and instead shifts the 
functional logic of a key control instrument. Checking the subjective goal of an access request is 
foreign to the GDPR system. The right to access is a key element of Article 8 CFR. It facilitates 
monitoring of the legality of processing and facilitates the exercise of further fundamental rights. 
The CFR does not provide for a purpose limitation of this right; it applies regardless of the 
individual’s motive. Accordingly, the CJEU has confirmed multiple times that the right to access is 
an objective control instrument and cannot be made dependent on additional requirements.24 
Restrictions must therefore preserve the essence of the law, be proportionate, and guarantee a high 
degree of legal certainty. The proposed changes do not fulfil these requirements.

2.2 On the weakening of information obligations 
New version of Article 13 (4) GDPR 
The European Commission intends to significantly expand the exception to information obligations 
in the case of direct data collection (Article 3 (5) DO-D). In future, the obligations under Article 13 (1) 
to (3) GDPR will not apply if the data are collected in a “clear and circumscribed relationship”, the 
activity of the controllers is “not data intensive” and they have “reasonable grounds to assume” 
that data subjects already know basic information (identity, contact details, purpose of processing 
and legal basis). The only exceptions to this are cases involving transmission to third parties, 
transfers to third countries, automated decisions or processing with high risk. 
It remains unclear which specific problem this new version should solve. The European 
Commission neither identifies an implementation deficit nor provides empirical evidence indicating 
that the existing transparency obligations systematically lead to overburden or disproportionate 
effort. It is not justified to slacken the key transparency obligations without a robust problem 
analysis. 
The proposals substantially change the scope and requirements of transparency obligations. They 
replace objective criteria with contextual assumptions and introduce new vague legal terms as well 
as a legal fiction of knowledge. This shifts the normative focus from the actual information status of 
the data subject to an assessment by the controllers. 
The new provision presents no technical specification and is instead a material change to the 
transparency regime of the GDPR. The transparency requirement is a key element of the principle 
“fair and transparent processing” and the requirement for the exercise of further individual rights. It 
is functionally closely linked to Article 8 CFR; limitations have to take into account the essence, be 
proportionate and be based on clear, predictable criteria. The proposed revised wording does not 
meet these requirements.

3. Gaps in new structure of tracking protection 
3.1 On the transfer of cookie regulations into the GDPR 
New Article 88a GDPR / change to Article 5 (3) ePrivacy Directive 
With Article 88a GDPR-D (Article 3 (15) DO-D) and the accompanying change from Article 5 (3) 
ePrivacy Directive (Article 5 DO-D) the European Commission is proposing a foundational revision 
of the protection of terminal equipment. In future, access to personal data on terminal equipment 
as well as its use should be regulated by the GDPR. Article 5 (3) ePrivacy Directive will only apply to 
natural persons if the access does not lead to the processing of personal data. Furthermore, the 
proposal introduces additional exceptions to consent and links subsequent processing in Recital 
44 DO-D to Article 6 (1) (f) GDPR. In addition, Article 88a GDPR-D introduces a new system of 
consent requirements. 
Article 88a GDPR-D shifts preventive protection of terminal equipment to subsequent processing. 
While Article 5 (3) ePrivacy Directive already limits access independent of the existence of a link to 
an identified or identifiable person, the GDPR only applies when personal data are processed. This 
is in direct conflict with Article 7 CFR. Access to terminal equipment already constitutes, in itself, 
an interference with privacy, regardless of whether personal data are subsequently processed. 
Were this preventive access protection to no longer exist, control would be missing at the point 
where the interference with fundamental rights actually occurs. Article 5 (3) ePrivacy Directive 
would only remain applicable in rare exceptional cases in which access neither involves personal 
data nor leads to such processing. In the context of modern web and app technologies this is hardly 
realistic. 
In contrast, the planned new definition of personal data currently drafted would lead to a systemic 
contradiction: much device data might no longer be considered personal data. Article 88a GDPR-D 
would then not apply so that once again Article 5 (3) ePrivacy Directive would have to be used – with 
the consequence that consent would still be required for various forms of technical access. Thus, 
the paradoxical situation would arise that cookie banners would not be reduced and would, in fact, 
be stabilised. The political objectives of the reform would be missed. 
The exceptions pursuant to Article 88a (3) GDPR-D also create significant legal and technical 
uncertainties and reduce the current level of protection. They apply independently of Article 6 (1) 
and 9 (2) GDPR: points (c) and (d) are phrased as absolute exceptions. This means that invasive 
measures can take place without a balancing of interests and without additional protective 
mechanisms. The broad and vague exceptions are therefore not sufficient for the requirements of 
Article 52 CFR – in particular foreseeability, necessity and precision – because they are neither 
clearly limited nor systematically circumscribed. 
In this way, the exception for audience measurement (c) remains undefined in terms of content. 
“Aggregated audience measurement” is not defined and no criteria are specified as to the 
conditions under which aggregation should take place and the time frames within which data may 
be combined. Without further technical protective measures there is a risk that supposedly 
aggregated data will remain re-identifiable. Without a precise definition of the purpose, measuring 
the audience can serve as a blanket term for extensive analysis or telemetry processing. Further, a 
right to object for data subjects that can be exercised at any time would also be necessary. 
The security exception (d) is also phrased too broadly. Under the pretext of maintaining or restoring 
the security of services or devices inappropriately broad access is possible. Significant risks arise in

the absence of time limitations and clear restrictions on measures that serve confidentiality,

limited to the necessary measures. Dark patterns must be prevented. To effectively protect 
consumers and reduce the burden on companies, it would, however, be overall more expedient 
to prohibit tracking and profiling for advertising purposes.30

3.2 On the introduction of automated, machine-readable preference signals 
New Article 88b GDPR 
Article 88b GDPR-D provides, for the first time, for a system of automated and machine-readable 
preference signals and thus establishes a technical core element of the new legislative framework 
(Article 3 (15) DO-D). Controllers would have to make it possible for data subjects to automatically 
reject consent requests and to digitally carry out objections pursuant to Article 21 (2) GDPR. In 
addition, data subjects should also be able to automatically give consent. These rules would 
however only come into effect if European standardisation bodies have developed appropriate 
standards. While media service providers would be exempt from the obligation to comply, browser 
providers would have to provide technical means for the transmission of such signals. 
The approach of machine-readable objection signals is reasonable. Uniform and binding rejection 
and objection signals can strengthen the rights of data subjects and reduce the number of 
manipulative consent banners. However, the proposal is still imprecise in many respects and leads 
to systemic risks that would weaken the protection level of the GDPR without corresponding 
benefits. 
The scope of application remains unclear. It is not clear whether Article 88b GDPR-D exclusively 
refers to consent within the meaning of Article 88a GDPR-D or to all consent under the GDPR. The 
reference to “consent under this Regulation” and the reference to Article 21 (2) GDPR indicate a 
broad scope of application. Following the CJEU jurisprudence31, “direct advertisement” covers all 
commercial appeals that are addressed “directly and individually to a consumer”, including 
personalised emails. The proposed provision would therefore mean that companies would have to 
also accept automated objections to individualised emails and direct postal advertising via their 
online interfaces. The relationship to Article 21 (5) GDPR also remains undefined. 
This uncertainty has practical consequences: controllers do not know which obligations apply and 
data subjects are not able to recognise whether their decisions are effective. This leads to different 
interpretations and inconsistent implementation that undermine the confidence in such signals. In 
addition, unintended reciprocal effects with other legal acts could occur – such as for example the 
Digital Markets Act (DMA) – that relate to the requirements of consent corresponding under the 
GDPR. 
The possibility of granting consent automatically is especially problematic. Informed consent 
according to Article 4 (11) GDPR requires that data subjects know and understand the purposes, 
risks, controllers, recipients and the consequences of the processing. A global, central set signal 
cannot systemically guarantee these requirements – especially not with regard to structurally non-
transparent adtech ecosystems. The withdrawal of consent also remains unclear, although Article 
7 (3) GDPR requires that this must be as simple as the granting of consent. Thus, there is a risk of 
long-term conditions that are difficult to revoke. Only the automatic rejection of consent requests 
and a machine-readable withdrawal are therefore appropriate and technically viable. 
The obligation of the provider to simply “respect” such signals clearly does not go far enough. A 
binding obligation to comply is necessary. The extensive exception for media service providers 
significantly worsens the problem. The definition of media service providers according to 
Regulation (EU) 2024/1083 can, for example, also include lifestyle pages, video and streaming

4.1 On the exception from the ban on processing sensitive data  
New Article 9 (2)(k) GDPR / new Article 9 (5) GDPR 
The proposal of the European Commission provides for a new exception to the prohibition on 
processing special categories of personal data (Article 3 (3) (a) DO-D). According to Article 9 (2) (k) 
GDPR-D, special categories of personal data would be allowed to be processed for the 
development and operation of AI systems if controllers meet the requirements set out in Article 9 
(5) GDPR-D. These include the obligation to implement appropriate technical and organisational 
measures to avoid the processing of special categories of personal data, to remove identified 
special categories of personal data or – if their removal requires disproportionate effort – to 
effectively protect these from being used to produce outputs, from being disclosed or otherwise 
made available (Article 3 (3)(b) DO-D) The scope of application of the exception is limited to 
residual data, i.e. the data that unintentionally remain in the dataset despite measures taken to 
avoid this. Purpose-driven processing of special categories of personal data must continue to be 
based on the existing categories set out in Article 9 (2)(a) to (j) GDPR. 
This provision represents a systematic deviation from the foundational structure of the GDPR. 
Article 24 GDPR obligates controllers to design their technical and organisational measures in a 
risk-adequate manner. The more comprehensive and complex the processing is, the higher the 
protection measures must be. The draft reverses this relationship by creating privileges precisely 
where vast amounts of unstructured data processing takes place, which are not transparent for 
individuals. This reduces the protection level for particularly invasive processing and circumvents 
the risk-based logic of the GDPR. 
Moreover, there is a significant conflict with CJEU jurisprudence on Article 9 (1) GDPR. The court 
has determined multiple times that if a dataset includes special categories of personal data this 
leads to the entire processing falling under the prohibition pursuant to Article 9 (1) GDPR – 
regardless of the intention of the controllers.33 The proposal of the European Commission however 
privileges the inseparable combining of sensitive and non-sensitive data and makes it the 
foundation of a new exception. This construction thus sheds considerable doubt on the proposal’s 
compatibility with Union law. 
In addition, it is problematic that data subjects lose the point of contact for legal remedies. If the 
processing of special categories of personal data is privileged by Article 9 (2)(k) GDPR-D, there is no 
breach of Article 9 (1) GDPR. Thus, protection is dropped particularly in cases of loss of control, 
which the CJEU recognises as non-material damage. The exception thus removes controllers from 
their responsibility under liability law without providing data subjects with an alternative effective 
protection tool or making a suitable equivalent available.

transparency”39, which without specific requirements will lead to fragmented or marketing-oriented 
information formats. 
The assessment benchmarks in Recital 31 are systematically problematic. The introduction of the 
criteria as to whether an interest is “beneficial for the data subject and society at large”, shifts the 
assessment to the benefit of abstract claims of the common good and to the disadvantage of a 
precise risk assessment. For foundation models whose specific use is not even known to the 
controllers such a check is in any case simply impossible. The criteria of “reasonable expectations” 
is structurally distorted. It privileges large platforms with an existing user base as expectations can 
arise only in existing relationships. It likewise offers no protective mechanisms against retroactive 
expectation constructions. 
In summary, it is shown that Article 88c GDPR-D does not address the structural deficits of the 
current jurisprudence40 – in particular the lack of effectiveness of the objection and delisting 
mechanisms as well as the unprotected position of non-registered users. Key requirements of the 
CJEU on necessity checks, purpose limitation and transparency are also disregarded. Thus, the 
provision fails in specifying the requirements of Article 6 (1) (f) GDPR or in making the balancing 
criteria operational. Instead, the draft shifts the balancing of interests systematically to the benefit 
of the controllers without appropriately addressing the risks linked with data-intensive AI 
development for data subjects. Against this background there is no coherent, risk-adequate 
protection regime, rather an essentially limitless special regime for AI-related processing whose 
vague requirements lead to legal uncertainty, illusory protection mechanisms and a structural 
reduction of the data protection level. 
Even limiting the proposal to mere AI training, it remains doubtful that a simple modification of 
Article 6 (1) (f) GDPR can do justice to the particular risks of such processing. As AI training 
fundamentally differs from conventional data processing – irreversible integration of data in 
models, lacking erasability and possible reproduction in outputs – it becomes clear that adapting 
the existing balancing tests does not do justice to these particularities. 
 
The processing of personal data for AI training requires an independent legal bases with strict 
material requirements.41 Necessary for this is proof of the subsidiarity with respect to synthetic 
or anonymised data, specific risk information before processing starts, an effective right to 
object also for third parties without a user account, clear technical protection measures against 
replication and identifiability as well as a consent requirement for the data of children.42

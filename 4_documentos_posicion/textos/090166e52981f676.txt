 
 
Emerson’s response to the call for feedback on the Digital 
Simplification Package 
Introduction 
Emerson Electric Co., a global technology and engineering company with significant 
operations in Europe, including approximately 19,500 staff and 45 manufacturing sites spread 
across 21 Member States, has reviewed the European Commission’s (Commission) Digital 
Package on Simplification, and welcomes the opportunity to share its views in response to the 
call for feedback. 
Emerson welcomes the Commission’s proposals to reduce the regulatory burden on 
businesses and eliminate overlaps between the artificial intelligence, cybersecurity and data 
requirements applicable to critical operators across existing digital legislation.  
That said, Emerson considers that the Digital Package on Simplification would benefit from 
introducing further measures to facilitate compliance with digital legislation. In particular, the 
requirements across the legislative framework should be proportion ate and sufficiently clear 
such that critical operators are able to fulfil their obligations, thereby strengthening the overall 
resilience of the digital ecosystem.  
Emerson therefore makes the following proposals to modify the Digital Simplification Package 
across three main areas: 
• Cyber: while finding the proposal to set a single entry -point for incident reporting 
commendable, further legal clarity as to the definitions of incidents and thresholds for 
reporting is needed. The overlap of applicable regulations and therefore different 
authorities that will process the information also needs to be addressed. 
• Artificial Intelligence (AI):  calibration of disclosures is necessary to avoid 
overreporting under the AI Act and providing clarity on definitions. The Digital 
Simplification Package should provide for a further partial pause on the obligations 
applicable to providers of high -risk AI systems, as necessary, until harmonised 
standards and sufficiently clear guidance are available to businesses to meet their 
obligations.  
• Data: the requirement under the Data Act for data service providers to facilitate the 
transfer of user data to third parties should be clarified and further guidance provided 
to mitigate the disproportionate burden this may have on providers where complex 
data transfers are involved.

1. Cyber 
We commend the Commission’s ambition to introduce a single entry-point process for incident 
reporting and help align the requirements that organizations may have to comply with (non -
exhaustively under NIS2, CRA, GDPR, DORA and the Network Code on Cybersecu rity 
(NCCS)). However, we believe that some challenges remain unaddressed or do not provide 
for sufficient legal clarity i.e., for aspects like the applicable country of main establishment, 
type of authority that should receive the notification, and reporting process and nature of the 
provided information. Specifically, we would like to share the following views: 
• Emerson is supportive of aligning reporting obligations through the single entry -point 
for incident reporting (SEPIR) and harmonised reporting framework through 
standardised reporting templates, thresholds and timelines. 
• However, the simplification package has not addressed the inconsistent terminology 
around thresholds of reportable incidents under NIS2, CRA and AI Act – which creates 
a burden for businesses. Further alignment would benefit businesses. Divergences 
under the Member States’ transposition of NIS2 alone may lead to reporting difficulties.  
For example, whilst NIS2 provides criteria for an incident to be considered “significant” 
under Art. 23(3), certain Member States, like Italy, have issued more stringent criteria, 
which increase the reporting burden for businesses and may result in inconsistent 
communications between organizations and the authorities.  
• As an example of the concerns expressed above, the overlap between NIS2 and CRA 
reporting remains partially unaddressed, as Paragraph 1 hints that SEPIR shall be built 
‘without prejudice’ to the CRA Single Reporting Platform, therefore implying that the 
CRA will keep its own reporting mechanism. However, another provision dictates that 
if a product manufacturer notifies an incident through SEPIR to comply with its 
reporting obligations under Article 14(3) of the CRA, it shall be deemed that the 
manufacturer has also carried out its obligations under Article 23(4) of NIS2. We 
believe that the Digital Simplification Package would benefit from introducing a system 
where a real one-stop shop reporting process can cover the compliance requirements 
under NIS2, CRA, GDPR, AI Act and NCCS alike.  
• In addition, it is important to note that the reporting of Digital Infrastructure providers to 
the country of main establishment as defined under NIS 2 has not been fully replicated 
in either the CRA or the AI Act. Consequently, even large organizations with extensive 
operations in Europe may face challenges in complying with reporting obligations that 
could potentially require multiple reporting. We would also invite the Commission to 
reflect on introducing a one -stop-shop process for the oversight of the conformity 
assessment obligations under the CRA. 
• Finally, we would call to clarify support periods under the CRA for different licensing 
arrangements, as well as hardware products manufactured serially in the industrial 
space. In addition, defining the support required for a model of product where a 
particular unit is out of support will help to clarify expectations. 
 
2. AI 
Emerson welcomes the Commission’s proposal to partially pause implementation of the 
obligations under the AI Act for providers of high-risk AI systems pending the availability of the 
relevant harmonised standards. Such standards are essential for businesses to have sufficient 
clarity on the requirements they must meet to ensure compliance with their obligations under 
the AI Act.  
Notwithstanding the above, the AI Act raises several concerns which have not been addressed 
by the Digital Package on Simplification.  
In particular, Emerson retains the following concerns raised in its response to the 
Commission’s call for evidence: 
• Disproportionate disclosures: providers of high-risk AI systems remain concerned that 
the requirement for broad disclosures, such as algorithms and related data processing 
details, may compromise commercial confidentiality and increase security risks.  
• Interpretation of definitions: several definitions under the AI Act remain open to 
misinterpretation. For instance, the “provider” is considered to be the person/ entity 
that develops/ places an AI system on the market, and the “deployer” is the one using 
it professionally. However, modifications, re-branding, or substantial changes to an AI 
system can shift liability a nd make a deployer a provider, which is likely to create 
disputes as to who must perform pre -market duties.  Furthermore, other definitions 
such as “AI system” and “safety component” in Article 3 of the Act are so broad and 
ambiguous that they make it difficult for many organizations to fully understand if their 
AI applications are truly deemed “high risk”. 
In addition, Emerson provides the following further recommendations: 
• Timely issue of harmonised standards and guidance: Emerson would welcome the 
issue of these documents as soon as possible to give high -risk AI providers clarity on 
their obligations. Their availability has already been significantly delayed. Emerson 
submits that the Digital Simplification Package should make provision for further 
pausing implementation of the obligations on high -risk AI systems, as necessary, to 
account for additional  delays. Given that non -compliance under the AI Act carries 
financial pena lties, it is imperative that businesses have sufficient clarity on their 
obligations and adequate time to prepare to meet them. 
• Documentation Requirements for AI Classification Assessment: the Commission 
should clarify whether the AI Act obliges providers and deployers to make an 
assessment that their AI system or use case does not fall under a high -risk 
category. If required, the Commission should clarify how (and if) a provider or deployer 
should internally document this assessment. Given the significant penalties for 
misclassification and the ambiguity in the definitions of “high -risk” systems, clear 
guidance o n the documentation  standards and evidentiary requirements for such 
assessments is essential to enable businesses to demonstrate compliance with their 
classification obligations under the AI Act. 
• Clarification on hybrid systems combining AI and non-AI components: the Commission 
clarified in its Guidelines on the definition of an artificial intelligence system established 
 
by Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act) ( the " Guidelines") that certain categories of 
system that have the capacity to infer in a narrow manner may still fall outside of the 
scope of the AI system definition. However, guidance is still required with respect to 
hybrid systems that combine AI and non-AI components. In particular, the Commission 
should address: (i) how to assess systems where AI is integrated with traditional 
software; (ii) the circumstances under which an AI component becomes material 
enough to trigger AI Act obligations for the whole system; and (iii) whether providers 
can treat AI and non-AI modules separately for compliance purposes. 
• Industry-Specific Application Guidance: Emerson would welcome guidance on how the 
AI Act applies to industrial automation and process control providers of AI systems 
used in the manufacturing, energy, and critical infrastructure sectors, including how 
safety-critical control systems should be evaluated under the framework of the AI Act. 
Emerson urges the Commission to address these concerns in the guidance promised by the 
Digital Package on Simplification on the application of the AI Act to high-risk AI providers.  
3. Data 
Emerson is committed to fostering an open and competitive cloud market. It welcomes the 
Commission’s proposal in the Digital Package on Simplification to facilitate customer switching 
between data service providers by introducing lighter regimes for certai n providers to reduce 
the administrative burden under the Data Act.  
Whilst Emerson supports the Data Act’s aim to enable user access to their data, data service 
providers are concerned by the requirement under Article 5(1) to facilitate data access to third 
parties at the user’s request. For critical infrastructure providers, such as Emerson, protecting 
the security of user data is paramount and should not be compromised when data is 
transferred. Critical operators will need to invest a significant amount of time and resources 
not only to build the necessary technical inter faces for each transfer but also to ensure that 
the process is sufficiently robust to maintain data security. Further in order to ensure that no 
confidential business information is transferred to competitors, critical operators may have to 
take additional  measures to ensure sensitive proprietary information exposed in their 
processing of the user’s data is stripped or sufficiently anonymised before transfer to a third 
party.  In light of the above, Emerson would welcome further guidance in the Digital 
Simplification Package on the application of this provision where facilitating user access and 
data sharing would place a disproportionate burden on businesses.  
Conclusion 
We hope that the above comments will be useful as the Commission’s Digital Package on 
Simplification is negotiated and finalised. We look forward to further engagement and 
providing support to help fulfil the Digital Simplification Package’s aim for digital legislation that 
aligns with the ‘better regulation’ principles.

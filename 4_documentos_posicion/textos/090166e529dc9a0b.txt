As outlined in our response to the European Commission’s consultation on the Consumer 
Agenda 2025-2030, Apple is committed to continuing to help foster a digital economy that 1
supports meaningful and safe experiences for European consumers. We support the 
European Commission’s ambition to simplify and streamline regulations with the Digital 
Omnibus proposal, while ensuring it maintains the highest standards of fundamental rights 
and privacy. We believe the proposal provides a meaningful opportunity to incentivize 
privacy-preserving innovation.

Apple Response - February 2026 2
At Apple, our mission is to create technology that empowers people and enriches their lives. 
We put our users at the centre of everything we do, from how we design our products, 
services, and software to the policies we support. 
We believe privacy is a fundamental human right. We design all of our products with a 
relentless focus on privacy, innovating to provide seamless user experiences that set the 
industry standard for privacy, security, and safety.  
Unlike many companies, Apple believes in collecting only the personal data required to 
deliver what users need. Whenever possible, we process and analyze personal data on-
device — and in instances where specific personal data is necessary, we minimize the 
amount we use to provide the intended service. Apple’s web browser, Safari, also includes 
state-of-the-art privacy features designed to help stop websites and data-collection 
companies from profiling users based on their browsing activity. 
The Digital Omnibus is an opportunity to incentivize privacy-preserving innovation 
while streamlining the regulatory framework. In this submission, Apple provides concrete 
recommendations to achieve this goal, including: 
‣Update the ePrivacy consent requirement to ensure more consistency between the 
treatment of personal and non-personal data. This will incentivize companies deliver 
innovative, privacy-by-design features, including on-device processing.  
‣Ensure that existing best practices, such as browser based by-default efforts to block 
user profiling, are recognized alongside efforts to simplify cookie consents.
Delivering a simpler regulatory environment which incentivizes privacy 
innovation 
Apple has long publicly supported the GDPR and ePrivacy legal frameworks. For years, 
these frameworks have served as a compass for legislators worldwide — demonstrating 
how to create an effective system of individual rights that is adaptable to the dynamism of 
data flows and the digital economy. In addition, we have been one of few companies to 
support modernizing the outdated ePrivacy regime to reflect technological advances. We 
welcome that the EU Omnibus legislation seeks to reignite those discussions. 
Apple supports the Digital Omnibus proposal’s regulatory simplification objectives. We 
believe the EU should maintain a streamlined, coherent regulatory environment that 
encourages innovation, provided that it maintains high standards for privacy and data 
protection. Reducing unnecessary complexity will create a more predictable environment 
that benefits European users and businesses alike, allowing us to focus resources on 
delivering innovative, secure and privacy-first products and services. 
The Digital Omnibus proposal introduces a number of positive measures, which can lead 
to simplification for organizations without lowering the privacy and security of personal and 
other data. These include: 
‣Efforts to introduce a single entry point for incident reporting. This acknowledges the 
need to streamline the current process, particularly as the Cyber Resilience Act vastly 
broadens the scope of potential reporting. 
‣Removing consent obligations for strictly limited non-intrusive audience measurement by 
first-party online service providers could reduce disruptive cookie consents. However, 
ensuring continued consent requirements for more intrusive tracking must remain in 
place to protect fundamental rights.   
However, the proposal as it stands would benefit from additional clarity to meet its 
simplification objectives while protecting user privacy. Most notably: 
‣Singling out audience measurement is limited. The law should also incentivize other types 
of non-intrusive first-party activities, including specific efforts to collect data that cannot 
be rendered identifiable, anonymization, and other privacy-enhancing practices such as 
the use of homomorphic encryption for data processing.  
‣Proposals allowing users to grant consent through automated digital signals, which must 
be respected by controllers, would advance simplification and enhance users’ control 
over their data. However, it is essential that these proposals are compatible with browser 
providers that continue to offer privacy-protecting default settings and allow other means 
of granting permission centrally.

We also invite legislators to ensure more consistency between the treatment of 
personal and non-personal data, with the aim of incentivizing privacy-preserving 
innovation. The GDPR was designed specifically to help safeguard personal data, reflecting 
the centrality of such data to user privacy and security. By contrast, in its approach to 
accessing data on terminal equipment in Article 88a, the Digital Omnibus proposal would 
impose a stricter standard for non-personal data than personal data. We believe this would 
encourage off-device, large-scale processing of personal data, which is inconsistent with 
the EU’s commitment to data minimization. This is a missed opportunity to incentivize 
further investment in privacy innovations that seek to minimize personal data processing.  
Additionally, the current proposal would add further complexity to an already fragmented 
legal regime, where access to personal data is regulated consistently across Europe under 
GDPR while access to non-personal data remains inconsistently regulated under local 
implementations of ePrivacy Directive, even for different data flows within the same service. 
This complexity is further exacerbated by the proposed Article 3.1, under which  
pseudonymous data is not always considered personal data, creating new opportunities for 
companies to seek and exploit loopholes. 
Concrete solutions 
The EU’s regulatory simplification efforts and high privacy standards can be mutually 
reinforcing. The EU Omnibus legislation is an opportunity to incentivize good privacy 
practices by rewarding such practices with regulatory simplification, which would create a 
virtuous cycle. We therefore invite the European Commission, Parliament and Council of the 
EU to take on board the following recommendations. 
Simplifying conditions to gain access to data stored on or to store data on terminal 
equipment - Article 5(3) 
At Apple, we design our products so that as much data processing as possible occurs 
directly on the user’s device. By performing computations locally, we help minimize the 
amount of data that needs to be shared with Apple or third parties. Some advanced features 
require processing capabilities that cannot always be delivered on a device alone, 
necessitating access to global knowledge hosted on servers. In those cases, we use a 
combination of technologies, such as homomorphic encryption, to help ensure these server 
lookups are private, efficient, and scalable.

Privacy-by-Default for Browsers - Article 88b 
Apple believes users should be able to control who can access their data and how that data 
is used. Since the early years of the web, a variety of technologies have been developed 
that enable companies to track users’ activity across websites and apps for advertising 
purposes. This tracking is pervasive: in some cases, individual web pages include more than 
100 trackers from different companies seeking to profile users, often without their 
knowledge or consent.  3
T o address this issue, Apple has developed Safari-based features such as Intelligent 
Tracking Prevention (ITP) and fingerprinting defense. Apple has expanded these protections 
against applications that track and collect data from users across other apps and websites 4
with our App Tracking Transparency (ATT) framework. These features are designed 
specifically to prevent users from being tracked and profiled across the web, either by 
blocking outright or requiring app developers to obtain consent before collecting data 
intended to be shared with third parties for the purpose of tracking users across apps and 
websites. Importantly, these protections are enabled by default to prioritize best privacy 
outcomes, reducing the need for users to navigate complex permission systems or rely on 
third-party intermediaries. This approach reflects another longstanding privacy-by-design

Article 88b of the proposal would require browsers to implement machine-readable and 
automated consent signals. While we understand the intention behind this provision, similar 
initiatives in the past — such as "Do Not Track" and global consent frameworks — have 
largely failed due to inconsistent adoption, widespread non-compliance, and a lack of 
enforcement. The ad tech industry is incentivized to continue finding ways of bypassing 
restrictions and tracking users. Without a robust legal framework and effective enforcement 
across the entire ecosystem, Article 88b risks creating an illusion of privacy. This is why it is 
essential that browsers are able to continue offering strong privacy default settings that 
protect users from unwanted tracking.

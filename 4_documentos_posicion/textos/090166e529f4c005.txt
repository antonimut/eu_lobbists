 
 
 
 
ITI’s Comments on the Digital Simplification Package and Omnibus 
 
ITI - the Information Technology Industry Council  - is the global trade association of the 
technology industry, representing 80 of the world’s most innovative tech companies . Our 
membership spans across the entire spectrum of technology, including global leaders on 
software and AI, hardware, cloud, infrastructure, cybersecurity and semiconductors. 
 
Introduction 
ITI welcomes the Digital Omnibus proposals as important steps toward improving legal clarity 
for innovators, reducing fragmentation, and supporting the competitiveness of businesses 
operating in the EU. At a time when companies face an increasingly complex and overlapping 
regulatory environment, these initiatives signal a recognition of the need to streamline the 
EU digital acquis and to pr ovide greater certainty for innovation and investment across 
Europe. We strongly support the overall direction of the p roposals and their objective of 
addressing challenges arising from the cumulative impact of EU digital regulation. 
A timely adoption of the proposals by co -legislators will be critical, especially for the AI 
omnibus. In particular, the Commission’s proposal to delay the entry into application of parts 
of the AI Act must be approved as soon as possible, since the releva nt requirements will 
otherwise apply as of 2 August 2026. A smooth and timely  legislative process will send a 
strong, positive signal to businesses that the EU is committed to supporting legal certainty 
and innovation. 
At the same time, greater ambition will be needed as the legislative process moves forward, 
particularly in the areas of data and cybersecurity. While the Data and Cyber Omnibus 
introduces positive elements, including the proposal for a Single Entry Point,  it does not yet 
resolve a number of critical issues. These include concerns around overlapping compliance 
and documentation requirements,  insufficiently consistent and harmonized oversight, a 
proliferation of auditing obligations across cybersecurity fra meworks, persistent legal 
uncertainty and complexity created by the Data Act and the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA). 
Addressing these shortcomings will be essential to ensure that the package delivers 
meaningful simplification in practice. 
Balancing timelines and ambition will be fundamental for the success of the proposals. This is 
why we believe the package should be understood as a first step rather than a finished work. 
Significant work remains to fully assess and address the interaction  and combined effects of 
EU legislative frameworks, including the AI Act, Data Act, GDPR, DSA, online platform 
regulations, and cybersecurity legislation such as the NIS2, CRA and DORA, as well as relevant 
sectoral rules. In this context, we welcome the wi der review of the EU’s Digital Rulebook in 
the form of the Commission’s announced Digital Fitness Check initiative and urge EU 
institutions to maintain strong momentum toward further simplification, alignment, and 
coherence across the digital regulatory landscape.

Detailed Comments: 
 
1. Clarify the delay of the application of high-risk AI systems rules – Art. 113 AI Act 
 
ITI strongly welcomes the proposal to delay the entry into application of the AI Act’s high-risk 
AI systems requirements (Chapter III of the AI Act). This measure is essential to support the 
EU innovation ecosystem, given the current lack of relevant stand ards, necessary guidelines 
and implementing legislation, as well as to ensure optimal preparedness for setting up 
oversight structures at national level.  
 
While the measure is positive, it currently lacks predictability  – which is needed to 
meaningfully produce legal certainty for companies. The proposal provides that the high-risk 
AI systems requirements of the AI Act would apply following a decision from the Commission 
establishing that ‘adequate measures in support of  compliance’ are in place. From that 
moment, companies would have 6 months (for Annex III) or 12 months (for Annex I) to comply 
with the regulation. The notion of ‘adequate measures in support of compliance’ lacks clarity, 
and makes the postponement in its current form unpredictable and discretionary. The 
proposal also provides that, in absence of such Commission decision, Chapter III of the AI Act 
would apply as of 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems, and as of 2 August 2028 for Annex 
I systems.   
 
A fixed deadline for compliance would provide more clarity for businesses , and help them 
better time their compliance preparedness and investments. We urge co -legislators to 
remove the references to a Commission’s decision establishing that ‘adequate measures in 
support of compliance’ are in place, and more simply set a practical and realistic deadline for 
compliance. Setting the deadline to 2 August 2028 for all high-risk AI systems would provide 
additional time for businesses to advance ongoing standardiz ation work and implement 
relevant standards and expected guidelines, and for regulators to ensure preparedness for 
oversight.  
 
Importantly, this measure will only be helpful if it is passed well ahead before the AI Act is 
poised to enter into application on 2 August 2026. This is why we urge co-legislators to swiftly 
conclude negotiations on the AI Omnibus. While our preference is that the whole package is 
passed as soon as possible, if it becomes clear that no agreement can be reached on the whole 
proposal on time, the Commission and co-legislators should separate this article from the rest 
of the AI Omnibus and fast-track its approval.

2. Expand the grace period for transparency obligations – Art. 111 AI Act 
 
The proposed Article 111 (4) provides for a 6 -month grace period for the generative AI 
transparency requirements in Article 50(2) of the AI Act – shifting the compliance deadline 
from 2 August 2026, to 2 February 2027. The grace period is however only limi ted to AI 
systems that have been placed in the market before 2 August 2026. While the proposal goes 
in the right direction, it should be significantly expanded to allow for feasible timelines for 
compliance.  
 
First of all, the grace period should be extended to 12 months. The requirements on marking 
and labelling for generative AI in article 50 (2) and (4) of the AI Act will need to be further 
specified in a Code of Practice, which is currently under preparation, and expected to be 
finalized in the summer of 2026. The Code of Practice is an important tool to provide technical 
and legal clarity on a complex and nascent subject matter – and companies will need several 
months after its finalization to implement it in existing and new products. We urge co -
legislators to extend the grace period to 12 months, since the proposed 6-month delay would 
be insufficient to appropriately implement the Code.  
 
Second, the grace period should be extended to all systems – not just those already in the 
market. Since the current proposal limits the grace period to systems that have been placed 
on the market before 2 August 2026, any system placed in the EU market after 2 August would 
have to be compliant immediately. This would be technically unfeasible and cou ld delay the 
placement in the EU market of new and innovative generative AI systems. This is why we 
recommend extending the grace period to any system that has  been placed in the market 
between 2 August 2026 and the (extended) grace period 2 August 2027. 
 
Third, the grace period should cover the entirety of article 50, and especially article 50(4).  
Since the forthcoming Code of Practice will clarify the transparency and marking obligations 
for deployers under Article 50(4), the lack of any grace period for deployers creates the same 
implementation challenges that justified delaying Article 50(2). To allow deployers adequate 
time to translate the Code’s requirements into robust engineering and operational practices, 
 
 
 
 
5 
the application timeline for Article 50(4) and the remainder of Article 50 should likewise be 
extended. This alignment will help ensure that both providers and deployers can implement 
the Code of Practice in a consistent, technically sound, and interoperab le manner that fully 
reflects the Commission’s forthcoming guidan ce. In addition, entities will find it challenging 
or even impossible to adhere to a Code that will only be finalized a few weeks before the rules 
will be enforced. 
 
Finally, as mentioned above, the grace period is only helpful if it is passed on time . This is 
why we reiterate that the proposal should be negotiated and agreed upon as soon as possible 
before 2 August 2026. Should that not be possible, this article should be negotiated separately 
from the rest of the proposal together with the amendments to article 113 discussed above.

3. Support the deletion of the registration requirements in Art. 49(2) AI Act 
 
We urge co-legislators to support the proposed deletion of Article 49(2) of the AI Act . The 
proposal exempts from registration requirements those AI systems that are not in scope of 
the high -risk AI requirements, even if they are intended to be used in the high -risk areas 
mentioned in Annex III – in line with relevant exemptions in article 6 of the AI Act. This would 
reduce administrative burden and security risks, as well as more proportionately calibrate the 
impact of the AI Act on non -high-risk AI system s. This provision ensures legal clarity and is 
fully consistent with the risk -based approach of the EU AI Act, i.e. the (high) level of risk 
mandates the (high) level of obligations and procedural tasks do not qualify in that matter. 
 
4. Support Article 4a AI Act on processing special categories of personal data for bias 
mitigation and detection  
 
The AI Act includes requirements to assess and mitigate potential biases in training, testing 
and validation datasets of high-risk AI systems (Article 10). Similarly, requirements for General 
purpose AI models with systemic risk (Article 55) require provid ers to assess and mitigate 
‘systemic risks,’ which could potentially include biases. To comply with these requirements, 
developers will need to process data about different topics – including sensitive topics – which 
may be restricted under the GDPR. This creates an important point of tension between the AI 
Act and the GDPR, which causes significant legal and operational uncertainty for companies. 
 
To address that, the EU AI Act currently allows for the processing of special categories of data 
(as defined in the GDPR) by providers of high-risk AI systems when it is “strictly necessary for 
 
 
 
 
6 
the purposes of ensuring bias monitoring, detection and correction.” Article 10(5) AI Act says 
this processing shall be “subject to appropriate safeguards for the fundamental freedoms of 
natural persons.” However, this allowance is limited to high -risk systems, creating a gap for 
other AI systems and, crucially, General Purpose AI (GPAI) models. 
 
The proposed Article 4a of the AI Omnibus would extend the current Article 10(5) allowance 
to providers and deployers of AI systems and models, and therefore increase legal certainty 
for the entire AI ecosystem and allow operators to adequately balance between bias 
mitigation and data privacy objectives. This is why we urge co-legislators to strongly support 
the newly proposed article 4a of the AI Act. 
 
In doing so, some adjustments are needed to make the measure more proportionate and 
risk-based, and aligned with the GDPR. The proposal currently sets out a list of safeguards in 
Art. 4a (1) (a-f) that should be in place before processing special categories of personal data 
for the purpose of bias mitigation and detection. However, these safeguards go beyond those 
set out in  article 9 GDPR and elsewhere in the GDPR – even though the GDPR already 
adequately addresses fundamental right risks related to processing of such special categories 
of data.  
 
Some examples of inconsistencies with the GDPR created by Art 4a (1) (a-f) include:  
 
• Article 4a(1)(b) mandates “state -of-the-art security and privacy -preserving measures.” 
However, when deciding on appropriate measures under the GDPR, one should not only 
look at ‘state of the art’ but also at the nature, scope, context and purposes of processing 
as well as the risk of varying likelihood and severity of risks for individuals. 
• Article 4a(1)(d) forbids access to the special categories of personal data for third parties. 
However, companies should be allowed to work with processors and subprocessors, as 
long as this is done in compliance with the obligations imposed by the GDPR. 
• Article 4a(1)(e) would require companies to delete the special categories of personal 
data once the bias has been corrected. This provision overlooks the fact that  bias is not 
a one-time issue but a dynamic risk that can emerge or evolve throughout the lif ecycle 
of an AI system - particularly when the system is implemented in real world setting. 
Framing bias as something that can be definitively “corrected” at a single moment 
creates a false sense of finality and would deprive providers of the ability to ve rify 
whether the system reintroduces bias later on. 
• Article 4a(1)(f) would require companies to include in the GDPR records of processing 
activities the reasoning as to why processing of special categories of personal data was 
necessary to detect and correct biases. Under the GDPR framework, the register of  
processing activities (RoPA) required by Article 30 is intended as a concise, factual 
inventory of processing operations, documenting who processes what categories of 
personal data, for what purposes, with which safeguards, and not as a vehicle for 
normative justification or legal ‑reasoning. Expanding RoPA to demand detailed 
justification for the necessity of special‑category data would effectively convert it into a 
quasi‑impact‑assessment tool, conflating distinct compliance instruments 
(documentation under Article 30, necessity/proportionality under Article 9, and 
risk‑based evaluation under Article 35). This conflation undermines the structural clarity 
 
 
 
 
7 
GDPR deliberately establishes, duplicates analyses already required in Data Protection 
Impact Assessments (DPIAs), and risks administrative overload, inconsistent 
documentation and blurred compliance responsibilities.  
 
To address these inconsistencies, we suggest the amendment below:  
 
Article 4a 
Processing of special categories of personal data for bias detection and mitigation 
 
1. To the extent necessary to ensure bias detection and correction in relation to high-risk AI 
systems in accordance with Article 10 (2), points (f) and (g), of this Regulation, providers 
of such systems may exceptionally process special categories of personal data, subject to 
appropriate safeguards for the fundamental rights and freedoms of natural persons in line 
with the safeguards set out in Regulations (EU) 2016/679 and (EU) 2018/1725 and 
Directive (EU) 2016/680, as applicable.  In addition to the safeguards set out in 
Regulations (EU) 2016/679 and (EU) 2018/1725 and Directive (EU) 2016/680, as 
applicable, all the following conditions shall be met in order for such processing to occur:  
(a) the bias detection and correction cannot be effectively fulfilled by processing other data, 
including synthetic or anonymised data;  
(b) the special categories of personal data are subject to technical limitations on the re -use of the 
personal data, and state -of-the-art security and privacy -preserving measures, including 
pseudonymisation;  
(c) the special categories of personal data are subject to measures to ensure that the personal data 
processed are secured, protected, subject to suitable safeguards, including strict controls and 
documentation of the access, to avoid misuse and ensure that on ly authorised persons have 
access to those personal data with appropriate confidentiality obligations;  
(d) the special categories of personal data are not transmitted, transferred or otherwise accessed 
by other parties;  
(e) the special categories of personal data are deleted once the bias has been corrected or the 
personal data has reached the end of its retention period, whichever comes first;  
(f) the records of processing activities pursuant to Regulations (EU) 2016/679 and (EU) 
2018/1725 and Directive (EU) 2016/680 include the reasons why the processing of special 
categories of personal data was necessary to detect and correct biases, and why that  objective 
could not be achieved by processing other data.  
 
2. Paragraph 1 may apply to providers and deployers of other AI systems and models and 
deployers of high -risk AI systems where necessary and proportionate if the processing 
occurs for the purposes set out therein and provided that the conditions set out under  the 
safeguards set out in this paragraph.  
 
5. Clarify the interplay between the EU AI Act and the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) 
 
We recommend adding clarifications to the AI Omnibus on the relationship between the AI 
Act’s cybersecurity requirements for AI systems and the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA). In 
particular, we urge co -legislators to confirm that a high -risk AI system which co mplies with 
the CRA’s essential cybersecurity requirements and holds an EU Declaration of Conformity 
under the CRA is deemed to meet the AI Act’s Article 15 requirements on cybersecurity, 
robustness, and accuracy. 
 
 
 
 
 
8 
In the absence of such clarification, providers face legal uncertainty and the risk of duplicative 
or inconsistent conformity assessments across overlapping EU regulatory frameworks. This 
alignment would prevent duplicative testing, reduce administrative b urdens for both 
providers and notified bodies, and ensure that the EU’s acquis functions as an integrated 
system rather than a set of parallel obligations.  
 
6. Clarify the new oversight powers of the AI Office – Art. 75 AI Act 
 
The proposed amendments to Art. 75 of the AI Act would extend the enforcement and 
supervision powers of the AI Office to cover AI systems in scope of the AI Act that also 
constitute or are integrated in Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs) or Very Large Onl ine 
Search Engines (VLOSEs) under the EU Digital Services Act (DSA).  
 
Overall, we agree that centralizing oversight for these categories of providers helps mitigate 
potential conflicts with existing digital legislation and aligns supervisory responsibilities with 
the approach under the DSA, where the Commission supervises the largest platforms in light 
of their cross -border impact. This approach can help prevent fragmented oversight and  
enforcement across the Single Market and therefore promote legal certainty for these 
providers.  
 
While we encourage co -legislators to support the Commission’s proposal, additional 
safeguards and clarifications are needed:  
 
1) Clarify independence and quality safeguards: Under the proposed amendments to 
Article 75, the Commission is set to become the sole organizer of pre -market 
conformity assessments for certain high-risk AI systems. It would be helpful to clarify 
in the law the independence (including from the rest of the Commission and AI Office 
themselves) and quality criteria that the AI Office would be subject to in its role as 
conformity assessment body, in line with the rules laid out in Section 4 of Chapter 3 
of the AI Act.  
2) Leave providers flexibility to choose other accredited third -party conformity 
assessors if applicable: The approach of having the Commission as the sole conformity 
assessment body for certain high -risk AI systems creates a single point of failure and 
potential bottlenecks for high-risk systems. We recommend amending this to ensure 
providers retain the flexi bility to choose accredited third -party conformity assessors 
directly, avoiding a lock -in to the European Commission that is inconsistent with 
standard EU product safety legislation.     
3) Include all internal and external AI systems and tools operated by or on behalf of 
VLOPs and VLOSEs:  It would be helpful to clarify that the broadened enforcement 
powers will include all internal AI systems and tools operated by or on behalf of VLOPs 
and VLOSEs, regardless of whether their use is external or internal in nature. This 
precision is necessar y to avoid gaps in oversight and to ensure that the regulatory 
framework is comprehensive, effective, and future-proof. 
 
7. Clarify interplay between GDPR’s Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) and the 
AI Act’s Fundamental Rights Impact Assessments (FRIAs) 
 
 
 
 
 
9 
The GDPR’s DPIAs and the AI Act’s FRIAs – while having a different focus – cover overlapping 
concerns in practice.  Conducting both assessments would lead to redundancy and increase 
the compliance burden for public authorities and companies in scope. To avoid unnecessary 
duplication in line with the EU’s simplification objectives, companies should be clearly allowed 
to leverage DPIAs to fulfill AI Act obligations. The FRIAs should only cover the parts that are 
not already addressed in the DPIA. This would re duce duplication, streamline internal 
governance, and ensure consistency in risk identification and mitigation. 
 
While Article 27(4) of the AI Act allows some alignment, it does not create sufficient legal 
certainty and it may be  applied inconsistently by different authorities across the EU. This is 
why we recommend clarifying that deployers may satisfy both requirements through a single 
comprehensive assessment.

9. Further streamline interplay between the AI Act and sectoral regulation – Annex I AI 
Act 
 
While the proposed AI Omnibus contains some helpful measures aimed at streamlining 
conformity assessment processes for AI systems covered by both the AI Act and sectoral 
regulations in Annex I of the Act, it still falls short of addressing key points of tension.  
 
Better alignment and more clarity are needed on the interplay between the EU AI Act and 
sectoral regulation, especially those listed in Annex I (section A), for which the AI Act applies 
directly. Market operators in these sectors – which are regulated by specific and well -
functioning vertical product safety frameworks - are facing growing uncertainty, which is 
impacting their ability to place products in the market in critical sectors and generally delay 
 
 
 
 
10 
innovation. For example, there are overlaps and inconsistent definitions across horizontal and 
vertical rules– such as the divergent definitions of ‘safety component’ or ‘safety function’ 
under the AI Act and the Machinery Regulation, or the definition of ‘substantial modification’ 
in the AI Act and in the Medical Devices Regulation and In Vitro Diagnostic M edical Devices 
Regulation. This uncertainty is compounded by delays in the preparations of harmonized 
standards, potential divergences between vertical and horizontal standards as well as 
questions related to the preparedness of the existing conformity assessment infrastructure 
to fulfil its functions under the AI Act, especially given the nascent nature of conformity 
assessment for AI.  
 
To address these issues, we recommend: 
 
• The Omnibus should move section A of Annex I under section B.  Section B of Annex I 
better takes into account the specificities of regulated sectors, by maintaining the 
governance and conformity assessment framework of existing sectoral regulations while 
specifying that the AI Act’s requirements will be taken into ac count in future delegated 
and implementing acts relevant to those legislations, on the basis of the technical and 
regulatory specificities of each sector. However, the Commission must ensure that  any 
future amendments of such regulations remain grounded in the principle of the risk -
based approach of the AI Act, to avoid overreach or excessively stringent interpretations 
in different sectoral rules.  
o The European Commission has already proposed this approach for the Medical 
Devices and In -Vitro Diagnostics Regulations in the recent medical devices 
simplification package (COM(2025)1023, 16 December 2025) , noting that parallel 
obligations in horizontal and vertical regulations would “lead to overlaps of 
requirements and stifle innovation.” This is very welcome. We believe this 
approach should be extended to all the sectoral frameworks in Annex I Section A of 
the AI Act.  
• Definition of safety component:  Products covered by Annex I sectoral regulations will 
fall in the scope of the AI Act as a high-risk AI system if two conditions apply – as defined 
in Article 6(1) of the AI Act: 1) an AI system is a ‘safety component’ of the product or is 
the product its elf; and 2) the product is required to undergo third party conformity 
assessment under the relevant Union harmonization legislation. Recital 55 of the AI Act 
clarifies that components used solely for cybersecurity purposes should not be 
considered safety c omponents, in the specific case of management and operation of 
critical infrastructure (as per Annex III point 2 of the AI Act).  
▪ However, draft Commission guidelines on the interpretation of the notion of 
safety component for the Radio Equipment Directive (RED) noted that 
cybersecurity components mentioned in Articles 3(3)(d -f) of the RED could be 
considered safety components under the AI Act. This would potentially bring into 
the scope of the AI Act a large number of products, beyond the initial targeted 
approach of the Act. 
▪ In order to provide clarity to operators in the market, the Omnibus should clearly 
state that components used solely for cybersecurity purposes should not qualify 
 
 
 
 
11 
as safety components under the EU AI Act.  This would be more in line with the 
targeted risk-based logic underpinning the AI Act.  
 
10. Address Diffusion of Enforcement Responsibilities Across Authorities & Fragmentation 
Risks 
 
The AI Act's enforcement structure creates significant complexity for businesses providing or 
deploying high-risk AI systems across multiple EU markets. While the GDPR allows companies 
to primarily work with a single national authority where they have their main establishment, 
the AI Act creates a more complex structure. Thus, some companies will be required to 
engage with multiple national Market Surveillance Authorities for general compliance across 
and within different Member States and additionally may face oversight from the European 
Commission's AI Office for matters relating to general -purpose AI models or systems. This 
complex structure poses challenges for companies regardless of size who must navigate 
multiple regulatory interfaces.  
 
As a result, different individual market surveillance authorities will need to decide how to 
regulate a specific use case, even if the same use case is provided in several Member States. 
Under this framework, similar products and use cases risk being subje ct to different AI Act 
provisions, depending on the market surveillance authority. In addition, national laws 
implementing the AI Act in different Member States may introduce diverging provisions which 
further fragment the Single Market and create inconsis tencies for companies operating in 
different Member States. The financial and administrative burden of this fragmented 
enforcement approach is substantial for companies of all sizes. 
 
The AI Act describes several coordination mechanisms for high-risk AI systems which build on 
product safety regulation mechanisms, such as mutual exchange of information and possible 
alignment of decisions. While these are positive, there is still a substa ntial risk of 
fragmentation of competencies within the same jurisdiction which creates complexity and 
harms legal certainty for businesses operating across jurisdictions. 
 
Our preferred solution would be to allow companies to interface primarily with one single 
national authority, rather than multiple national market surveillance bodies, following the 
country-of-origin/main establishment principle. However, as an immediate s implification 
measure, it would also be helpful to establish a mechanism for mutual recognition between 
national market surveillance authorities , enabling companies to operate EU -wide without 
country-specific product modifications. This mechanism would red uce administrative 
burdens, particularly for SMEs, by establishing that when a market surveillance authority 
(MSA) conducts a comprehensive risk classification assessment, other MSAs should rely on 
that determination unless significant new evidence emerges . This approach would provide 
legal certainty for providers scaling AI solutions across the EU Single Market while maintaining 
robust oversight. 
 
11. Introduce a common duty for regulators to promote AI innovation  
 
 
 
 
 
12 
The implementation and application of the AI Act should aim to deliver on its original 
objective: creating an ecosystem of excellence and trust in Europe. This requires fostering an 
environment where technological advancements can flourish while ensuring t he protection 
of all individual rights and safety. To achieve that, competent authorities should be equipped 
with the necessary tools to maintain a delicate balance between different policy objectives 
such as fundamental rights, competition, innovation, pr ivacy, and security. In addition, to 
avoid fragmentation in the Single Market and boost legal certainty for companies, increased 
efforts must be made to ensure a coherent application of EU rules applying to AI across 
Member States. 
 
To address that, the AI Omnibus can be used to introduce a common statutory duty for all 
regulators and authorities tasked with enforcing the AI Act at EU and national level to 
promote technological innovation, growth and competition as part of their manda tes. An 
explicit mandate is needed to help reflect the AI Act objectives as well as the EU’s ambition in 
the AI Continent Action Plan.  
 
12. Avoid limiting simplification measures to SMEs and SMCs 
 
The proposed AI Omnibus contains several measures aimed at reducing the regulatory burden 
for small and medium sized enterprises (SMEs) and small -mid cap enterprises (SMCs). While 
we agree that it is important for EU regulations to remain simple and consid er the needs of 
smaller companies, we believe that  asymmetric regulation is not the right approach.  
 
For simplification to be truly effective and deliver concrete benefits to the EU tech ecosystem, 
streamlined processes and proportionate obligations should be delivered for companies of all 
sizes. Importantly, asymmetric regulation could fragment the Singl e Market and create 
unintended impact in B2B relations and partnerships between smaller and larger companies, 
where divergent regulatory standards apply. Moreover, the AI Act is a risk-based framework, 
whereby obligations are tied to the specific risk to f undamental rights and safety posed by a 
specific system – not to the size of the provider of said system. Therefore, the proposed 
asymmetric treatment of smaller companies runs counter to the risk-based logic of the Act.

Data and Cyber Omnibus 
 
Summary of ITI’s Key Recommendations: 
 
1. Clarify the scope and functioning of the Single Entry Point (SEP) for cyber incident 
reporting:

Privacy: GDPR and ePrivacy 
 
1. Support and clarify the changes to the definition of personal data – Art. 4 GDPR 
 
We support the Commission’s proposed changes to the definition of personal data in Article 
4(1) of the GDPR. These changes help in clarifying and harmonizing the definition of personal 
data under the GDPR. The proposal confirms that information qualifies as personal data only 
where a specific controller can reasonably identify an individual using the means available in 
practice, de-facto codifying the "relative approach" applied by the Court of Justice of the EU, 
for example in the Breyer ruling of 2016 (Ca se C -582/14) and several times since then, 
including the Nowak, CRIF, Scania, OLAF and IAB Europe cases, and most recently in the  EDPS 
v SRB ruling.  This codification from a theoretical to an operational standard strengthens 
legal certainty and supports more consistent enforcement across Member States , while 
repeating what the CJEU has said and maintaining the core protections and principles of the 
GDPR. 
 
The proposal underlines the entity-relative test for identifiability (i.e., information is not to be 
considered personal data for entities who cannot identify the person to whom that 
information belongs.) This makes it clear  that the GDPR applies only where identification is 
realistically feasible. 
 
However, we urge co -legislators to further clarify the proposal . In particular, it would be 
helpful to clarify the notion of “means reasonably likely to be used by that entity” to identify 
a natural person. The Omnibus should explicitly require that the assessment of whether 
information constitutes personal data takes  into account factors such as the time, cost, 
technological resources, and safeguards available to the controller, as well as lawfulness and 
foreseeability of identification, and the specific c ircumstances of the processing (e.g., where 
data is processed for security/performance purposes and an entity lacks the means for 
identification). Crucially, illicit activities like hacking or unauthorized access by rogue 
employees constitute security brea ches or legal violations rather than "reasonable means" 
for identification and should not factor into the identifiability threshold. Adding these 
considerations would reinforce the principle of proportionality and ensure closer alignment 
with Recital 26, s upporting a more balanced and coherent application of the Regulation. In 
addition, the proposed amendment should also explicitly state that the assessment of 
whether the entity has reasonable means available to identify the data subject applies even 
in the case of data sharing within the same corporate group. One entity may treat data as 
 
 
 
 
21 
personal while another does not, provided each conducts a genuine assessment of its own 
means of identification. This clarification would ensure that obligations are proportionate and 
based on actual identification capability, not on what another entity could theoretically do. 
 
Finally, we urge co ‑legislators to ensure that the Commission meaningfully involves a broad 
range of stakeholders, including industry and business representatives in the consultations 
foreseen under Article 41a (4) on the criteria for pseudonymisation and identification. Such  
engagement is essential to ensure that the implementing acts reflect real ‑world technical 
constraints and operational practices. Companies across sectors work daily with diverse 
datasets, complex data architectures, and modern privacy ‑enhancing technologies, and can 
provide concrete, evidence‑based insights into how pseudonymisation techniques perform in 
practice, where residual re ‑identification risks persist, and which safeguards are technically 
feasible at scale. Without this practical expertise, there is a risk that implementing criteria 
may be misaligned with existing data processing realities, difficult to operationalize, or 
inadvertently hinder innovation. Consultation with industry will therefore be critical to ensure 
that the im plementing acts are workable, proportionate, and capable of delivering 
meaningful legal certainty for all actors in the data ecosystem.

2. Support and clarify changes to Article 9 GDPR on special categories of personal data 
 
ITI welcomes the Commission’s changes to Article 9 of the GDPR, related to the processing of 
special categories of personal data for the purpose of AI training and operation and urges co-

At the same time, article 9 imposes strict safeguards which aim to protect fundamental rights.

A. The proposal should clarify the scope of measures in Article 9 (1) GDPR.  
 
The scope of Article 9 has expanded beyond its original intent to protect the most sensitive 
categories of data due to court decisions and wide interpretations from Data Protection 
Authorities (DPAs). For instance, the CJEU’s Lindenapotheke ruling confirms that Article 9 may 
apply even where there is only a potential for data to reveal special category data (SCD), while 
the Bundeskartellamt and OT v Vyriausioji tarnybinės etikos komisija decisions further extend 
coverage to incidental or indirect processing, regardless of intent.  
 
These expansive interpretations have been reinforced by DPAs and have resulted in almost 
any personal data risking classification as ‘special category of data,’ thus making it increasingly 
challenging to process data in the absence of explicit consent. Labelling too many data types 
as “sensitive,” without a clear risk rationale dilutes the focus on truly high -risk data uses, 
leading to inefficient allocation of privacy resources and barriers to safety and innovation. 
This approach is impractical and misalig ned with the original purpose of Article 9, especially 
in the context of AI and large -scale automated systems. This is why we urge co-legislators to 
support amending Article 9(1) of the GDPR in the Omnibus.

C. Refine the proposed biometric provision in Article 9 (2)(l) 
 
The Omnibus proposal introduces in Article 9(2)(l) of the GDPR an explicit legal basis for the 
processing of special categories of data (SCD) in the context of biometric verification. The 
proposal is helpful and it should aim to ensure data can be used to support the deployment 
of innovative industrial applications in Europe (e.g. workforce identity, access management, 
infrastructure management, authorizations for high -risk machinery, supply chain logistics 
security, etc.). While we generally support the provision, we urge co-legislators to refine the 
proposed language.  
 
As drafted, the provision may inadvertently narrow the lawful use of biometric 
authentication. By creating a bespoke Article 9(2) exception, the proposal implicitly assumes 
that all biometric authentication is special category processing and can impact a r ange of 
authentication practices. The reference to “sole control” is ambiguous and risks excluding 
 
 
 
 
24 
common, privacy-preserving architectures, such as hardware -secured, on-device matching, 
simply because device manufacturers retain control over firmware, security patches, or 
platform integrity. What matters in practice is not exclusive possession, but effective control, 
meaning that only the data subject can authorize use of their biometric data for verification, 
and that technical and contractual measures make it impossible for any third party to access 
or repurpose the biometric template. 
 
This is why we suggest replacing “sole control” with “effective control” and clarifying that the 
assessment of “necessity” must be internal to the chosen authentication method. If the 
exception then applies only where “sole control” is met, mainstream implementations, device 
based verification, secure enclaves, or ecosystems with cloud linked enrolment may fall 
outside the exception and be pushed into explicit consent models where none was previously 
required.  A shift to “effective control,” combined with a purpose -specific necessity test, 
would provide legal certainty while supporting secure, user -controlled biometric 
authentication

(l) “Processing of biometric data is necessary for the purpose of confirming the identity of 
a data subject (verification) through means of biometric data processing , where the 
biometric data or the means needed for the verification is under the sole effective control 
of the data subject”

The proposed new Article 88b of the GDPR would introduce a framework for consent 
interfaces to transmit machine -readable consent signals. Controllers would be required to 
recognize and interpret such signals, with certain media service providers benefiting  from 
specific exemptions. In addition, the proposal would establish a new “trusted flagging 
infrastructure” to complement existing consent mechanisms. The lack of legal and technical 
workability of Art.88b introduces profound uncertainty that risks underm ining existing ads -

funded business models and the open internet. As such, we urge co-legislators to remove it 
from the Commission’s proposal.       
 
Efforts in establishing centralized, EU -wide consent tools (e.g., the ePrivacy  Regulation and 
Cookie Pledge initiative) have repeatedly failed due to technical and political challenges. The 
reality is that no interoperable technical standard exists for universal, machine -readable 
consent signals across browsers, operating systems, a pps, and devices. In light of this, a 
mandatory, infrastructure-level consent signal rule appears unrealistic and misaligned with 
the Omnibus Proposal’s core objectives and should be deleted from the Omnibus proposal. 
 
Such tools fail to satisfy the core requirements of the GDPR and are unlikely to resolve the 
issue of "banner fatigue." Since signals are inherently broad, they cannot provide the 
"specific" and "informed" consent required by the GDPR. A single setting renewed every few 
months cannot possibly account for the unique data practices of millions of different 
websites. As a result, consumers cannot express a service-by-service consent and the law will 
continue to require publishers to obtain and record valid consent by prompting users to make 
choices, meaning the total number of banners will remain largely unchanged. Furthermore, 
browsers lack the oversight capabilities and legal responsibility to monitor how individual 
sites actually handle data, which creates a significant risk of transparency gaps and fraud.    
 
This centralized approach also threatens the financial stability of the digital ecosystem. By 
limiting personalization and lowering consent rates, these mechanisms drastically reduce the 
value of advertising , making it harder for European businesses to fund their operations or 
reach new customers. Even contextual advertising is at risk; essential backend functions like 
frequency capping and fraud detection rely on the very cookies that would be blocked. 
Ultimately, this loss of revenue will force publishers  to adopt paywalls, creating a "two -tier" 
internet where quality information is no longer free to everyone.     
 
The proposed exemption for media service providers would additionally and significantly 
complicate implementation of this system. The exemption would create a two -tiered 
approach to consents, distinguishing between “media service providers” and non -“media 
service providers”.  This would be unworkable to implement. Such an arrangement would 
require companies to  identify GDPR-exempt “media service providers” that may override 
central consent choices, as opposed to others that must adhere to them. This complexi ty 
would likely increase  consumer confusion, as users would continue to  encounter cookie 
banners when visiting media sites. The potential appointment of one or 
more standardization bodies to develop standards for interpreting machine -readable 
indications o f data subjects’ choices does not provide a satisfactory solution to these 
technological challenges. 
 
As mentioned above, the most effective solution to resolve the “cookie fatigue” problem 
would be to expand Article 88a by exempting contextual advertising and basic ad 
functionalities (e.g. frequency capping, ad fraud and brand safety and or third-party analytics) 
from consent. Since these technical processes are low risk and do not involve personal 
profiling, exempting them offers a practical path to decluttering the web of consent banners 
while incentivizing the adoption of privacy-preserving practices.

2. Further increase protections for trade secrets and other sensitive data under Article 4 
of the Data Act  
 
The Data Act introduces provisions on compulsory sharing of trade secrets (Article 4). Data 
holders can refuse sharing their trade secrets in certain cases, but are required to 
demonstrate the high likelihood of serious economic damages stemming from the disclosure 
before they are allowed to exercise all of their protective rights. The Commission’s Omnibus 
proposal introduces additional protections for trade secrets by allowing data holders to refuse 
disclosing trade secrets if that disclosure ‘poses a high  risk of unlawful acquisition, use, or 
disclosure to third country entities […] subject to jurisdictions offering weaker or non -
equivalent protection compared to that under Union law.’  
 
While this amendment is positive, it still falls short from addressing the substantial concerns 
created by the Data Act. The Data Act’s obligation to disclose trade secrets eviscerates the 
value of trade secrets, which indeed resides in their non -disclosure, and places a higher 
threshold to enforcement than other IP rights. It is imperative in a trade secret regime that 
trade secret owners have the final say as to whether or not to share information because 
sharing always bears a risk, and the trade secret owner is never made completely whole when 
a trade secret is misappropriated. 
 
We urge co -legislators to strengthen the protection of trade secrets and sensitive 
information under the Data Act . Our preference would be to do so by repealing the 
mandatory sharing of trade secrets and inserting provisions in Article 4(2) of the Data Act 
allowing users and data holders to contractually restrict access and sharing data where this 
undermines the confidentiality of the data holder’s trade secrets. 
 
This approach would replace this burdensome model with clear contractual safeguards, 
allowing users and data holders to agree upfront on restrictions where disclosure would 
undermine the confidentiality of trade secrets. This approach ensures legal certainty, reduces 
compliance costs, and provides proportionate protection for sensitive information while 
preserving the Data Act’s objectives of data portability and interoperability. 
 
As a fallback position, the trade secret owner should be able to impose minimum protection 
measures on the recipient , and the recipient should be liable for any damages the trade 
secret owner suffers from non -compliance. Moreover, Article 4 currently requires the trade 
secret holder to demonstrate that the sharing of trade secrets would lead to high likelihood 
of suffering economic damage. As mentioned, this creates an extremely high threshold for 
the enforcement of trade secrets, more than other IP rights.  To ensure a proportionate 
approach, the burden should be on the entity requesting access to trade secrets to 
demonstrate that it will take sufficient protective measures for the sensitive data it seeks to 
access.  
 
In addition, the “Security handbrake” in Article 4.2 of the Data Act should be reinforced by 
clarifying that it also includes situations where data sharing would foreseeably introduce or 
exacerbate cyber vulnerabilities, security risks, or cyber incidents.

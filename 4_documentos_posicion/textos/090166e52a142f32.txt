1. About Market, Opinion and Social Research   
 
• Market research is comprised of all forms of market, opinion, and social research.  
 
• Market, opinion and social research is the systematic gathering, analysis and 
interpretation of information about individuals or organisations. It uses the 
statistical and/or analytical methods and techniques of the applied social, 
behavioural, data and other sciences to generate insight and support decision 
making by providers of goods and services, governments, non-profit organisations 
and the general public. 
 
• Market, opinion and social research stands at the heart of well-informed 
commercial, social and political decisions. Its purpose is to deliver information and 
insights about people’s behaviour, needs and attitudes to inform decision making by 
providers of goods and services, governments, individuals, and society at large. 
Insight into what makes the success of a product, business initiative, consumer 
behaviour or government policy strategy is often the hidden – yet defining – factor 
that determines success and failure. It is our sector that provides the deeper

2. Towards a Harmonised and Streamlined European Approach to Data Protection 
EFAMRO and Esomar welcome the European Commission’s commitment to launch a 
coherent simplification strategy for the EU digital rulebook. In light of this important 
initiative, our associations would like to contribute several considerations to support 
the successful adoption and subsequent implementation of the strategy. 
Legal clarity in the GDPR . To support the European Commission’s simplification 
objectives, we strongly recommend the development of clearer and more harmonised 
guidelines on the application of the GDPR in complex data chains like those frequently 
found in market, opinion and social  research use-cases. There are several areas where 
greater clarity would significantly benefit the research sector: 
 
Clarifying roles and responsibilities in data processing chains . We support the need 
for accurate and factual delineation of the roles and responsibilities of controllers, 
processors and third parties under the GDPR. In the context of market, opinion and social 
research activities, the dichotomy of controller, joint controllers and processor is not 
always clear or reflective of the complexity of a data chain, resulting in legal uncertainty 
around liability and responsibility in the personal data processing chain. Emerging case 
law and regulatory guidance seem to suggest that the GDPR increasingly places the 
responsibility for data controllership on the client commissioning a research project, due 
to the assumption from non -practitioners that the client deter mines the ‘purpose’ and 
the ‘means’ when commissioning market , opinion and social  research projects . This 
narrow interpretation of the GDPR  ignores the EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 (points 25 and

Commissioning clients often do not determine the methodology or specific detailed

commissioning client by default. 
Clients often commission market, opinion and social  research service providers  to 
provide independent, evidence -based answers to specific business or strategic 
questions. These market, opinion and social research projects typically involve complex 
data processing chains, where responsibilities and decision -making are distributed

straightforward way. For example, in a on e-off client project where a commissioning 
client instructs a research service provider on how to conduct a survey and report 
anonymised market, opinion and social  research results, the research service provider 
acts as a processor, following the client’s instructions. However, the research service 
provider may simultaneously take on a controller role for specific market, opinion and 
social research processing activities it determines independently, such as quality control 
or fraud detection, which fall outside the scope of the client’s instructions. This

EFAMRO and ESOMAR would welcome working with the European Commission to 
support the codification of sector -specific GDPR Codes under Article 40 of the GDPR 3. 
While the industry could have previously moved to develop such codes to address data 
processing activities specific to the sectors context and practices , progress has been 
delayed by the misaligned assumptions about controller attributions across the data 
supply chain. 
 
In effect, the industry has deferred formal codification because the current default , 
which assumes a research -commissioning client is always a controller , prevents a 
realistic application of GDPR roles. EFAMRO and E somar are ready to mobili se these 
codes for market, opinion, and social research provided that a case-by-case assessment 
of role attribution is accepted, and the  blanket assumption of client controllership is no 
longer applied, and instead factual attributions take effect.

We welcome the proposal for the inclusion of a definition of scientific research in 
the GDPR (Article 4(38) Digital Omnibus proposal). EFAMRO and Esomar strongly 
recommend that the scientific research definition applies a broad and inclusive 
interpretation of Article 89 GDPR, reflecting the  diversity of legitimate research 
activities, including those serving a commercial interest, as is the case for  some 
market, opinion and social research activities.  
 
In this context, Recital 159 GDPR should also be explicitly referenced, as it clearly 
emphasises that scientific research is to be interpreted broadly, including 
privately funded research. Market, opinion and social research frequently fall 
within this cat egory. Privately funded market research is an integral part of the 
European research and innovation system. For decades, it has provided 
scientifically grounded contributions to labour market, social, education, 
economic and broader societal analyses, regu larly carrying out research on 
behalf of both public and private entities 
 
We also welcome the reference to “ethical standards” in the proposed definition, 
as it recognises the important role of international codes of conduct, such as the 
4ICC/Esomar International Code on Market, and Social Research and Data 
Analytics and national research codes , in promoting  responsible data use , 
transparency and accountability within the market, opinion and social  research 
sector. As such, these codes can support decision -makers in leveraging high -
quality, ethical market, opinion and social research to develop effective strategies 
that ultimate benefit citizens and end-users alike. 
 
It is therefore important that the legislative text explicitly reflects the established 
understanding of scientific research under Recital 159 of the GDPR. The current 
proposal no longer expressly refers to “privately funded research” , which creates 
legal uncertainty and risks narrowing the established broad interpretation. 
For this reason, “privately funded” should be explicitly included in the last 
sentence of Recital 1595 which should read as follows: 
 
“scientific research” means any research which can also support innovation, such 
as technological development and demonstration. These actions shall contribute 
to existing scientific knowledge or apply existing knowledge in novel ways, be 
carried out with the aim of contributing to the growth of society’s general

• Article 4 (1) GDPR; New Art. 41a, Proposal Art. 3, Point 1 (a); Proposal Art. 3, Point 
10 
Definition of personal data . We welcome the adoption of a more explicit , risk-
based approach to identifiability, particularly for controllers and recipients, in line 
with the CJEU’s reasoning in the Deloitte Judgment. This approach will facilitate 
responsible data sharing and analytics in circumstances where the risk of re -
identification is demonstrably low for the recipient, and where appropriate 
technical and organisational safeguards are in place. 
 
However, greater legal clarity and certainty in this area will be essential. In 
particular, controllers and processors require clear, objective, and operational 
criteria to assess re-identification risk in a consistent and defensible manner, 
and to rely on those assessments with confidence, without diminishing the level 
of protection afforded to data subjects. This need for clarity is especially acute in 
the context of complex data chains, such as those commonly found in market, 
opinion and social research. Research service providers frequently receive 
pseudonymised datasets subject to strict contractual, organisational, and 
technical safeguards, and typically have no access to re-identification keys nor 
any operational reason or ability to re-identify individuals. Despite the minimal 
privacy risk in such scenarios, uncertainty persists as to whether these data 
  
remain fully subject to all GDPR obligations when processed by recipients who 
lack realistic means of re-identification. 
 
To address this uncertainty and support the objectives of the Digital 
Simplification proposals, we recommend that the European Commission: 
a. Formally recognise pseudonymisation as a valid and effective safeguard 
when implemented through appropriate technical, organisational, and 
contractual measures, particularly where re-identification by the recipient 
is neither possible nor intended. 
b. Clarify the circumstances in which the use of robust pseudonymisation 
may justify a proportionate application of certain compliance obligations, 
such as individual information rights, where there is no realistic means of 
re-identification by the data recipient. 
c. Encourage the use of established sectoral codes of conduct, such as the 
ICC/Esomar International Code and national codes, which already set out 
rigorous standards on data minimisation, pseudonymisation, and 
respondent confidentiality. 
 
• Art. 9 (2) GDPR, Proposal Art. 3, Point 3 
Biometric Data and Special Category Biometric Data  
As the Digital Omnibus is developed, it should adopt a similarly nuanced and 
proportionate approach to biometric data as that taken by the UK Information 
Commissioner’s Office6, which clearly distinguishes between biometric personal 
data and special category biometric data. The ICO’s approach recognises that 
not all biometric data are processed for the purpose of uniquely identifying an 
individual and that only biometric data used for identification or authentication 
should be subject to the heightened protections applicable to special category 
data. Embedding a comparable distinction within the Digital Omnibus would 
enhance legal clarity and consistency, reduce over-classification, and support 
innovation in low-risk uses of biometric technologies, such as those practices in 
market, opinion and social research, while ensuring that genuinely high-risk 
biometric processing continues to benefit from enhanced safeguards and 
oversight.

• New Article 88a, Proposal Article 3 Point 15 
Storing or Accessing Data on Terminal Equipment . We also welcome the 
introduction of a new Article 88a, which establishes a clearer and more 
harmonised framework for the storing of, or access to, personal data on users’ 
terminal equipment. By incorporating into the GDPR the consent and lawful -use 
requirements currently addressed under the ePrivacy framework, the Proposal 
helps consolidate governance for cookies and trackers where personal data are 
involved. This approach should streamline compliance, reduce inconsistencies 
between overlapping regulatory regimes, and provide greater clarity on the use of 
legitimate interests in low -risk contexts such as audience measurement and 
service analytics used for research and statistical purposes, while continuing to 
ensure a high level of protection for individuals.

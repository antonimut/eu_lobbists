AI  OMNIBUS  Position  Paper  on  COM  (2025)  836  ﬁnal    
 JANUARY  2026   
The  European  Tech  Alliance  (EUTA)  welcomes  the  European  Commission’s  Digital  Omnibus  on  Artiﬁcial  Intelligence (AI)  as  a  constructive  step  towards  ensuring  that  the  AI  Act  (Regulation  (EU)  2024/1689)  can  be  implemented  in  a  manner  that  genuinely  supports  Europe’s  competitiveness,  innovation  capacity  and  legal  certainty.  European  
tech
 
companies
 
require
 
stable,
 
workable
 
and
 
proportionate
 
rules
 
to
 
invest
 
in,
 
develop,
 
train
 
and
 
deploy
 
AI
 
systems
 
at
 
scale
 
within
 
the
 
European
 
Union.
  This  position  paper  builds  on  EUTA’s  long-standing  engagement  on  AI  regulation,  including  its  previous  AI  Act  position  papers and  the  broader  simpliﬁcation  agenda  outlined  in  Reset  the  Win  Code.  It  focuses  on  targeted  improvements  to  the  Commission’s  proposal  to  ensure  that  the  AI  Act  remains  risk-based,  operationally  
feasible
 
and
 
innovation
‑
enabling.
 
 
General  assessment    
EUTA
 
supports
 
the
 
Commission’s
 
objective
 
to
 
simplify
 
and
 
clarify
 
the
 
implementation
 
of
 
the
 
AI
 
Act.
 
The
 
proposed
 
postponement
 
of
 
certain
 
high-risk
 
obligations,

delay
 
of

rightly

that

and
 
institutional

are

Eﬀorts

and

are

 However,  postponement  alone  will  not  resolve  the  underlying  implementation  challenges.  
European-born

1.  Preserve  the  risk-based  approach    
A
 
core
 
strength
 
of
 
the
 
AI
 
Act
 
is
 
its
 
risk-based
 
structure.
 
Article
 
6(3)
 
is

to
 
fundamental

are
 
not
 
automatically
 
classiﬁed
 
as
 
high-risk.
 
 However,  the  proﬁling  clause  included  in  Article  6(3)  risks  undermining  this  safeguard.  In  
particular,
 
in
 
employment
 
and
 
recruitment
 
contexts,
 
the
 
concept
 
of
 
proﬁling
 
is
 
extremely
 
broad
 interpreted  under  the  General  Data  Protection  Regulation  (GDPR)  (Regulation  (EU)  2016/679)  and  often  captures  routine  and  benign  processing  activities.  If  interpreted  expansively,  this  clause  
could
 
result
 
in
 
virtually
 
all
 
AI
 
systems
 
used
 
in
 
employment
 
being
 
classiﬁed
 
as
 
high-risk,
 
regardless
 
of
 
their
 
actual
 
harm
 
to
 
the
 
health,
 
safety
 
or
 
fundamental
 
rights
 
of
 
natural
 
persons
 
(such
 
as
 
employees,
 
candidates
 
or
 
jobseekers)
 
and
 
regardless
 
of
 
their
 
material
 
inﬂuence
 
on
 
the
 
outcome
 
of
 
decision-making
 
in
 
the
 
employment
 
ﬁeld
 
or
 
for
 
fraud
 
prevention
 
at
 
the
 
payment
 
stage
 
when
 
purchasing
 
goods
 
or
 
services
 
online.
 
 
 Such  an  outcome  would  be  diﬃcult  to  reconcile  with  the  AI  Act’s  stated  commitment  to  
proportionality
 
and
 
risk-based
 
regulation.
 
EUTA
 
therefore
 
encourages
 
the
 
co-legislators
 
to
 
narrow
 
the
 
proﬁling
 
clause
 
in
 
Article
 
6(3),
 
in
 
order
 
to
 
preserve
 
the
 
eﬀectiveness
 
of
 
the
 
exception
 
mechanism
 
and
 
avoid
 
regulatory
 
overreach.
 
 
2.  Provide  practical  tools  to  operationalise  high-risk  obligations  
Even

Obligations

 The  AI  Omnibus  should  be  accompanied  by  clear,  practical  Commission  guidance,  including

Compliance

3.  Make  transparency  obligations  proportionate,  workable  and  
standards-aligned
  Support  postponement  and  extend  the  grace  period  The  transparency  obligations  under  Article  50  of  the  AI  Act  are  essential  to  maintaining  trust  in  
AI-generated

Their

Given

on
 
the

on
 
the
 
market
 
after
 
2
 
August
 
2026,

to

  Align  timelines  with  the  forthcoming  Code  of  Practice  The  grace  period  should  cover  the  entirety  of  Article  50,  including  Article  50(4).  The  forthcoming  
Code
 
of
 
Practice

to

  Ensure  proportional  and  risk-based  application  Transparency  obligations  must  remain  proportionate  and  context-speciﬁc.  In  the  case  of

The

A

obligations
  There  is  a  clear  need  to  clarify  the  deﬁnition  of  “connected  products”  to  ensure  that  specialised  
ﬁnancial
 
hardware,
 
such
 
as
 
point-of-sale
 
(POS)
 
terminals,
 
is
 
not
 
inadvertently
 
brought
 
within
 
the
 
scope
 
of
 
the
 
Data
 
Act.
 
While
 
these
 
devices
 
are
 
connected,
 
their
 
primary
 
function
 
is
 
the
 
secure
 
execution
 
of
 
regulated
 
ﬁnancial
 
transactions,
 
not
 
the
 
generation
 
of
 
behavioural
 
or
 
environmental
 
data
 
for
 
consumer
 
use.
 
Applying  broad  data  access  and  portability  obligations  to  payment  terminals  would  create  
security
 
risks
 
and
 
regulatory
 
duplication.
 
The
 
ﬁnancial
 
sector
 
is
 
already
 
subject
 
to
 
comprehensive
 
data
 
access
 
and
 
sharing
 
frameworks,
 
notably
 
under
 
Payment
 
Services
 
Directive
 2  (PSD2)  and  the  emerging  framework  for  Financial  Data  Access.  The  Commission  has  previously  indicated  that  the  Data  Act  was  not  intended  to  create  new  data  access  rights  in  this  sector.  
Moreover,  under  the  current  deﬁnition  in  Article  2(5)  of  the  Data  Act,  any  device  that  “obtains,  
generates
 
or
 
collects
 
data”
 
could
 
qualify
 
as
 
a
 
connected
 
product.
 
For
 
payment
 
terminals,
 
however,
 
the
 
data
 
processed
 
is
 
strictly
 
functional
 
and
 
often
 
highly
 
sensitive
 
from
 
a
 
payment
 
security
 
perspective.
 
Opening
 
access
 
to
 
such
 
data
 
could
 
undermine
 
the
 
integrity
 
of
 
the
 
payments
 
ecosystem
 
and
 
increase
 
exposure
 
to
 
fraud.
 
➡  Article  2  of  the  Data  Act  should  explicitly  exclude  hardware  whose  primary  function  is  the  
processing
 
of
 
regulated
 
ﬁnancial
 
transactions,
 
thereby
 
avoiding
 
double
 
regulation
 
and
 
preserving
 
the
 
security
 
of
 
Europe’s
 
payments
 
infrastructure.
 
11.  Ensure  fair  switching  without  undermining  contractual  certainty  
While
 
the

of
 
software-as-a-service
 
(SaaS)
 
within
 
the
 
scope
 
of
 
“data

promotes

reduces

SaaS  providers  often  provide  signiﬁcant  upfront  value,  including  software  customisation,  
integration

and
 
adapted

for
 
a
 
ﬁxed-term
 
commitment
 
from
 
the
 
customer.
 
They

on
 
a
 
business
 
model
 
built
 
on
 
stable,
 
predictable
 
subscription
 
revenue
 
(ARR),
 
and
 
this
 
model
 
funds
 
long-term
 
R&D
 
and
 
innovation,
 
supporting
 
investment,
 
stability
 
and
 
service
 
quality.

12.  Avoid  extending  gatekeeper  concepts  beyond  the  DMA 
EUTA  is  concerned  by  proposals  to  extend  restrictions  applicable  to  “gatekeepers”  under  the  Digital  Markets  Act  (DMA)  into  the  Data  Act.  As  a  matter  of  principle,  applying  DMA  concepts  outside  their  original  framework  risks  undermining  legal  coherence  and  regulatory  predictability.  
More  fundamentally,  this  form  of  “gatekeeper  creep”  risks  discouraging  European  tech  
companies
 
from
 
scaling.
 
By
 
creating
 
an
 
implicit
 
threshold
 
beyond
 
which
 
regulatory
 
obligations
 
increase
 
sharply
 
and
 
unpredictably,
 
it
 
may
 
disincentivise
 
growth,
 
ambition
 
and
 
investment
 
in
 
Europe.
 
Any
 
extension
 
of
 
restrictions
 
linked
 
to
 
gatekeeper
 
status
 
should
 
therefore
 
be
 
based
 
on
 
a
 
robust
 
assessment
 
of
 
market
 
realities
 
and
 
competitive
 
dynamics,
 
and
 
carefully
 
consider
 
the
 
impact
 
on
 
consumers’
 
access
 
to
 
innovative
 
and
 
competitive
 
services.
 
➡ Delete  the  references  to  the  DMA  that  introduce  this  artiﬁcial  scope  expansion,  notably  in  
Article
 
1.18
 
of
 
the
 
Digital
 
Omnibus
 
(Data
 
Act
 
Articles
 
32c(d)(iv),
 
32q(6),
 
32r(4)
 
and
 
32y(5)).

This  fragmentation  undermines  the  level  playing  ﬁeld  and  increases  costs  for  companies

Introduction 
The European Commission’s Digital Omnibus Package has the right goal of enhancing the 
competitiveness of Europe’s digital economy through regulatory simplification and coherence. Over 
recent years, the EU’s digital regulatory landscape has expanded significantly , but not always with a 
view of how various measures may interact, overlap and make compliance difficult for businesses. The 
Omnibus offers an opportunity to make the EU’s digital rules more coherent, including the bloc’s rules 
on artificial intelligence (AI). 
The Commission’s Digital Omnibus on AI Regulation proposal (‘AI Omnibus’) takes steps to streamline 
and harmonise rules to the benefit of AI innovation and uptake in the EU. However, there are elements 
of the proposal which still require further clarity. Chief among these is the issue of sequencing: the EU 
must first stabilise the AI Act’s implementation before considering further adaptations to sectoral or 
employment-specific laws. This ensures that any future rules are evidence-based and aligned with the 
overarching horizontal framework . Duplicative rules make the EU a less competitive place for 
companies to pursue AI innovation. 
The recommendations put forward in this paper are  intended to help the European Parliament and 
the Council of the EU  maintain the necessary ambition to deliver a genuinely clear, coherent and 
globally competitive AI framework. 
 
Positive amendments  
1. Streamlining enforcement and implementation 
The proposal’s intent to clarify the roles within the enforcement and implementation of the AI Act 
is positive. O verlapping competences risk creating a fragmented and prohibitive compliance 
landscape for industry. To truly alleviate this complexity, the AI Omnibus must provide a definitive 
hierarchy of authority that prevents conflicting mandates from different regulators. 
While Paragraph 26 introduces a requirement for national Market Surveillance Authorities (MSAs) 
and public authorities to cooperate, this amendment does not go far enough to be  effective. 
Beyond this cooperation requirement, it should include a mutual recognition clause for MSA 
decisions across the EU. Such a mechanism is vital to ensure that the Act is enforced coherently ; 
without it, companies lack the legal certainty of a reliable body of case law and face the risk of 
litigating the same compliance issues in multiple jurisdictions. 
Additionally, the AI Office should be explicitly empowered as the primary entity responsible for 
streamlining and ensuring cross-border coherence. Given that significant portions of the AI Act’s 
implementation will be defined through secondary legislation, the AI Office must ensure these 
delegated rules are coherent, cohesive and strictly aligned with the primary text of the Act.       
2. Reducing the registration burden for non-high-risk uses of AI in high-risk areas  
The proposal to exempt AI systems that perform narrow or procedural tasks from the registration 
requirements in high-risk sectors under Annex III is a step in the right direction. This proportional,

the actual risk profile of such systems. 
Critically, this streamlining does not erode the AI Act’s protection; rather, it enhances it. By 
removing ‘noise’ from the regulatory system, both providers and MSAs can concentrate their 
resources and oversight on the high-impact systems that truly affect the safety and fundamental 
rights of EU citizens. A targeted database would offer transparency to regulators, preventing 
‘compliance fatigue’ and ensuring the law’s rigorous standards are applied where they are most 
needed. 
 
Amendments requiring more clarity  
While the proposal offers practical solutions to several industry concerns, certain areas require further 
refinement to ensure legal clarity and technical feasibility. 
1. Extending the timeline for high-risk and transparency requirements      
The extension of the deadline for high -risk requirements and transparency requirements  is 
necessary. However, the time required for the European Parliament and Council to agree on the 
final text of the full AI Omnibus brings considerable uncertainty to the length and timely 
confirmation of the extensions. This uncertainty  makes it challenging for companies to allocate 
resources and prepare compliance systems with confidence, thereby defeating the main purpose 
of the timeline extension.  For this reason, the co-legislators should de-couple the timeline 
extensions from the rest of the proposal and make it their priority to adopt them  as soon as 
possible. Otherwise, such adoption could become meaningless if it occurs shortly before – or even 
after – the deadlines have already passed. 
Similarly, delays in confirming key compliance timelines also affect the process for adopting 
secondary guidance.  The proposal empowers the AI Office  to determine which  guidance and 
standards are adequate for compliance. Once decided, this will  trigger a six -month timeline for 
companies to comply. This arrangement creates significant legal uncertainty, as the deadline for 
compliance would not be set in time. Additionally, the six-month grace period after a possible AI 
Office decision would be insufficient f or companies to adhere to newly issued guidance and 
standards. AmCham EU therefore suggests amending the proposal to require the AI Office to issue 
an Implementing Act. This Implementing Act should specify a fixed delay to the implementation 
timeline until December 2027, with a  further extension if final guidelines and standards are not 
available by the end of 2026.  
Article 50 Obligations: While Article 50 obligations apply from August 2026, the Code of Practice 
will not likely be finalised until June 2026 at the earliest – leaving less than two months for 
compliance. The proposal to extend this deadline is welcome, but can be improved by: 
1) Extending the period to 12 months to ensure sufficient time for compliance; 
2) Applying the extension to all AI systems placed on the market before the deadline; 
3) Including deep fake obligations under Paragraph 4, which face the same timing challenges 
since the mentioned Code will also specify these provisions.

2. Simplifying supervisory architecture for AI oversight 
 
The EU AI Act’s proposed supervisory architecture introduces unnecessary complexity that risks 
fragmentation, particularly for institutions already subject to strong oversight. Requiring Member 
States to designate at least one (but potentially multiple) market surveillance and notifying 
authorities creates potential overlaps and coordination challenges.   
 
This complexity is particularly acute for sectors with established sector -specific regulators who 
already possess deep insight into their supervised firms’ operational structures and risk 
management. For example, financial markets ’ securities supervisors already conduct 
comprehensive assessments of governance, model risk and data. This existing infrastructure 
should be leveraged for oversight of all AI systems – not only high -risk financial use cases – to 
avoid duplicative structures. To make the most of this experience, the co-legislators should amend 
the Level 1 text to clarify that existing sectoral supervisors shall serve as the lead authorities for 
AI-related oversight where they possess adequate tools and access.   
 
3. Extending provisions for SMEs and SMCs to all providers 
 
The flexibility introduced in Paragraphs 8, 9 and 21 to ensure proportional risk management 
should apply to all providers, regardless of company size. Establishing arbitrary limitations based 
on corporate scale undermines the uniform safeguards and consistent rules essential for both 
stakeholders and customers, as product safety and fundamental rights expectations do not vary  
based on the size of the developer. A truly risk -based approach should focus on the specific use 
case and potential impact of the AI system, ensuring a predictable legal environment and 
maintaining high safety standards across the entire Digital Single Market. 
 
4. Ensuring flexibility in choosing high-risk conformity assessors 
 
The Omnibus would benefit from a targeted amendment to Article 75 to ensure providers retain 
the flexibility to choose accredited third-party conformity assessors, operating at both the EU and 
Member State level. The current proposal, which suggests the Co mmission could act as the sole 
organiser of pre-market conformity assessments for certain high -risk AI systems, introduces the 
risk of a single point of failure and resulting processing delays. Maintaining a choice of competent 
bodies is important for ensu ring a functional compliance process that is consistent with the 
approach taken in standard EU product safety legislation. 
 
Proposed targeted additions 
The proposal can take  further steps to support AI -driven innovation and eliminate disproportionate 
compliance burdens. While the swift adoption of extended deadlines remains the immediate priority, 
legislators should also address targeted corrections in specific risk categories. These refinements will 
ensure that the AI Act remains technically feasible and focuses on genuine risks rather than 
administrative friction. 
 
1. Aligning definitions and interpretations of automated decision-making

The Omnibus introduces targeted clarifications intended to improve coherence between the 
GDPR and the EU AI Act. It clarifies that certain automated decisions that are necessary for 
entering into or performing a contract may fall within the scope of Articl e 22 GDPR, addressing 
divergent interpretations across Member States.  
 
Despite these clarifications, significant uncertainty remains regarding the interplay between 
Article 22 GDPR, the AI Act and Chapter III of the Platform Work Directive. The definitional 
divergences risk inconsistent application and legal uncertainty for t he same technical systems 
across regulatory regimes. For example, transparency and explainability obligations under Article 
22 GDPR increasingly overlap with sector -specific requirements, particularly for decisions 
affecting access to work, task allocation or pricing. Without clear and consistent guidance on how 
Article 22 applies to complex, multi-step platform processes and to the notion of ‘legal or similarly 
significant effects’, fragmented interpretations and duplicative obligations risk undermining legal 
certainty and user understanding without delivering additional protection.  
 
Clear, coordinated guidance on the interpretation and application of Article 22 GDPR, considering 
the AI Act and the Platform Work Directive, is therefore necessary. To make the Commission and 
European Data Protection Board  (EDPB) provide this guidance, the legislative proposal should 
include an obligation for  the two institutions  to issue joint guidelines within a reasonable 
timeframe after the adoption of the Omnibus.  
 
2. Harmonising CRA overlaps  
 
Given the substantial legal overlap between the cybersecurity provisions in the AI Act and the 
Cyber Resilience Act, the Omnibus should include an explicit ‘compliance-by-equivalence’ clause, 
where relevant. Formally clarifying that compliance with the CRA constitutes sufficient fulfilment 
of the cybersecurity obligations under Article 15 of the AI Act would eliminate legal uncertainty 
and prevent companies from being forced to navigate two sets of nearly identical technical 
requirements for the same system. 
 
3. Streamlining incident reporting  
 
In alignment with the broader Digital Omnibus cybersecurity objectives, a single EU-level portal 
for incident reporting marks a positive step toward streamlined reporting. It offers an opportunity 
to align reporting obligations for entities in the scope of cybersecurity legislation and the AI Act. 
Our position paper on the  Digital Omnibus proposal provides further recommendations on how 
to make reporting simplification workable for businesses.   
 
4. Deferring text-watermarking requirements 
 
The co-legislators should defer mandatory text watermarking requirements until technologically 
mature and effective global standards for watermarking exist. Currently, there is a lack of reliable 
technological solutions which means there is a risk of impos ing an obligation that is technically 
flawed. Furthermore, AI-generated code must be explicitly exempted from such requirements, as 
watermarking code poses significant risks to system integrity, functionality and security by 
introducing potentially non-functional characters or syntax errors.

5. Establishing a coherent approach to cybersecurity in the AI Act 
 
The Omnibus should ensure a consistent categorisation for AI systems used solely for 
cybersecurity purposes. While Recital 55 helpfully clarifies that such systems are not ‘safety 
components’ in the context of critical infrastructure (Annex III Point 2), t his logic is not currently 
extended to the rest of the Act. This omission leads to significant uncertainty - for instance, a draft 
Commission document for the Radio Equipment Directive (RED) proposed the opposite view, 
potentially qualifying all such integrated systems as high-risk. To prevent a fragmented landscape 
where various bodies issue conflicting classifications independent of the AI Act principles, the 
Omnibus should clarify that the language in Recital 55 applies to all AI systems used solely for 
cybersecurity. This would ensure these technologies can be deployed with the legal certainty 
required to benefit European industry, governments and citizens. 
 
6. Lowering penalty thresholds 
 
Penalties for non-compliance under the AI Act are highly punitive, with maximum fines reaching 
€35 million or 7% of annual turnover for non-compliance with prohibited use case provisions and 
€15 million or 3% of annual turnover for non-compliance with other provisions. The combination 
of regulatory ambiguity and costly penalties creates a significant innovation deterrent for firms, 
potentially leading them to withhold system developments outside the EU to avoid inadvertent 
non-compliance. Such an innovation  climate risks making the EU less competitive compared to 
global competitors with less punitive frameworks, risking a loss of market share and innovation 
momentum. The co -legislators should therefore consider lowering these penalty thresholds to 
ensure that the enforcement framework encourages compliance without stifling the EU’s digital 
growth. 
 
Conclusion 
The Digital Omnibus on AI Regulation Proposal is a vital opportunity to create a coherent and 
competitive AI framework that enhances Europe’s position as a global leader in digital innovation. 
However, achieving these goals requires thoughtful amendments and clarifications to ensure the 
regulatory envir onment is both clear and flexible enough to support diverse industries and 
technological advancements. 
By clarifying timelines, streamlining enforcement mechanisms, simplifying the supervisory 
architecture and addressing overlaps with existing regulations, the EU can minimi se unnecessary 
administrative burdens on businesses while maintaining the highest standards for safety and 
fundamental rights. 
We look forward to further discussions and collaboration to ensure that the final regulatory 
framework delivers tangible benefits for businesses, consumers and society at large.

Introduction 
The European Commission’s Digital Omnibus Package has the right goal of enhancing the 
competitiveness of Europe’s digital economy through regulatory simplification and coherence. Over 
recent years, the EU’s digital regulatory landscape has expanded significantly , but not always with a 
view of how various measures may interact, overlap and make compliance difficult for businesses. The 
Omnibus offers an opportunity to make the EU’s digital rules more harmonised. 
The Commission’s Digital Omnibus Regulation Proposal on data and cybersecurity acquis takes steps 
to ease compliance for businesses and accelerate the uptake of digital technologies  in the EU  – all 
while maintaining the highest standards for safety and fundamental rights. Amendments to the GDPR, 
for example, promise to make the Regulation more fit for purpose in the AI age.  
However, in certain areas, the Digital Omnibus still does not go far enough. For example, the proposal 
lacks ambition in areas like cybersecurity, where the complexity of existing rules requires real

a single portal – without deeper reform – risks centralising fragmentation and producing additional 
threats rather than protecting against them.  
To truly strengthen Europe’s resilience, the European Parliament and the Council of the EU must look 
beyond the reporting interface to address the underlying regulatory burden. An effective framework 
requires six core actions:

Operational Resilience Ac t (DORA), the Cyber Resilience Act ( CRA) and other rules that incorporate 
the ‘one-stop-shop’ principle to the EU Member State of their main establishment.  
The use of the main establishment principle already applies to certain services under NIS2, though it 
has not been applied consistently by EU Member States. To streamline reporting, its use should be 
expanded to cover more sectors – such as public electronic communications services – which are often 
offered by vendors on a cross-border basis within the EU. However, policymakers should acknowledge

scope of duplicative obligations.

still face parallel and often inconsistent reporting.

The Digital Omnibus should introduce greater transparency by requiring ENISA or the Commission to

obligations applicable to the SEP, of course exclusive of its own security system. This would help 
ensure its proper functioning, reliability, integrity and confidentiality. The Digital Omnibus should also 
clarify how ENISA conducts risk assessments, enabling manufacturers, providers of critical 
infrastructure and other reporting entities to align their own risk management processes accordingly.  
Finally, the Digital Omnibus should clearly define how reporting obligations are fulfilled if an incident 
affects the SEP itself. As entities rely on the SEP to store and transmit their notifications, any outage, 
cyber incident or period of unavailability could prevent them from accessing previously submitted 
reports or proving that they met their deadlines. The legislation should therefore specify how 
reporting entities can demonstrate compliance when the SEP is not functioning, how audit trails can 
be restored or validated once the system is back online and how users beyond national authorities 
will be informed of such incidents. This clarity is essential to ensure that entities are not exposed to 
legal liability due to failures of the reporting system rather than their own conduct. There should also 
be an official mechanism in place that allows stakeholders  – specifically entities submitting 
notifications – to flag issues directly to ENISA or the Commission regarding the functioning, reliability, 
integrity or confidentiality of the SEP. Operational bugs or security vulnerabilities in the reporting 
pipeline may be detected first by reporting entities rather than the Commission. A formal feedback 
loop is essential to ensure that if the SEP fails, entities can swiftly transition to ‘alternative means’ of 
reporting as referenced in Recital 57, without facing legal liability for missed deadlines.  
1.4. Engage industry in the SEP development  
The Digital Omnibus mandates that ENISA develop the technical, operational and organi sational 
specifications for the SEP in cooperation with ‘the European Commission, the CSIRTs network and the 
competent authorities’. Currently, the proposal does not mention consulting entities that will be 
required to use the system to submit incident notifications.  
Entities submitting notifications must be involved in designing and assessing the functioning of the 
SEP. This is particularly important given the requirement in Article 23a(3)(d) that the system be 
interoperable with European Business Wallets for identifi cation and authentication. Excluding the 
primary users (reporting entities) from the design phase risks creating a system that is theoretically 
sound for authorities but operationally burdensome or technically incompatible with industry 
workflows. The co -legislators should therefore  amend Article 23a(3) to explicitly include industry 
stakeholders in the consultation process for technical specifications and API standards. 
1.5. Provide liability protections  
To build the trust necessary for robust information sharing, the EU must address the liability clauses 
of both NIS2 and the CRA. While Article 23(1) of NIS2 and Article 17(4) CRA specify that the mere act 
of notification does not subject the notifying entity to increased liability, these laws must also clarify 
that reporting companies are protected from potential ‘spill-over’ effects caused by the act of 
notification itself, such as a cybersecurity notification inadvertently triggering a secondary 
investigation by a Data Protection Authority under the GDPR, even if no personal data was 
compromised.

Reporting templates should enable businesses to prioriti se incident remediation rather than 
administrative exercises. Reporting thresholds should be sufficiently high to capture only incidents 
that could have a material impact on the wider economy or that could disrupt business operations. 
Current detailed templates covering low -level incidents require businesses to triage all incidents 
according to the EU’s criteria. In some cases, 20-100 incidents are triaged for every single reportable 
event. Triaging serves no meaningful risk management purpose, creates cybersecurity skills shortages, 
removes investment from productive activity such as handling incidents and increases the cost of 
doing business in the EU.  
The current EU approach is burdensome and often results in overreporting. Several common 
classification criteria proposed by the EU drive overreporting of less significant incidents that do not 
require authority intervention. The following classification c riteria, for example, could be removed 
across NIS2, GDPR, ePrivacy, DORA and CRA reporting: 
• Recurring incidents: An incident is considered recurring when the same root cause leads to 
two repeated incidents across a six -month period and, together, they breach reporting 
thresholds. The root cause criteria across EU incident reporting rules are high -level and 
inconsistent ( eg, a  change management related incident could result from human error, 
infrastructure implementation or a supplier issue, despite being all in scope for a recurring 
incident). This results in a high administrative burden, with unclear policy benefit.  
• Geographic spread: Incident reporting regimes require companies to determine if an incident 
has affected multiple EU Member States, which is a criterion under DORA. This occurs 
frequently due to cross-border transactions and the dispersed locations of subsidiaries and IT 
infrastructure. The spread has little to no correlation with the significance of the IT incident 
and drives excessive reporting.  
• Weekend reporting: EU incident regimes often mean in practice that companies must report 
over the weekend or within a certain time frame on Monday morning. This is impractical, as 
authorities are often not operational on weekends, reporting portals may be unavailable and 
no new information is produced in relation to the incident (eg, market impact for trading does 
not occur outside trading hours). This causes an unnecessary burden on companies with 
limited policy upside. Highly significant incidents will still be addressed and face authority 
intervention even without a weekend reporting requirement.  
• Costs: Incident reporting often requires businesses to subjectively quantify costs of an 
individual cybersecurity or operational incident in a highly prescriptive manner. DORA, for 
instance, requires a business to quantify the staff salary cost for reporting team s which 
manage triage activities for all reporting requirements (a financial institution , for example,  
may be subject to 100+ global reporting rules). Attributing costs to staff salaries for incident 
response is complex, subjective and often unrelated to the incident’s severity. The 
requirement to estimate economic impact during the initial reporting phases is operationally 
infeasible; accurate quantification requires post -incident analysis that cannot be performed 
within short notification windows (eg, 24 hours) without diverting  critical resources from 
immediate incident containment and mitigation. Cost reporting requirements should be 
simplified – or ideally removed – to allow businesses to concentrate on remediation.

1. Data Act 
1.1.  Narrow the definition of 'connected product' 
The definition of ‘connected product’ in the Data Act is excessively broad, potentially encompassing a 
vast range of hardware and associated digital services. This extensive scope risks creating clear 
compliance burdens, particularly on manufacturers of pr oducts where data generation is incidental 
rather than a primary function, or for products already well -understood by consumers, such as 
personal computers, tablets and smartphones.  
To foster innovation and reduce unnecessary regulatory burdens, the European Parliament and 
Council of the EU must narrow this definition. Clarifying the status of common consumer electronics 
like personal computers, tablets and smartphones, would significantly simplify compliance and allow 
businesses to focus resources on core Data Act objectives for relevant industrial data. 
1.2.  Clarify switching obligations for data processing services 
The narrow exemption proposed in the Digital Omnibus for legacy contracts fails to address the 
fundamental structural incompatibility of applying portability obligations to software - and platform-

platform as a service (PaaS)  lies in speciali sed customi sation and deep integration into complex 
workflows, making forced interchangeability both technically and commercially unworkable. A 
regulatory framework that mandates portability in this context actively disincentivises investment by 
eroding the unique differentiation central to these business models. Rather than relying on arbitrary 
temporal exemptions, the co-legislators should fully exclude SaaS and PaaS to ensure the regulation 
does not inadvertently undermine the viability of these highly specialised markets. 
Furthermore, to ensure the framework remains technically viable, further flexibility on some of the 
technical and contractual requirements is necessary. In particular, policymakers should give careful 
consideration to the upcoming repository of harmonised standards and/or common specifications. 
Digital services are in constant state of evolution  with disruptive innovation creat ing whole new 
classes of digital services that force older technologies to become obsolete. Careful consideration 
needs to be given to how such open interoperability specifications are identified to ensure a future -
proof framework.

3. ePrivacy 
3.1.  Adopt targeted measures to reduce cookie fatigue 
Browser-level consent 
 
The Commission is right in its  assessment that simpler rules must address  user ‘consent fatigue’. 
Unfortunately, the current proposal (Article 88b) attempts to solve this via browser -based consent 
settings, a flawed concept that fails to meet the GDPR’s criteria for specific and informed consent. This 
solution has been dismissed in previous legislativ e debates during the discussions on the ePrivacy 
Regulation and the Cookie Pledge, precisely because it is legally and technically infeasible. By 
resurrecting this concept, the proposal misses the opportunity for a thorough functional ePrivacy 
solution and risks creating further regulatory confusion. 
 
For users, this does not solve the issue of cookie fatigue: a binary ‘Accept All’ or ‘Reject All’ signal from 
a browser lacks the granularity required by the GDPR, which mandates that consent be ‘specific’ to 
the processing purpose. Consequently, websites would still be legally obligated to intervene with pop-
ups to clarify distinct data uses – such as distinguishing between advertising and analytics – or to ask 
users to reconsider a rejection in exchange for content access. Rather than simplifying the user 
experience, this proposal introduces a hidden layer of technical complexity while failing to eliminate 
the visual clutter of consent banners from users’ screens.  
 
To solve cookie fatigue, we propose for the Commission to extend the exemptions for low-risk data, 
introduced in Article 88(a), to include contextual advertising and basic ad functionalities, including 
third-party analytics, frequency capping, traffic validation and basic ads measurement. Since these 
technical processes do not involve behavioural profiling, exempting them offers a practical path to 
decluttering the web of consent banners without compromising user privacy. The consent exemption 
for security purposes should also cover security, fraud and related software updates.

Integrating ePrivacy 5(3) into GDPR (Article 88a) 
While the integration of e-Privacy into the GDPR is a positive step, the current proposal (Article 88a) 
introduces a ‘split regime’ for both companies and users. 
 
Because cookies and trackers rarely distinguish neatly between personal and non -personal data, the 
proposal subjects companies to double jeopardy: they must report to the GDPR’s One -Stop-Shop for

Furthermore, the Commission’s proposal would result in the old consent regime for cookies 
continuing to apply to non-personal data. In practice, this results in different regulatory treatment for 
non-personal data compared to the new framework applicable t o personal data. Such an outcome 
undermines the objective of avoiding consent fatigue, as users would still be required to consent to 
the collection of non-personal data. This cannot be the intended policy goal. 
To provide legal certainty for businesses while keeping a high level of user protection , all terminal 
equipment rules must move entirely to the GDPR. In the context of IoT products, users will retain 
control over their non-personal data under the Data Act. 
More generally, it is worth remembering that  the e-Privacy Directive from 2002 is an outdated and 
disjointed legal instrument that regulates a non-homogenous group of services and aims to pursue a 
disparate set of goals  – among which  is the governance of  processing of terminal equipment 
data. The Digital Omnibus is a perfect opportunity to  simplify the  e-Privacy Directive  entirely and

Conclusion 
The Digital Omnibus Regulation Proposal on data and cybersecurity acquis presents a vital opportunity 
to create a coherent and competitive cybersecurity and data framework that enhances Europe’s 
position as a global leader in digital innovation. However, a chieving these goals requires thoughtful 
amendments and clarifications to ensure the regulatory environment is both clear and flexible enough 
to support diverse industries and technological advancements. 
By clarifying definitions, thresholds and timelines, as well as streamlining legislation and addressing 
overlaps with existing regulations, the EU can minimi se unnecessary administrative burdens on 
businesses while maintaining the highest standards for safety and fundamental rights. 
AmCham EU looks forward to further discussions and collaboration to ensure that the final regulatory 
framework delivers tangible benefits for businesses, consumers and society at large.

Digital Omnibus call for evidence 
ENGIE’s re commendations  
Executive summary 
ENGIE welcomes the publication of the Digital and AI omnibus proposals as a positive step toward rationalizing 
and clarifying the EU  digital acquis  and addressing existing implementation challenges . While the texts 
introduce relevant improvements, further systemic changes and a more pragmatic regulatory approach will be 
necessary to ensure a simple , clear and consistent digital rulebook. In this spirit , ENGIE would like to submit 
additional recommendations to fully leverage the simplification potential of the current proposals.  
The implementation of the digital rulebook continues to be hindered by two major challenges : fragmented 
interpretation and application across Member States, and insufficient visibility on the availability of secondary 
legislation combined with tight compliance timelines. While certain proposals appropriately address these issues 
(e.g. further guidelines by the Commission, postponement of the compliance deadline for high -risk AI systems 
etc.), such issues risk remaining. To tackle them, ENGIE reiterates the need for maximum harmonization and 
calls for the Commission to provide stakeholders with greater advance clarity on the timing and content of 
secondary legislation and ensure realistic compliance deadlines to limit unnecessary internal costs. 
More specifically on the proposed amendments, ENGIE would like to highlight the following recommendations. 
As for the GDPR, we support the proposal’s objective of fostering  a competitive, balanced and pro -innovation 
data protection framework, while identifying areas where further clarification/simplification would be beneficial.  
• With respect to the definition of personal data  taking into account  pseudonymisation (art. 4(1) GDPR ), 
clarity is needed regarding its implications on subcontracting and on data processors’ obligations. In such 
cases, ENGIE recommends clarifying either that controllers are not required to comply with Article 28 GDPR 
or that processors fall outside the scope of the revised Article 4(1), thereby avoiding adverse  contractual 
implications for data controllers. 
• As regards the exceptions to the  use of legitimate interest  for the processing of personal data for AI 
development (art. 88c), ENGIE notes a lack of clarity in their perimeter. We therefore recommend defining 
clear guiding principles rather than an exhaustive list, ensuring baseline harmonization across Member 
States while allowing regulators to adopt a broad interpretation of legitimate interest.  
• Concerning the harmonisation of DPIAs (art. 35 and 70(1)), ENGIE supports the rationale but stresses that 
EDPB proposals should be clear and easy to interpret in order to prevent fragmented implementation. 
On cyber-related provisions , ENGIE supports the creation of a single-entry point but notes that reporting 
simplification remains partial. We therefore recommend the following complementary actions: 
• Strengthening the legal significance of standardized reporting templates  referenced in recital 54 by 
introducing an explicit obligation for their development in Article 23a.  
• Additionally, ENGIE would like to recommend the establishment of a unified, tiered reporting framework 
with harmonized deadlines based on incident severity rather than sector-specific requirements.  
Regarding the Digital Omnibus on AI, while welcoming efforts to promote a pro-innovation approach, we would 
like to address the following concerns:

ENGIE welcomes the simplification effort carried out by the European Commission via the publication of separate 
Digital and AI omnibus proposals . The approach in favour of rationalising, consolidating and clarifying the 
existing digital acquis while addressing some of the implementation challenges faced by market actors is 
strongly supported by ENGIE and represents a key first step in the direction of supporting EU’s competitiveness 
in the digital domain in a way that is compatible with its values and ambitions. However, the amendments put 
forward in the two omnibus proposals seem to fall short tackling broader overlaps and inconsistencies currently 
hampering the implementation and effectiveness of the EU digital rulebook. In order to  ensure a regulatory 
framework that is as simple,  clear and consistent as possible, more structural changes and a more pragmatic 
approach are needed. In this spirit, ENGIE firmly supports the launching of the upcoming Digital Fitness Check to 
“stress-test” the digital acquis and complement the simplification efforts already undertaken via the two 
omnibus proposals. 
Overall, both the Digital and AI omnibus contain key improvements of existing texts . As regards the Digital 
Omnibus, the repealing of the Free Flow of Data Regulation, Open Data Directive, Data Governance Act, and the 
subsequent consolidation  and streamlining of their rules  into the Data Act is a substantial step forward in 
clarifying and simplifying rules on the Data Economy. At the same time, ENGIE also values very positively the set-
up of a single -entry point for NIS2, GDPR, DORA, CER Directive to streamline cybersecurity incident reporting 
and reduce the administrative burden on market players. Concerning the AI omnibus, the postponement of the 
entry into force of the obligations for high-risk AI systems subject to the availability of relevant standards and 
specifications along with the expected publication of guidelines clarifying the interpretation of key provisions will 
also be paramount in supporting market actors’ compliance.  
Nevertheless, ENGIE believes that further simplification and clarity is possible in the framework of these two 
omnibus proposals. In general, while firmly supporting the efforts of the Commission to clarify the interpretation 
and implementation of digital rules by means of guidelines, we would like to warn against the risk of it resulting 
in even further fragmentation across Member States  with negative consequences in terms of increased 
compliance costs for multinational companies like ENGIE . Therefore, for the sake of ensuring a harmonised 
application of the EU digital legislation, we recommend the Commission to limit to the extent possible the 
flexibilities granted to Member States when applying secondary legislation, and/or to integrate the clarifications 
directly in the concerned text when possible and relevant.  
Concerning the areas where ENGIE sees further margins  for simplification, please see the proposals below 
related to the Digital omnibus and the Digital omnibus on AI. 
1 Digital omnibus proposal 
1.1 GDPR 
ENGIE is strongly committed to the protection of personal data and values the potential and ambition of the 
regulatory framework put in place  via the GDPR . Nevertheless, its implementation has prove n increasingly 
challenging. This results from both the evolution of the overall EU digital framework  and subsequent 
proliferation of pieces of legislation sometimes leading to overlaps and inconsistencies, and from the adoption 
of a “maximalist” approach to application adopted by data protection authorities. Additionally and from a global 
perspective, the current approach risks putting large European companies at a potential disadvantage when 
competing against business domiciled in countries with less stringent approaches to the detriments of both our 
companies and the European consumer. For these reasons, ENGIE values the simplification efforts towards the 
4 
GDPR as conducive to a more competitive, balanced and pro-innovation approach to the protection of personal 
data.  
While pointing in the right direction, ENGIE would like to address areas of further improvement which would 
contribute to the reduction of compliance complexity and consequent costs, notably:  
• Scope of the d efinition of personal data and subsequent modification  taking into account 
pseudonymisation (art. 4(1) GDPR):  
The amendment introduced by the Digital omnibus intends to clarify the scope of the definition of personal 
data by stating that information is not to be considered personal data for a given entity when it does not 
have means reasonably likely to be used to identify the natural person to whom the information relates.  
While the amendment is welcome and rightly incorporate s relevant jurisprudence from the CJEU, further 
clarity is needed regarding the  application and consequences  of the revised article in cases of 
subcontracting, and on the related obligations of data processors involved. The definition as currently 
introduced by the Digital omnibus might entail  conflicting/contradictory obligations on service providers 
which receive or access pseudonymised data and/or serve as processors and  bear relevant contractual 
implications for data controllers . In such circumstances, t he data would simultaneously be considered 
personal data from the perspective of the data controller, and non-personal data from the perspective of 
the service provider  which would at the same time be a processor . Based on the revised article 4(1), the 
service provider could therefore refuse to comply with obligations related to its “processor” qualification, 
insofar as the data is, in its view, non -personal (e.g. refusal to enter into a  Data Processing Agreement, 
implement the necessary security measures etc.).  
Given the substantial contractual implications of the current formulation, ENGIE recommends the co -
legislators to further clarify the impact of revised article 4(1) for subcontracting. 
o One way of doing this would be to specify that controllers are not required to comply with article 
28 of the GDPR whenever the processor cannot identify the data subjects, taking into account all 
means reasonably likely to be used. 
o Alternatively, the text could clarify that processors are excluded from the scope of the new Article 
4(1) of the GDPR, for example by replacing the terms “other person or entity” with “third party”. 
 
• Processing of personal data in the context of the development and operation of AI  systems (revised art. 
88c): 
Revised a rticle 88 (c) in the GDPR specifies that the processing of personal data in the context of the 
development and operation of an AI system may be carried out for purposes of a legitimate interest within 
the meaning of Article 6 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, “except where other Union or national laws explicitly 
require consent, and where such interests are overridden by the interests, or fundamental rights and 
freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular where the data subject 
is a child”.  
In the current wording, the exceptions provided for in Article 88c lack clarity and risk leaving the door open 
to diverging interpretations by national supervisory authorities regarding the scope and width of the 
exceptions exemplified.  
Therefore, ENGIE recommends the co-legislators to work on the definition of clear principles  to identify 
the exception s to Article 88 c rather than adopting a list.  This approach would simultaneously ensure a 
baseline harmonisation across Member States on how to identify such exceptions while guaranteeing that a 
broad interpretative approach is encouraged among regulators for the notion of legitimate interest.

• Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) – amendments to articles 35 and 70(1) of the GDPR and role of 
the European Data Protection Board (EDPB): 
The Digital Omnibus revises Articles 35 and 70(1) of the GDPR concerning the controller’s obligation to carry 
out a data protection impact assessment. It assigns to the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) the task 
of submitting to the European Commission a set of proposals aimed a t harmonizing DPIA templates and 
methodologies, while clearly specifying which processing operations are mandatorily subject to the 
assessment and which are not.  
ENGIE strongly supports the harmonisation rationale behind the proposed revision of article 35 and 70(1) 
and sees great value in ensuring a uniform EU assessment methodology and template while providing data 
controllers with further clarity over the processes that do not require a mandatory DPIA. Nevertheless, such 
a harmonisation attempt will prove beneficial only if the methodologies, templates and lists of processing 
operations are clear, easy to interpret and leave only very marginal (where needed) flexibility to supervisory 
authorities to adapt them. Therefore, ENGIE recommends that the proposals put forward by the EDPB are 
as clear and as easy to interpret as possibl e to avoid unnecessary and counterproductive fragmented 
implementation across Member States. 
1.2 Cybersecurity 
ENGIE strongly supports the proposal to set up a single-entry point to streamline and reduce the administrative 
burden and related compliance costs of existing incident reporting obligations across various pieces of EU digital 
legislation. Despite representing a key first step in the right direction , the proposal of a single-entry point 
addresses only partially the need to truly simplify reporting obligations.  
To this end, ENGIE would like to recommend the following complementary actions  to further cut reporting 
complexity and related costs, reduc e confusion, accelerat e coordinated crisis response, and transform the 
current fragmented regime into a genuinely unified European incident management ecosystem: 
• Standardized Cybersecurity Reporting Templates – recital 54: 
In addition to the creation of a single-entry point for incident reporting under multiple cyber-related texts, 
the development of standardised reporting template s will be key in expediting the reporting process for 
companies. The introduction of standardised templates would support the emergence of a common format 
easier to complete and reuse across pieces of legislation; allow automation through existing security systems 
(e.g. SIEM integration); reduce follow-up requests from authorities thanks to clearer, standardized data; and 
improve consistency and comparability of incident data across the EU.  
On the matter, the omnibus proposal rightly points to the need  to develop such standardised reporting 
templates in recital 54. Despite the mention being warmly welcome, the lack of specific binding provisions 
in the main articles makes the recommendation overall weak. Therefore, ENGIE recommends the co -
legislators to provide for stronger legal significance to the recommendation on the  alignment and 
streamlining of reporting templates by explicitly including in article 23a of the omnibus proposal  an 
obligation for their elaboration.  
 
• Harmonisation of deadlines and unified reporting framework - currently not addressed:  
One key barrier to the emergence of a predictable and coherent reporting framework for cyber incidents 
has been the proliferation of reporting regimes under different pieces of EU digital legislation characterised 
6 
by differing reporting timelines. While the provisions proposed so far in the Digital omnibus already provide 
some relief, addressing the fragmentation of reporting deadlines and frameworks will be the real game 
changer in reducing existing administrative burdens and related costs. For this reason, ENGIE would like to 
recommend the co-legislators to include in the framework of the Digital Omnibus or in the future Digital 
Fitness Check a proposal for the establishment of a unified, tiered reporting framework with harmonised 
deadlines based on incident severity rather than sector-specific rules.  
2 Digital omnibus on AI 
ENGIE acknowledges the relevance of the provisions contained in the AI Act for the development and 
deployment of AI in accordance with EU values , and at the same time supports the targeted amendments 
introduced by the proposal of a Digital Omnibus on AI , which aim to adopt a more “pro-innovation” approach 
while ensuring that European businesses have the necessary regulatory and technical means to fully comply with 
its provisions.  
While welcoming the simplification efforts put forward, we would like to address the following points:  
• Postponed entry into force and application for high-risk AI systems (art. 113) 
ENGIE values the rationale behind the propos ed postponement of the application of high -risk AI system 
obligations in line with the availability of the needed standards, specifications and Commission’s 
guidelines. While an alignment between the implementation and publication of 
standards/specifications/guidelines is paramount to allow full compliance, so is the need for regulatory 
certainty and visibility for concerned companies to adopt the organisational changes and set up the required 
internal processes to enable the proper application of the AI Act provisions. As currently drafted , the 
proposed postponement risks falling short of providing the much-needed regulatory certainty, especially in 
light of the upcoming August 2026 implementation deadline. In light of such considerations:  
o ENGIE calls for the European Commission to provide stakeholders with sufficient advanced clarity and 
visibility on the publication and availability of secondary legislation , standards, and guidelines. The 
internal costs and complexities that European large companies face when implementing the adjustments 
needed to comply are compounded by tight application deadlines. It will therefore be key to envisage 
realistic timeframes for compliance by companies to reduce related internal costs. 
o ENGIE urges the co -legislators to ensure a swift agreement  on the omnibus proposal to clarify and 
confirm the proposed postponement while keeping the scope of the changes targeted and as aligned as 
possible to the areas of intervention identified by the Commission's proposal. 
 
• Data subject’s “unconditional” right to object processing (art. 88c) 
ENGIE would like to raise an additional point of concern with regards to the mention in Article 88c of the 
need for data controllers to provide data subjects with an “unconditional” right to object the processing . 
The “unconditional” nature of the right to object – even when conducted on the basis of legitimate interest 
– appears disproportionate and might entail unintended negative consequences both on the controller’s and 
on the data subjects’ side. In fact, from a controller’s perspective the exercise of such right to object in an 
unconditional manner could entail increased costs stemming from the need to retrain an AI system on new 
data sets. From the data subject’s perspective, a recurrent exercise of this right may eventually result in more 
biased results from the AI system.  
7 
Therefore, ENGIE recommends softening the “unconditional” nature of the right to object while recognizing 
the importance of such right when it comes to the processing of personal data. 
 
• Creation of a single portal collecting AI providers’ compliance information - currently not addressed: 
In cases where companies rely on third -party providers, relevant information regarding providers’ 
compliance with the AI Act is currently disseminated across multiple sources, difficult to access, and time - 
and resource -intensive to gather, thereby creatin g a significant administrative burden for deployers and 
representing a key obstacle to effective implementation and AI adoption. 
ENGIE therefore recommends the establishment of an EU-level single, regularly updated portal compiling 
relevant information on third-party providers’ compliance with the AI Act . Such mechanism, which is not 
currently foreseen in the omnibus proposal, would reduce administrative burden, enhance legal certainty, 
and support the uptake of AI solutions.

EE-ISAC & Simplification – Digital Package and Omnibus| Response to Call

1. Summary of the call for evidence & EE-ISAC participation rationale  
The European Energy Information Sharing and Analysis Centre (EE-ISAC) welcomes the European 
Commission’s Digital Package and the Digital Omnibus initiative. As an industry-driven network of 
trust, EE -ISAC unites energy utilities, grid operators, solution p roviders, academia, and public 
institutions to strengthen the cybersecurity and resilience of Europe’s energy infrastructure 
through structured information sharing and joint analysis. 
The Digital Omnibus endeavours to simplify the EU digital regulatory framework and reduce 
administrative burdens while maintaining high standards of protection.  
As the European energy sector is traversing an increasingly complex regulatory environment that 
encompasses the NIS2 Directive, Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), Cybersecurity Act, Radio Equipment 
Directive (RED), GDPR, AI Act, CER Directive, and Network Code on Cybersecurity , reforms are 
particularly significant because clearer and more streamlined rules on cybersecurity 
requirements – through technical, operational and organisational measures , notification and 
reporting, data use, and artificial intelligence can release operational capacity, reinforce cross -
border coordination, and enhance resilience during the green and digital transition . While each 
instrument serves a legitimate policy objective, their cumulative effect has often resulted in 
administrative duplication and fragmented operational processes.  
Against this backdrop, the Digital Omnibus emerges at an opportune moment and therefore 
represents a timely opportunity to simplify compliance, strengthen regulatory alignment, and 
allow operators to focus resources on effective risk mitigation and system resilience. 
2. EE-ISAC perspective and recommendations  
2.1 Single Entry Point as an operational enabler 
EE-ISAC strongly supports the creation of a Single -Entry Point (SEP) for cybersecurity incident 
reporting, which, in our view, should  be developed and operated by ENISA , particularly 
considering the recent developments and provisions of the Cybersecurity Act 2 that further 
empower ENISA to strengthen and enhance Europe’s overall cybersecurity resilience . Energy 
operators currently face overlapping reporting duties under NIS2, GDPR,  national and/or

For the energy sector, the Single-Entry Point should function not only as a compliance mechanism 
but as a critical operational enabler. To be effective, the platform must reflect the operational 
realities of electricity systems, including the need for high -frequency and low-latency reporting 
in line with the NCCS. We note that this mechanism is expected to build upon the Single Reporting 
Platform currently being established by ENISA under the CRA and in alignment with the guidance 
stemming from the Cybersecurity Act 2, meaning that it would already start from a solid structure 
and only minor changes would be needed for it to function. Leveraging this existing operational 
infrastructure will accelerate implementation, avoid duplicative public spending, and ensure 
technical consistency across the EU’s digital ecosystem. 
Given the multiplicity of existing reporting regimes, ENISA is uniquely positioned to act as a 
trusted European hub capable of securely receiving incident notifications, routing them to the 
appropriate national and sectoral authorities, and aggregating information at the Union level. EE-
ISAC therefore encourages the Commission to ensure that ENISA’s mandate, resources, and 
governance arrangements fully support this expanded operational role. A harmonised, ENISA -
administered, and secure Single-Entry Point, complemented by sanitised feedback of aggregated 
threat intelligence to trusted communities such as EE -ISAC, would transform reporting from a 
compliance obligation into a driver of collective resilience. 
 
2.2 Key impacts of the Digital Omnibus for the energy sector 
EE-ISAC supports clarifications to data protection rules that facilitate the responsible use of data 
to enhance operational resilience. Legal certainty regarding the use of legitimate interest for AI 
training supports predictive maintenance, grid optimi sation, and anomaly detection, while 
safeguards such as pseudonymi sation continue to protect individual rights. Simplification of 
consent requirements for low -risk analytical tools further reduces unnecessary administrative 
burden. 
EE-ISAC advocates for the practical adaptation of the AI Act to ensure safe and effective use of 
high-risk systems in critical energy infrastructure. Staggered compliance timelines allow for safe 
integration of AI-driven grid management tools, while controlled processing of sensitive data for 
bias detection ensures fairness in automated decision -making. Regulatory sandboxes are

In the area of cybersecurity, alignment of SEP reporting with NIS2 timelines enhances legal

Finally, supply chain security remains a significant challenge under the NIS2 Directive, and the 
Digital Omnibus proposal regarding European Digital Identity Wallets for business offers a 
practical solution to this issue. Despite its name, this initiative functions effectively as a digital 
passport for legal entities rather than a financial instrument. By enabling the use of digital identity 
wallets to verify the credentials of legal persons, energy operators can automate Know Your 
Business (KYB) checks. Th is process simplifies the vetting of contractors and cross -border 
suppliers by ensuring they hold valid security certifications, such as ISO 27001, via tamper-proof 
credentials. Consequently, this mechanism reduces administrative overhead for energy operators 
and mitigates the risk of vendor impersonation attacks, thereby strengthening the overall security 
posture of the energy supply chain. 
 
      2.3 EE-ISAC insights and recommendations 
Reciprocal information sharing : Incident reporting should not be a one -way street. The 
framework should adopt a reciprocal model where aggregated and anonymized threat 
intelligence is shared back with operators of critical infrastructures, either directly or via sectoral 
ISACs. This feedback loop is vital for strengthening collective defence and enabling proactive risk 
mitigation across the industry. 
Cross-border prioritization and regulatory alignment: Resources should be focused on incidents 
with cross-border impacts, particularly those affecting electricity flows and shared infrastructure. 
To avoid confusion, the Digital Omnibus must bridge NIS2 and the CRA, ensuring supply chain 
vulnerabilities are reported once and addressed consistently across the entire value chain. 
Technical interoperability and automation : The SEP must be technically interoperable with 
existing sector-specific platforms, such as the Malware Information Sharing Platform (MISP). To 
support compatibility with  OT environments, API-based integration is essential, enabling 
automated, machine-speed reporting that keeps pace with modern threats , minimising manual 
effort and allowing security teams to focus on better-informed and effective incident response 
and operational continuity. 
Coherent implementation and data sovereignty : Member States are encouraged to prioritize 
streamlined implementation by mitigating unnecessary national -level duplications and avoiding 
the imposition of supererogatory requirements that could exacerbate compliance complexities

3. Conclusion 
Digital Omnibus is a strategic blueprint for Europe’s energy and digital resilience. By shifting the 
focus from administrative compliance to proactive risk management, it empowers energy 
operators to anticipate threats, strengthen cross -border coordination, and s afeguard critical 
infrastructure. The ENISA-led SEP and harmonised reporting frameworks transform fragmented 
systems into a cohesive, continent -wide defense while creating a secure environment for 
advanced technologies such as artificial intelligence and data analytics. 
Leveraging the expertise and collective intelligence of EE -ISAC, the proposed reforms can be 
translated into substantive, measurable improvements, ensuring that regulatory simplification 
delivers tangible real -world resilience. In this ecosystem, EE -ISAC i s prepared to act as a key 
operational partner to the SEP in close cooperation with ENISA. Once the platform aggregates 
and sanitises incident data, EE -ISAC can support the timely dissemination of this intelligence 
across the energy community, ensuring tha t the platform’s output functions not merely as a 
compliance repository but as a dynamic and actionable alert mechanism, potentially leveraging 
the existing member-shared MISP platform. 
At the same time, EE -ISAC recognises the complexity created by the numerous regulatory 
reporting obligations, including provisions on who may report, to whom, and within what 
timelines. To prevent this complexity from becoming a barrier to implementation, a phased and 
pragmatic approach should be pursued. EE-ISAC therefore recommends the gradual expansion of 
the SEP, starting with the integration of closely aligned frameworks, such as requirements 
stemming from NIS2 and the CRA, before progressively incorporating sector-specific regimes and, 
in later stages, more heterogeneous regulatory domains. Such an incremental pathway would 
allow the platform to mature into a comprehensive reporting hub over time, avoiding paralysis 
caused by excessive initial scope while ensuring early, practical benefits. 
By bridging not only cross -border but also cross -sectoral divides, this mechanism would 
strengthen the collective defence of interdependent critical infrastructures. Through this 
approach, the European Union can reinforce its strategic autonomy while prior itising resilience 
and operational effectiveness. The Digital Omnibus would thus represent a decisive step toward 
a secure, reliable, and interconnected European energy ecosystem, capable of addressing 
evolving threats and safeguarding the continent’s energy future.

 
PUBLIC SAP Point of View: EU Digital Omnibus & Digital fitness check As a global leader in enterprise applications and business AI, SAP SE enjoys the specific perspective of a European innovation company, active for over 50 years and present on five continents. Having supported and accompanied European companies and public bodies in their digitization, we want to offer our insights on the regulatory framework and the rightful effort to simplify and streamline it. It has become clear that the wave of digital legislation adopted between 2019 and 2024 is too burdensome for Europe’s businesses and economies. Regulatory simplification in digital legislation is crucial to enable European industrial innovation in the era of cloud and artificial intelligence, and to make sure Europe is at eye level with other partners in the world. Failure to maintain competitive AI adoption rates will pose severe consequences for EU competitiveness, digital resilience, and strategic autonomy. Lagging behind in cloud and AI will result in elevated production costs, inflexible and vulnerable supply chains, slower innovation, and eroding profit margins – collectively weakening Europe’s standing in the global economy and threatening its long-term sovereignty. Against this backdrop, SAP welcomes the publication of the Digital Omnibus Package as a constructive foundation for reducing regulatory complexity in Europe. The proposed amendments to the AI Act and GDPR represent positive steps forward and signal readiness to change and reform digital legislation. However, the proposals fall short of delivering the transformative reforms Europe needs, especially in the areas of data and cybersecurity. While the effort to streamline processes is welcome, administrative optimization alone will not secure Europe’s position as a global leader in digital innovation. While some of the reforms can be undertaken under the AI & Digital Omnibuses, the rest of them should be tackled within the Digital Fitness Check process. From SAP’s perspective, three legislative areas merit particular attention within this reform process: Data Act: Digital Omnibus does not address core issues SAP welcomes the Commission’s effort to refine the Data Act through the Digital Omnibus proposals, notably regarding retroactivity and early termination penalties. However, the proposed modifications do not resolve the core issues. The Data Act has significantly disturbed innovation, digital transformation and competitive advantages compared to tech providers outside the EU. Deeper adjustments are necessary to restore conceptual coherence (i.e. refocus the scope to Platform-as-a-Service (PaaS) and Infrastructure-as-a-Service (IaaS) only), improve legal certainty by clarifying definitions and harmonise enforcement – while preserving the Data Act’s objective of lowering switching barriers where lock-in is a reality.  Cyber Resilience Act: Adjustments and alignment with other EU cybersecurity frameworks required The proliferation of overlapping cybersecurity requirements – particularly between the Cyber Resilience Act and existing frameworks – has intensified regulatory fragmentation rather than reducing it. This fragmentation imposes substantial administrative burden and compliance costs on companies without delivering commensurate improvements in cyber risk management or fostering cloud innovation, especially in the B2B context. More widely, the legislation does not reflect technical reality of business software environments. SAP recommends postponing CRA implementation until comprehensive and workable compliance guidance, targeted revision of unrealistic provisions, and harmonised European standards for presumption of conformity are established. The absence of such clarity creates unacceptable uncertainty that stifles innovation, compromises preparedness, and puts in danger market access for affected entities.  AI Act: Preserve good improvements of AI Omnibus Rapid agreement on the postponement of further enforcement is critical and requires immediate action. Getting a swift agreement on predictable compliance timelines for high-risk AI systems confirming a new clear and predictable compliance deadline is an immediate priority. Until postponement is confirmed, companies must continue compliance efforts without finalized guidance or harmonized standards, forcing resource-intensive preparation based on assumptions. However, outstanding issues and process streamlining should also be addressed. Data Act SAP welcomes the Commission’s Digital Omnibus efforts to refine the EU Data Act, particularly on retroactivity and termination penalties. However, the proposed changes fail to resolve fundamental issues:  1. Retroactivity and the “custom-made” exemption The new “custom-made” exemption is a welcome acknowledgement by the EU Commission that services which cannot be provisioned or operated without substantial service provider interaction are, in principle, different from the resource layer services the Data Act set out to regulate. SAP regards this as an important step in the right direction and as confirmation of what Data Act’s Article 2(8) already entails: a resource centered definition that focusses on user side access to computing resources with minimal service provider interaction, not on function driven, bespoke enterprise SaaS.  The Digital Omnibus introduces a draft provision in Article 31(1a) that fails to establish a coherent framework for retroactivity. SAP’s primary concern with the Data Act centers on the ambiguous treatment of retroactive application regarding cloud switching provisions under Chapter VI. The Data Act’s Chapter VI should explicitly stipulate that its provisions apply exclusively to agreements concluded on or after 12 September 2025. This reflects the commercial reality that cloud service agreements are not standardized, click-through contracts but rather individually negotiated arrangements involving complex trade-offs concerning scope, duration, and pricing structures. SAP proposes a fundamental reconsideration of Article 31(1a). Should the Commission choose to address retroactivity, two viable approaches exist: • Explicit Exclusion: Exclude Chapter VI entirely from retroactive application, or • Coherent Transitional Regime: Adopt a balanced, technology-neutral transitional framework modeled on the approach established in Article 50(5)–(6). To make this effective, this logic should be carried through consistently: in addressing the topic of retroactivity, the Omnibus should adopt a coherent transitional approach rather than introduce new, technically imprecise categories. If necessary, derogations in a new Article 31(1a) should be strictly anchored to the data processing service (DPS) definition in Data Act’s Article 2(8) and clarify the layered model and resource access criteria to make clear that Chapter VI does not extend to application level enterprise Software-as-a-Service (SaaS). 2. Early-termination penalties (“ETP”) SAP welcomes the positive recognition by Digital Omnibus confirmation that non-Infrastructure-as-a-Service (IaaS) providers may continue to operate on fixed-term agreements and apply proportionate ETP. However, the concerns related to what is meant by “proportionate” remain unaddressed. This critical gap creates significant legal uncertainty: without ex-ante criteria, the interpretation of proportionality will fall to national authorities and courts, inevitably resulting in inconsistent application across Member States’ divergent civil law traditions.  To ensure legal certainty and harmonized implementation, the "proportionate" requirement must be clarified through ex-ante criteria. This could be achieved by prohibiting punitive or coercive effects, limiting ETP to objectively unrecovered costs directly caused by early termination or switching or requiring transparent calculation methods. Since the structural non comparability between resource layer services (IaaS/PaaS) and function driven B2B SaaS is also evident here, indicative caps could be considered for DPS, provided it became expressly clarified that such caps do not apply to pure application layer SaaS. 3. Refocusing scope on the intended market: IaaS/PaaS  In Data Act Article 2(8), a DPS is defined by on demand access to computing resources with minimal management effort or service provider interaction – the hallmarks of IaaS/PaaS. By contrast, enterprise B2B SaaS is application-layer and process driven: users engage with business logic, not with computing resources. A literal application of Chapter VI does not reflect how enterprise SaaS is delivered. Adoption typically follows months of negotiations and requires lengthy technical integration and configuration to fit a customer’s IT architecture and operational needs. Imposing Chapter VI here creates artificial, burdensome compliance work and inconsistent interpretations - underscoring the need for functional guidance aligned with broadly used technical standards (e.g., NIST). 4. Definition of “exportable data” should be reviewed and narrowed We have been experiencing a growing number of conversational AI assistants and agents being embedded and used within cloud services (data processing services). The Data Act defines “exportable data” in Recital 82 and Article 2(38) as any input and output data, including metadata, that is directly or indirectly generated or co-generated by the

customer’s use of the data processing service. If a data processing service includes and utilizes a conversational AI assistant or agent, such a broad definition may impose an excessive burden on its provider.  The obligations set out in Articles 23c and 25(2a) of the Data Act can be interpreted to mean that these providers are required to make all conversational data (prompts and questions entered by end users to the AI assistant or agent, as well as generated outputs) available for export in the event that a customer switches to another service provider or to their own ICT infrastructure. Therefore, the definition of exportable data should be reviewed and narrowed to fulfill the purpose of the Data Act without placing an undue burden on providers.  Key Asks We propose that the Data Acts recitals and guidance should therefore: • Define “computing resources”, “on demand”, and “minimal management effort/service provider interaction” in practical, resource layer terms. • Provide a simple self-classification test: if users can provision, configure, scale or release compute, storage or network resources on demand (UI/API), the service is likely a DPS; if use is process driven with no user control of resources, it is application software and out of scope. • Clarify that software falls within scope only where its functionality operates at the resource layer, not where it merely delivers predefined application functions. • Narrow the definition of exportable data to ensure that this does not impose undue technical and operational burdens on providers, particularly regarding conversational AI data such as prompts and generated outputs. These adjustments would restore conceptual coherence, improve legal certainty and harmonise enforcement – while preserving the Data Act’s objective of lowering switching barriers where lock-in actually arises. 
Cyber Resilience Act & EU cybersecurity framework SAP fully supports enhancing cybersecurity across the supply chain and strengthening cyber resilience for customers. SAP already embraces its role as a critical cloud service provider under both the NIS2 Directive and Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA).  However, the CRA, rightly intended to complement these existing cybersecurity laws with product-specific provisions, has become overly complex and unclear. It covers a wide range of products (hardware and software, consumer and industrial) and seemingly extends to cloud services, causing uncertainty and overlap with the other two laws mentioned. Consequently, the CRA risks becoming more a barrier to digital innovation and competitiveness than a tool to enhance Europe’s cybersecurity posture. SAP supports the CRA's goals but calls for changes of specific provisions that will help companies comply. Without a clearer scope and harmonized standards, implementation challenges threaten digital advancements and market readiness. Companies will be required to make considerable investments and take strategic decisions regarding their products today, without knowing the exact – crucial – details of the CRA framework.  SAP notes with concern that the Digital Omnibus proposal does not address the CRA at all. SAP hopes that the forthcoming Digital Fitness Check will provide appropriate remedial measures. SAP recommends delaying CRA enforcement until core compliance tools like clear scope guidance and harmonized standards are ready – mirroring the AI Omnibus proposal approach. This additional time could be used for a deeper harmonization of EU cybersecurity acquis, and revision of provisions that are unrealistic to comply with by B2B software providers. Key Concerns  1. Provide a workable, timely clarification of CRA scope and respective compliance obligations • Cloud service providers lack legal certainty on CRA applicability to Software-as-a-Service (SaaS). • It is unclear whether cloud services must comply with CRA's product-specific rules and how these relate to NIS2's service-specific cybersecurity requirements.  • It would be beneficial to establish a clear distinction between a mobile app and its corresponding cloud service, treating the mobile app as a standalone product with digital elements when the cloud service is also accessible through other user-facing channels (e.g., web browsers). Such a distinction would simplify the regulatory landscape: NIS2 would govern cybersecurity for the cloud service, while the CRA would apply to the mobile app. Even if the CRA ultimately extends to cloud services, treating the cloud service as an independent product would be far more manageable than classifying a mobile app and its interactions with one or multiple cloud services as a single complex product with multiple remote data processing functionalities.

Remove the CRA obligation for manufacturers to provide security updates free of charge.  • This obligation will fundamentally disrupt the B2B software licensing model for two main reasons: a. Technical infeasibility: Enterprise software evolves continuously, therefore a version from five years ago differs substantially in functionality than the current one. Isolating pure security updates from functional ones (which are not for free) would require maintaining dozens or hundreds of product baselines, which is commercially unfeasible. Reduce regulatory overlap with NIS2 and DORA • Duplicative – or interpreted differently – requirements across NIS2, DORA, and CRA frameworks. • The same cloud services are treated either as products or services under different laws, which creates multiple, redundant and complex compliance processes for the same solution.  • Unnecessary redundancy in conformity assessments, audits, and documentation increases costs and diverts resources from actual security improvements. • Needed: A coherent, layered framework; such framework would define baseline and tiered cybersecurity requirements (e.g., basic, medium, high) based on sensitivity and sector (e.g., critical infrastructure, finance),  • Needed: A streamlined and consistent set of definitions across EU cybersecurity, and even digital, legislations (“operational resilience” or reportable “incident”) would increase legal clarity and certainty. • Needed: Mutual recognition of documentation and audits, standardized to the extent possible. Complement the Single Entry Point for incident reporting with harmonized reporting timelines and templates • Mandatory early-warning notifications within hours of detection should be replaced by a minimum reporting deadline of 72 hours, aligned with the GDPR. Earlier notifications should remain voluntary and risk-based, allowing companies to focus first on addressing incidents. • A single core incident-reporting template should be provided, triggered by streamline and consistent reporting thresholds and taxonomies, applicable across all regimes, covering incident description, scope, impact, and mitigation measures. Any sector-specific requirements should be addressed through clearly delimited modules built on the same core structure. This approach promotes interoperability, legal certainty, and efficiency, while avoiding duplication and inconsistent reporting formats. The EU cybersecurity framework should make Europe more secure, not less competitive. A smart, strategic pause of the CRA and a commitment to simplification would send a strong signal to Europe’s digital and industrial ecosystems.   AI Act SAP welcomes the Digital Omnibus on AI proposal and shares the European Commission’s commitment to advancing “forward-looking, innovative policies that strengthen the EU’s competitiveness and lighten the regulatory burdens on people, businesses, and administrations,” as stated in the Simplification Agenda.  The proposed measures to reduce administrative burden under the AI Act and the postponement of high-risk AI system requirements are much needed. Requiring companies to establish compliance frameworks before relevant guidance and harmonized standards become available is fundamentally impractical and undermines effective implementation. Clearly defined compliance deadline for high-risk systems of August 2028:  • This postponement needs to be clear for businesses without conditions that may or may not be applied.  • We therefore propose extending this period with a clear date to:  (1) provide the European Commission and European Standardization Organizations with appropriate time to work and consult on the draft standards and the guidelines and  (2) to provide companies with sufficient time to analyse and assess these compliance tools, then adjust their processes accordingly.  • This is especially critical for the Commission’s extensive guidance on high-risk AI system classification and requirements, which will be issued gradually and necessitate significant changes to internal SecDevOps workflows. Under the current proposal, the grace period applies only to AI systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026, meaning that systems introduced after that date would need to achieve immediate compliance based on a Code that becomes available simultaneously with the deadline. This compressed timeline creates significant legal uncertainty and operational risk. Provisions that SAP fully supports and recommends their retaining:  1. Remove the registration obligations for AI systems determined by providers to be non-high-risk for the following reasons: • Prevent cascading registration burdens in integrated HR suites: HR management software (e.g. SAP SuccessFactors and SmartRecruiters) include many features that operate in Annex III employment and learning contexts, yet functions like writing or translation assistants are low impact support tools rather than decision making systems. Still, these use cases are also integrated with other features and could thus lead to potentially dozens or even hundreds of AI and non-AI functionalities captured by association. Beyond the untenable workload for both providers who must complete registrations and authorities who must review them, this approach would create system wide uncertainty about whether integrated non-AI components also need to be registered. • Clarify exemptions and avoid semi high-risk liability exposure: Some of these cases may theoretically carry higher risk, but strong product design, particularly enhanced human oversight and transparency, can place them within the Act’s exemptions. Article 6(3) nevertheless requires providers to register these exempted systems and to specify an intended purpose, even though intended purpose is the organizing principle of the high-risk regime and its key requirements. In essence, registering an intended purpose for an exempted system creates a quasi-high-risk designation but without the corresponding safeguards. Thus, if a provider must publish an intended purpose but does not implement those guardrails, authorities or courts may treat deviations from the registered purpose as misuse and attribute responsibility to the provider, despite the absence of tools such as post market monitoring or prescriptive deployer instructions. • Not only the mere fact of registration is required: As we understand it, the requirement to prepare technical documentation applies only to high-risk systems. It appears the semi-high-risk effect created by the registration obligation for Article 6(3) and the need to define the intended purpose can lead even experts to assume that high risk requirements apply to systems that are not high risk. In principle, Article 6(3) is intended to lower the overall compliance burden so that attention can be devoted to genuinely high-risk use cases. However, it is not only registration that is required. Annex VIII, Section B also calls for more burdensome elements, including the intended purpose and a justification for the exemption. Also, several of these items may require ongoing updates. For example, the justification if safeguards are modified, the AI system trade name, the status of the AI system, and the Member States in which the AI system has been put into service. For instance, the last item could change daily depending on which customer turns on the feature.

General Data Protection Regulation  Experience over time shows that GDPR implementation has produced growing legal uncertainty, driven by divergent and conservative national interpretations and uneven enforcement. Companies need practical, risk-based guidance rather than strict uniform enforcement which has so far discouraged experimentation with new technologies, including AI and data-driven services. Reform is needed to ensure the GDPR operates in a genuinely risk-based and proportionate way balancing and ensuring innovation. Hence SAP strongly supports the Digital Omnibus proposal to amend the GDPR but argues it must go further to resolve barriers to innovation and technological modernization.  Overall, the requested GDPR-regime relaxations are not in conflict with Data Protection but aim for a modernization of protection mechanisms. The goal is a shift from rigid, bureaucratic control ("consent for everything") to a risk-based approach to data protection, where the actual danger to the individual Data Subject is decisive. This modernization is necessary to align with Big Data and AI realities and to allow innovation in these domains. SAP supports the Commission’s targeted approach to amending the GDPR to address practical problems and barriers to innovation. The proposal addresses several reform needs identified by industry, including: • Clarification of personal data definition – shifting toward realistic identifiability and actual risk rather than hypothetical re-identification • Measures to reduce abusive data subject requests – particularly tactical access requests • Research privilege improvements – facilitating further processing for scientific research and innovation Positive Elements that SAP supports • Article 12(5) – New provisions for handling manifestly unfounded/excessive requests • Article 13(4) & (5) – Information requirement simplifications, although carve-backs should be deleted • Article 33 – Single Entry Point for breach notifications substantially reduces burden, even though should be complemented by harmonized templates and timelines (as per the justification above). Currently, no clear standards exist for the "quality" of reporting required, creating significant challenges given that different legal frameworks mandate distinct forms of input. Harmonisation in this domain is critically needed. Additionally, greater clarity is essential regarding risk qualification.

Digital Omnibus / FBF comments – 12/03/2026 
 
Introduction  
On November 19 th, 2025 , a s part of its simplification agenda , the European Commission 
published two proposals for regulation (the “Digital Package”  / “Digital Omnibus”) aimed at 
simplifying the digital legislative framework on personal and non-personal data, AI, and platforms, 
and proposing a single-entry point for reporting cybersecurity incidents.  
 
The French Banking Federation (FBF), the professional organization representing the 324 banks 
operating in France, welcomes this simplification initiative, which has been widely acclaimed 
in the financial sector, particularly in view of the need for innovation and competitiveness in an 
international context and cybersecurity rules.  
 
Indeed, digital regulations have  been accumulating for several years and are confronting 
companies with an avalanche of new requirements  which, while pursuing laudable objectives, 
greatly increase the burden and complexity of procedures.  Moreover, implementing measures 
(guidelines, delegated or implemented acts, RTS, standards , etc.) are increasingly being 
published late, sometimes after the entry into application of the regulations (DORA, AI Act for 
example). Banks must therefore deal with considerable legal uncertainty  as they have to 
anticipate these Level 2 measures, at the risk of having to backtrack with recommendations or 
guidelines defined later, with significant associated costs. 
 
The FBF is aware t hat this should not result in  deregulation, but simplification is essential and 
should aim to eliminate any overlaps or inconsistencies between legal requirements that make 
them difficult for banks to interpret and implement. This simplification should also result in an 
administrative relief making it possible for companies to anticipate and to fully comply with 
their obligations, while enabling operational teams to focus on their initial tasks.  
 
This would reduce compliance costs for banks, thereby improving their competitiveness.  
 
However, in a tense geopolitical context, a greater importance should be given to  issues of 
sovereignty and competitiveness at an international level , by ensuring that the regulation 
does not  hamper the competitiveness of the European economy or  ultimately increase 
dependence on non -European players . To this end, it is vital to have regulatory impact 
assessments taking the European companies’ needs into account . Besides, to remain 
competitive with foreign  digital companies (especially the American ones), banks  support the 
development of national or European digital infrastructures that would be trustworthy  and 
capable of meeting their requirements in terms of cybersecurity, data protection and 
confidentiality.

1. Simplification on the Data Acquis  
1.1. Data sharing (One Data Act) 
In Articles 1 and 2 of the Digital Omnibus, the European Commission provides that all the rules 
related to data sharing  (Data Governance Act / DGA 1, Open Data  Directive / ODD2, Free-flow of 
data regulation / FFDR3) are consolidated in one legal act – the Data Act – for giving legal certainty. 
This simplification measure is welcome in that it makes the requirements for sharing and reusing 
both public and private data much clearer to read and analyse. The existence of a single 
legislation bringing together all the concepts, principles and modalities of data sharing and 
serving as a basis for sector -specific regulations makes perfect sense and allows for 
harmonization at the European level, including across sectors4. 
If we correctly understand the amendments made by the Digital Omnibus, the Data Act will 
provide requirements for data sharing between businesses and consumers (B2C), between 
businesses (B2B), between businesses and the public sector (B2G and Open Data), and will 
incorporate the concepts of data spaces and data intermediary services. This clarifies the 
different regimes applicable to data sharing and can only be beneficial in terms of ensuring 
a common understanding of the requirements in the various sectors. 
Further work is needed to clarify definitions, particularly those relating to data holders. 
The principles of a common data sharing framework can only be welcomed by the banking sector. 
The Data Act refers to contractual and compensation mechanisms for data sharing between 
businesses. We also understand that the DGA is shifting towards a voluntary mechanism for the 
registration of data intermediaries due to an overestimation of market needs in the impact 
assessment, since only 27 of the expected 100 -150 data intermediation service pro viders have 
registered to date5. Furthermore, the DGA is basing data spaces on standards that enable 
interoperability, given that technical standards have already been defined by European 
standardization bodies. 
Maintaining the protection of intellectual property, trade secrets, and sensitive and strategic data 
for a company is also essential, and excluding the sharing of such data is vital to preserving the 
competitiveness and sovereignty of European companies. In this sense, Article 1(3) of the Digital 
Omnibus, which provides new grounds for refusing to share sensitive data, is welcome. Under the 
Data Act, data holders could refuse to disclose trade secrets when they consider that there is a 
high risk of unlawful  disclosure to entities subject to foreign jurisdictions that do not offer an 
equivalent level of protection. 
In general, the European Commission's will to “enhance the EU businesses’ global 
competitiveness, reducing financial losses and improving operational stability”  and

However, a few clarifications can be made regarding compensation. Access to a company's data 
cannot be free when the data constitutes strategic assets in which investments have been 
made (for data collection and processing) and when access is based on a service that is costly to 
provide. A distribution of value based on a give -and-take mechanism is essential to promote 
innovation on the one hand and to encourage data holders to invest in the quality of their data on 
the other. In that sense, compensation should be freely determined  so as not to undermine  
the very principle of contractual freedom. Otherwise, reasonable compensation should not 
be defined at a minimum level, but the methods of calculation should be based on market 
valuation logic so as not to undermine the competitiveness of data holders. 
Furthermore, the Digital Omnibus provides  that micro-enterprises, SMEs, and even small mid-
cap companies could be exempted from certain data sharing obligations. While simplification 
is particularly important for small businesses, this should not lead to distortions of 
competition. These exemption measures must not contravene the principle of equal treatment, 
insofar as, if a measure is not suitable for micro -enterprises or SMEs, it will not be suitable for 
larger players either. On the one hand, it should be remembered that the value and sensitivity of 
data are not proportional to the size of a company. On t he other hand, some players, such as 
payment data aggregators, are small businesses that nevertheless process large volumes of data 
(sometimes more than banks), which is often highly sensitive. For competition but also data 
protection reasons, exempting micro-enterprises, SMEs and small mid-cap companies would not 
be justified and would even be counterproductive.  
While the principles of the Digital Omnibus on the Data Acquis are a step in the right direction, 
they need to be applied and taken further within the framework of the Digital Fitness Check 
in order to truly make the Data Act the only regulatory basis for data sharing, including within 
a sector.  
1.2. Data protection (GDPR, ePrivacy Directive) 
 
a. Definition of personal data 
Article 3(1) of the Digital Omnibus amends Article 4(1) of the GDPR on the definition of personal 
data in order to clarify, in light of the Court’s judgment in case C-413/23 P, that information would 
not be considered personal data for an entity when it does not have the “means reasonably likely” 
to identify the natural person to whom the information relates.  
The Commission’s intention to clarify the complex  issue of defining personal data  is welcome. 
Debate on re -identification is essential, particularly in the development of AI. However, this 
amendment, as currently drafted, raises several fundamental legal and practical questions (e.g., 
contractual governance, technical compliance) that warrants careful consideration  and an in-
depth impact assessment. 
The FBF recommends withdrawing this Article 3(1) from the Digital Omnibus which, due to its fast-
track process, does not allow for in-depth impact assessment and consideration of this complex

While the principle of a discussion on the definition of personal data with regard to re -

advancements in our global open data landscape, and the associated risks. 
b. Cookies 
Article 88a (3)(c) of the Digital Omnibus provides a new exception for the creation “of aggregated 
information on the usage of an online service to measure the audience of such a service, where 
[this operation] is carried out by the controller of that online service solely for its own use”. This 
new exception is welcome for it will lead to a reduction in consent banners and will allow for 
harmonization at the European level of the regime currently in force in France (CNIL doctrine).  
In addition, Article 88a (3)(d) provides that the exception aimed at “maintaining or restoring the 
security of a service provided by the controller and requested by the data subject or the terminal 
equipment used for the provision of such service”. This will clarify a legal uncertainty that weighed 
on data controllers and called into question the IT security measures that could be implemented.  
Nevertheless, certain provisions could supplement the Commission's proposals with a view to 
simplifying the applicable framework and the user experience.  
First, in order to increase security for users and bank customers, it is proposed to include an 
exception to the requirement to obtain consent for any read or write operations on user terminals 
in order to fight fraud. This phenomenon is responsible for significant losses for the banking 
sector, its customers, and public authorities. Not subjecting read or write operations on users' 
terminals for the purpose of fighting fraud to the consent regime would therefore increase the 
detection of fraudsters. In this sense, read or write operations on users' terminals for the purpose 
of combating money laundering and terrorist financing could also benefit from an exception to the 
consent requirement. The following amendments are therefore suggested:

terminal equipment of a natural person without consent, and subsequent processing, shall be

First, Article 88a(4)(a) requires data controllers to allow users to refuse to give their consent with

amendments are therefore suggested: 
Article 88a - Processing of personal data in the terminal equipment of natural persons 
(4) Where storing of personal data, or gaining of access to personal data already stored, in the

(a) the data subject shall be able to refuse or accept requests for consent in an easy and 
intelligible manner with a single-click button or equivalent means;   
Second, Article 88a(4)(b) provides that “if the data subject gives consent, the controller shall not 
make a new request for consent for the same purpose for the period during which the controller 
can lawfully rely on the consent of the data subject”.  
Article 88a(4)(c) provides that “if the data subject declines a request for consent, the controller 
shall not make a new request for consent for the same purpose for a period of at least six months”.

browser, either manually or automatically at regular intervals, the data controller is no longer 
technically able to determine whether that user has given or not their consent to these operations.

2. Single-Entry Point for the reporting of cybersecurity incidents  
While the FBF is in favo ur of strengthening cybersecurity across the European ecosystem, 
incident reporting raises numerous operational difficulties that clearly illustrate the need for 
regulatory simplification. In addition to European cybersecurity regulations (NIS and NIS 2 
directives, Cybersecurity Act (CSA), Cyber Resilience Act (CRA)), there are  also sector-specific 
regulations, such as the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) for the financial sector, as well 
as obligations to report personal data breaches (GDPR or ePrivacy) or incidents under European 
digital legislation (e.g., the AI Act).  
In this regard, the FBF warmly welcomes the principle of a single -entry point for reporting 
cybersecurity incidents. This addresses the issue of having to notify multiple authorities.   
However, it should be noted that, at this stage, no details have been provided on how this platform 
would be set up or to what extent it would be sufficiently secure to receive sensitive information. 
At a minimum, the portal should be simple enough to be used as effectively as possible , should 
use standards/formats that are suitable for crisis management  and should be usable in the 
context of future regulatory developments requiring incident reporting.  
Furthermore, the CRA does not fall within the scope of the Digital Omnibus, which raises 
questions. Articles 13 and 14 of the CRA require manufacturers of products containing digital 
components to report actively exploited vulnerabilities or serious incidents.  
For the sake of simplification, it would therefore make sense to integrate CRA reports into the 
single-entry point provided for by the Digital Omnibus, especially since, in some Member States, 
the competent national authority would not necessarily be the same as for other cybersecurity 
legislation, which contributes to a fragmentation of competent authorities and, consequently, to 
an increase in reporting.  
The inclusion of the CRA within the scope of the Digital Omnibus is also important in that it is 
necessary to simplify and clarify the relationship between this text and the DORA regulation 
for the financial sector, given that many obligations overlap. French banks understand that the 
approaches are different, with the CRA aiming to protect consumers and DORA aiming to both 
secure information systems and maintain financial stability. Nevertheless, the financial sector is 
   
 
7 
 
one of the most regulated sectors, with high security requirements. It is therefore reasonable to 
assume that the DORA requirements cover all critical ICT services provided by banks and are at 
least equivalent to those of the CRA. It would therefore be useful to presume that banks 
comply with the CRA because they comply with DORA.    
Furthermore, returning to the single-entry point, it should be noted that two difficulties remain.  
On the one hand, this single-entry point does not resolve the issues related to the lack of 
harmonization of procedures and definitions of incidents to be reported . Indeed, a security 
incident will be defined differently and according to different criteria and thresholds (“major 
incident” as defined by DORA, “significant incident” as defined by NIS 2, “personal data breach” 
as defined by the GDPR, ‘vulnerability’ as defined by the CRA, or “serious incident related to high-
risk AI” as defined by the AI Act).  This will therefore require operational teams (which will not 
necessarily be the same within a  company) to carry out the same impact assessment several 
times in order to define the incident, collect the necessary information, and report it. 
On the other hand, some Member States, including France and Germany, have already expressed 
their opposition to this single -entry point, particularly for reasons of national security . If French 
companies, and especially banks, must continue to report incidents to national authorities, the 
single-entry point at the European level no longer serves any purpose. 
In summary, French banks are in favour of the principle of a single -entry point, albeit with 
some reservations: 
- There should be no residual procedures (particularly with national laws or regulations 
outside the scope like the CRA), 
- Maximum security for the platform/single -entry point with sufficient security and 
compartmentalization measures.  
It would be appropriate to have a platform that solely addresses the issue of administrative 
simplification by enabling the legal obligations to report security incidents under the various 
texts to be fulfilled quickly (with a simple alert template allowing the scope of the incident to be 
defined and the competent authorities to be identified), while maintaining the direct 
relationship between the entity and the authority for support  (this being separate from the 
simple administrative formality of reporting ). This would avoid the need to transmit detailed 
information about the cyber incident or other sensitive data within this platform.   
In addition to this single-entry point, other mechanisms would need to be put in place, such as: 
- Enforcing the designation of a single authority within Member States (as provided for 
in Article 19 of the DORA Regulation) for all cybersecurity incident reporting obligations, 
- Aligning the definitions of security incidents  to be reported or notified in the various 
European regulations (DORA, NIS 2, CRA, AI Act), as well as the main terms relating to 
incident lifecycles (detection, classification, notification procedures, trigger thresholds), 
- Exempt the financial sector from the application of the CRA thanks to a delegated act 
of the European Commission due to the existence of equivalent obligations in the DORA 
regulation, in accordance with Article 2(5) of the CRA, or, at the very least, make 
compliance with DORA a presumption of compliance with the CRA, 
- Make NIS 2 and lex specialis, such as DORA for the financial sector, the basis for 
security and risk management requirements  across the entire European regulatory 
framework, specifying where necessary the specific features of cyber risk management in

an annex to the relevant regulations in order to avoid any redundancy with the provisions 
already established in the framework, 
- Consider banking groups as a whole for risk management and incident reporting , 
taking into account systemically important institutions (SSIs) in their supervision in order 
to avoid multiple incident reports and differentiated management between legal entities 
within the same group, leading to multiple uncoordinated interactions with the authorities 
(European Central Bank, competent national authorities), 
- Ensure a balance between protecting sensitive, strategic, or personal data and 
increasing exposure to the risk of data leaks due to the opening of the financial data 
market, 
- Distribute responsibility for ICT supply chain security between suppliers and 
customers, providing for penalties for subcontractors in the event of cyber regulatory 
breaches, so that banking institutions do not bear all the responsibility. 
3. Simplification of harmonised rules on Artificial Intelligence (Digital Omnibus AI) 
The AI Act is a complex regulation and many uncertainties remain regarding elements that 
are essential to understanding the text and its implementation.  
Beyond the lack of designation of competent national authorities on AI in certain Member States 
(such as France), it is important to highlight the delay in standardization work on AI and the fact 
that many guidelines (a dozen) are still pending.  
Furthermore, the rapid evolution of technologies raises new questions and uncertainties on 
issues that have already been decided at the European level. The definition of AI, for example, 
remains problematic despite the publication of guidelines, leading to significant legal uncertainty 
for businesses (for example, the exclusion of logistic regressions for the financial sector should 
be reaffirmed). The development of agentic also raises questions about the definition of AI , 
demonstrating the importance of having a technologically neutral legal framework, so that 
the AI Act does not need to be revised each time a technological advance occurs.   
It is therefore essential to simplify the rules on AI within the framework of the Digital 
Omnibus and to allow sufficient and realistic deadlines for companies to fully adapt to the 
new requirements. 
3.1. Postponement of deadlines for AI 
Although the Digital Omnibus AI Act provides for new implementation deadlines (6 to 12 months 
from the publication of the guidelines and no later than 2 December 2027), legal uncertainty 
persists due to a lack of visibility on the publication of the guidelines and standards. This makes 
it particularly complex for companies to implement the AI Act, as they must nevertheless move 
forward and sometimes take the risk of blindly anticipating European developments.  
This legal uncertainty is compounded by the lack of visibility on the adoption of the Digital 
Omnibus Package and the confusion of European developments that are being carried out in 
parallel (the implementation of the AI Act with the publication of forthc oming guidelines on the 
one hand, and the European legislative procedure on the Digital Omnibus on the other), which are 
causing further operational disruption and regulatory overload, particularly for economic players 
who will have to review their processes, evaluate their technical solutions and deploy corrective 
measures in line with future guidelines. 
   
 
9 
 
The FBF therefore supports the proposals of the Cypriot Presidency and the draft reports of 
the European Parliament (both IMCO/LIBE and JURI committees) to postpone the deadlines 
for the application of the AI Act to 2 December 2027 for AI systems in Annex III and to 2 August 
2028 for AI systems in Annex I. This will provide greater visibility and legal certainty for economic 
players. 
Moreover, if the aforementioned guidelines are not published by the end of 2026, a further 
postponement should be provided.  
3.2. Interplay between the GDPR and AI 
While simplification must not turn into deregulation and fundamental European values, such as 
personal data protection, should be safeguarded, the high priority given to consumer protection 
in the AI Act, sometimes to the detriment of European competitiveness and sovereignty, risks 
being counterproductive and effectively stifling innovation in Europe, thereby increasing the 
European Union's lag in AI compared to American and Asian players and thus increasing the 
risks of technological dependence. 
A fair balance must therefore be struck between consumer protection and economic needs in 
order to promote European innovation and competitiveness.  
In the context of AI, this requires facilitating the use of personal data within a framework that 
continues to provide protection. In this sense, we believe that the proposals in the Digital 
Omnibus AI are simplified without being deregulatory. However, ce rtain adjustments or 
clarifications still need to be made. 
• Use of legitimate interest for the AI systems 
Article 88c of the Digital Omnibus expressly provides for the use of legitimate interest as a legal 
basis for the processing of personal data in the context of AI. While it is indeed necessary to 
facilitate the use of AI, the risk-based approach adopted by the GDPR should not be called 
into question, leaving the data controller free to justify the use of one legal basis rather than 
another. 
We therefore do not believe it is appropriate to specify the legal basis for AI in the GDPR . 
However, clarification of the possibility of using legitimate interest as a legal basis, without 
having to seek the consent of data subjects for the use of personal data in the context of AI, would 
be welcome in guidelines, perhaps relating to the relationship between the GDPR and the AI Act. 
This would streamline and facilitate the deployment of AI in European companies. However, the 
protection of personal data is not being overlooked due to the large volume of data processed in 
the context of AI and the impossibility of tracing personal data.  
This raises the question of the re-identification of data subjects in the context of an AI system and 
therefore the definition of personal data (see paragraph 1.2.a. of this document). 
• Use of specific categories of data to avoid bias 
Furthermore, Article 1(5) of the Digital Omnibus AI  proposes adding an Article 4a to the AI Act 
for the use of sensitive data within the meaning of Article 9 of the GDPR in order to test high -risk 
AI systems and thus avoid bias. This proposal is welcome because the issue is crucial, especially 
since Article 10 of the AI Act requires providers of high-risk AI systems to have data sets “to the 
best extent possible, free of errors and complete” . This implies that the provider takes the 
“appropriate measures to detect, prevent and mitigate possible biases identified” .  
   
 
10 
 
However, Article 1(5) of the Digital Omnibus AI specifies that this possibility of using sensitive data 
must remain exceptional and be subject to several cumulative conditions. In particular, this 
possibility is conditional on the fact that the detection a nd correction of bias cannot be done 
otherwise, for example through the use of synthetic or anonymised data.  
As mentioned above, it is crucial to be able to verify data sets and to be able to detect and correct 
biases so that European companies can equip themselves with reliable AI systems and thus be 
more competitive. 
While we understand the will to keep exceptional cases where sensitive data may be used, the 
conditions may need to be relaxed. Indeed, the use of synthetic or anonymised data is not always 
feasible for various reasons (cost, reliability, feasibility, etc.). The challenges of detecting and 
correcting bias are important for the reliability of systems, and it is necessary to give European 
companies the means to carry out the necessary checks, including through the use of sensitive 
data. For example, in the banking sector, it is essential to be able to use this sensitive data in order 
to detect and correct any bias in credit worthiness systems and thus avoid the risk of 
discrimination.    
• Issues not addressed in the Digital Omnibus AI 
The Digital Omnibus does not mention certain topics that would nevertheless merit clarification 
or simplification concerning the relationship between AI and personal data protection.  
On the one hand, it would seem helpful to provide for the pooling of data protection impact 
assessments (DPIAs) and fundamental rights impact assessments (FRIAs). As one cannot exist 
without the other, it would be appropriate to adapt the DPIA document (personal data is 
processed almost systematically) by incorporating the requirements of the AI Act for the impact 
on fundamental rights.  
On the other hand, it would be useful to incorporate the point raised by the Hamburg data 
protection authority in its study on personal data in large language models (LLMs)7.. In particular, 
it states that the mere storage of an LLM does not constitute processing within the meaning of 
Article 4(2) of the GDPR, since no personal data is stored in it. 
Finally, the definition of AI should be clarified for certain uses. In the financial sector, logistic 
regression is based on well -understood and highly explainable statistical methods that are 
already covered by existing banking risk management frameworks (documentation, governance, 
backtesting, validation, etc.).  
Applying all the obligations relating to high-risk AI to these models would distract regulators from 
emerging technologies that present genuinely higher risks. 
This would impose an excessive compliance burden on banks and create a mismatch between 
the high-risk requirements of the AI Act and the actual risk profile of logistic regression models. 
This clarification could be included in recital (12) of the AI Act via the Digital Omnibus AI. Failing 
that, the exclusion of logistic regressions from the definition of AI should at least be provided for 
in the guidelines on the interplay between AI and the financial sector regulations. 
Finally, as mentioned above, while simplification is particularly important for small 
businesses, it must not lead to distortions of competition . Here again , if a measure is not

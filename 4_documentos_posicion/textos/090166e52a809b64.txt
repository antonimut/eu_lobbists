INTRODUCTION  
 
IBM welcomes the timely publication of the AI Omnibus Regulation, aiming at 
simplifying and harmonising the implementation of the EU AI Act, which is a good 
starting point toward cutting red tape for all businesses operating in Europe, and will 
help support innovation in this area. 
 
Simplifying rules for AI is essential to foster innovation within European industries. Should 
Europe lag in AI adoption, the repercussions for the EU’s competitiveness and digital 
resilience could be severe, and would lead to increased production costs, less flexible and 
more vulnerable supply chains, slower innovation, and shrinking margins. 
 
To fully support Europe’s competitiveness objectives, IBM offers the following 
recommendations to strengthen the AI Omnibus proposal.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE  
 
AI Act timelines:  
• Art. 1(31) of AI Omnibus (ref. to Art 113 of AI Act) 
• Article 1(30)(b) of AI Omnibus (ref. to Art 111 of AI Act) 
 
IBM welcomes the proposed additional postponement to implement high-risk 
requirements, which will allow more time for harmonised standards to be developed. As a 
leading contributor to the ongoing AI Act standardisation work, IBM is best placed to 
witness the challenges that come with developing harmonised European standards in this 
new area. The original timeline created much uncertainty for industry, which is why a more 
reasonable timeframe will provide the necessary clarity and time for industry to adopt high-
quality standards for the successful implementation of the AI Act.  
 
However, we believe this delay should be clearer for businesses without conditions that 
may or may not be applied: indeed, recital 22 indicates that extensions for high-risk 
requirements would only apply after the Commission’s decision confirms the availability of 
“measures in support of compliance, which may include harmonised standards, common 
specifications and Commission guidelines”. This creates significant uncertainty for 
companies as those extensions would need to be implemented promptly for them to have 
enough time to comply. Additionally, harmonised standards should be the primary 
instrument for compliance, and as such, the deadline to implement the high-risk 
requirements should be linked to the availability of such standards.   
 
Therefore, we propose to further simplify and clarify this provision with a set date of 2 
August 2028 for both Annex I and III. This will provide sufficient time for such standards 
to be finalised and published and allow companies the necessary time to implement 
compliance processes.  
 
Additionally, given that the Code of Practice on Transparency obligations have been delayed 
and are only expected in June 2026, it is unrealistic to expect companies to achieve 
compliance by August 2026. We therefore recommend introducing a 12‑month grace 
period applicable to all systems falling under Article 50, not only those placed on the 
market before August 2026. This additional time will allow providers to properly implement 
the obligations under Article 50 and the forthcoming Code of Practice once it is published.

Registration of AI systems: Art. 1(14) of AI Omnibus (ref. to the deletion of Art. 49(2) of the 
AI Act) 
 
IBM also welcomes the clarification that AI systems, which are used in high-risk areas but 
for which the provider has concluded that they are not high-risk as they are only used for 
narrow or procedural tasks, will be exempted from registration. This would not only reduce 
administrative burden but also security risks. This deletion provides added legal clarity and 
is entirely consistent with the risk-based approach of the EU AI Act, i.e. the (high) level of 
risk mandates the (high) level of obligations and preparatory tasks do not qualify in that 
matter. 
 
Transparency obligations for providers and deployers of certain AI systems: Amending 
Article 50(2) of the AI Act (NEW) 
 
Article 50(2) of the EU AI Act requires providers of AI systems to clearly mark AI-generated 
content as artificial, including synthetic text. This is not supported by the current 
technological state of the art. No technically feasible, reliable and interoperable solutions 
exist to ensure persistent, machine‑readable marking of AI-generated text. Retaining this 
obligation would therefore create legal uncertainty and impose requirements that cannot be 
effectively complied with, contrary to the Regulation’s own feasibility and proportionality 
safeguards. As such, we propose the AI Omnibus amends this article by removing “text” 
from the modality list or introduce a more flexible marking regime for AI-generated 
text.

SIMPLIFYING THE EU DIGITAL RULEBOOK 
IBM’S POSITION ON THE DIGITAL  OMNIBUS  PROPOSAL  
MARCH 2026  
 
 
 
INTRODUCTION  
 
IBM welcomes the timely publication of the Digital Omnibus proposal, aiming at 
simplifying the EU’s digital rulebook in cybersecurity, data governance and privacy. We 
believe these proposals are a good starting point toward cutting red tape for all 
businesses operating in Europe and will help support innovation. We specifically 
welcome additional clarifications in GDPR to support the development and deployment 
of Artificial Intelligence (AI). 
 
However, we believe that overall, the proposals fail to fully address the 
competitiveness gaps the EU is facing, and which many industries and heads of states 
have identified – also highlighted by the Draghi report – and which require bold and 
urgent reforms. The proposals clearly lack ambition in some areas, notably in 
cybersecurity, where the complexity of existing rules require real simplification 
measures that go beyond streamlining incident reporting obligations.  
 
Additionally, we are very concerned about some proposed changes to the cloud 
switching regime of the Data Act that would prevent some cloud providers (i.e. 
Infrastructure as a Service or IaaS providers) to charge proportionate early termination 
fees for fixed contracts. This change would not simplify regulatory compliance but

CYBERSECURITY  
 
Single Entry Point (SEP) - Art. 6 of Omnibus (ref. to Art. 23/30 of NIS2): 
 
The Single-Entry Point or SEP for reporting is a good first step towards harmonising 
incident notifications to the relevant national competent authority. We believe 
companies in all sectors should be allowed to leverage their country of main 
establishment for all cybersecurity reporting. This will offer a compromise between 
harmonising reporting while limiting security risks associated with having a single point 
of entry at EU level. The SEP platform should serve as a connecting layer for national 
CSIRTs to disseminate notifications to other affected member states. Finally, the EU 
should clarify that vulnerability and incident reporting under the CRA is also 
covered by the SEP mechanism. 
 
However, both NIS2 and CRA are missing liability protection clauses which are 
necessary to build trust so that companies are comfortable sharing information. This 
furthers the goals of information sharing for enhanced cybersecurity while also 
protecting reporting entities. While Article 23(1) of NIS2 provides that notification shall 
not subject the notifying entity to increased liability, the law should also clarify that 
reporting companies should not be liable for potential spill-over effects caused by the 
act of notification. 
 
Additional recommendations to simplify the EU Cyber acquis:  
 
Despite some improvements, IBM believes that the Omnibus proposals strongly fail 
to address the urgent need to simplify EU cybersecurity rules, which have created 
resource-intensive and costly processes, without leading to meaningful improvements 
in risk management. These inefficiencies ultimately increase costs for end-users 
without achieving the goal of enhanced cybersecurity. Companies need clarity and

simplification now, rather than later after overlapping rules and requirements go into 
effect.   
 
This is why we urge EU legislators to include the following proposals to drive meaningful 
efficiencies for enhanced cybersecurity: 
 
a) Aligning the concept of “main establishment”: 
 
Article 14(7) of the CRA defines “main establishment” differently from Article 26(2) of 
the NIS2 Directive. This concept is also present in other (non-cyber) EU legislation, such 
as the GDPR or the Digital Services Act (DSA). However, Member States have adopted 
varying interpretations of “main establishment” when transposing or implementing 
these rules. This lack of consistency undermines the EU’s objective of reducing 
regulatory fragmentation and creates significant challenges for companies in 
determining their main establishment—particularly when trying to align it with the 
location most relevant for compliance. 
 
This is why IBM believes that companies established in the EU should be able to use 
the same country of main establishment across all legislation that includes this 
one-stop-shop principle. We encourage EU legislators to focus on the ultimate goals, 
i.e. improving and simplifying notifications and reporting procedures, as well as 
reducing unnecessary administrative burden and fragmentation of the single market. 
 
a) Addressing overlap of audits and conformity assessments: 
 
Companies that are in scope of NIS2, DORA, GDPR, and CRA face multiple processes, 
conformity assessments and audit requirements to demonstrate compliance with each 
of these regulations. While each regulation has its specificity and scope, numerous 
security requirements under one regulation could qualify as compliance with another, 
particularly among those that are applicable to critical infrastructure (i.e. NIS2 and 
related sector-specific laws such as DORA). In practice, the lack of a coordinated EU-
wide oversight framework has created a fragmented and unnecessarily complicated 
compliance environment. Financial institutions and public entities classified as critical 
infrastructure often impose overlapping audit and certification requirements on their 
third-party service providers to meet supervisory expectations.  
 
This results in repeated audits where providers must demonstrate the same risk 
controls to multiple clients and government authorities throughout the year, 
increasing unnecessary administrative burden and costs, and requiring providers to 
 
4 
 
allocate limited resources to manage repeated audits, rather than focus on other areas 
that could be more impactful.   
 
Additionally, the harmonisation goal of NIS2 is challenged by varying conformity 
assessment processes required nationally, which would risk further fragmentation. 
For example, NIS2 requires similar risk management measures across the EU, yet 
companies would have to undergo multiple conformity assessments in various forms 
(e.g. certification or third-party audits) in each of the countries they operate, even 
though this requirement is similar for all member states.  
 
To address this issue, IBM recommends a common set of tools to demonstrate 
conformity with NIS2 and mutual recognition of national certifications and frameworks.  
This would streamline supervision, avoid duplication, and enhance overall efficiency 
without weakening oversight. 
 
Sector-specific rules, such as DORA and the Network Code on Cybersecurity 
(NCCS), should be closely aligned with NIS2, as the horizontal framework for critical 
infrastructure. For any upcoming sector-specific cybersecurity rules, we encourage 
member states to leverage Recital 22 of NIS2 Directive. Following the principle of this 
provision, NIS2 should be a baseline legal framework, and more specific sectoral rules, 
if necessary, should be introduced as implementing acts. 
 
The proposed EU-level “certificate of cyber posture” in the proposed revised 
Cybersecurity Act is a positive step to reduce administrative burden and compliance 
costs for companies (i.e. certifying once at EU level). However, we would like to see 
further and more immediate simplification of conformity assessments across all EU 
cybersecurity legislation (including CRA). 
 
 
DATA GOVERNANCE  
 
We welcome the Commission’s proposal to merge provisions from the Data Governance 
Act, Free Flow of Non-Personal Data Regulation, and Open Data Directive into the Data 
Act, creating a single, streamlined framework. It repeals outdated regulations while 
maintaining core principles such as the prohibition of data localisation. This will help 
simplify compliance by reducing fragmentation. However, we would like to outline the 
following concerns/need for clarification:

Cloud switching termination charges for fixed-term IaaS contracts - Art. 1(15) of 
Omnibus (ref. to Art. 31(1b) of the Data Act): 
 
We are concerned about some provisions that would significantly alter the regime 
for cloud switching. Indeed, Art.1 (15) of the Commission’s Omnibus proposal includes 
a provision into the Data Act setting that a cloud provider may include termination 
charges in contracts of fixed duration other than IaaS (Infrastructure-as-a-Service) 
contracts. However, recital 18 recognises that “for reasons relating to financial planning 
and attracting investment, providers of data processing services, especially SMEs and 
SMCs, may prefer and offer contracts of a fixed duration. It is necessary to clarify that 
providers of data processing services may include provisions on proportionate early 
termination penalties in those contracts as long as they do not constitute an obstacle to 
switching.” 
 
Yet the adopted Data Act clearly reaffirmed that nothing in the Regulation prevented the 
parties’ contractual freedom of agreeing on proportionate termination charges in line 
with Union or national law. This reflects common practice in B2B relationships, where 
enterprise customers understand and agree that committing to a fixed-term contract is 
generally more cost-efficient but entails termination charges if ended before the 
commitment ends. These charges are clearly stipulated in the contract upfront and 
hence agreed by clients and providers.  
 
Therefore, we encourage EU legislators not to use the Digital Omnibus Regulation to 
insert an arbitrary exception to contractual freedom. While the Digital Omnibus 
Regulation expressly states that it does “not intend to introduce changes to the 
achievements of the Data Act”, it substantially modifies the equilibrium reached during 
the negotiation of the Act. By including distinctions between IaaS, PaaS and SaaS 
providers, between SMEs and other providers, and including a category of custom-made 
cloud services (on top of the already existing custom-built services), not only does the 
Regulation create ambiguity and uncertainty but it also seeks to introduce different 
cloud switching regimes depending on the type of cloud service and cloud provider.  
 
Consequently, to remove this uncertainty and reinstate the achievements of the 
Data Act, it should be confirmed that termination fees can be charged by providers 
irrespective of the type of cloud contract (SaaS, PaaS or IaaS) in accordance with 
Union or national law, as set forth in the Data Act.

Trade secrets protection - Art. 1(3) of Omnibus (ref. to Art. 4(8) of the Data Act): 
 
We support the clarification on trade secrets safeguards, thereby protecting 
companies’ intellectual property in cross-border contexts. We would encourage EU co-
legislators to further reinforce trade secrets protection in these provisions, by 
including a genuine possibility to avoid the sharing of these valuable business secrets, 
and by clarifying that data should not be shared when it risks exposing cybersecurity 
vulnerabilities. 
 
 
GENERAL DATA PROTECTION REGULATION (GDPR)  
 
Legitimate interest - Art. 3 of Omnibus (ref. to Art 6(1) of GDPR) 
Definition of personal data - Art. 3 & 4 of Omnibus (ref. to Art 4 of GDPR) 
Automated decision-making - Art. 3(7) of Omnibus (ref. to Art 22 of GDPR) 
 
IBM supports the confirmation that businesses can rely on legitimate interest as a 
legal basis for the development of AI models and systems, as well as the clarification 
of the concepts of personal data (e.g., pseudonymised or aggregated customer data is 
not personal data). We therefore encourage EU co-legislators to support these 
provisions.  
 
We also believe that the proposed changes to Article 22 enabling automated decision-
making under safeguards will provide much needed flexibility and clarification to 
support the development of AI innovations like Agentic AI, particularly in a B2B 
context, for example when deploying AI-powered tools for Know-Your-Customer (KYC) 
verifications.  
 
Proposed exemptions to cookies’ consent rules - Art. 3(15) of Omnibus (ref. to new Art 
88a of GDPR) 
 
IBM welcomes the Commission’s decision to expand the list of exemptions of required 
cookies, where processing is lawful without consent, notably for low-risk purposes, 
such as data analytics. However, this exemption is only valid to the extent the 
“aggregated information about the usage of an online service to measure the audience of 
such a service is carried out by the controller of that online service solely for its own use”.  
This scope remains quite narrow and would not sufficiently benefit B2B companies 
 
7 
 
leveraging data analytics technologies, usually through third-party providers, to allow 
better data–driven decisions based on deep business customer insights.  
 
We would suggest further clarifying that this provision includes cases where the 
measurement is done by controllers via the use of third-party cookie providers 
acting as authorised third‑party processors that operate strictly under the 
controller’s instructions (i.e. for analytics activities that remain internal and data 
cannot be used by the third-party for its own purposes) 
 
Other proposed provisions IBM supports and proposes to strengthen include: 
• Further clarifying data subjects’ information rights and data access requests 
(Art. 3 of Omnibus, ref. to 15 of GDPR), to help reduce the amount of abusive 
data access requests and unjustified compensation claims. Ideally, the EU could 
further clarify this provision by requiring organisations to perform only 
"reasonable and proportionate searches”, when responding to data subjects’ 
requests. 
 
• Extend the simplified data‑breach notification process (Art. 3(8) of Omnibus, 
ref. to Art 33 of GDPR) to data processors, i.e. applying the same risk-based 
threshold to limit overreporting, and clarifying the conditions to notify “without 
undue delay, taking into account the nature and gravity of the incident.” In B2B 
contexts, applying the same standard across both roles would streamline 
procedures, reduce burden along the service supply chain, avoid duplication, and 
support faster, more consistent reporting. 
 
• Harmonising Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) processes (Art. 3(9) 
of Omnibus, ref. to Art. 35 of GDPR), with a single EU-level vs. different national 
lists to reduce administrative burden and fragmentation.

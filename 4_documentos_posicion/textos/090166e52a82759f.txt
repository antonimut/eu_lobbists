1. Introduction 
The European Commission digital omnibus package proposals1 are a broadly welcome 
milestone in the path towards a simpler and coherent EU digital regulatory framework2. 
Ibec3 shares the European Commission’s ambitions to streamline rules, reduce the regulatory 
burden on businesses operating in Europe, encourage innovation, and boost trusted technology 
deployment, all of which will contribute towards strengthening incentives to invest in the single 
market and the EU’s competitiveness and resilience. Ibec responded to the European 
Commission’s call for evidence on a digital package and omnibus4 in October 2025. 
The current EU digital framework presents real challenges for competitiveness. Without more 
ambitious simplification, particularly in cybersecurity and cross‑regime coordination, European 
businesses risk diverting extensive resources toward overlapping compliance obligations rather 
than innovation, resilience and growth. A bold and urgent simplification agenda is needed to 
maintain Europe’s global competitiveness. 
We urge EU policymakers to stay the course and strengthen this much-needed regulatory 
simplification agenda. The omnibus proposals take important steps towards addressing 
legitimate and long-standing demands from European Member States and business owners 
who want to innovate, grow, and lead globally, and should not be seen simplistically as yielding 
to external pressure. It is positive to see some flexibility given to the AI Act implementation in the 
proposal, along with efforts to simplify incident reporting obligations and to provide some legal 
clarity around GDPR interplay. These measures must be strengthened and fully implemented.  
This paper builds on Ibec’s October 2025 position and renewed Member State calls for 
regulatory simplification5. It is intended as a constructive contribution to the goal of regulatory

2. General Comments 
The priority of the simplification process must be to enable Member States and organisations to 
implement, harmonise and adapt to Europe’s complex legislative framework so that it delivers 
shared digital ambitions and competitiveness, avoiding increased scope, complexity or burden 
for businessi. 
To achieve this, any new regulation must be introduced only where regulatory gaps are proven 
and the benefits shown to outweigh the costs (including economic impact assessments). 
Where new rules are justified, they must be targeted, evidence-based, risk-based and 
proportionate way. The interplay between any proposed new laws and existing rules must work 
with, not against, innovation and the goal of EU competitiveness.  
A priority area for Irish business in the digital simplification process is the streamlining of 
reporting obligations (see Section 3). The European Commission and Council should task 
regulators with consulting on and developing procedures that assist compliance across 
intersecting rules and support EU competitiveness and regulatory simplicity. Guidance on EU 
legislation must be informed by meaningful stakeholder consultation and produce actionable 
and workable outcomes. Member States must ensure coherent application of all digital 
regulation (see principles in Section 3b) with risk-based enforcement. Industry involvement in 
the development of certification schemes and codes of practice6 must be the norm. The 
co‑legislators should enhance processes that improve legislative quality, engagement, and 
consistent implementation to avoid fragmentation of the Single Market.

3. Thematic legislative issues and recommendations 
a) Data 
Legislation: Data Act7 (DA) Data Governance Act8 (DGA), ePR9 and GDPR10 
Issues Recommendations

and clarity. 
1. Reinforce the security and trade secrets handbrakes in DA (Art. 4(8)) by (i) not limiting the trade secrets handbrake to a 
governance process but to a genuine protection; and (ii) expanding the security hand brake to explicitly include cybersecurity 
issues. Trade secrets protections should also enable providers to avoid disclosure of highly sensitive information when necessary 
to safeguard intellectual property, including in cross‑border contexts. Where users believe a refusal of access is unjustified and 
would cause them disproportionate harm, they should be able to contest it before independent dispute-settlement bodies or, 
ultimately, before the courts. 
2. Clarify the GDPR without reopening it. Use targeted amendments to streamline compliance requirements, foster trusted AI 
innovation and safeguard rights. 
3. Encourage data flows, encourage digital trade. Address any concerns with data transfers to third countries through multilateral 
solutions and the actual likelihood of risk. Clarify risk-based approach (Arts. 24 and 32 GDPR) applies also to measures for data 
transfers to (non-EU) third countries (Chapter V). Avoid unnecessary restrictions. We acknowledge the continued importance of 
adequacy, but it could be made simpler e.g., create a positive presumption for recognising intra-company transfers during general 
business when company has self-certified to appropriate safeguards.

4. Re-examine and align cookie exemptions11. ePR rules on cookies should be aligned with the risk based GDPR approach and the 
one stop shop model. Expand Art. 88(a) in GDPR to exempt low-risk non-profiling activities essential for the digital ecosystems 
e.g., contextual advertising and associated processing. Exemptions should expressly apply where analytics are performed by 
third‑party processors acting strictly under controller instructions and without independent use of data. Consider other legal 
bases for cookie use, to make rules more flexible and adaptable to different business models while safeguarding stakeholders and 
reducing consent fatigue. Consider expanded ‘whitelisting’ of low-risk essential activities (e.g., security monitoring, software 
updates, anti-fraud, first-party analytics and , advertising verification and measurement to support billing, frequency capping).

5. Delete (Article 88b) and focus on expanding Art. 88(a): Article 88b introduces profound economic uncertainty that threatens the 
viability of the ad-supported open web, disproportionately impacting EU publishers, retailers, and SMEs. It would also add a new 
mandatory consent signal on top of existing GDPR rules, creating further legal and technical complexity. Focus efforts on better 
incentivizing contextual advertising and exempting low-risk activities from consent banner requirements. 
6. Explicitly recognise proportionality and a risk-based approach in demonstrating compliance with GDPR12. Such recognition 
would offer controllers certainty, flexibility and consequently reduced burden in compliance. We welcome Omnibus proposals to 
simplify record keeping 13and help SMEs and SMCs 14. 
7. Improve data breach notification procedures (Art. 33 GDPR) – apply risk-based thresholds to limit overreporting, clarifying time 
limits15 and conditions to notify “without undue delay, taking into account the nature and gravity of the incident. " Clarify the SEP 
can be used to submit data breach reports, and that a single report could satisfy both data breach and cybersecurity incident 
reporting obligations. Prioritise aligning the reporting template for GDPR with the template to be developed for cybersecurity 
reporting. 
8. Support innovation and responsible AI. Confirm legitimate interest as a legal basis for the development of AI models and 
systems. Further enable legitimate interest for AI innovation16 and security17. The EDPB opinion18, while helpful, is limited. Remove 
language allowing Member States to potentially bypass the GDPR by mandating consent via national laws, which risks creating 27 
fragmented regulatory regimes19. A targeted clarification or a ‘white list’ that ensures legitimate interest can be relied on when data 
is processed with appropriate safeguards for: (a) product and service development and testing; and (b) security and cybersecurity 
measures would be welcome. Recognise privacy enhancing technologies as appropriate safeguards that can reduce the 
compliance burden.  
9. Clarify the use of pseudonymised and anonymised data, in line with recent CJEU work20, as to when it can be treated as non-
personal data21. Apply a risk-based approach in defining personal data. Ensure industry and stakeholder involvement in any 
further development of guidance.

1. Remove or reduce the requirement to register non-high-risk AI systems27. Most systems will not qualify as high risk in 
practice. This requirement is an administrative burden that impacts innovation/adoption and offers little in terms of risk 
management or transparency. 
2. Re-align the application of the AI Act timeline28with the delivery of key implementation tools. Align application of 
high‑risk requirements with the availability of key implementation tools, especially harmonised standards. Enforcement 
should proceed only after these tools are finalised to ensure legal certainty and avoid disproportionate impacts on 
SMEs. A firm and unified application date is necessary. The Omnibus should establish a single date of 2 August 2028 for 
both Annex I and Annex III systems, allowing adequate time for standards to be finalised and incorporated. In principle, 
proposed triggers for accelerating compliance should be subject to meaningful stakeholder consultation, have clearer 
conditions and not be activated hastily to reduce uncertainty. 
3. As a general principle, timelines for the implementation of the AI Act must be realistic. 
4. Ensure providers and deployers benefit from the proposed grace period on transparency. A grace period for 
enforcement of Article 50(2) is welcome but should be extended to all of Article 5029 for coherent implementation. 
Currently no grace period is provided to AI deployers that would need to disclose AI-generated content, even though AI-
marking may not be available at that time. Providers and deployers should also get adequate time to prepare 
for/implement the code of practice. Extend deadlines for transparency provisions (Art. 50) to align with the full scope of 
the currently negotiated Code of Practice (which will not be ready until mid-2026). To provide legal clarity, the Omnibus 
should extend the compliance period for Article 50 to at least one year (rather than a proposed six months). This 
extension should apply to new systems as well as those already on the market and must cover all relevant transparency 
obligations (Article 50(4) in addition to Article 50(2)) and require changes to Article 113 of AI Omnibus.

5. Transparency obligations for providers and deployers of certain AI systems. Article 50(2) of the AI Act requires 
providers of AI systems to clearly mark AI-generated content, including synthetic text, as artificial. No technically 
feasible, reliable and interoperable solution exists to ensure persistent, machine‑readable marking of AI-generated text. 
The Omnibus should address this unworkable obligation and the legal uncertainty it would create by removing “text” 
from the Article 50(2) marking list. 
6. Harmonise the AI Act and the MDR/IVDR through established sectoral legislation to provide legal clarity on 
obligations for the health sector.  We support the Commission’s proposal in the MDR/IVDR targeted revisions to move 
the MDR/IVDR from Section A of Annex I of the AI Act to Section B of Annex I of the AI Act, allowing AI requirements to be 
implemented through sectoral product frameworks. Implementing and delegated acts for the MDR/IVDR should only 
incorporate those AI Act obligations that are additional to existing obligations in the MDR/IVDR. We recommend 
including this proposal in the AI omnibus. Medical devices and products are planned and developed years in advance. 
The main issue is ensuring producers have legal clarity on exactly which additional AI Act obligations apply to their 
products and when. Ease of understanding around requirements and clarity on timelines is key, given how long the 
development process for medical devices is. 
7. Ensure coherence in application of the EU AI Act (and all digital regulation) at Member State level. Member states 
should complete the designation, staffing and resourcing of regulators as a matter of urgency, ensure relevant statutory 
deadlines are metand guidance is in place to inform and support compliance.  Enforcement should be aligned, 
proportionate and sequenced after timely guidance. Supporting compliance should be the regulators' priority, 
sequenced ahead of enforcement.  This will be particularly important for operators not regulated by the AIO. 
8. Ensure simplification is a dynamic process which involves monitoring any barriers to compliance so that these can be 
addressed in real time including as part of the implementation of new rules such as the AI Act. 
9. Ensure implementation of the GP-AI Code of Practice is proportionate, aligns with the AI Act scope and 
requirements and supports wide adoption across the EU’s digital economy. The Code should not create uncertainty in 
the supply chain or a de facto means to create further legislation. Avoid legal uncertainty and overburdening of 
downstream entities. Fine-tuning requirements should focus on risk and model report updates should be limited to only 
when modification makes the previous report incorrect. 
10.    Ensure coherence on legal basis of data processing between AI Act and GDPR. Support the legitimate interest base 
for AI training e.g., bias detection and mitigation. 
11. Enhance certainty. The Commission and co-legislators should adopt this proposal as quickly as possible, in any event 
before the existing AI Act deadlines take effect. If the co-legislators cannot adopt the full omnibus at speed, the 
Commission should de-couple the implementation/enforcement delays from the rest of the omnibus and fast-track 
their adoption (Virkkunen's Cabinet has suggested this is possible).

1. Ensure cyber simplification is delivered swiftly and that proposals are included in the Omnibus legislation 
and not tied to the EU Cybersecurity Act review process, as the latter is expected to take time to negotiate and 
implement. It is also likely to take effect after cyber legislation that is currently both entering into force and being 
simplified simultaneously (e.g. NIS2, CRA), and any further delays would add regulatory uncertainty and be a 
missed opportunity to drive meaningful simplification.  
2. Deliver a Single-Entry Point (SEP) for reporting that facilitates entities in scope of multiple legal incident 
reporting obligations to submit one report to be compliant with all applicable rules.  
o Clarify that vulnerability and incident reporting under the CRA are also covered by the SEP mechanism. 
More broadly, clarify how the SEP will operate in practice, including the role of national regulators in 
incident response. 
o Ensure that the creation of the SEP does not delay the introduction of the new ‘high-risk’ threshold and 
reporting timeline, or the development of the template. The SEP will require a secure, centralised IT system 
which will be complex to establish. This process should not delay these other aspects which will deliver 
significant immediate simplification for many companies. 
o Mandate Integration: Strengthen the provision to require that the single-entry point is fully integrated with, 
or built directly upon, the CRA platform. 
o Industry Consultation: Amend Article 23a(3) to explicitly include industry stakeholders in the consultation 
process regarding technical specifications and API standards. 
o Establish Feedback Loops: Establish a formal mechanism allowing reporting entities to flag issues 
regarding the functioning, reliability, or integrity of the single-entry point. 
3. Address overlap of audits and conformity assessments. Beyond streamlining incident reporting, meaningful 
simplification requires addressing duplication of audits and conformity assessments across NIS2, DORA, GDPR 
and the CRA. Entities currently face repeated and overlapping assessments that evaluate similar security controls,

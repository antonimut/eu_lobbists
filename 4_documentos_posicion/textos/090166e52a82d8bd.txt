About 
 
Technology Ireland 1 is the leading representative body for the technology sector in Ireland 
consisting of the ICT, Digital and Software industry. The Association is a proactive membership 
organisation with companies located throughout Ireland.  With origins dating back to 19 68, the 
Association was formed in 2017 by the merger of ICT Ireland and the Irish Software Association. 
We advocate on behalf of Ireland’s indigenous and foreign direct investment (FDI) technology 
companies to Government and policymakers. 
 
Overview 
 
The proposed Digital Omnibus acts as a starting point to simplify the landscape for businesses 
operating in Europe . The proposal broadly reflects legitimate and longstanding demands from 
European Member States and business owners, who want to innovate, grow, and lead globally.  
Technology Ireland  (TI) supports the European Commission’s ambition to promote the 
simplification agenda and to establish measures that are supportive of innovation, including AI. 
At the same time, the proposal in its current form fails to fully optimise simplification and address 
the competitiveness gap that is faced by the European Union  (EU), which requires further bold 
and urgent action to be taken. 
 
The Commission itself has stated that the aim of the Digital Omnibus is to “quickly reduce the 
burden on businesses2’” and the Draghi3 report clearly outlines the steps Europe needs to take to 
ensure it remains competitive and to promote regulatory coherence. Our  response outlines 
where industry believes further steps could achieve this aim.  
 
The simplification agenda that underpins the Digital Omnibus represents a crucial opportunity to 
ensure a prosperous and coherent regulatory landscape for Europe, embedding innovation as a 
core principle. To advance this agenda and leverage this opportunity, the EU must act with both 
urgency and impact beyond what is outlined in the current proposal. 
 
This paper outlines several recommendations across (i) Data Governance and Privacy, (ii) 
Cybersecurity, and (iii) Artificial Intelligence. Our core recommendations are summarised in the 
Executive Summary below.

Executive Summary 
 
Core recommendations 
Take an ambitious approach to simplification to drive EU competitiveness 
1. Act quickly, take bold action and maintain momentum 
2. Encourage greater accountability for co-legislators in the drafting and implementation of 
legislation to ensure harmonisation. 
3. Ensure simplification is a dynamic process . Monitor any barriers to compliance  and 
address them in real time as part of the implementation of new rules. 
4. Embed transparency and accountability, ensuring all national regulators and EU bodies 
align with a common framework . This will  improve the stability and predictability of 
regulation to support long term business decision-making and investment. 
5. Prioritise the coherent application of all digital regulation at the Member State Level with a 
risk-based enforcement approach. 
Enable meaningful stakeholder engagement 
6. Allow for meaningful stakeholder consultation when drafting Guidance on EU legislation. 
7. Ensure broad industry involvement in developing/participating in the development of 
certification schemes and Codes. 
Mandate innovation focused regulation 
8. Empower the European Commission and Council to task regulators with consulting on and 
developing procedures on intersecting areas of regulation that aid compliance. 
9. Deliver a clear mandate for regulators to promote innovation and explicitly require DPAs to 
be tasked with promoting a balanced approach supporting data protection and economic 
growth.

Advertising, Cookies and Consent rules: 
 
Legal base 
 
❖ Provide additional clarity on the rationale behind the design of Article 88(a) which 
entrenches consent as the legal base for cookies. 
Technology Ireland members would welcome additional clarity on the rationale behind the 
Commission’s design of Art. 88(a) which entrenches consent as the legal base for cookies. This 
does not achieve the primary policy goals which was to reduce consent fatigue for consumers 
and simplify rules for low-risk cookie uses. The Digital Omnibus proposal only partially migrates 
Art. 5(3) ePrivacy into the GDPR (Article 88 (a)) which splits the regulation of cookies. Those 
involving personal data would be regulated under the GDPR and those that do not remain under 
Art. 5(3). This further fragments key personal data processing rules across multiple frameworks, 
a situation that is more complex than today.  
 
❖ Delete Article 5(3) and revise Article 88 (a) to fully integrate with the GDPR to achieve 
legal certainty, regulatory simplicity, and sustainable privacy outcomes for all 
stakeholders. Further clarify that this provision includes cases where measurement is 
done by controllers via use of third -party c ookie providers acting as third -party 
authorised processors operating strictly under controller instructions. 
 
Deleting Art. 5(3) and revising Art. 88(a) to fully integrate with the GDPR is the most effective way 
to achieve legal certainty, regulatory simplicity , and sustainable privacy outcomes for all 
stakeholders.  
 
The proposed Omnibus includes just two new exemptions to cookie consent which we believe is 
a missed opportunity. Other essential and low risk  uses should be considered including fraud 
prevention, ad verification and measurement. Additionally, the proposed exemptions don't seem 
to apply to entities acting as third parties so don't work in complex supply chains like publishing 
and digital advertising where entities  must work collaboratively and rely on the same cookies 
(e.g. providers of audience measurement, fraud prevention or consent management services to 
publishers or advertisers).  Technology Ireland supports more specific exemptions from cookie 
consent rules as the current proposal does not include many low risk advertising-related uses. 
 
We also request further clarification that this provision includes cases where measurement is 
done by controllers via use of third -party cookie providers acting as third -party authorised 
processors operating strictly under controller instructions.

❖ Move ePrivacy Directive Article 5(3) to the GDPR to unify all cookie rules under one 
regime, the risk-based approach and one-stop-shop. 
 
Art. 5 of the Omnibus seems a good faith effort to offer some simplification for cookies which 
involve or lead to the processing of personal data but in practice , it would create a dual regime 
for cookies, with some moving to the GDPR regime while others remain under the ePrivacy 
Directive Art. 5(3). This would not simplify compliance, rather make it vastly more complex and 
not address the concern raised in the Explanatory Memorandum that “the dual regime of ePrivacy 
and General Data Protection Regulation [has] l ed to different national authorities being 
competent to supervise the rules of the two legal frameworks” as it was also rightly emphasised 
by the EDPB/EDPS joint Opinion on the Data and Privacy Omnibus. True simplification would be 
achieved by moving ePrivacy Directive Art. 5(3) to the GDPR so that all cookie rules are unified 
under one regime, the risk -based approach and one -stop-shop. This would remove the 
additional cost  of resourcing compliance across multiple regulators and allow providers to 
streamline their compliance in a single jurisdiction. 
 
One-click consent 
 
❖ Replace the provision regarding the one-click button, where consent is required, with a 
risk-based approach expanding Article 88(a)’s exemption to contextual advertising and 
low-risk activities removing the consent obligation for low -impact, non -intrusive 
activities (i.e., contextual advertising, third -party analytics, ad fraud prevention, and 
audience measurement). 
 
Technology Ireland believes that the one -click button , where consent is required,  
presents problems.  It is not technologically neutral, entrenches today's prescriptive EDPB 
guidance, creates a new layer of consent and would remove flexibility to modify guidance over 
time as technology changes.  This provision should be deleted. 
 
Instead, the Digital Omnibus should adopt a risk -based approach that expands Art. 88(a)’s 
exemption to contextual advertising  and low -risk activities. This would remove the consent 
obligation for low -impact, non -intrusive activities such as contextual advertising, third -party 
analytics, ad fraud prevention , and audience measurement. Lifting this requirement would 
simultaneously promote  privacy-preserving innovations and  practices while diminishing user 
consent fatigue by naturally reducing consent banners. This approach would ensure  consent is 
still required but reserved where user choice and control matter most – such as for personalised 
advertising. The Digital Omnibus should incentivise other types of non -intrusive first party 
activities, including the collection of data that is anonymised or uses privacy enhancing practices 
such as homomorphic encryption for data processing. 
 
This risk-based approach is also consistent with the approach taken by the EDPB/EDPS in their 
joint Opinion on the Data and Privacy Om nibus where they invite policymakers to exempt 
contextual advertising and its plumbing functions (fraud, capping and measurement) from 
consent.

Centralised consent interfaces  
 
❖ Delete Article 88 (b) as centralised consent controls are legally flawed, economically 
harmful, and counterproductive to simplification. 
 
Technology Ireland proposes the deletion of Art. 88(b) as centralised consent controls are legally 
flawed, economically harmful, and counterproductive to simplification. Mandating browser -
based consent intermediaries does not resolve the fundamental issue of third-party interference 
in the direct relationship between service providers and users.   We see significant unintended 
effects, including:  
 
• Economic Damage and Market Distortion : A centralised mechanism would enable 
users to inadvertently block critical measurement and advertising tools with a single 
click, severely undermining the revenue models that sustain the open web. This would 
disproportionately harm SMEs, who rely on digital advertising for customer acquisition, 
and force many publishers’ currently free services behind pay walls. 
 
• Failure to Reduce User Fatigue: Far from eliminating banners, this proposal would likely 
result in the “worst of both worlds”: a layer of centralised settings overlaid by persistent 
website banners. Since broad, centralised settings generally fail to meet the GDPR’s 
strict standards for “specific” and “informed”  consent, publishers would be forced to 
continue displaying their own consent interfaces to ensure legal compliance. 
 
• Legal Uncertainty: The proposal creates significant ambiguity regarding liability between 
interface providers and publishers, conflicting consumer choices (e.g., browser settings 
vs on-site preferences), and the validity of consent across complex supply chains. 
 
• Unfair Competition via Media Exemption: The proposed exemption for media providers 
aims to assist media owners but is unworkable  in practice particularly in complex 
advertising supply chains and would not achieve the intended beneficial outcome for 
media service providers. This carve-out is a band -aid solution that fails to address the

Incident reporting: 
 
❖ Standardise the reporting clock to 72 hours for all regulators covered by the SEP. 
Technology Ireland  welcomes the single-entry point (SEP) for incident reporting . We also

However, the current proposal on centralised incident reporting seems to only centralise the 
submission of reports rather than remove the requirement to prepare multiple reports for the

needs of several reporting obligations is a necessity. 
 
To operationalise the single entry point (SEP), the Omnibus should standardise the reporting 
clock to 72 hours for all regulators covered by the SEP . Currently, entities face divergent deadlines 
(e.g., 24 hours vs 72 hours) for the same incident, diverting resources from response to 
administrative tracking, as well as mandating incomplete and urged reports before the incident 
can be investigated properly. 
 
We also propose the deletion of the proposed 96-hour GDPR data breach disclosure in the Digital 
Omnibus to ensure it remains aligned with the 72-hour standardised reporting timeline.

❖ Include the aligned concept of ‘main establishment’ (CRA/NIS2 vs GDPR/DSA) and

Technology Ireland supports the inclusion of the below points in the Omnibus rather than in the

(iii) Artificial Intelligence (AI) 
 
AI Act16: 
 
While the AI Act proposal confirms that the AI Office (AIO) will also oversee the high -risk regime 
for VLOPS, it still lacks a clear mandate for regulators at both EU and national levels to promote 
innovation. This is an ongoing barrier across the different pieces of legislation regulating digital 
technologies and if not addressed, will hamper inn ovation, investments and ultimately - 
competitiveness. 
 
❖ Ensure coherence in application of the EU AI Act (and all digital regulation) at Member 
State level.  
 
Member States should complete the designation, staffing and resourcing of regulators as a 
matter of urgency, ensure relevant statutory deadlines are met and guidance is in place to inform 
and support compliance. Enforcement should be aligned, proportionate and seq uenced after 
timely guidance. Supporting compliance should be the regulators' priority, sequenced ahead of 
enforcement. This will be particularly important for operators not regulated by the AI Office (AIO). 
 
❖ Adopt the AI Omnibus proposal as soon as possible before the existing AI Act deadlines 
take effect. If this cannot be achieved, decouple the implementation/enforcement 
delays from the rest of the Omnibus to fast-track their adoption. 
 
Technology Ireland agrees with the European Commission that co -legislators should adopt this 
proposal as quickly as possible, and before the existing AI Act deadlines take effect.  If the co -
legislators cannot adopt the full Omnibus to achieve this at speed, the Commission should de -
couple the implementation/enforcement delays from the rest of the Omnibus and fast-track their 
adoption.  
 
❖ Remove or reduce the requirement to register non-high-risk AI systems17.  
 
Most systems will not qualify as high risk in practice. This requirement is an administrative burden 
that impacts innovation  and adoption and offers little in terms of risk management or 
transparency. Art. 49 of the AI Act requires providers to register AI systems in an EU database, 
regardless of whether they are ultimately classified as high-risk.

❖ Amend Art . 50(2) to remove ‘text’ from the content list required to be marked as AI 
generated to reflect the current lack of technically feasible, reliable and interoperable

Art. 50(2) of the EU AI Act requires providers of AI systems to clearly mark AI -generated content 
as artificial, including synthetic text. This is not supported by the current technological state of 
the art. No technically feasible, reliable and interoperable solutions exist to ensure persistent, 
machine-readable marking of AI-generated text. Retaining this obligation would therefore create 
legal uncertainty and impose requirements that cannot be effectively complied with, contrary to 
the Regulation’s own feasi bility and proportionality safeguards. As such, we propose the AI 
Omnibus amends this article by removing “text” from the content list. 
 
❖ Re-align the application of the AI Act timeline  with the delivery of key implementation 
tools.  
As per Art. 6 of the AI Act, high -risk AI systems must comply with the EU AI Act starting August 
2026. However, the development of harmonised standards needed to demonstrate compliance 
is delayed and may not be fully available until late 2026. There is significant concern on whether 
it realistic to expect enterprise to meet the current requirements on time.  The current structure 
of Art. 113 creates shifting goalposts, undermining the regulatory certainty required for effective 
compliance planning. 
Technology Ireland members recommend aligning the application of high‑risk requirements with 
the availability of key implementation tools, especially harmonised standards. Enforcement 
should proceed only after these tools are finalised to ensure legal certainty and avoid 
disproportionate impacts on SMEs. A firm and unified a pplication date is necessary. The 
Omnibus should establish a single date of 2 August 2028 for both Annex I and Annex III systems, 
allowing adequate time for standards to be finalised and  incorporated. In principle, proposed 
triggers for accelerating compliance should be subject to meaningful stakeholder consultation, 
have clearer conditions and not be activated hastily to reduce uncertainty.

Conclusion 
 
Our position contains three primary sections addressing (i) Data Governance and Privacy, (ii) 
Cybersecurity, and (ii) Artificial Intelligence. Our key recommendations are summarised in the 
Executive Summary at the beginning of this paper. 
 
Technology Ireland and its members urge EU policymakers to stay the course and to strengthen 
the simplification agenda through the Digital Omnibus. Further to the recommendations outlined 
above, Technology Ireland recommends that the EU follows three overarching principles – 1) take 
an ambitious approach to simplification to drive EU competitiveness, 2) enable meaningful 
stakeholder engagement, and 3) mandate innovation focused regulation.

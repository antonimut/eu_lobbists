egta – Position Paper on the Digital Omnibus 
 
egta is the international trade body of multiplatform TV and audio businesses. We represent 
more than 180 members in over 40 markets. Our mission is to contribute to the commercial 
sustainability of the media industry. We enable our members to flourish as they  connect 
brands with audiences. Through our unique network of experts, we foster collaboration and 
engage with policy makers. We drive the industry forward through cooperation, benchmarking 
and alignment on standards. egta is a knowledge-sharing and innovation hub for its members 
and the broader industry. 
egta welcome s the European Commission’s initiative to simplify and modernis e the EU’s 
digital framework through the Digital Omnibus Proposal. A lighter and more coheren t 
framework has the potential to reduce administrative and compliance burdens, while 
improving competitiveness for the European audiovisual industry. 
However, we would like to underline the need to ensure proportionality, coherence and clarity 
when proposing changes to existing legislations. Any amendments must balance innovation, 
business sustainability and user trust, particularly in the context of the growing dominance of 
big tech platforms. 
 
I. Personal Data Processing: Simplification Without Compromising Workability 
Legal s implification should not come at the  cost of clarity  and additional technical and 
commercial challenges . The ePrivacy framework require s a revision to properly address 
ambiguities, outdated considerations  and excessive compliance burdens . The current 
interpretation of cookie consent rules under Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive  is 
responsible for causing ‘consent fatigue’ among users, as well as a fragmented 
implementation and enforcement across Member States. Changes are needed to improve 
user experience and cut administrative costs. 
The Digital Omnibus Proposal explicitly recognises the issue of ‘consent fatigue and 
proliferation of cookies banners’ and the need for ‘a regulatory solution’ in its explanatory 
memorandum. While Recital 44  acknowledges the necessity to define a ‘limitative list of 
purposes for which the processing should be permitted without consent’ when it comes to ‘low 
risk’ processing activities to reduce the compliance burden and provide legal clarity to 
controllers, the exemptions in Article 88a(3) are too restrictive. More low risk uses need to 
be recognised to enable the proper functioning of media services.  The UK Information 
Commissioner’s Office (ICO) Guidance on the use of storage and access technologies could 
serve as a useful consideration in this regard. 
Article 88a  of the Digital Omnibus Proposal  creates a rigid system that jeopardise s the 
media’s ability to rely on the processing of personal data, positioning ‘consent’ as the sole 
possible legal basis. Clearly defined additional exemptions and alternative legal bases would 
be more appropriate. 
Other low-risk processing activities that would not de facto require consent from users , for 
example core service delivery and access management (login, updates, service maintenance, 
accessibility features...) , user experience (first -party analytics) and advertising processing 
(frequency capping, contextual advertising), should thus be explicitly acknowledged. As such,

Article 88a(3) could introduce a new subpoint (e) “Providing low-risk processing activities as 
per Guidelines to be issued by the Commission after consultation with national regulatory 
authorities and relevant stakeholders.” 
Article 88a(4) also introduces challenging amendments, making the legal basis of consent 
unreliable. Restrictions on consent requests should be removed or at the very least relaxed, 
as it is crucial for media companies to be able to engage continuously with audiences. As 
such, a six-month ban on follow -up requests as provided in Article 88a(4)(c) would have a 
negative effect on media companies,  restricting their capacity to engage in a trusted and 
regular dialogue with their audiences.  This ban would be particularly problematic in cases 
where there has been a change in service that should be covered by consent, or where, for 
example, the provider concerned has decided to introduce a Pay or Consent system. The 
capacity to refuse consent requests via a single click as per Article 88a(4)(a)  is also 
problematic for media service providers, especially for providers who financially rely on a Pay 
or Consent solution. If such a ban were to be maintained, we would suggest reducing it (e.g. 
2 months) to allow for consent request at proportionate intervals. 
We therefore recommend deleting Article 88a(4) paragraphs (a) and (c) , as well as  
adding Article 88a(3)(e) and amending Recital 44 as per below:  
Article 88a  
(3) Storing of personal data, or gaining of access to personal data already stored, in the terminal 
equipment of a natural person without consent, and subsequent processing, shall be lawful to 
the extent it is necessary for any of the following: 
(...) 
(e) Providing low -risk processing activities as per Guidelines to be issued by the 
Commission after consultation with national regulatory authorities and relevant 
stakeholders. 
 
(4) Where storing of personal data, or gaining of access to personal data already stored, in the 
terminal equipment of a natural person is based on consent, the following shall apply: 
(a) the data subject shall be able to refuse requests for consent in an easy and intelligible 
manner with a single-click button or equivalent means; 
(b) (...) 
(c) if the data subject declines a request for consent, the controller shall not make a new 
request for consent for the same purpose for a period of at least six months. 
 
Recital 44 paragraph 3 
“With a view to reducing the compliance burden and providing legal clarity to controllers, and 
given that certain purposes of processing pose a low risk to the rights and freedoms of data 
subjects or that such processing may be necessary to provide a service requested by the data 
subject, it is necessary to define a limitative list of purposes for which the processing should be

permitted without consent. Such low risk processing include: core service delivery and 
access management (login, updates, service maintenance, accessibility features...), user 
experience (first -party analytics) and advertising processing (frequency capping, 
contextual advertising ). As regards storing of personal data, or the gaining of access to

II. Audience Measurement: Essential for Media 
Among the low-risk exemptions, audience measurement in Article 88a(3)(c) stands out as a 
priority for  the sustainability of the media sector.  Media service providers depend on 
meaningful and comprehensive measurement to understand their audiences, but equally for 
their financing through advertising, which is at the core of their funding. This is what enables 
them to reach users with news, as well as to innovate, protect their intellectual property, and 
compete effectively with gatekeeping platforms.  
However, the current wording in Article 88a(3) would cause significant harm to the audience 
measurement ecosystem , limiting the exemption to first party controllers only. This 
fundamentally ignores how audience measurement works, which is via authorised and trusted 
third parties. 
We therefore suggest introducing the following amendment: 
Article 88a  
(3) Storing of personal data, or gaining of access to personal data already stored, in the terminal 
equipment of a natural person without consent, and subsequent processing, shall be lawful to 
the extent it is necessary for any of the following: (...) 
(c) measuring the audience of an online service in order to creat eing aggregated 
information about the usage of an online service to measure the audience of such a service, as 
defined by article 2(16) of Regulation 2024/1083,  where it is carried out by the controller of 
that online service , solely for its own use,  or entitled third part ies jointly authorised by 
advertisers and publishers, solely for the measurement of the overall market 
performance; 
Recital 44 paragraph 3 
“(...) The controller, such as a media service provider, may mandate a processor or an 
authorised third party, such as a market research company and Joint Industry Committee, 
to carry out the processing on its behalf. (...)”

III. Centralised Consent: Risks for Media Sustainability 
Article 88b introduces browser-level centralised consent, placing further control in the hands 
of browser manufacturers leading to significant intermediation risks and depriving smaller 
players of any control over their services.  Offering more control and agency to gatekeepers 
would directly go against the established goals of the Digital Markets Act.  
Moreover, the proposed mechanism does not meet GDPR criteria, not least the requirement 
for ‘specific, informed and unambiguous’ consent, as the introduction of a centralised consent 
mechanism would prevent users from expressing such consent. Furthermore, this would lead 
to lower opt-in rates, coercing publishers to convert their services from “free” to “paid” in order 
to sustain their ecosystem. 
While we welcome and appreciate the introduction of a ‘media exemption’, this carve -out 
would be technically impossible to implement. A further consequence of the media exemption 
would be increased user scrutiny of media owners’ websites as they would be the only pages 
displaying cookie banners.  
Efficient consent should remain decentralised and service -specific in order to ensure 
transparency, accountability, and proportionate control, while offering a level-playing field for 
all actors in the digital ecosystem. Browser-level centralised consent would be a disservice to 
the digital ecosystem, instead introducing additional legal uncertainty, dependence on already 
dominant players and additional compliance burdens that come with it, as well as severe 
competition issues. 
For these reasons, we recommend that the co-legislators delete Article 88b, or at the very 
least substantially amend it to make it workable, acknowledging the abovementioned 
difficulties that would otherwise negatively affect the digital ecosystem. 
Should Article 88b not be deleted, the media exception becomes indispensable for ensuring 
a minimum level of protection for the financing of media services, provided that its 
shortcomings are addressed and market realities are taken into account.  
 
IV. Conclusion 
The European Commission’s aim to reduce unnecessary regulatory burdens while maintaining 
a high level of data protection is  a significant step forward that we welcome, and could help 
provide media service providers with greater legal clarity and certainty. 
However, in their current form, Articles 88a and 88b fall short of these objectives, and risk 
producing unintended consequences that would undermine the sustainability of the media 
sector rather than alleviate its compliance burden . In particular, the lack of adequate 
exemption for low-risk data processing activities, combined with the introduction of centralised 
consent mechanisms, would have serious implications for the media financing model, 
audience measurement, and the ability of media services to enga ge meaningfully with their 
audiences. 
Media services play a unique societal role by financing entertainment, trusted news and 
journalism, contributing to European cultural diversity and democratic engagement. We 
believe that, through more dialogue and consultation with stakeholders, it is possible to reach 
a genuine and legally sound simplification that would benefit the media sector’s sustainability 
and competitiveness.

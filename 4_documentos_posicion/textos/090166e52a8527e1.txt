⁠⁠⁠⁠⁠⁠⁠ Cigref’s views on the Digital Package and Omnibus initiative 
 
1. Introduction 
Cigref, representing 150 leading French companies and public administrations, welcomes 
the publication of the Digital Package and Omnibus Initiative on 19 November 2025. 
We broadly support the Commission’s objective to simplify and streamline the European 
digital acquis, ensure consistent application across Member States, and strengthen legal 
clarity. Our members, as major users of digital services, experience daily the operational 
and strategic consequences of fragmented, overlapping, and sometimes inconsistent EU 
digital regulations. 
The central purpose of this package must remain clear throughout the legislative process: 
to strengthen European competitiveness by simplifying obligations and reducing legal 
uncertainty within the internal market and towards third States laws.  
Removing overlaps and contradictions is essential to lower compliance costs and 
administrative burdens, which otherwise undermine Europe’s capacity to innovate and 
compete globally. 
While we welcome many of the proposed improvements, further clarifications and 
adjustments are necessary. 
 
2. A regulation serving European competitiveness 
In a context of intensifying international competition, Europe must reaffirm a clear ambition: 
to strengthen the competitiveness of its businesses and economy by supporting those that 
create value, innovation and jobs. 
Regulation must be a strategic instrument for competitiveness, not a structural constraint. 
A more proportionate and predictable regulatory environment is essential to: 
• Reinforce Europe’s attractiveness for investment 
• Enable large-scale digital transformation 
• Reduce cumulative compliance costs 
• Provide legal certainty for long-term planning and innovation. 
The cumulative and sometimes overlapping obligations stemming from the AI Act, the Data 
Act, the GDPR, NIS2, DORA, the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), and sectoral legislation risk 
maintaining high compliance costs and legal uncertainty.

We call for: 
• A coherent and comprehensive approach across digital legislation; 
• Proportionate, B2B-adapted regulation; 
• Strong safeguards against abusive practices that exploit technological 
dependencies (DMA, Data Act, competition law). 
Simplification must remain the guiding principle. Digital regulation should be a lever for EU 
competitiveness, not an obstacle to innovation and growth. 
 
3. AI Act – General Comments on the Omnibus Proposal 
We welcome several proposed improvements to the AI Act, including: 
• Requalifying AI literacy requirements as a best practice rather than a strict 
regulatory obligation; 
• Removing the obligation to register certain exempted high-risk AI systems in the EU 
database; 
• Extending transitional periods for compliance with high-risk AI system requirements; 
• Introducing procedural improvements for notified bodies; 
• Replacing mandatory post-market monitoring templates with guidance; 
• Creating a legal basis for real-world testing under Annex I, Section B; 
• Centralising oversight for certain GPAI-model-based systems; 
• Enabling EU-level regulatory sandboxes. 
These measures move in the right direction and should be preserved. 
 
Some provisions require more legal clarity:   
• Many of our members have already made significant investments in compliance  
and governance structures to adapt to the IA Act. The AI Act remains fully applicable 
pending adoption of the Omnibus amendments. Companies are therefore required 
to continue compliance efforts under the current framework  and s imultaneously 
anticipate potential regulatory changes.  This creates operational and legal 
uncertainty. We therefore call on the Parliament and Council to accelerate the 
adoption of the AI Omnibus proposals to ensure legal and operational certainty.  To 
facilitate the process, t imeline postponements should be urgently adopted as a 
standalone proposal to ensure legal certainty well before August 2026. 
 
• The milestone of 2 August 2026 , triggering high-risk AI obligations under Annex III 
and Annex I , depends critically on the availability of harmonised standards.  Yet

standardisation timelines are slipping and several standards are expected only after 
formal entry into force.  This creates significant legal uncertainty and 
implementation bottlenecks. The proposed targeted postponements are therefore 
justified and necessary. 
 
• The proposed dual -trigger enforcement delay (default extension combined with 
Commission acceleration power) creates a moving target.  To ensure predictability 
for planning, budgeting and implementation, the Omnibus should  establish firm 
application dates for high -risk systems  and thus a void compliance timelines 
dependent on future administrative decisions. 
 
• The structural issue of parallel application of high-risk AI requirements and sectoral 
rules remains unresolved and should be addressed.  
 
• We support strengthening the AI Office’s enforcement role, including for AI systems 
provided by GPAI model providers and VLOPs and VLOSEs under the Digital Services 
Act. However, centralisation must be accompanied by structured coordination with 
national and sectoral regulators . Business associations with recognised expertise 
should also be meaningfully involved . Overlapping enforcement between the 
Commission and national authorities must be avoided. 
 
• For AI systems used exclusively within a corporate group, information and 
documentation requirements should be alleviated, as responsibilities can be 
assumed internally without duplicative ex-ante documentation. 
 
4. Cybersecurity  
We welcome the objective of simplifying cybersecurity -related reporting obligations.  
Businesses currently face significant burdens due to overlapping obligations under  GDPR, 
NIS2, DORA, eIDAS, CRA, CER and sectoral legislation 
In the perspective of streamlining the process, we support a single EU-level entry point for 
major ICT incident reporting. 
To achieve real simplification, we recommend to : 
• extend the single-entry point to all relevant regimes, including the AI Act; 
• rely on ENISA’s CRA reporting platform rather than creating parallel systems; 
• establish one harmonised core incident-reporting template; 
• align reporting timelines across frameworks. 
The current layered model , based on the GDPR 72 -hour rule and supplemented by early 
warnings and follow-ups, diverts resources from incident response to compliance.

Greater simplification requires one notification satisfying all relevant obligations. 
Given the overlap between CRA requirements and AI Act, compliance with the CRA should 
constitute sufficient compliance with AI Act Article 15 . Parallel assessments and 
documentation should be avoided. 
Reporting obligations must reflect operational realities, including  weekend incidents; Clear 
definitions of “known incident”; Authorities’ capacity constraints. 
Without adequate responsiveness from authorities, simplification risks remaining purely 
formal. 
 
5. Data Act and Data Framework 
We support: 
• Consolidation of the Open Data Directive, Data Governance Act and Free Flow of 
Non-Personal Data Regulation within the Data Act; 
• Replacement of “exceptional need” with the more precise “public emergency” 
threshold for B2G data sharing; 
In line with the objective of protecting European businesses against risks from third 
countries, we welcome the clarification of exceptions related to trade secrets in Article 4 of 
the Data Act. The new Article 4(8) rightly provides that a data holder may r efuse to share 
a trade secret in cases of high risk of unlawful disclosure, particularly towards third 
countries with weaker protection standards than the EU. 
Definitions across the AI Act, Data Act and Data Governance Act must be reviewed and 
aligned. For example, the concept of “data space” is inconsistent and insufficiently defined. 
The proposed changes to cloud switching risk reintroducing ambiguity by: 
• Distinguishing between IaaS, PaaS and SaaS; 
• Differentiating between SMEs and other providers; 
• Introducing new service categories. 
To restore legal certainty, we recommend confirming that proportionate termination fees 
may be charged regardless of service model, in line with Union or national law as 
reaffirmed in the adopted Data Act. 
The success of the Digital Omnibus depends on  a c lear allocation of responsibilities 
between the AI Office, ENISA, national authorities and sectoral regulators . It is also 
important to avoid the overlapping mandates and penalty systems . A s tructured 
coordination among market surveillance authorities would also be important. 
We welcome the inclusion of correlation tables clarifying amended, repealed and 
maintained provisions. This approach should become standard in future simplification 
initiatives.

6. GDPR Provisions 
We support codifying CJEU case law in the definition of personal data. This may strengthen 
the link with the Data Act and improve predictability, provided drafting ensures futureproof 
clarity. 
We also support : 
• New Article 41a on pseudonymisation implementing acts; 
• A more unified, risk-based approach; 
• Clarification of interactions with AI Act, cybersecurity and sectoral legislation. 
The overlap between  GDPR Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIA)  and the AI Act 
Fundamental Rights Assessments (FRA) creates duplication. 
We recommend: 
• Allowing AI providers to determine whether a DPIA sufficiently covers FRA 
requirements; 
• Harmonising DPIA processes at EU level (single EU list rather than national lists). 
The AI Act requires bias validation potentially involving access to personal data, while GDPR 
may restrict such processing. Greater convergence between both frameworks is necessary 
to avoid regulatory contradiction. 
 
7. Conclusion 
Cigref strongly supports the Commission’s ambition to simplify and rationalise Europe’s 
digital regulatory framework. 
The Digital Omnibus must: 
• Reduce cumulative compliance burdens; 
• Eliminate overlaps and contradictions; 
• Ensure legal certainty and predictability; 
• Strengthen Europe’s competitiveness in a challenging global environment. 
Simplification must remain the guiding principle throughout the legislative process. 
Cigref stands ready to continue contributing constructively to this work.

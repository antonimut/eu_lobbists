3.  High-risk  AI  classiﬁcation:  Risk  of  overly  broad  scope  
The  current  framework  under  Article  6  risks  capturing  a  large  number  of  AI  systems  that  do  
not,

pose
 
a

In

Annex
 
III

if

proﬁling
 
of

.
 
This

may
 
lead
 
to

of
 
systems
 
whose

on
 
individuals
 
is
 
limited
 
or
 
incidental.
 
The  Digital  Omnibus  on  AI  does  not  address  this  issue.  In  parallel,  the  continued  delay  in  the  
Commission’s
 
guidance
 
on
 
high-risk
 
AI
 
classiﬁcation
 
leaves
 
companies
 
in
 
a
 
prolonged
 
state
 
of
 
uncertainty
 
as
 
to
 
whether,
 
and
 
to
 
what
 
extent,
 
their
 
AI
 
systems
 
will
 
fall
 
within
 
the
 
high-risk
 
3  
 
category.  This  uncertainty  complicates  compliance  planning  and  may  lead  to  overly  
conservative
 
classiﬁcations,
 
with
 
limited
 
additional
 
protection
 
for
 
individuals.
 
Recommendation:  
To  ensure  a  proportionate  and  genuinely  risk-based  application  of  the  AI  Act,  Wolt  
recommends
 
that
 
Article
 
6
 
be

to

high-risk
 
classiﬁcation

of
 
proﬁling.
 
 
The  framework  should  clearly  distinguish  between  proﬁling  that  produces  legal  or  similarly  
signiﬁcant
 
effects

individuals

limited
 
or
 
incidental
 
operational

Aligning  the  concept  of  high-risk  proﬁling  with  established  EU  data  protection  principles,  in

“legal
 
or

  Taking  into  account  the  wide  range  of  AI  systems  used  in  day-to-day  business  operations,  as

Otherwise,

and
 
operational
 
efﬁciency
 
without
 
delivering
 
commensurate
 
beneﬁts
 
for
 
individuals.
 
4.  High-risk  AI  obligations:  Limited  practical  workability  and  lack  of  simpliﬁcation  
Once  an  AI  system  is  classiﬁed  as  high-risk,  the  AI  Act  imposes  a  wide  range  of  obligations  on  
providers,
 
including
 
requirements
 
relating
 
to
 
risk
 
management,
 
human
 
oversight,
 
robustness,
 
data
 
governance,
 
and
 
quality
 
management
 
systems.
 
Many
 
of
 
these
 
obligations
 
remain
 
vaguely
 
deﬁned
 
or
 
difﬁcult
 
to
 
apply
 
in
 
practice
.
 
The
 
Digital
 
Omnibus
 
on
 
AI
 
does
 
not
 
address
 
these
 
structural

As

even
 
where
 
timelines
 
are
 
adjusted,
 
the
 
underlying
 
complexity
 
and
 
ambiguity
 
of
 
the
 
high-risk
 
obligations
 
remain
 
unchanged.
 
Risk  management:  Providers  are  required  to  identify,  assess,  and  mitigate  risks  to  fundamental  
rights.
 
However,
 
the
 
AI
 
Act
 
does
 
not
 
provide
 
a
 
structured
 
methodology
 
or
 
practical
 
tools
 
for
 
conducting
 
such
 
assessments.
 
In
 
the
 
absence
 
of
 
clear
 
templates
 
or
 
guidance,
 
companies
 
may
 
4  
 
default  to  overly  defensive  compliance  strategies  or  delay  deployment  altogether,  with  limited  
added
 
protection
 
for
 
individuals.
 
Human  oversight:  The  AI  Act  sets  ambitious  expectations  for  human  oversight  of  high-risk  
systems,
 
requiring
 
that
 
humans
 
are
 
able
 
to
 
understand,
 
monitor,
 
and
 
intervene
 
in
 
system

where

In
 
practice,

many

operate
 
at

level

or

that

or

human

unrealistic.
 
A

would

on
 
transparency,

and
 
well-deﬁned
 
escalation
 
and
 
feedback
 
mechanisms
.
 
The

and

Robustness  and  resilience:  The  requirement  that  high-risk  AI  systems  be  “as  resilient  as

This

Quality  management  systems:  Quality  management  system  (QMS)  requirements  apply

This

Such

risks

from

risk

and
 
responsible
 
iteration.
 
Recommendation:  
To  ensure  that  the  AI  Act  can  be  implemented  effectively  in  practice,  Wolt  believes  that  the

to

 ○  Compliance  expectations  be  technically  feasible  and  proportionate ,  taking  into  account

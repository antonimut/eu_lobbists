 EFPIA position paper on the Digital Omnibus  I. Introduction EFPIA and its members are committed to advancing patient care through responsible innovation, underpinned by high standards of data protection, ethical research and regulatory compliance. Data is a critical enabler of pharmaceutical research, development and manufacturing, as well as of emerging AI-driven tools that support discovery, clinical development and healthcare delivery. As highlighted in Mario Draghi’s report on The Future of European Competitiveness, Europe is currently under-utilising data as a strategic asset. The report identifies fragmented data governance, legal uncertainty and overlapping regulatory requirements as key barriers to innovation, particularly in data-intensive and highly regulated sectors such as life sciences.  In this context, EFPIA welcomes the European Commission’s Digital Omnibus package as a timely opportunity to improve coherence across the EU digital acquis, including the GDPR, the AI Act and the Data Act. While the Omnibus is presented as a technical alignment exercise, it introduces several substantive changes that will materially affect how data is used for scientific research, AI development and digital health. This paper outlines EFPIA’s positions on key components of the Digital Omnibus proposal and identifies areas requiring further refinement. II. General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) A. Definitions  1. Personal data EFPIA welcomes the incorporation into Article 4(1) GDPR of the principles established by the SRB judgment (Case C-413/23 P), in which the Court of Justice of the European Union clarified the concept of personal data. In that case, the Court confirmed that whether information qualifies as personal data depends on the circumstances of the processing and the entity’s ability to identify the data subject, rather than being an absolute characteristic of the data itself. Specifically, in the context of the transfer of pseudonymised information, the Court held that pseudonymised data may continue to be personal data for the controller who retains additional information enabling re-identification, but may cease to be personal data for a recipient that cannot reasonably identify individuals from that data. This clarification represents an important shift towards a risk-based and contextual assessment of identifiability, ensuring that GDPR obligations apply where there is a genuine and realistic risk of identification, rather than on the basis of hypothetical possibilities. It directly addresses long-standing legal uncertainty affecting complex data-sharing environments, including those typical of pharmaceutical research, development and manufacturing activities and reinforces the approach confirmed by the Court of Justice in the SRB case: namely that identifiability must be assessed in light

of the actual means reasonably likely to be used and the real-world likelihood of reidentification. Concerns about potential misuse do not undermine this logic, as information will qualify as personal data again as soon as an entity has the realistic ability to reidentify individuals, thereby reactivating GDPR protections. Likewise, the notion of “singling out” should be understood in line with the GDPR’s wording (singling out “to identify the natural person”: in which case the data are indeed personal data), not any singling out. 2) Pseudonymisation and Anonymisation EFPIA welcomes the proposal to empower the Commission to adopt implementing acts to the GDPR specifying means and criteria to assess when data resulting from pseudonymisation no longer constitutes personal data for certain entities. This initiative has the potential to significantly improve legal certainty, harmonisation and operational consistency across the EU. For this mechanism to be effective in practice, EFPIA stresses that the resulting means and criteria must be workable, proportionate and grounded in real-world data processing scenarios. Additionally, the Commission’s mandate should not be limited to pseudonymised data. The assessment of whether data is personal or anonymous for a given entity arises in a variety of technical and organisational contexts. Implementing acts should therefore explicitly cover all situations in which data may be considered anonymous for certain entities, regardless of the technique used, in line with the contextual approach endorsed by the SRB judgment. Additionally, the development of implementing acts should involve meaningful engagement with relevant stakeholders, in addition to the EDPB. In this context, EFPIA underlines the importance of aligning the timing of the issuance of the amended rules with the European Health Data Space implementation timeline, in order to ensure a consistent and coherent approach to anonymisation and pseudonymisation across EU data frameworks. Finally, EFPIA considers it essential to clarify that processing activities undertaken for the sole purpose of pseudonymisation or anonymisation do not require a separate or new legal basis, nor a derogation under Article 9 GDPR. These activities constitute core risk-mitigation and data-protection measures and should be actively encouraged. All other GDPR obligations, including security, governance and safeguards, would continue to apply in full. Taken together, these clarifications would support responsible data use while maintaining a high level of protection for individuals. B. Scientific Research 1) Definition  EFPIA welcomes the introduction of a broad definition of scientific research that explicitly recognises technological development, innovation-oriented research and research conducted in both academic and industrial settings. To ensure future-proof and consistent application, EFPIA recommends clarifying that the concept of scientific research includes the training, testing, validation and evaluation of AI systems and digital health applications. These activities are integral to pharmaceutical R&D and are already recognised under the European Health Data Space framework. Explicit clarification is necessary to avoid divergent interpretations and unintended barriers to AI-enabled research. 2) Compatibility of further processing for scientific research EFPIA welcomes the proposed amendment to Article 5(1)(b) GDPR confirming that further processing for scientific research purposes is considered compatible with the initial purpose, independent of the conditions set out in Article 6(4) GDPR. This clarification strengthens legal certainty by codifying the

principle of default compatibility for scientific research and addressing inconsistent supervisory practices. It is a critical enabler of secondary use of data for research, subject to the safeguards provided for under Article 89 GDPR.Additionally, it should be clarified that where health data has been lawfully collected for a primary purpose, it can be further processed for scientific research without requiring a new legal basis under Article 6 GDPR or a new derogation under Article 9 GDPR, provided that Article 89(1) safeguards are applied. For example, data collected in clinical trials or healthcare settings may be reused for secondary research such as safety analyses or methodological studies, without duplicative legal requirements that do not enhance data subject protection. EFPIA also proposes for Article 9(2)(j) GDPR to explicitly recognise codes of conduct approved under Article 40 GDPR as a valid basis for the scientific research derogation. Codes of conduct provide a robust, supervised and harmonised mechanism to operationalise safeguards under Article 89 GDPR and are particularly well suited to cross-border research in highly regulated sectors. These changes would enhance harmonisation, reduce fragmentation and increase legal certainty while preserving strong protections for data subjects. C. Data Subject Rights EFPIA welcomes the proposed clarifications and targeted limitations to data subject rights which reflect a more balanced and realistic application of data subject rights in research contexts. D. Security Breaches EFPIA welcomes the proposed amendments to the personal data breach framework, including the focus on high-risk breaches, the extension of notification deadlines, and the introduction of a single EU-level notification form and submission point. These changes improve proportionality, efficiency and consistency without lowering the level of protection for individuals. III. Data Act EFPIA welcomes the clarification of the definition of data holder, which better reflects complex data governance models in the pharmaceutical sector, and the consolidation of rules on data localisation and transfers of non-personal data, supporting the free flow of data within the EU while addressing legitimate public interest concerns. EFPIA also welcomes the strengthened provisions on intellectual property and trade secrets, as strong and effective IP safeguards are a foundational principle for the pharmaceutical and life sciences sector, underpinning innovation, investment and the development of new medicines and health technologies. We envisage the application of the same principle across all data related legislations, particularly the European Health Data Space (EHDS) which currently does not offer the same level of protection of Data Holders’ IP rights. The proposal to strengthen the section in the Data Act on IP protection is an important signal that the objective of improving data sharing and access must be pursued without undermining legitimate IP rights or exposing commercially sensitive information, including know-how, confidential business information and trade secrets. IV. AI Act EFPIA welcomes the Commission’s acknowledgement in the explanatory memorandum that further guidance is needed on the practical application of the AI Act’s research exemptions, in particular on how these exemptions apply in sector-specific contexts, including use of AI in medicine lifecycle.

 V. Conclusion EFPIA supports the overall objective of the Digital Omnibus to improve coherence, legal certainty and effective enforcement across the EU digital acquis. For the pharmaceutical and life sciences sector, the Omnibus represents a critical opportunity to address long-standing uncertainty around scientific research, data reuse and AI-enabled innovation. EFPIA stands ready to engage constructively with EU institutions to refine the Digital Omnibus proposal and ensure that it supports both high standards of data protection and a globally competitive European life sciences ecosystem.

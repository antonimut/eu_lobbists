I. Introduction
On 19 November, the Commission revealed 
the Digital Omnibus package. This package is 
only one in a series of omnibus bills that com-
prise the Commission’s deregulatory agenda. 
The new College of Commissioners argues that 
reducing barriers for businesses will help them 
grow in a market defined by comparatively 
less regulation. The narrative of the Commis -
sion is about “cutting red tape” and “reducing 
bureaucracy”, which are appealing concepts. 
However, this narrative does not explain how 
fundamental rights will be protected and how 
we plan to ensure there is legal certainty if we 
dismantle recent, first-of-their-kind laws, and 
if, on top of that, we simply stop enforcing the 
laws the Commission adopted only a few years 
ago. In short, where the deregulation rolls 
back on fundamental rights, workers’ rights, 
or environmental protections, there is only one 
winner: the business sector. 
It was revealing that during the presentation 
of the digital omnibus, the Commissioner for 
democracy, justice, the rule of law and con -
sumer protection, Michael McGrath, only 
referred to the feedback from European busi -
nesses demanding these changes. Civil socie -
ty’s input appears to have been ignored in the 
preparation of this Digital Omnibus.

The Digital Omnibus proposes a significant 
retrenchment of key safeguards that have 
underpinned recent advances in the pro -
tection of fundamental rights in the digital 
environment, including those established by 
the GDPR and the AI Act. These rollbacks 
appear to reflect concessions made in response 
to pressure from major technology companies, 
national business sectors, and broader geopo -
litical considerations. 
In our position papers, we discuss the major 
shifts in both the Digital Omnibus on AI 
Regulation Proposal 1 (which addresses the 
AI Act) and the Digital Omnibus Regulation 
Proposal2 (which proposes modifications to the 
GDPR, ePrivacy, and other pieces of legisla -
tion that address data protection and privacy). 
This paper relates to changes in the GDPR. 
For the changes to the AI Act, see our parallel 
position paper.3
II. Changes to the 
definition of personal 
data
The Commission has initiated an Omnibus 
process on the GDPR that significantly alters 
several of its core pillars. Notably, the draft 
proposal introduces a revised definition of

personal data in Article 4. Under the current 
framework, personal data encompasses any 
information that enables the identification of 
an individual. The Commission’s proposed 
amendment would add a subjective element by 
assessing whether a person can be “reasonably” 
identified. This shift from an objective criterion 
to a context-dependent standard is a substan -
tial departure from the existing legal interpre -
tation and may have far-reaching implications 
for data protection practices.
Under the proposed approach, information 
may fall outside the scope of the GDPR if 
the company or public authority holding it 
asserts that it cannot re-identify the individ -
ual concerned, even if re-identification would 
be feasible for other actors. In practice, this 
means that data associated with indirect iden-
tifiers, such as pseudonyms, could cease to 
qualify as personal data and would no longer 
benefit from GDPR protections. Such a shift 
would substantially broaden the category of 
data considered non-personal, thereby creating 
significant regulatory gaps. This development 
would primarily advantage entities seeking to 
leverage personal information without being 
subject to EU data protection obligations.
Liberties is of the opinion that the proposed 
reform constitutes a direct challenge to the 
foundational principles of the GDPR. The 
revised definition of personal data is in conflict 
with Article 8 of the EU Charter of Funda -
mental Rights and the case law of the Court 
of Justice of the European Union (CJEU). 
The conflict between the Regulation and the 
Charter could lead to annulment of the law 
at the CJEU. 
The new definition of personal data could have 
tangible negative effects for individuals. Data 
controllers could potentially deny the ability to 
re-identify data subjects, due to limited capac-
ity, technical expertise or a strategic interest in 
treating personal data as non-personal. As a 
consequence, the burden of proof would shift 
to individuals, who would need to demonstrate 
that their data meets the threshold of personal 
data in order to invoke GDPR safeguards. This 
development would further exacerbate the 
existing power asymmetry between data con -
trollers and data subjects. With this change, the 
burden of proof is inverted, requiring people to 
prove that their personal data is personal data.

III. Personal data as an 
asset for AI
The Digital Omnibus has some of the most 
explicit goals to facilitate the use of personal 
data for AI applications. Large language mod-
els, AI applications such as ChatGPT, Deep -
seek, Gemini and any other that may come 
in the future will be able to exploit personal 
data under the already flexible term “legitimate 
interest” in Articles 9(2)(k) and 88c) that allows 
processing personal data, which becomes an 
even broader term.
Under the suggested changes, what used to be 
an exception for low-risk situations (e.g., the 
local sports club of which you are a member has 
a legitimate interest to use your personal data 
to send you direct marketing with new offers 
from the club) will allow  AI companies to use 
our personal data (including intimate photos, 
private documents or chat history) without our 
permission. This subtle but powerful change 
will empower AI companies to expand their 
use of personal information without needing 
to ask people for permission.
The new text  says (highlight is our own) that 
“such processing may be pursued for legiti -
mate interests within the meaning of Article 
6(1)(f) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, where 
appropriate, except where other Union or 
national laws explicitly require consent , and 
where such interests are overridden by the 
interests, or fundamental rights and freedoms 
of the data subject which require protection 
of personal data, in particular where the data 
subject is a child.” It is unclear whether the 
reference to Union or national laws requiring 
consent will provide any additional protection 
for people. Guidance from the Commission and 
the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) 
might be needed, as otherwise this expansion 
of legitimate interest could be a blank check for 
AI companies to do as they please with per -
sonal data without asking for permission.
The interactions between AI and GDPR con-
tinue in the new paragraph 5 of Article 9, which 
addresses special categories of data used in AI 
systems. If the company in charge of those sys-
tems “failed” to prevent sensitive data in their 
systems (including sexual orientation, political 
views, trade union affiliation), the company 
may deny the removal of that data if it “requires 
disproportionate effort”. It will only be obliged 
to prevent that data from being used to produce 
content or being disclosed in any way. 
Even though preventing the re-use of sensitive 
data to produce output can reduce the dissem-
ination and re-use of very intimate personal 
information, we are concerned that, with the 
fact that that information was taken in the first 
place and that the company may simply allege 
that removing that data will take “dispropor -
tionate efforts”, this will allow them to keep 
using that information. 
All of this is even more concerning, given the 
parallel deregulation of the AI Act covered 
in our separate paper. The delay in enforcing 
high-risk applications and the rollback of 
transparency requirements in the AI Act will 
allow even more of this personal information 
(now transformed overnight into “non-per -
sonal data”) to put people at even greater risk 
for longer.

IV. The right to access 
data will be reduced for 
people
One of the key aspects of the GDPR and 
of data protection laws in general is that 
people should have access to their personal 
data collected by any given company or pub -
lic authority. The idea behind this right is 
to prevent abuse and rebalance inequality, 
as governments and companies that have 
too much personal information about a per -
son can become very powerful surveillance 
machines. The proposed changes to Article 
12, which regulates access to personal data, 
will make it more difficult for people to 
exercise this right. Even if access remains 
free of charge, it may create difficulties for 
data subjects in accessing their data. The 
new text, primarily inspired by the proposal 
from the German industry association, 4 now 
dictates that if a data subject abuses  their 
rights requesting their information for issues 
unrelated to the protection of personal data (for 
example, requesting their personal data for 
a civil lawsuit or to obtain information for 
a labour law related case against Amazon 5) 
the entity that has your personal data (your 
employer or local authority) may deny them 
access to their personal data. In Liberties’ 
opinion, this is again a clash with Article 8 
of the Charter, which includes the right to 
rectification. If a data subject is not aware

V. Automated Decision-
Making may become 
more common 
Another important aspect of the GDPR is 
automated decision-making (ADM). ADM 
has been the target of many controversies, 
including the infamous scandal in Dutch 
social welfare distribution and the problems 
caused by the discriminatory algorithm used 
in Poland’s unemployment scoring system, 
among others. The changes to Article 22 will 
allow ADM to be used more intensively, as 
we will see a reduction in the right to object 
to decisions made by machines on issues such 
as job applications (the increase in AI use in 
job applications is evident). In the current text, 
we can argue that ADM (at least in theory) 
could have been applied without using techni-
cal means (for example, by having a recruiter 
analyse a job application themselves). Now, 
regardless of whether humans review the deci-
sion, the new version would strip people of 
their right to object to ADM.

VI. No consent needed for 
audience measurement 
or for security purposes
The new Article 88a touches on the possibil -
ities for devices such as Smart TVs, smart -
phones, tablets, and other connected devices to 
use personal data, such as TV viewing habits, 
web browsing history, and other related infor-
mation, to measure audience or for “security” 
purposes without requesting consent from 
people. One exception added that allows for 
the processing of personal data without asking 
for consent is audience measurement: There are 
good arguments for not having to provide con-
sent constantly when an online service is using 
data in an aggregated form: When the data 
collected by the website or application is aggre-
gated (for example, if the website does not col-
lect the information “Greg who lives in Berlin 
is accessing this website at this time” but rather 
“X number of people from Central Europe 
have accessed this website at some point in the 
afternoon”) the impact on fundamental rights 
is smaller. The limitations in the new text may 
be helping to delimit audience measurement 
for specific purposes in an aggregated form.
The other exception is for security purposes: if 
an application needs to check whether someone 
installed malware by mistake, it makes sense 
for Android to detect and remove it swiftly. 
However, this additional exception was not 
needed as there was already an exception for 
security integrated in legitimate interest. This 
existing security exception contains a balanc -
ing test (balancing the potential security threat 
vs. the amount of private information I need 
to access, for example). This balancing test is 
nowhere to be found in the new Article 88a, 
which makes the exception more arbitrary 
than the existing text and creates more legal 
uncertainty. Moreover, this exception can lead 
to “broad and massive searching of locally

VII. Conclusion
Contrary to what was announced on behalf of 
the Commission, the changes to the GDPR 
via the Digital Omnibus are not targeted, will 
not significantly reduce bureaucracy, and will 
not bring legal certainty. On the contrary, the 
Digital Omnibus will increase the imbalance 
of power: companies and public authorities 
that collect personal data will reduce people’s 
rights to access their personal data and to 
refuse surveillance.
Liberties calls on EU legislators to reject this 
Digital Omnibus proposal. Lawmakers have 
the power to reject this draft and refuse to 
abandon their commitments to fundamental 
rights. The GDPR is the gold standard world-
wide, offering high-level protection for people 
against companies and governments.  Using 
the omnibus process to rewrite significant 
data protection safeguards, thereby eliminat -
ing court cases, would be a betrayal of Euro -
pean citizens.

I. Introduction
The European Commission is in the process of 
introducing a number of legislative proposals, 
known as omnibuses, that aim to streamline 
EU legislation to make laws easier to under -
stand and follow, and thereby make the EU 
more economically competitive. In theory, this 
process should – and certainly could – be done 
without weakening laws’ fundamental rights 
protections. However, it has become axiomatic 
that this process is turning into deregulation, 
with the result that omnibus legislation rolls 
back rights protections, standards, and trans -
parency requirements in order to grease the 
wheels of business. As part of the most recent 
omnibus package, the Commission recently 
unveiled its Digital Omnibus, split into two 
parts: one covering data protection, 1 and one 
for artificial intelligence. This paper exam -
ines the artificial intelligence proposal 2 and 
discusses how these changes could negatively 
impact fundamental rights, democracy and the 
rule of law. 
The Digital Omnibus on AI proposal repre -
sents a significant threat to fundamental and 
digital rights, as well as core EU values like 
the rule of law. It proposes to delay or remove 
entirely essential transparency requirements,

requirements for

AI system are marked in a machine-readable 
format and detectable as artificially generated 
or manipulated” – in other words, AI-gen -
erated content like deepfakes must be clearly 
marked as such.

to enter into force from 2 August 2026, and it

V. Conclusion
If unchanged, the proposal on artificial intel -
ligence in the Digital Omnibus will signifi -
cantly weaken fundamental rights protections 
and basic safeguards that EU legislators have 
already approved. The proposals in the arti -
ficial intelligence omnibus stand to benefit 
only the tech industry and certain political 
factions. They do not make the laws easier to 
understand, and they do not streamline laws 
to better protect fundamental rights and EU 
values. Instead, they reduce several critical yet 
extremely modest requirements for AI provid-
ers, and people’s rights are taking a backseat to 
the interests of businesses and politicians. 
These changes also pose a risk to the fair and 
impartial application of AI in extremely sensi-
tive fields. Postponing safeguards for high-risk 
AI systems may allow many new systems to 
be rushed to market between when the law 
should have applied and when it actually will 
be under the omnibus, thereby skirting respon-
sibilities and putting at risk both fundamental 
rights and the rule of law. The erasure of basic

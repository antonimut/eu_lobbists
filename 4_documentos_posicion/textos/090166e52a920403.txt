DOT Europe response 
Digital Omnibus – Ex-post Consultation 
DOT Europe supports the proposals for the Digital Omnibus and the AI Omnibus. Both proposals are a 
step in the right direction towards simplification and streamlining. The focus on removing unnecessary 
reporting obligations, reducing overlapping requirements and clarifying rules is welcome and should 
be further built on. However, we believe  
 there is room for more ambition. Bigger, structural changes are needed to fully achieve the EU’s goals. 
We would like to take this opportunity to present our proposals on the  two Omnibus, but also make 
recommendations for a more ambitious agenda. 
Priorities on the two Omnibus 
Digital Omnibus 
We would like to highlight the following areas of interest: 
• Definition of personal data : While there is broad support for the principle that the 
qualification of pseudonymised data depends on context, the proposed changes to the 
definition of personal data mostly amount to a simple codification of existing CJEU caselaw. 
While such clarification is not unwelcome and clearly well-intended, it does not fundamentally 
change the existing acquis. The new article 41(a), which introduces powers for the European 
Commission to lay down specific criteria on pseudonymisation must explicitly incentivise the 
tools such as Privacy-Enhancing Technologies that make this safety possible.  
• Harmonising while broadening the Single Entry Point (SEP) mechanism: The SEP mechanism 
is very much welcome, however without a sufficiently harmonised framework covering 
terminology, thresholds, triggers and timelines, entities subject to multiple Union legal acts 
would continue to face legal uncertainty, duplicative report ing and increased compliance 
costs. It is therefore important to seek further harmonisation. Furthermore, routing the AI Act 
serious-incident reporting through ENISA’s Single Entry Point would meaningfully reduce 
fragmentation and administrative burden while improving coordination, oversight, and trust 
across the EU. Progressively, other legislations with incident reporting obligations should be 
incorporated into the SEP mechanism and align with the proposed reporting template in order 
to further streamline reporting obligations and reduce administrative. burden. 
• Legitimate interest to use personal (sensitive) data in AI context: Proposed Article 88c of the 
GDPR confirms that legitimate interest can be a legal basis in the context of AI development 
and deployment. The new provisions on special categories usage (articles 9 (2)(k) & 9(5)) 
confirm that legitimate interest can also be used for sensitive data, and that their usage in an 
AI context is unavoidable in certain situations. These revisions are beneficial for Europe’s 
innovative potential, and reflect the CJEU caselaw regarding online search or the CNIL’s 
guidance on sensitive data processing. While we understand that they need to be 
accompanied by safeguards, we caution against overly restrictive wording that would curtail 
future use cases.

• Cookies rule:  The explanatory memorandum rightly acknowledges that the dual regime 
created by the ePrivacy Directive and the General Data Protection Regulation has resulted in 
fragmented supervision, legal uncertainty, and increased compliance costs for providers. 
While integrating certain ePrivacy rules on cookies into the GDPR through the Digital Omnibus 
is presented as a step toward greater coherence, the proposal ultimately falls short of 
delivering meaningful simplification.  
o By maintaining  parallel regime s for personal and non -personal data, it perpetuates 
structural fragmentation and continues to impose overlapping and sometimes 
inconsistent obligations , particularly burdensome for SMEs and cross -border 
operators.  
o The continued primacy of consent for the use of cookies, alongside the separation of 
rules for tracking, direct marketing, and traffic data between the two frameworks  
depending on whether cookies engage personal data or not, adds new   complexity 
rather than simplifying it. 
o The proposal therefore misses a valuable opportunity to more fundamentally 
reconsider and streamline the ePrivacy framework. Greater legal certainty and 
simplification would be achieved by fully integrating Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy 
Directive into the GDPR, unifying the regimes under the risk-based approach and the 
one-stop-shop mechanism.  
o Moreover, the newly proposed Article 88a provides only very narrow exemptions 
from the consent requirement for processing personal data stored on or accessed 
from a user’s device. Other low -impact uses should be exempted including ad 
measurement and verification, and ad frequency capping and ad fraud detection. In 
addition, the exemption for security purposes should be broadened to clearly cover 
security, fraud prevention, and related software updates. 
o With regard to the proposed Article 88b, imposing consent obligations at the browser 
level and requiring service providers to act on user consent expressed via centralised 
tools raises significant legal and technical feasibility concerns which would inevitably  
have unintended consequences for European online publishers and service providers.  
o In addition, the introduction of mandatory centralised consent management 
platforms (CMPs) presents tangible  legal and commercial  risks for the broader 
European economy, including SMEs and the digital advertising ecosystem.  Such 
substantive amendments go beyond what was originally framed as a simplification 
exercise. These far-reaching changes to EU legislation should be reconsidered absent 
by a thorough impact assessment, which has not been provided in the context of the 
Digital Omnibus proposal. 
• New definition of scientific research (article 4(38)):  We welcome this definition, which 
confirms that the GDPR specific rules and derogations for research apply also to research 
carried out for society’s knowledge and wellbeing, even when furthering commercial 
interests. This is aligned with GDPR recital 159  (which pleads for a broad research concept) 
and will help stimulate Europe’s innovative potential, while being accompanied by solid 
safeguards.

• DPIA template: While the introduction of allowed and disallowed  lists of processing 
operations is a positive step that can improve guidance and legal clarity, the DPIA template 
should remain voluntary. Making the template mandatory risks undermining existing, 
effective DPIA practices and imposing unnecessary administr ative burden without clear 
benefits.  
• Clarity on the withdrawal of the Platform to Business Regulation  (P2B): The omnibus 
mentions that some provisions of the P2B regulation will remain applicable for a transition 
period until 31 December 2032. Although this is covered in recitals, it should also be included 
in the operative part of the omnibus. 
• Consider non -legislative support for businesses:  Parallel to the legislative process on the 
Digital Omnibus and the ongoing Digital Fitness Check consultation, the Commission should 
identify several priority areas where non -legislative action could be taken to provide 
immediate relief for businesses. One  area we have identified where this could be provided 
would be supporting companies assessments regarding international data transfers (TIAs). 
• GDPR challenges stem from oversight practice, not from legal text: Although the omnibus is 
welcome, we do not believe that a targeted reopening of the GDPR is the best way forward . 
We maintain that the main GDPR-related challenges stem not from the Regulation’s wording, 
but from inconsistent national interpretations and the prioritisation of enforcement as the 
principal measure of success. This creates legal uncertainty,  instability and unpredictability 
and challenges operating cross-border in the EU, stifles innovation, divert ing DPA resources 
away from aiding company compliance.  
AI Omnibus 
Our priorities focus on the following elements of the proposal: 
• Replace conditional grace periods with  fixed deadline s for Annex III & I : The Omnibus’ 
conditional and staggered grace periods for high-risk AI systems create legal uncertainty due 
to unclear triggers and fragmented timelines; single, fixed deadlines for Annex I and Annex III 
systems in line with the position of the Council and the European Parliament  would provide 
clarity and enable effective implementation. 
o In case, negotiations are delayed in trilogue negotiations, we believe it is essential to 
prioritise this point over the rest of the AI Omnibus and consider separating it from 
the rest of the legislative text. This accelerated instrument will allow to have  an 
agreement on the deadline well ahead August 2026.   
• Integrate AI Act serious incident reporting into SEP mechanism:  As mentioned previously, 
integrating AI Act incident reporting into the single EU -level platform (e.g. ENISA’s CRA 
mechanism) will reduce unnecessary reporting requirements , prevent fragmented national 
investigations, and enable coordinated, efficient, and consistent oversight across authorities. 
• Extend and broaden the Article 50 grace period: As the Code of Practice will be finalised only 
shortly before Article 50 applies, the current limited grace period is insufficient and is 
formulated in such a way as to only benefit providers who launch systems prior to the 2 August 
2026. To ensure legal certainty and a level playing field, the enforcement deadline for Article 
50 of the AI Act should be delayed in its entirety, or at least, for both Articles 50(2) and 50(4),

until 2 August 2027. This extension should apply in general, via Article 113, and not only to 
systems put on the market before 2 August 2026 via a transitional grace period.  
• Exempt AI -generated code from watermarking and defer text watermarking obligations:  
Watermarking techniques suitable for text or images are not appropriate for code, where 
even minor alterations can break syntax, compilation, or execution and undermine code 
integrity and security. AI generated code should therefore be explicitly exempted  from 
watermarking. More broadly, text watermarking obligations should be deferred until at least 
12 months after the Commission confirms the availability of effective and r eliable technical 
solutions. Clarify and strengthen AI Office’s role in enforcement: We support strengthening 
the role of the AI Office to ensure more consistent EU -wide enforcement and, in particular, 
encourage the mutual recognition of decisions adopted by national competent authorities. At 
the same time, it is essential to clearly delin eate responsibilities between the AI Office and 
national authorities to avoid overlaps, preserve sector-specific oversight for Annex I high-risk 
systems, and ensure effective coordination across the enforcement architecture. We also 
support extending the A I Office’s mandate to cover the enforcement of Article 50 
transparency obligations. Furthermore, the text should clarify that the Commission’s 
supervision of AI systems based on GPAI models applies not only to models and systems 
developed by the same provi der, but also to providers within the same corporate group.  
However, support for greater consistency in enforcement must not be conflated with the 
proposed centralisation of pre-market conformity assessments under the Commission (Article 
75), which we oppose. Centralising pre-market assessments for high-risk GPAI systems would 
risk creating a single point of failure and significant bottlenecks. To safeguard regulatory 
independence, efficiency, and timely market access for innovative solutions, providers mus t 
retain the flexibility to choose among different accredited conformity assessment bodies at 
both EU and Member State level. 
• Preserve the risk-based approach: Only extending penalty privileges from SMEs to small and 
mid-cap companies (SMCs) undermines the AI Act’s risk-based approach, as obligations should 
depend on the risk posed by AI systems , not company size . This approach is discriminatory 
and creates and is not conducive to a level playing field. 
Recommendations for better law-making 
Although the Digital Omnibus is a positive first step, we believe that the ambition should be bigger. 
The political impetus behind simplification presents us with a unique opportunity to address 
underlying shortcomings that have undermined the quality of legislation. DOT Europe has undertaken 
substantial work on this topic and we are pleased to share our recommendation s, touching on the 
rule-making and implementation levels. 
At the rule making level 
• We urge policy makers to consider a codification of the digital rulebook. It is indeed important 
for the European Union to streamline, simplify and consolidate its rapidly growing, and often 
fragmented, body of digital legislation into a more cohesive and manageable framework.  
Although this will be a challenging exercise, it will help guide companies in cases where 
overlaps exist between rules, for example on online content moderation.

• We propose strengthening the Impact Assessment process , by including an examination of 
impacts on regulatory governance, as well as the timing and entry into force of proposed rules. 
The Scrutiny Board process should also be strengthened. 
• Where appropriate, we suggest undertaking impact assessments or inter -institutional  
consultations before trilogues to ensure that the text that comes out of the co-legislators has 
been adequately assessed and operationally feasible, to avoid  drastic last-minute changes  to 
the text, which would have unintended consequences or introduce unachie vable deadlines. 
For example, the eEvidence package was amended at the last minute to bring forward the 
implementation deadline and require a centralised IT system which could not be delivered in 
the time available. Another example is the the AI Act  which introduced an entire new section 
on General Purpose AI without any impact assessment. There have also been several 
proposals impinging on Digital Services Act provisions. 
At the implementation level 
• Regulators often interpret rules  in isolation without  regard for economic repercussions or 
competitiveness which could result in decisions in tension with the conclusion of the Draghi 
report.. We suggest that regulators should have a statutory duty to have regard to the impact 
of their decisions on competition, innovation, and growth to avoid working on issues through 
a single lens and unintended consequences. 
• Pre-implementation dialogues with Member States (MS) should be instituted; Member States 
also often struggle with implementing rules so they should have a say in this early on.  The 
effects of new rules on national administrations can often be substantial but are not always 
front of mind before and during the legislative process. 
• Regulators should issue actionable and workable  guidance before any enforcement action 
and allow for an enforcement grace period for new rules, while the details are being ironed 
out. This approach will facilitate compliance and help companies adjust to new rules  in a 
seamless way, while supporting continuity of business 
• Encourage more cooperation between s upervisory authorities  to avoid conflicting 
requirements imposed on companies.  The complex regulatory framework has created 
situations where different regulators examine the same practice and impose conflicting 
requirements on companies. Increasing cooperation  between regulators, supported by 
transparent and accountable processes  will help minimise these instances, making  the 
compliance process smoother.  
• Member states should encourage domestic regulators to consult on and publish annual 
programmes of work setting out priorities and work plans.  Indicate timelines will help 
businesses know what to expect when and plan their engagement accordingly.  Regulators 
should also engage in EU-level bodies with a clear understanding of their stakeholders’ needs 
and concerns, informed by regular engagement and consultation.

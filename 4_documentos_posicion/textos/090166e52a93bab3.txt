1.  Data  Act  
While  APPLiA  supports  the  Data  Act’s  goal  of  fostering  a  fair  and  innovation-friendly  data  
economy,
 
the
 
regulation,
 
as
 
adopted,
 
raises
 
serious
 
concerns
 
over
 
the
 
protection
 
of
 
trade
 
secrets
 
and
 
confidential
 
business
 
information.
 
Mandatory
 
sharing
 
of
 
potentially
 
sensitive
 
data
 
with
 
users
 
and
 
third
 
parties
 
lacks
 
sufficient
 
safeguards,
 
creating
 
legal
 
uncertainty
 
and
 
competitive
 
risks
 
for
 
the
 
European
 
industry.
  
1.  Inadequate  protection  of  trade  secrets  
The  current  “handbrake,”  requiring  an  agreement  on  technical-organisational  measures  
(TOMs)
 
with
 
the
 
user,
 
is
 
inadequate
 
and
 
practically
 
unworkable,
 
as
 
it
 
risks
 
trade
 
secrets
 
being
 
widely
 
shared
 
or
 
aggregated
 
to
 
reveal
 
confidential
 
information
 
through
 
multiple
 
data
 
requests.
 
1.  Reversal  of  trade  secret  protection: The  Data  Act  shifts  ultimate  protection  to  
the
 
(often
 
unknown)
 
data
 
recipient,
 
making
 
enforcement
 
nearly
 
impossible
 
and
 
increasing
 
the
 
risk
 
of
 
data
 
leakage
 
or
 
reverse
 
engineering
 
by
 
third-party
 
competitors.
 
Art.
 
4(6)-(12)
 
and
 
Art.
 
5(9)(12)
 
Data
 
Act
 
allows
 
users
 
to
 
request
 
access
 
to
 
data
 
generated
 
by
 
connected
 
products
 
and
 
services,
 
even
 
if
 
such
 
data
 
is
 
protected
 
as
 
trade
 
secrets.
 
 2.  Ineffective  TOMs: Legal  uncertainty  surrounds  what  constitutes  adequate  TOMs.  
Measures
 
like
 
encryption,
 
digital
 
rights,
 
or
 
hashing
 
do
 
not
 
reliably
 
prevent
 
malicious
 
disclosure,
 
and
 
breaches
 
of
 
non-disclosure
 
agreements
 
are
 
often
 
undetectable.
 
Unlike
 
GDPR,
 
where
 
processors
 
protect
 
data
 
in
 
their
 
possession,
 
releasing
 
sensitive
 
data
 
to
 
multiple
 
actors
 
cannot
 
realistically
 
ensure
 
protection
 
after
 
disclosure.
 3.  High  threshold  for  refusal  and  proof: Under  Recital  31,  Art.  4(8),  and  Art.  5(11),  
demonstrating
 
“serious
 
economic
 
damage”
 
or
 
“irreparable
 
loss”
 
ex-ante
 
is
 
extremely
 
difficult.
 
The
 
potential
 
financial
 
risk,
 
even
 
with
 
TOMs
 
in
 
place,
 
may
 
not
 
be
 
evident
 
to
 
the
 
data
 
holder
 
before
 
disclosure.

Article  4  (6)    Trade  secrets  shall  be  preserved  and  shall  be  disclosed  only  where  the  data  holder  and  the  user  take  all  necessary  measures  prior  to  the  disclosure  to  preserve  their  confidentiality  in  particular  regarding  third  parties.  The  data  holder  or,  where  they  are  not  the  same  person,  the  trade  secret  holder  shall  identify  the  data  which  are  protected  as  trade  secrets,  including  in  the  relevant  metadata,  and  shall  agree  with  the  user  proportionate  technical  and  organisational  measures  necessary  to  preserve  the  confidentiality  of  the  shared  data,  in  particular  in  relation  to  third  parties,  such  as  model  contractual  terms,  confidentiality  agreements,  strict  access  protocols,  technical  standards  and  the  application  of  codes  of  conduct.

The  data  holder  or,  where  they  are  not  the  same  person,  the  trade  secret  holder  shall  identify  the  data  which  are  protected  as  trade  secrets,  including  in  the  relevant  metadata. 2.  Excessive  temporal  scope  of  application  
Some  obligations  of  the  Data  Act  will  apply  retroactively  to  products  and  services  
placed
 
on
 
the
 
market
 
before
 
September
 
2025,
 
including
 
the
 
right
 
to
 
data
 
access,
 
the
 
data-sharing
 
obligation,
 
and
 
the
 
requirement
 
for
 
the
 
data
 
holder
 
to
 
secure
 
a
 
right
 
to
 
use
 
the
 
data
 
(Art.
 
4(13)).
 
This
 
poses
 
significant
 
challenges:
 
1.  Legacy  data  governance: Older  products  were  not  designed  with  data  
management
 
processes
 
in
 
place,
 
making
 
it
 
difficult
 
to
 
identify,
 
collect,
 
process,
 
and
 
render
 
data
 
understandable.
 
Technical
 
systems
 
for
 
data
 
access
 
and
 
transfer
 
are
 
often
 
lacking.
 2.  Identifying  users: Contacting  data  users  for  legacy  products  can  be  difficult  
or
 
impossible.
 3.  Obtaining  data  licenses: Updating  contractual  terms  with  existing  
customers
 
may
 
be
 
costly
 
and
 
complex,
 
and
 
refusal
 
could
 
prevent
 
data
 
holders
 
from
 
using
 
essential
 
data
 
for
 
diagnostics,
 
product
 
optimisation,
 
or
 
development
 
of
 
related
 
services.

transpose
 
and
 
'modernize'
 
the
 
access
 
rules

Article
 
5(3)
 
of
 
the
 
ePrivacy
 
Directive.
 
While
 
intended
 
to
 
simplify
 
the
 
'cookie'

creates
 
a
 
legal
 
overlap
 
for
 
the
 
home
 
appliance
 
sector.
 
By
 
shifting
 
these
 
rules
 
into
 
the
 
GDPR,
 
the
 
Commission
 
subjects
 
physical
 
terminal
 
equipment,
 
such
 
as
 
connected
 
household
 
appliances,
 
to
 
a
 
rigid,
 
browser-centric
 
consent
 
framework
 
that
 
was
 
never
 
designed
 
for
 
the
 
Internet
 
of
 
Things
 
(IoT).

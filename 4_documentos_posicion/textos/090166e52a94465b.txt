Intesa Sanpaolo Position Paper on the Proposal for a Digital Omnibus on AI  
and Digital Omnibus Regulation 
 
Digital Omnibus on AI 
 
Introduction 
 
Intesa Sanpaolo welcomes the European Commission (EC) proposal “Digital Omnibus on AI”, as the 
need for simplification and clarification of the AI regulatory framework is urgent.  
Overall, w e fully support the EC’s commitment to a clear, simple and innovation -friendly 
implementation of the AI Act, as outlined in the Regulation’s Explanatory Memorandum. We also 
welcome the EC’s ongoing efforts to provide additional guidance , offering practical instructions for 
applying AI Act. 
 
However, we would like also to share several concerns, as follows: 
 
1. Definition of “AI system” 
 
We would like to reiterate our concerns about the current definition of “AI system” provided by the 
AI Act under Art.3 (1). This is excessively complex and operationally burdensome, making it difficult 
for stakeholders to consistently determine whether a given system falls within the regulatory scope. 
Existing guidance brought by the EC’s Guidelines on the definition of an AI system has not provided 
sufficient clarity, for instance, with regards to the concept of “inference”. Further clear guidance on 
the practical application of the definition is needed to enable immediate and consistent 
identification of AI systems in scope,  regardless of sector or technical ecosystem in which they are 
used. We therefore c all for binding guidance to clarify the scope of this definition, and formally 
exclude traditional statistic models (e.g., linear/logistic regression) which are not capable of 
performing inference as defined by Art.3(1) and Recital 12.  
 
2. Application date for high-risk AI systems under Annex III 
 
As noted in the Digital Omnibus on AI, effective implementation has been impacted by “delays in 
designating national competent authorities and conformity assessment bodies, as well as a lack of 
harmonised standards for the AI Act’s high-risk requirements, guidance, and compliance tools” (see 
pg.2, Explanatory Memorandum). We appreciate this acknowledgement, and we generally support 
the proposal for an extension of the application date for high -risk AI systems under Annex III  in 
consideration of the absence of detailed guidance and standardization , which are necessary for 
the practical implementation of High-risk AI obligations.  
However, to provide market operators with a predictable timeline , we strongly suggest setting 
December 2027 as the postponed application date, thus deleting the transitional period of 6 months 
which leads to uncertainty, additional complexity, and it nullifies the purpose of simplification brought 
by Digital Omnibus itself. Moreover, we also request a clear and predictable timeline for the 
publication of Guidelines, Code of practise and standards (CEN/CENELEC) by the end of 2026 and 
the inclusion of a mechanism for further extension , if guidance is not available by this deadline.

Considering that the possible use of high -risk AI systems under Annex III by business operators will be 
accompanied by the necessary safeguards, additional time might be useful from an operational 
perspective (e.g., technical documentation and record-keeping purposes). 
 
Finally, it is worth noting that the postponement covers only Chapter III Sections 1, 2 and 3, but there 
are obligations for High-risk AI systems which are prescribed outside the referred chapter, such those 
prescribed by Chapter IX (including monitoring, incident information sharing, and remedies). To 
ensure coherent application of such requirements, we request the postponement to be applicable 
to all High-risk AI obligations. 
 
3. Overlapping with sectoral laws 
 
While we welcome the horizontal simplification efforts of Omnibus, we regret that duplication of 
relevant provisions between the AI Act and the financial sector legislation remain, in particular with 
regard to the interplay between the prudential framework applicable to IRB models (i.e. CRR/CRD 
and second level legislation) and the AI Act. Please see the Annex at the end of this position paper. 
 
4. Registration requirement 
 
While we understand the need  to remove the registration requirement for non -high-risk systems 
(Art.6(4)) following the self -assessment of the provider according to Art.6(3)  as it could be seen as 
too onerous, we are seriously concerned that under the revised Article 6(4), providers relying on the 
registration exemption would not be required to proactively inform deployers of their self-assessment 
that the AI system is no high-risk. We would therefore strongly recommend maintaining (more limited) 
transparency obligations, requiring providers relying on  Article 6(3) exemption to proactively 
communicate their se lf-assessment (or a summary thereof) and reasoning to deployers before the 
system is provided to them. Indeed, we deem it crucial to strengthen deployers’ safeguards to avoid 
any knowledge gap, since deployers may unknowingly implement what could potentially be a high-
risk AI system, thereby incurring in compliance obligations and liability under the AI Act without being 
aware of this risk.  Moreover, transparency provisions are essential for the data controller to comply 
with their accountability obligations under the GDPR  (please also refer to the Joint Opinion EDP B-
EDPS 2026/1). 
 
5. GPAI liability in the supply chain 
 
We urge the EC to provide standardized contractual clauses (SCCs) at EU level for the 
implementation of the provider s’ obligations towards both downstream providers and deployers , 
and which are currently available only for the Public Administration. SCCs should be mandatory; they 
should not be waived by the GPAI providers and built on those  ones already available under the 
GDPR. 
 
6. Alignment between GDPR and AI Act; DPIA and FRIA 
 
We ask for recognizing the GDPR as the main personal data framework for the AI Act  (thus making 
the GDPR as “lex specialis” of the AI Act)  promoting joint guidance to align assessments, templates 
and methodologies to comply with art. 9 (risk management system) and art. 27 (FRIA) of the AI Act 
and art. 35 (DPIA) of the GDPR. 
On the DPIA and FRIA, we advocate for an “Evaluative Interoperability” approach rather than a full 
merger of the Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) under Art . 35 of the GDPR and the 
Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (FRIA), under Art.27 (4).  While we support the development 
of a consistent underlying methodology, we believe the two assessments should remain distinct. This 
modularity allows different specialized departments within a financial institution (e.g., DPO, Risk 
Management, Compliance ) to maintain their evaluative autonomy and accountability, while

simultaneously streamlining the process through a 'cross -referencing' mechanism. By allowing the 
FRIA to 'borrow' technical descriptions and data-flow insights from the DPIA (and vice versa regarding 
bias and fairness metrics), institutions can avoid the d uplication of efforts and ensure that each 
fundamental right is analysed with the necessary depth and specific expertise. This approach is also 
more aligned with the CJEU’s jurisprudence, which recognizes the distinct nature of the various rights 
enshrined in the Charter. 
 
7. Machine-readable markers in AI-generated output  
 
We ask to align the applicability deadlines for machine -readable markers in AI -generated content 
with those set for high-risk AI systems given that implementation depends on forthcoming Commission 
guidance, technical specifications and the sectoral Code of Conduct (par. 30, amending Art.111 of 
the AI Act). 
 
8. Training of the human overseers 
 
While we acknowledge the EDPB/EDPS concerns regarding AI literacy, we maintain that while large 
enterprises are equipped to develop internal training, SMEs require coordinated support from 
Member States and the AI Office to establish comprehensive and safeguarding training frameworks. 
However, we recommend a clear distinction for human oversight training. Specifically, training for 
human overseers should remain the responsibility of individual companies. This training must be 
recognized as distinct from gen eral AI literacy, given its highly interdisciplinary nature and its 
alignment with the concept of 'moral intellect' as promoted within the AI Pact framework .

9. Definition of Personal Data 
 
We welcome the European Commission’s proposal to clarify  that data will not be considered 
personal from the perspective of an entity whe re that entity cannot identify the natural person to 
whom the information relates , considering the means “reasonably likely to be used” by the same 
entity. This seems to potentially apply to pseudonymized data (e.g., Jack Doe becoming John Smith) 
but also to data that can be considered “partial” (e.g., IP address with the last octet removed) 
because in both cases the receiving entity might not have the additional information nee ded to 
identify the natural persons.  Therefore, we kindly request confirmation that both scenarios would be 
potentially relevant under the new definition of personal data. Also, while this provision comes from 
a ruling by the Court of Justice of the European Union, its practical interpretation and implementation 
may require companies to perform an additional assessment on a case-by-case basis. Therefore, we 
support further regulatory Guidelines, as envisaged by the newly proposed art. 4 (1)a GDPR, to 
provide clarifications on the actual implementation of the new definition of personal data.  
 
10. Legitimate Interest as a legal basis to develop and operate AI systems  
 
We welcome the provision under the n ew Article 88c , regarding the explicit confirmation that 
legitimate interest under Article 6(1)(f) GDPR may be relied upon for AI development and operation 
(as supported by EDPB and EDPS in their Joint Opinion 2/2026 p. 16). However, we would appreciate 
clarification on if only this legal basis would require additional safeguards  (described in the second 
paragraph of the Article 88c) and if so, we recommend seeing this clarification included in a Recital 
rather in an Article. Finally, we propose to delete the expression  “where appropriate”  because it 
would have the effect of decreasing rather than increasing legal certainty.

11. Processing of Special Categories of Personal Data  
 
We welcome the introduction of a derogation from the prohibition on processing special categories 
of personal data in the context of the development and operation of AI systems. Nonetheless to 
ensure legal certainty  we would also welcome further guidance on the related safeguards, with a 
view to ensuring that they remain proportionate and do not result in a limitation to the practical 
applicability of the derogation. 
Furthermore, w e call for a clear extension of the Art. 4a  (1) of Digital Omnibus ’s AI legal basis for 
processing special categories of personal data to all AI Systems for the mitigation of discrimination 
risks, ensuring consistency with the Digital Omnibus AI’s Recital (6), which states that it should apply 
to “other AI systems and AI models as well as deployers of high -risk AI systems" (while Art. 4a(1) uses 
the wording “may apply”) and ensuring legal certainty and consistency with the GDPR. 
 
12. Processing of Biometric Data for Confirming the Identity of a Data Subject  
 
We welcome the introduction of a derogation allowing the processing of biometric data for the 
purpose of confirming the identity of a data subject. To further strengthen legal certainty and ensure 
consistent requirements at the intersection of AI and data protection regulations, we would welcome 
clarification on the following points: (1) the precise scope of the definition of “biometric data” under 
both the GDPR and the AI Act; (2) the distinction between the concepts of “verification” and “unique 
identification”; and (3) the correct interpretation of the phrase “under the sole control of the data 
subject”. For instance, for verification and authentication purposes, controllers usually engage with 
processors to carry out identity verification. 
To this end, w hile Recital 34 of the Proposal intends to clarify this point (“where the biometric data 
are securely stored solely at the side of the data subject or are securely stored at the side of the 
controller”), it is unclear whether data stored by the controller can truly be considered to be "under 
the sole control of the data subject".  
 
13. Data Breach Notifications 
 
We support the extension of the data breach notification deadline, the introduction of a single-entry 
point and the development of a common notification template by the EDPB together with a list of 
the circumstances in which a personal data breach is likel y to result in a high risk to the rights and 
freedoms of a natural person. We propose to align, to the greatest possible extent, the definitions of 
notifiable data breaches and/or security incidents across the European legislation (e.g. GDPR, CER 
Directive, DORA and NIS II Directive), to ensure a consistent application and reduce administrative 
and procedural burdens.  Thereby fostering the harmonisation, simplification and efficiency of 
regulatory reporting processes. 
We highlight, however, that from a practical and legal -certainty perspective, an hour -based 
deadline remains problematic, especially for incidents that occur on Fridays or immediately before 
public holidays. We therefore suggest aligning the calculation of  the notification period with 
Regulation No1182/71, expressing time limits in working days (as opposed to hours) and automatically 
rolling over a deadline expiring on a Saturday, Sunday or public holiday to the next working day. This 
would enable controllers to comply with the legal requirements while ensuring that investigations and 
internal clarifications can be carried out with the necessary quality.  
If co-legislators do not find it feasible, then the 96-hour deadline should instead be defined in business 
hours, which would provide companies with a greater margin of action.  
 
14. Transparency Obligations 
 
With regard to controllers’  obligation to inform data subjects about the processing of their personal 
data, and the possibility to waive this obligation where there are reasonable grounds to assume that 
the data subject already has the relevant information (art. 3 par. 5 Digital Omnibus), we support the

development of further guidelines, with practical examples, to clarify the conditions under which 
such a presumption of prior knowledge may be considered valid. In particular, we would welcome 
clarification as to whether the mere publication of the informa tion on the entity’s website may be 
deemed sufficient, and whether such knowledge may be presumed where the data subject is the 
direct source of the data.  For example, clarification would be particularly useful in situations involving 
natural persons connected to a legal entity (such as legal representatives or shareholders), where it 
could reasonably be presumed that they are all already aware of the information contained in the 
privacy notice provided to the legal entity. 
 
Cybersecurity 
 
15. Overlap DORA/CRA 
 
We support a banking exemption from the CRA and the recognition of DORA as lex specialis for the 
financial sector, in line with the approach already adopted under NIS2. Currently, DORA and the 
Cyber Resilience Act cover the same banking systems and digital infrastructures, creating 
overlapping requirements - for example in incident notification, where banks must report to multiple 
authorities under different timelines - rather than improving the resilience. 
 
16. Incident reporting hub 
 
The creation of a Single-Entry Point for incident reporting should be implemented as the sole reporting 
channel, fully replacing all existing mechanisms and enabling real simplification through a 
harmonized and standardized model with aligned definitions, thresholds and timelines across DORA, 
GDPR, NIS2 /CER, CRA, eIDAS and the AI Act . In this regard , the use of the DORA templates as a 
methodological reference standard for incident reporting  would support in the harmonisation of 
reporting activities.  Furthermore, to be effective, submissions made through the Single -Entry Point 
should be fully recognized by national authorities as meeting all reporting obligations, thereby 
eliminating duplication and preventing the introduction of additional reporting layers.

ANNEX  
Clarification on the Scope of "High -Risk AI Systems" – Exclusion of approved models used for 
prudential purposes to calculate credit institutions’ and insurance undertakings’ capital requirements  
We welcome the opportunity to provide comments on “the Digital Omnibus on AI” (i.e. Regulation 
EU 2024/1689) and  fully support the Commission’ s commitment to a clear, simple and innovation -
friendly implementation of AI Act, as outlined in the Regulation’s Explanatory Memorandum.  
We also appreciate the Commission’s ongoing efforts to  review the EU digital rulebook through the 
Digital Fitness Check consultation. 
In this context, this contribution aims to highlight the interplay between prudential requirements, such 
as those applicable to IRB model (i.e. CRR/CRD and second level legislation) and Regulation EU 
2024/1689 (i.e. “AI Act”), with a focus on the classification of credit scoring models 
Recital (58) of the Regulation EU 2024/689 states th at “AI systems provided for by Union law for 
prudential purposes to calculate credit institutions’ and insurance undertakings’ capital requirements 
should not be considered to be high-risk under this Regulation”. 
At the same time, the AI Act's Annex III, 5.b, currently classifies AI systems used for evaluating the 
creditworthiness of natural persons as High-Risk.  
Although recital (58) does not envisage any direct impact of the requirements set out for High-risk AI 
system on IRB model, the Annex III determines an “indirect substantial effects” on them via the “use-
test” prudential requirements1.  
For this reason, in order to ensure legal consistency between the rationale of the Recital and the 
provision under Annex III, 5, we deem it important to further clarify how these two provisions should 
apply in situations where the same IRB model used for capital -requirements calculations is also part 
of the AI system used in the loan-origination process to assess the creditworthiness of natural persons 
(CWA). We support the general intention to avoid a breach of fundamental rights (introducing 
unlawful discrimination), nevertheless we consider that a direct application of the AI Act's High-Risk 
classification to  AI systems used for C WA when they rely on IRB models already employed for 
prudential capital calculations would generate regulatory overlap and inefficiency, contrary to the 
simplification principles underpinning the Commission’s proposal. In particular: 
1. Duplicated Validation Requirements : IRB models are already subject to continuous, rigorous 
validation and approval by the European Central Bank (ECB) , in its capacity as the primary 
prudential supervisor, or by National Competent Authorities (NCAs). 
2. Regulatory Redundancy: Classification as high-risk under the AI Act would impose a second, 
extensive validation regime, resulting in significant regulatory overlapping without adding 
substantial safeguards, as the ECB's validation process is extremely robust 2. 
3. Additional administrative burdens : the co -existence of multiple authorities 
(prudential/conduct authorities and Market Surveillance Authorities - MSAs) supervising 
financial entities would further increase compliance complexity for financial institutions. 
To promote regulatory efficiency, consistency, and align the legal text with the stated intent of 
Recital 58, we propose a targeted amendment to Annex III, point 5(b ) and 5(c) of the AI Act. The 
proposed amendment adds an explicit exception for AI systems that utilize approved models  for

respecting the robust risk management framework already supervised by the ECB.

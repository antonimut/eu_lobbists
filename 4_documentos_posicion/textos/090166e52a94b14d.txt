Art 4 (1). Definition of ’Personal data” 
The insurance sector supports the proposed  revised definition of personal 
data, as it brings much needed legal certainty to a core concept of the GDPR.  
  
By moving away from an “absolute” interpretation of personal data, this clar-
ification resolves longstanding uncertainty and brings the legal framework 
into line with judicial interpretation while maintaining the high level of data 
protection.  
 
The proposal codifies recent CJEU case law, notably  EDPS v SRB , by con-
firming that information is not personal data for an entity that does not have 
the means  reasonably likely  to identify the individual, and that such pro-
cessing therefore falls outside the GDPR for that entity.   
The clarification will bring much needed simplification to especially entities 
only using data internally.   
 
We therefore support the proposal in the Commission’s proposal. 
 
We also support that the main part of the definition is kept unchanged. Thus, 
keeping in place protection for both direct or indirect identification within 
company means. 
 
This will ensure, that any entity which receives data that due to the com-
pany’s processing capabilities will become personal (directly or indirectly) 
must still be treated as personal data. Thus, a legal basis must be established, 
and information requirements has to be fulfilled.

On exchange or sale of data: 
Concerns have been raised regarding the clarification of personal data caus-
ing weakened protection. These concerns are almost entirely raised about 
scenarios where data is exchanged between entities (and this primarily re-
garding big tech).  
 
We support the suggestion, as it adds much needed clarity and simplification 
for a single entity posing data solely for internal use. 
 
If data is exchanged we see the primary guardrails as unchanged: If data is 
exchanged between entities the GDPR protection will apply as soon the data 
becomes indirectly or directly identifiable, this is necessary to keep in place 
the intended protection. 
 
If an entity collects additional data, which allows identification, the GDPR 
protection will also apply.  
 
To address the concerns, further clarification could be considered to main-
tain the necessary level of protection. But it should be avoided, that concerns 
limited to exchange of data (and possibility of identification before and after 
exchange) effect compa nies using data strictly for internal purposes where 
no external sharing, matching, or re -identification risks arise, as this would 
impose disproportionate compliance burdens without providing correspond-
ing privacy benefits. 
 
Objective triggers for “reasonably likely” 
We also see some concern on the effect amendment as the wording “reason-
ably likely” adds a subjective dimension to the amendment. In legal terms 
we agree, that the wording should be as objective as possible, but as the 
term “reasonable likely” is already present in current the GDPR. Thus, it is 
difficult to see how additional unclarity is added by reusing it. 
But if needed clarification could be addressed by specifying (more) objective 
triggers. I.e. as those already mentioned in the amended recital 26: time, cost 
and labour. 
 
Recommendation: 
We support the amendment as is.  
 
We can also support necessary changes to clarify protection in situations 
where data is exchanged (possible focus on very large online platforms and 
search engines, VLOP/VLOSE).

Personal data and pseudonymization 
Regarding pseudonymization we support the addition of article 41a and 
building a framework defining when data resulting from pseudonymization 
is no longer personal data. 
 
If the pseudonymization is sufficient and identification (direct or indirect) is 
not reasonably likely (possible) for a given entity. The data would not be per-
sonal.  
This follows the logic of the updated article 4(1) and would add needed clarity 
that data can be personal for one entity, but not for another.  
 
GDPR-protections are kept in place, as protection is introduced if/when an 
entity can identify an individual - directly or indirectly - in a pseudonymized 
dataset or by combining with other data. 
 
The overall framework would – as proposed – only be an element to demon-
strate when pseudonymized data is no longer personal data. Adding the 
framework does not change the fundamental protection.  
Residual identification “risks” when using the framework would need to be 
assessed case by case in relation to the “means reasonable likely” to be used 
by a given entity. 
 
Further on the amendment to the definition of the concept of per-
sonal data (GDPR Article 4, point 1): 
The addition to the definition is considered relevant not only in relation to 
the use of information for the development of AI models, but also, for exam-
ple, in relation to data processor arrangements (including third country 
transfers), where pseudonymised data, moving forward, will in a number of 
specific situations not be able to lead to the identification of the data subjects. 
 
However, the addition necessitates further clarification and exemplification 
of when pseudonymisation is sufficient. In this context, reference is made to 
the CJEU judgment C413/23 P. There is therefore a need to ensure that an 
addition to the definition is considered to be in accordance with the CJEU's 
rulings in the area (also see C582/14 and C434/16), as well as taking into 
account that C413/23P has actually resulted in the referral back to the Gen-
eral Court at first instance regarding questions about when information con-
stitutes personal data capable of leading to identification.

Article 9(2)(k) and 9(5): AI and special categories of personal data  
We support the additions regarding AI and processing of special categories 
of personal data.  
 
GDPR was meant to be technology neutral. But given the continuous uptake, 
ever-expanding use and transformative application of AI. It would be due 
diligence to put specific AI provisions in place. 
 
If amending the proposal, we recommend maintaining the current “3 step 
process” as this is important in order not to implement an “AI privilege”.  
 
The current proposal most importantly sets extra requirements when using 
AI by placing two levels of additional guardrails. 1) avoid collection and pro-
cessing of special categories of personal data. 2) Remove data if found. 
These two steps put additional requirements on AI use. 
 
The last step allows for residual data to remain if removal requires dispro-
portionate effort. If doing so, that data cannot be used to produce outputs or 
be disclosed. 
 
While the last step can be seen as giving “privilege” to AI. It has to be consid-
ered in conjunction with the two preceding levels of protection, which puts 
additional guardrails on AI. Combined we do not consider the proposal to 
give AI “privilege”. Overal l, the combined process is a welcome addition of 
AI-specific guardrails in relation to special categories of personal data. 
 
That said the restrictions under  the proposed new Article 9(5) must  also be 
workable in practice.  As currently drafted, the provision  appears to re-
quire the unconditional avoidance of processing sensitive data during AI 
training and operation.  
 
To ensure a proportionate and operationally feasible framework, we recom-
mend clarifying that sensitive  data processing should be avoided  as far as 
reasonably possible or, alternatively, linking the obligation to the  prin-
ciple of data minimisation , as reflected in Article 89(1) GDPR. This 
would preserve high standards of protection while still allowing essential and 
justified processing in clearly defined circumstances.

Legitimate interest as legal basis for AI training  
The insurance sector supports the European Commission’s proposed new Ar-
ticle 88c and Art. 9(2)(k) under the GDPR. Together, these provisions clarify 
that the training and operation of AI models may be carried out on the basis 
of legitimate interest, including, where appropriate, the processing of sensi-
tive data, provided that robust and proportionate safeguards are in place.  
The change will guarantee more legal certainty while continuing to ensure a 
high level  of data protection. Legitimate interest  in fact  remains sub-
ject to strict safeguards including, among others,  purpose limitation, neces-
sity and balancing tests and transparency obligations.

Clarify the appropriate legal basis for solely automated decision 
making  
The proposed clarification  concerning the "necessity test" in Article 22  is a 
positive development.  
The application of  this Article is  frequently interpreted restrictively.  Some 
data protection authorities assert that  solely automated  decisions cannot 
be deemed "necessary" if similar tasks have previously been carried out by 
humans. As a result, they con clude that automated decision -making is not 
permissible under this provision.  
Furthermore, they  maintain that valid consent under Article 22(2)(c) and 
Article 7(4) GDPR can only be obtained if the data subject is offered the  op-
tion to have their data processed by a human from the beginning.  
This restrictive interpretation of necessity inhibits insurers and consumers 
from fully realising the advantages brought by technological progress.

Legal uncertainty around special categories of data in insurance  
In order to lawfully process special categories of data, insurers must rely on 
both a lawful basis under Article 6 GDPR and a specific condition under Ar-
ticle 9. The processing of health data is essential for underwriting, claims 
handling and reinsurance. However, the current broad interpretation 
of health data, which extends protection to any personal data from which 
health information can be inferred, creates various legal and practical prob-
lems.  
 
In practice, almost any personal data could be treated as health data if some-
one could  infer health -related information, even when the data itself  con-
tained no medical information.

This is problematic as it can make  the scope of Article  9 unpredictable and 
detached from the actual risk the data poses to the individual.  
 
Recommendation:  
Clarifying that health data covers only personal data that directly reveal in-
formation about a person’s health status  would make the framework more 
predictable and usable.  
A more precise definition would improve legal predictability for controllers 
and supervisory authorities, enable proportionate risk -based compliance, 
and support responsible data use in areas such as research, public policy, and 
innovation without weakening protection for individuals’ core health infor-
mation.

Extension of abusive requests, Art. 12 (5) GDPR  
The insurance sector welcomes the proposed extension of safeguards against 
the abuse of data access rights under Article  12(5) GDPR. Allowing control-
lers to refuse or charge for access requests that are manifestly unfounded or 
excessive, including where data subject rights are exercised for purposes un-
related to data protection, is a step in the right direction.   
  
However, important challenges  remain. In particular, the burden of proof 
still rests with the controller to demonstrate that a request is abusive. There 
is a need for clarification regarding how a company should determine 
whether the motivation behind a request concerns something other than the 
protection of personal data, and, on that basis, whether there is misuse of the 
rights. In practice, this is difficult to meet, as data subjects  are not required 
to justify their requests and controllers often lack the evidence needed to es-
tablish abusive intent.   
 
The wording of the amendment to Article 12(5) “…for requests under Article 
15 because the data subject abuses the rights conferred by this regulation for 
purposes other than the protection of their data…” can be interpreted to 
mean that companies have the right to ask about the motivation behind an 
access request in order to refuse it if the purpose is deemed to be misuse. 
This appears to contradict the original position that the motivation behind 
the request was not significant. Article 15 is widely recognised as providing 
the data subject with the right to access the information a company may pro-
cess about them, so that they can check its legality and point out, for example, 
errors in the information. The starting point has not been that companies 
could question the motivation behind a data subject’s request for access. 
The examples listed in recital 35 are considered to be already known today 
and do not create grounds for challenges, as the intention behind them is 
very clear.

Reporting of Security Breaches and Notification Obligations 
(GDPR Art. 4, No. 12, and Art. 33) 
We welcome the change. The amendment to the reporting deadline, and the 
adjustment that only data breaches assessed as potentially leading to a high 
risk for the data subject must be reported, will bring relief in day-to-day op-
erations. 
 
According to recital no. 39, the Data Protection Board must also draw up a 
list of circumstances that may result in a high risk for the data subject. In this 
context, it is important to emphasise that it is still the individual risk assess-
ment based on the actual circumstances and facts of the breach that is deci-
sive for the risk evaluation. Therefore, the list must not be interpreted or im-
plemented as requiring reporting in the mentioned circumstances. 
 
If one wished to further simplify or ease existing administrative/operational 
burdens, this could, for example, be achieved by relaxing the documentation 
requirement in Art. 33(5) regarding the log of data breaches that companies 
must maintain. In practice, all data breaches are currently recorded with in-
formation equivalent to what is required in notifications to the national au-
thority, as this is the simplest way to document whether a given data breach

plate for the content of the log could help reduce the burden of ongoing doc-

We are very positive about the principle of “report once – share many”.

Incident management and reporting across regulatory frame-
works 
With the Digital Omnibus, the European Commission has already proposed 
the establishment of a single reporting platform. The initiative aims to en-
sure that security incidents across a range of regulatory frameworks are re-
ported only once, in line with the “report once, share many” principle. At 
the same time, reporting templates are to be harmonised. In practice, this 
 
 
 
 
Insurance & Pension Denmark 
 
 
 
 
Page 9 
would mean that companies in the future would report to a single central 
EU-level platform rather than to different national authorities, such as the 
financial supervisory authority under DORA and the data protection au-
thority under the GDPR, at least in Denmark 
However, a single incident may potentially trigger several parallel and over-
lapping reporting processes. This can create unnecessary duplication of 
work and increase the risk of non-compliance. 
An insurance or pension company may find itself in a situation where an in-
cident must be reported to the authorities under the following regimes: 
• GDPR: notification of a personal data breach to the data protection 
authority within 72 hours (extended to 96 hours under the Digital 
Omnibus). 
• AI Act: notification of an incident or malfunction within 15 days. 
• DORA: notification of a major ICT-related incident within 4 hours. 
• CRA (if the financial sector is covered): early notification of a vul-
nerability or incident within 24 hours. 
In addition to leading to significant resource-related costs for companies, 
the fragmented rules on incident reporting also mean that companies must 
carry out extensive duplicate work, as deadlines and reporting obligations 
are not streamlined. The fragmented reporting landscape requires compa-
nies to spend critical time on regulatory compliance rather than focusing on 
addressing the incident or vulnerability itself. 
The report once share many initiative is a good first step towards streamlin-
ing reporting and notification obligations, but it does not resolve the under-
lying challenges posed by fragmented legislation. There is therefore a need 
for the Commission to streamline reporting processes and criteria—particu-
larly deadlines and taxonomies—across digital legislation. Moreover, stand-
ardised templates will help ensure that the information provided is relevant 
and sufficient for authorities, while reducing the risk of errors and omissions 
caused by varying requirements. Finally, it should be possible for multiple 
parties to fill out reporting templates simultaneously given the difference in 
deadlines across regulation. A single, streamlined reporting process would 
lead to a significant reduction in compliance costs for companies and would 
redirect resources towards substantive security work. 
Additional proposal for removing administrative burdens, that should be 
considered for the digital omnibus.

Clear exemption from Cyber Resilience Act for financial sector 
entities subject to DORA 
The overlap between the CRA and DORA gives rise to serious implementa-
tion challenges for the financial sector. The CRA introduces horizontal rules 
for digital products, while DORA establishes a comprehensive resilience 
framework tailored to the financial sector. The lack of coordination between 
these regulatory regimes entails a risk of creating overlapping obligations for 
financial institutions, which may lead to inefficient use of resources and at 
the same time run counter to the Commission’s objectives of regulatory co-
herence and competitiveness. 
The financial sector calls for a clear exemption from the CRA (via a delegated 
act, as prescribed in Article 2(5) of the CRA) for financial entities that are 
subject to DORA. Financial services delivered through digital channels are 
already covered by DORA, which imposes strict and comprehensive require-
ments on the ICT systems and services of financial entities. DORA covers the 
entire lifecycle of these systems  - from development to decommissioning  - 
and includes risk -based governance, incident management, vulnerability 
management, and strategies for communication with customers.

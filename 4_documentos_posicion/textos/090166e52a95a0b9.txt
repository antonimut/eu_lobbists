E.ON Consultation Response: Digital Omnibus 
 
 
 
 
 
E.ON welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on the Commission proposal for the Digital Omnibus.  
In general, E.ON welcomes the Commission's ambition for simplification and has repeatedly advocated for the reduction 
of bureaucracy and the streamlining and simplification of legislation at both national and European levels. 
Reducing bureaucracy is a key prerequisite for effectively advancing the energy transition and leveraging the great po-
tential of digitalisation in the EU. There exist opportunities for simplification, improvements and/or clarifications inter 
alia within the following legislative frameworks, outlined in greater detail in the documents attached: Data Act, GDPR, 
NIS2 and CER Directive and AI Act. 
 
Digital Omnibus on the Digital Acquis 
Data Act 
E.ON appreciates the European Commission’s clarification on the Data Act and welcomes the constructive approaches 
reflected in the draft of the Digital Omnibus. The integration of key elements of EU secondary legislation, such as the 
Open Data Directive and the Data Governance Act, into to the Data Act represents an important step towards simplifi-
cation and regulatory streamlining. We also welcome the clearer limitation of government access to private sector data 
to genuine public emergencies instead of the concept of “exceptional need”. 
Furthermore, E.ON appreciates the strengthened protection for trade secrets, including the explicit right for data holders 
to refuse disclosure in case of high risk of unlawful acquisition or misuse, or where disclosure may involve entities sub-
ject to certain third-country regimes. The reduction of cloud switching obligations, such as the exclusion of tailor-made 
solutions and the general limitation of switching requirements for service provided by SMEs, is also a positive develop-
ment.  
Building on these positive developments, E.ON would like to suggest several further improvements. Regarding specific 
provisions of the legislation, E.ON proposes the following points that should be reflected in the Digital Fitness Check: 
Simplification potential 
• With regard to personal data in product or related service data and where the user is not the data subject, the data 
holder shall be in a position to rely on authorisation from the user (who must be responsible for any breach of data 
protection law), unless the data holder is itself a controller of such personal data. 
• Statutory permission should be introduced to the Data Act for any transfer of personal data from the data holder to 
the user and/or any third party. It would also be helpful if the model contractual terms (MCTs) provided a clear legal 
basis for processing by data holders in accordance with Article 6(1)(b) GDPR (even if the user who signs the MCTs 
is not the data subject). 
Targeted recalibration of use rights under the Data Act 
• The ability of the data holder to gain value from data generated by the use of a product and/or related services it 
provides to the user is too limited and entails the risk of lack of incentives for the data holder to innovate.

• Usage of data for defined purposes of the data holder without prior consent by the user shall therefore remain pos-
sible and include purposes such as innovation, research and development, products safety, etc. A representative 
example: the usage of metering  and energy consumption data e.g. for billing purposes cannot be dependent on 
consent by the User (see above). 
• Regarding trade secrets, the current limitation of data holders’ rights remains too far-reaching, particularly regard-
ing their right to define technical protection measures that users or data recipients must apply to protect those trade 
secrets. Otherwise, this might jeopardize trade secrets of the data holder. This would also be more in line with the 
principles set forth in Directive (EU) 2016/943. In the current legislation, the safeguards for intellectual property 
rights of the data holder, including trade secrets, seem far too limited still. 
• The Data Act contains too limited financial incentives and protection of data holders’ investments in data -driven 
products and services. After all, it remains true that the making of such products and related services requires the 
investment of considerable human, technical as well as financial resources. This continues to justify the recognition 
of certain rights of use of the data holder to the data as well as an overall better recognition of the legitimate inter-
ests of the data holder.  To address this, i t should (at least) be clarified that products that are not used as such or 
generate data independently but only collect and/or forward data (from other connected assets) are not connected 
products within the meaning of the Data Act  (example: Home Energy Management Systems, HEMS). This would 
also eliminate contradictions with the definition of “product data” according to which only data generated by the 
‘use’ of the product is considered product data (HEMS boxes are not “used”).  
Clarification and coherence of sector-specific data access and use rights and their interplay with the Data Act 
• Applicability of the Data Act to Smart Meters: 
- Ownership ambiguity: Consumers typically do not own smart meters, nor lease / rent them, depending on Mem-
ber States‘ approach, complicating the definition of “user” under the Data Act. (Art. 2.12). It is thus unlikely that 
Smart Meters do fall under the Data Act; nevertheless, further clarification is still needed. 
- Even if Smart Meters were considered or will be considered a "connected product" under the Data Act, there 
must be limitations to user rights as the usage of metering data that is required for various purposes, including, 
but not limited to, billing, observability, system stability, and flexibility cannot be dependent on user consent. 
- The question of limitations of user rights under the Data Act is an important one. These rights cannot be unlim-
ited, just as the rights of the data subjects under GDPR also face specific limitations e.g. in social security con-
text, etc. Against this backgr ound it is important to clarify whether flexibility services (i.e., demand response 
services, grid flexibility offerings, energy balancing mechanisms, etc.) are legally considered “related services” 
under the Data Act, especially for Smart Meters. 
• Divergent Treatment of Smart Meters and Dedicated Measurement Devices (DMD): DMDs are more likely to meet 
Data Act criteria in terms of ownership, contractual use, or integration in appliances. This could lead to unequal 
consumer rights depending on the device used. 
• Terminology inconsistencies: “Eligible parties” (Electricity Directive) vs. “third parties” (Data Act) vs. “relevant mar-
ket participants” (Electricity Regulation). It seems, “eligible parties” implies some sort of limitation regarding to 
which 3rd party the data can be made available under IR 2023/1162; and the IR takes precedence over the Data Act. 
Lack of harmonised definitions create legal uncertainty as does a lack of coherence between various legal acts. 
• Coherence: It seems that Art. 44 (2) of the Data Act leaves room for its applicability even in cases where sector -
specific data access and use rights exist. Again, if the Smart Meter was a “connected product” in the sense of the 
Data Act, the user would pr imarily have the rights under IR 2023/1162. Still, outside the scope of IR 2023/1162, 
the Data Act might be applicable to Smart Meter data, and it remains unclear what this would entail exactly.

General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) 
E.ON appreciates the positive approaches taken by the Commission, such as the balanced approach to enabling the 
processing of personal data for AI development and operation while maintaining strong safeguards for individuals’ data. 
We also welcome the clarification distinguishing absolute from relative identifiability, specifying that data should only 
be considered “personal” where a given entity has the means reasonably likely to be used to identify the natural person 
concerned. 
The proposed measures to address consent fatigue, such as consent exemptions for certain types of cookies and the 
introduction of validity periods for both consent and refusal, represent a user -friendly improvement to the current 
framework. E.ON also welco mes the simplification of breach reporting obligations, including extending the reporting 
deadline to 96 hours and limiting mandatory notifications to data breaches likely to result in “high risk” for individuals. 
We further support the clarification that data subject access requests may be refused or subject to reasonable charges 
if they are excessive, abusive, or unfounded. 
E.ON proposes the following recommendations to further improve the GDPR which should be reflected in the Digital 
Fitness Check:  
• Processing of pseudonymised personal data shall be permitted whenever a strong pseudonymisation method is 
applied, not only for the predefined lawful bases listed in Art. 6(1)but also by recognising additional lawful bases, 
such as for the development of new products. Consequently, binding recommendations of strong pseudonymisation 
concepts shall be provided by competent bodies. Alternatively, it would be beneficial to receive binding recommen-
dations when personal data is considered anonymised.  
• The operation of critical infrastructure and the assurance of its security should be defined in the GDPR as a “task 
carried out in the public interest”, so that sector -specific, EU-wide uniform legal bases according to Article 6(1)(c) 
and 6(1)(e) in conjunction with Article 6(3) can be established. This aspect should also be incorporated into Article 
23(1) GDPR, to enable the exceptions listed there for data processing in connection with the operation and safe-
guarding of critical infrastructure. This would additionally reduce the requirements for subsequent purpose changes 
pursuant to Article 6(4) GDPR. 
• A more practical approach is needed as to when processing of personal data can be based on legitimate interest / 
statutory permission. 
Hands-on guidance for specific sectors - like the energy sector - could eliminate legal uncertainty when legitimate 
interest for a controller exists. Examples in the energy sector include grid operation and congestion management, 
flexibility, grid expansion and other processes that have demonstrable direct climate benefits. 
• Establishing clear rules is needed, whenever the transfer of personal data is based on SCC; conducting a TIA should 
follow a risk-based approach. 
A TIA should be conducted whenever there is a real risk to the rights and freedoms of a natural person, not just a 
remote, theoretical possibility. There is often the mere possibility of access to a very limited data set from a third 
country for a second-level support. Such possibility of access to a limited data set alone stands in no proportion to 
conduct a burdensome TIA. Small, low‑risk situations should not trigger a full, burdensome TIA 
• Ensure a more consistent approach to the application of the GDPR. 
Even in a single Member State there are often conflicting opinions from numerous data protection authorities. As a 
multi-national company, it is challenging and expensive to implement standardised processes across the group, 
when there are differing regula tory opinions across the EU. It also creates considerable uncertainties that entail a 
potentially high financial risk. 
• Assess the intricacies between GDPR and Data Act for data generated by connected products in energy critical in-
frastructures. The Data Act goes yet beyond some GDPR requirements, like for users’ rights to share data directly 
with third parties comprising also non-personal generated data.

Cybersecurity rules 
With regards to cybersecurity rules, E.ON appreciates the Commission’s approach to reduce administrative burden for 
companies facing overlapping reporting rules as well as enhancing legal certainty by harmonising requirements. 
E.ON welcomes the streamlining of reporting obligations for dual-trigger incidents. When an incident triggers reporting 
requirements under both NIS2 and CER Directive, organisations may now submit one coordinated report through a 
shared submission channel. Together with the introduction of a Single Entry Point for incident reporting, this represents 
a meaningful step towards more efficient and workable reporting obligations. The strengthened role of ENISA is also an 
appreciated move towards harmonised European technical rules. 
In addition, E.ON puts forward a number of recommendations to further enhance and simplify the NIS2 and CER Direc-
tives. 
NIS2 Directive 
Usage of components (Articles 18, 21): 
• “State of the art” is defined by technical standards rather than by political wishes. It must be clarified that expert 
communities can quickly and specifically define appropriate standards, and detailed technical specifications should 
not be lost in bureaucratic processes of authorities. Otherwise, overlapping and contradictions might occur. This is 
also why there should be no obligation for certification of any ICT products. This would narrow down markets and 
lead itself to additional risks.  
ICT product and service supply chain (Articles 5, 18): 
• “Security and Privacy by Design” includes the accountability for OEMs and solution providers of ICT/OT products 
for security standards. These parties have full overview of data processing and storage, have all capabilities includ-
ing direct access and should be obliged to provide full security services for all operators.  
Information sharing and cybersecurity reporting obligations (Article 20): 
• E.ON supports the obligation to report significant IT security incidents. However, reporting obligations should re-
main limited to previously defined, significant IT security incidents. In most cases, a warning within 24 hours cannot 
provide value due to missing essential information. Same applies, if a threat cannot be specified.  
• Multiple and competing reporting obligations and responsibilities must be avoided (especially regarding the CER 
Directive). It must be ensured that reports from entities to national CSIRTs are shared with Member States and then 
spread again to all potentially affected entities. Also, the handling of 0 -day vulnerabilities should be explicitly cov-
ered in the Directive. 
CER Directive 
• Overlapping regulations must be avoided, and regulation must be consistent on both European and national level. 
There should be a uniform scheme to identify critical operators: Only companies with cross-sectoral or cross-border 
impact should be covered by the CER Directive. Otherwise, the threshold for critical operators of national regulation 
should apply. 
• The task of maintaining the resilience of critical IT - infrastructures should also consider cross -sectoral aspects in 
the Directive. National authorities only have the task to define risk scenarios and the potential impact of individual 
operators on society. 
• Compliance with resilience requirements based on international standards should be sufficient, additional inspec-
tions should be avoided. There must be no additional legal requirements or bureaucracy for support services offered 
by the EU. This should be the accountability of Member States. 
• Information reported to the national authority should be forwarded to all other national authorities within the EU 
and from there back again to all operators of critical infrastructures.

Digital Omnibus on AI 
E.ON argues for a workable legislative framework on AI that keeps a practicable balance between protecting the safety 
and fundamental rights of people and fostering European innovation and competitiveness to realize the opportunities 
associated with this technology. 
• We agree with the Commission that the legal framework itself is not a sufficient basis for implementing the require-
ments for high-risk AI systems. We therefore support the proposal to postpone entry into force of the corresponding 
provisions.  
• In any case, we require a reasonable minimum period for implementation which should not be less than 24 months 
for high-risk AI systems starting only after publication of the Commission guidelines, standards and supporting doc-
uments, provided, this is ensured, the exact legal approach - fixed versus variable timing - is irrelevant to us. 
• AI Act, Art. 4 on AI literacy and AI Omnibus: E.ON shares the Commission's analysis that it is difficult if not impossible 
to enforce an obligation that must be implemented individually by each company. The legal obligation appears un-
necessary given the “natural” interest of companies in equipping their employees with the necessary qualifications. 
 
The definition of safety component in the operation and management of critical infrastructure must be clear, unambig-
uous, and restrictive , ensuring that only components with genuinely critical and direct safety function fall under this 
category. E.ON recommends introducing material and measurable threshold levels in the application of the AI Act to 
ensure that the assessment of disruption involving AI safety components is proportionate and impact based. To support 
consistency and proportionality, E.ON therefore suggests that the thresholds of the CER Directive, Art. 7, shall  be ap-
plied in the area of AI to determine the extent of a disruption or failure of critical infrastructure and its duration. 
• The AI Regulation itself does not contain any provisions defining the magnitude or the conditions under which the 
materiality threshold (“at large scale”/ “appreciable disruptions”/ “significant”) is deemed to be reached or exceeded 
in the event of a disru ption. E.ON proposes to introduce material threshold levels in the application of the AI Act 
with regard to the consequences of a disruption of AI safety components. Thresholds of the CER Directive, Art. 7, 
shall also be applied in the area of AI to determine the extent of disruption or failure of critical infrastructure and its 
duration, i.e. a failure should be deemed to lead to an appreciable disruption only if it affects a defined number of 
individuals for certain minimum duration of hours. An AI system would therefore only qualify as a safety component 
if its failure or malfunction may affect such number of people for such period of time. 
• Clarification is needed in a legally binding way regarding: 
- “large scale” and "appreciable disruptions”, which E.ON interprets as a “materiality threshold”, which includes 
the elements of “dimension/ extent” and a “temporal/ duration” aspect, 
- the nature of the (direct) connection between the system failure or malfunction and the risk for the physical 
integrity of critical infrastructure, including a clarification of what ‘direct’ means, especially in light of the exam-
ples of ‘safety components’ in Recital 55 that are said to ‘directly lead to risks’ or ‘protect directly’ ; in section 
“2.3 ‘Directly or indirectly’ caused” of its DRAFT GUIDANCE ARTICLE 73 AI ACT- INCIDENT REPORTING (HIGH-
RISK AI SYSTEMS) , the EU Commission expressed what constitutes a direct /indirect causation and provided 
examples for indirect causation; we believe that the examples mentioned would be an appropriate guidance to 
distinguish between direct and indirect causation in the context of “safety component”; we would be pleased to 
receive confirmation from the EU Commission on whether the findings from its DRAFT GUIDANCE ARTICLE 73 
AI ACT- INCIDENT REPORTING (HIGH-RISK AI SYSTEMS) on indirect causation can be transferred to help inter-
preting the “directness” of the causation required in Recitals 55 of the AI Act in relation to “safety component”, 
- regarding the reference to other risk-mitigating measures (switching measures, system redundancy, multiple 
layers of safety systems etc. in critical infrastructure), which may be used in parallel when assessing the “safety

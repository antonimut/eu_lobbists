I. General 
 
The Association of German Public Banks (VÖB) welcomes the European 
Commission’s objective to modernise , streamline and simplify the EU 
digital legislative framework while preserving a high level of protection for 
fundamental rights. For public -sector banks, promotional banks and 
Landesbanken, legally certain and operationally workable rules are of 
particular importance. They operate in a highly regulated environment and 
in many cases perform statutory or public-interest tasks. In such a setting, 
simplification must not be reduced to formal deregulation. What is needed 
is a framework that is coherent, propor tionate and genuinely usable in 
practice. 
 
Against this background, the Digital Omnibus Regulation, published by the 
European Commission (EU Commission) on 19 November 2025 , is a 
valuable opportunity to improve the interaction between the EU General 
Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)  and other horizontal and sector -
specific digital legislation, in particular the AI Act and DORA. The GDPR 
remains a cornerstone of the European legal order. At the same time, it was 
adopted in a very different technological context. The practical experience 
of recent years shows that targeted adjustments and clarifications are 
needed in order to reduce unnecessary administrative burden, close grey 
areas, and allow institutions to focus their resources on meaningful risk 
management and effective protection. 
 
VÖB therefore supports the overall direction of the proposal. However, 
several elements require further clarification or adjustment in order to 
deliver real legal certainty for financial institutions and public -sector 
banks. In particular, the proposal sho uld more clearly address the 
distinction between personal and non -personal data, create a robust and

II. Article 3 of the Digital Omnibus - Amendmends to the GDPR proposed by the 
European Commission 
 
1. Specification of the scope of application of data protection law – Article 4 GDPR 
VÖB welcomes the clarification in Article 4(1) GDPR and the proposed new Article 41a  to reflect recent 
CJEU case law . In practice, the distinction between personal and non -personal data, especially in 
relation to pseudonymised datasets, is one of the most important and at the same time one of the most 
difficult threshold questions for data-driven business models, data sharing and AI-related use cases. A 
clearer legal framework in this area would substantially improve legal certainty. 
 
At the same time, the notion of means that are ‘ reasonably likely to be used ’ can be interpreted in 
different ways. To ensure uniform application, the criteria for ‘reasonably likely’ means of identification 
must be further specified to include the cost, time, and actual technical effort required for re -
identification From a banking perspective, it is also important that the proposal does not weaken the 
controller’s ability to insist on an Article  28 GDPR arrangement where, in a concrete outsourcing 
relationship, this is considered necessary for reasons of legal certainty and prudential governance. 
 
 
2. Processing personal data for the development and operation of AI systems 
While VÖB generally supports the intention to create a specific and more reliable legal basis for the 
development and operation of AI systems and models , the current approach, which often requires a 
phase-by-phase analysis under general legal bases, is relatively complex and creates avoidable 
uncertainty. From a systematic point of view, however, it would be preferable to anchor such an AI -
related legal basis directly in Article 6(1) GDPR, rather than in a separate provision. This would make the 
relationship with the general architecture of the Regulation more transparent and easier to apply. 
 
Should the co-legislators decide to maintain the current structure with a separate Article 88c, it should 
at least be clarified that the limitation in Article 6(1) GDPR, second sentence, does not preclude reliance 
on that provision when public entities are processing data in the context of public -interest tasks. This 
is particularly relevant for promotional and development banks, as well as other public -sector 
institutions that rely on digital and AI -supported processes when carrying out their statutory dut ies. 
Furthermore, the new framework should not impose additional vague requirements on top of existing 
GDPR and AI Act obligations. The proposal should establish legal certainty, not create a new source of 
interpretative friction. Furthermore, VÖB calls for the deletion of the unconditional right to object in 
Article 88c(2) of the GDPR, as this proposed right is more restrictive than Article 21(1) of the GDPR and 
ignores the technical impossibility of deleting data from already trained AI models. 
VÖB also welcomes the new possibilities for processing special categories of personal data for AI 
training and bias mitigation, as well as biometric data for authentication , provided that  the data

remains under the sole control of the data subject. These elements can support the development of 
trustworthy AI  systems, secure digital onboarding  processes and robust customer authentication  
methods. However, for legal certainty and consistent enforcement, indeterminate concepts such as 
‘prevent as far as possible’ and ‘effectively protect’ should be further specified. In this context, it would 
be useful to recognise common privacy -enhancing techniques , such as tokenisation, strict access 
controls, purpose -based segregation and appropriate model -governance measures , as suitable 
safeguards in this context. 
 
 
3. Limiting rights to information and access in a proportionate manner – Article 12, 13 GDPR 
VÖB supports the amendment to Article 12(5) GDPR, which is intended to address manifestly unfounded 
or excessive requests, including the abusive use of access rights. It is important that data-subject rights 
remain free of charge in normal cases. At the same time, the legal framework must allow controllers to 
react to clearly abusive patterns in a consistent and predictable manner. Therefore, EU-level guidance 
with practical examples would be highly desirable in order to avoid diverging national thresholds. 
 
VÖB also welcomes the proposed new Article 13(4) GDPR. A simplified information regime for situations 
involving a clearly defined relationship, low data intensity and a reasonable expectation of being 
informed is a pragmatic way to reduce information fatigue without lowering the level of protection. To 
prevent fragmentation in practice, however, the relevant notions should be further operationalised, for 
example through safe-harbour examples or standard templates for typical low -risk banking products 
and services. Beyond the current proposal, VÖB continues to consider a structured two-step approach 
to Articles 13 and 14 GDPR to be appropriate, under which core information is provided up front and 
more detailed information is made readily accessible in a second layer. 
 
 
4. Automated individual decision-making – Article 22 GDPR 
VÖB supports moving away from the concept of treating automated individual decision -making as a 
prohibition in principle. A framework that defines the conditions under which automated decisions are 
permitted, combined with safeguards such as transparency, human review, and the right to contest, is 
more consistent with the realities of modern financial services, as well as with the regulatory logic of 
the AI Act. This approach also recognises that, when designed and governed appropriately, digital 
processes can improve accuracy, consistency and accessibility. 
 
To achieve full legal certainty, however, the revised provision should be accompanied by a clarification 
of what constitutes 'legal effects' or 'similarly significant effects', with sector -specific examples 
provided, such as credit scoring, account opening , and fraud prevention. Similarly, the interface 
between Article 22 of the GDPR and the Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment under the AI Act should 
be clarified, to ensure that institutions do not face overlapping obligations that are not aligned in 
concept.

5. Notification of personal data breaches – Article 33 GDPR 
VÖB welcomes the proposal to limit the notification obligation under Article 33 GDPR to cases that are 
likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals. This is an important 
proportionality correction. VÖB also supports extending the deadline beyond the current 72 hours, 
since practical experience shows that a short reporting window often leads to incomplete notifications 
and weak factual substantiation. 
 
From an operational and legal -certainty perspective, however, a rigid hour -based deadline remains 
problematic, especially where incidents occur shortly before weekends or public holidays. In such 
cases, controllers may be pushed into premature reporting me rely to protect themselves against 
procedural risk. VÖB therefore favours a working -day based approach, for example by aligning the 
calculation of the period with the method reflected in Regulation No 1182/71. At a minimum, the revised 
rule should ensure that the longer period actually provides meaningful additional time for fact-finding. 
This approach would ensure higher quality and more substantiated reports. 
 
 
6. Simplifying data protection impact assessments 
VÖB supports the idea of creating an EU -wide list of processing operations that require  a data 
protection impact assessment. A more uniform approach would reduce fragmentation and make it 
easier to predict the requirements for cross-border business models. However, such a list should be c 
risk-based and complemented by clear criteria for when an assessment is not required. 
 
In addition, the relationship between the GDPR data protection impact assessment and the 
Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment under the AI Act should be explicitly addressed. Where the two 
assessments overlap in scope, institutions should be able to combine them into one integrated 
assessment process. This would prevent unnecessary duplication of work and documentation, 
minimise the risk of conflicting assessments across intern al functions and enhance regulatory 
coherence. Joint guidance from the relevant EU bodies would be especially valuable in this area. 
 
 
7. Further need for improvement in the GDPR 
Beyond the amendments already proposed by the Commission, VÖB sees further need for improvement 
in several areas that are particularly relevant to banks and public-sector institutions.  
 
• First, the accountability principle should be applied in a more clearly risk-based and 
proportionate manner . Existing documentation under sectoral regimes such as DORA and 
comparable supervisory frameworks should be recognised more systematically in order to 
avoid parallel documentation exercises that do not generate additional protection. 
 
• Secondly, the GDPR should acknowledge the primacy of sector-specific data protection 
legislation more clearly. Credit institutions are subject to numerous specialised banking 
supervisory requirements (such as DORA or national risk management standards), which 
necessitate the processing of personal data. Rather than the individual controller, it is the

responsibility of the legislator to ensure that these specific legal regulations are compatible 
with the GDPR. To avoid legal contradictions and prevent companies from being overwhelmed 
by conflicting mandates, the GDPR must clearly take precedence over su ch sector -specific 
regulations. 
 
• Thirdly, the Regulation should address responsibility along the technological value chain more 
coherently. In particular, Article 25 GDPR should better reflect the role of manufacturers and 
providers of digital products and services , whose design choices often determine whether 
controllers can comply in practice. Similary, the concept of joint controllership in Article 26 
GDPR is too general and should be refined to prevent disproportionate organisational burdens 
and liability consequences in cases where th e parties’ influence on the relevant processing 
differs significantly. 
 
 
III. Single entry-point for incident reporting 
 
VÖB supports the objective of making incident reporting across EU digital legislation more efficient and 
consistent. The proposed Single Entry Point (SEP) represents a meaningful contribution to reducing 
administrative burdens for companies. Importantly, the SEP does not entail institutional or regulatory 
shifts. Rather, it focuses exclusively on the technical simplifica tion of submitting notifications while 
leaving the existing supervisory structure unchanged. The fact that the portal itself is not intende d to 
store data related to notifications further underlines that no new central data repository is being 
created. 
 
At the same time, the SEP has the potential to significantly reduce duplicate reporting. Today, incidents 
may be reportable in parallel under several legal frameworks, for example under the NIS2 Directive, the 
GDPR, or —within the financial sector — DORA. From the banking sector, VÖB is  well aware of the 
considerable operational effort this can entail. Without a SEP, this means dealing with multiple 
reporting portals, different formats, different timelines, and different competent authorities.  A single 
access point can therefore help reduce multiple reporting obligations while preserving the existing 
responsibilities of the relevant authorities. 
 
We would appreciate, if the lessons learnt from the financial sector under DORA, where a high degree 
of harmonisation of incident reporting already exists today, would be reflected. Reporting is carried 
out uniformly under DORA. DORA applies as lex specialis in relation to NIS2 and CER, however, further 
substantive harmonisation of existing reports under DORA, NIS2 and beyond may be meaningful. 
Incident reports under the GDPR and the AI Act, by contrast, differ significantly in terms of objectives, 
content and structure—both from each other and compared to reports under DORA, NIS2 and CER. 
Substantive. Here a harmonisation of fields, definitions and timelines could be a relevant first step to 
ease the current burden and provide meaningful information which can be aggregated and 
feedbacked in order to help to increase resilience even more.

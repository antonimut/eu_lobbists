Key takeaways 
▪ EMMA and ENPA support the simplification of the digital regulatory framework and 
strengthening Europe’s competitiveness. However, the Digital Omnibus’ proposed new rules 
on cookies and consent would have the opposite effect.  
▪ Imposing centralised consent settings and prohibiting individual consent requests at website 
level would lead to a dramatic decline in consent rates for digital services and turn browsers 
and similar software into new ‘consent gatekeepers’. 
▪ Digital press offerings depend on data processing for their functioning and financing, and such 
processing can only happen with user consent. Where said consent is required, the law must 
also enable press publishers to request and manage consents autonomously. 
▪ Attempting to reintroduce centralised consent, despite previous rejection during the ePrivacy 
Regulation negotiations and furthermore without an impact assessment , must be opposed 
and Art. 88b of the GDPR draft should be deleted. 
▪ The “media exemption” in Art. 88b(3) is a positive signal but insufficient in practice as it does 
not cover the media’s indispensable partners and vendors.  
▪ We welcome the intention to support media financing, which can be achieved with 
amendments to Art. 88a of the GDPR draft. 
▪ Art. 88a(4)(a) should be deleted. It imposes a one-click “Reject all” button at the first layer of 
the consent interface. This would reduce consent rates, impairing the financing of digital press 
offerings, and it is incompatible with Consent or Pay models.  
▪ Art. 88a(4)(b) and (c) should also be deleted. While limiting the frequency of consent requests, 
the system would be unworkable. Purposes, partners, and services change more frequently 
than both the duration of consent and the six months opt-out period.  
▪ To introduce genuine simplifications and enhance competitiveness, we recommend 
strengthening legitimate interest as a legal basis for data processing.

A. Summary 
EMMA and ENPA share the objectives of simplifying the digital regulatory framework and strengthening 
Europe’s competitiveness. However, the proposed rules on cookies and consent (Articles 88a(4) and 
88b of the GDPR draft, as introduced by Article 3(15) of the Digital Omnibus) would have the opposite 
effect, further set back the European digital economy and  endanger the provision and financing of 
digital press offerings. 
Digital publications depend, for their operation and financing, on obtaining and managing a wide range 
of specific user consents. According to existing European data protection law and to its interpretation, 
essential functions, including interest -based advertising, audience analytics, reach measurement, 
content embedding, fraud prevention, and more, can only be carried out with specific user consent. 
Where policymakers and Data Protection Authorities make the functioning of digital press offerings 
dependent on user consent, the right of  press publications to request and manage consents 
autonomously must be recognized as a core component of press freedom. By obstructing the 
collection of such consents and effectively prohibiting consent requests for key functions, Articles 
88a(4) and 88b of the GDPR draft put almost all core functions of digital publishing at risk. 
This applies in particular to the indispensable financing of editorial operations through personalised 
advertising, which is of critical importance for a very large number of press publishers. 
▪ This is especially evident for press outlets that are primarily advertising -funded and publish 
their editorial content online without a paywall. These publications are important for citizens 
to inform themselves beyond the media offerings they pay for, or for those who cannot or opt 
not to pay for press content. It can be estimated that purely advertising-funded editorial media 
generate – and would lose – up to 90% of their revenues from interest-based advertising. 
▪ Additional advertising revenue is often indispensable  also for publications that generate 
distribution revenues via subscriptions and paywalls, to finance the freely accessible articles 
that lead users to subscri be. For digital press offerings with paid content, revenues from 
interest-based advertising may account for up to 30% of total revenues. 
▪ Digital distribution and advertising revenues are just as essential online as they are in print. In 
addition, digital offerings must compensate for the  ongoing decline in revenues from falling 
print circulation in order to finance editorial work, business operations, and investment. 
Without these revenues, a free press cannot be sustained.  
The focus must therefore be on regulatory conditions that improve the revenue base of digital press, 
not interventions that undermine still functioning business models and add red tape. 
Against this backdrop, we appreciate the attempt to safeguard the financial sustainability of the media 
in the proposed Digital Omnibus. In fact, while Article 88b imposes centralised consent settings and 
prohibits specific consent requests at website level, Article 88b(3) exempts media services from such 
requirements. This is welcome and may be helpful for functions such as first -party analytics, service 
integrity, and simple advertising formats that do not involve third-party data processing. 
Nevertheless, we recommend deleting Article 88b as a whole , as mandatory centrali sed consent 
would lead to a dramatic decline in consent rates for digital services, turn browsers and similar 
software into ‘consent gatekeepers’, favour already dominant platforms, and weaken Europe’s digital 
economy. For these reasons, centralised consent was rejected during the negotiations on the ePrivacy 
Regulation. Therefore, reintroducing centralised consent despite this rejection and without an impact 
assessment must be  opposed, and any attempt to prioritise automated general settings over 
individual, specific consent requests be removed.

Even without a centrali sed consent obligation, the current draft still would still make it much more 
difficult for service providers to collect the consent required for the data processing at the basis of 
their functioning. This is all the more surprising in a proposal that striv es for simplification and 
maintains consent as the predominant basis for most data processing.  
In fact, Art. 88a(4)(a) would introduce a mandatory single-click “Reject all” button at the first layer of 
the consent request. This proposal represents a significant tightening of the current legal framework, 
would substantially reduce consent rates, impair the financing of digital press offerings , and  is 
incompatible with indispensable Consent or Pay models. As such, this paragraph should be deleted. 
Furthermore, 88a(4)(b) prohibits renewed consent requests until consent remains valid, while Article 
88a(4)(c) imposes a six-month prohibition on renewed consent requests following a refusal. However, 
purposes, partners, and services typically change more frequently than the duration of consent / than 
every six months, and such updates require renewing consent, which is prevented by these provisions.  
This could discourage innovation, as it would be unworkable for publishers to show users different 
versions of their website depending on when they were asked for consent – in addition, without data 
processing, publishers are often unable  to determine whether or when a user previously refused  
consent. Moreover, it is unclear how these provisions can be reconciled with Consent or Pay models. 
Therefore, both paragraphs should be deleted. 
Nonetheless, the Digital Omnibus should not only refrain from introducing new  restrictions and 
interventions, but it should also introduce genuine simplifications and enhance competitiveness. In 
particular, legitimate interest as a legal basis for data processing should be strengthened. If, for 
reasons of competitiveness, the Digital Omnibus allows data processing for AI training on the basis of 
legitimate interest, this should all the more  be possible for the far more limited, transparent, and 
transient data processing required to finance and operate a free press. 
In conclusion, w ithout the changes described above and more in detail below , the Digital Omnibus 
risks further weakening the economic foundations of the free press and undermining its own stated 
objectives of simplification, innovation, and enhanced competitiveness.

B. EMMA-ENPA Position  
EMMA and ENPA , which together represent tens of thousands of newspaper and magazine titles 
across the EU, both in print and digital, strongly support the European Commission’s stated objectives 
of simplification, reduction of administrative burdens and strengthening Europe’s digital 
competitiveness in the Digital Omnibus Proposal. These objectives are urgently needed, including for 
European press and media companies operating in an increasingly concentrated and Gatekeeper-
dominated digital environment. 
However, the Digital Omnibus ’s Article 3  (15) would introduce into the General Data Protection 
Regulation (GDPR) new provisions on cookies and consent, namely Articles 88a and 88b (hereafter, 
just “Article 88a” and “Article 88b”), that  risk producing the opposite of competitiveness and 
simplification. Instead, they would introduce a profound restructuring of the consent framework that 
cannot be regarded as a simple review exercise and, despite the appreciated media exemption, would 
seriously undermine the digital business models of publishers and other European digital companies. 
Already data-rich Gatekeepers will not be impacted in the same manner.  
Due to their negative impact on the digital economy and on competition, such far-reaching changes to 
the consent framework should not have been proposed without a proper impact assessment . It is 
essential that legislators at least allow sufficient time for careful consideration and avoid precipitating 
the legislative process to avoid introducing unnecessary complexity and harmful effects. 
We must reiterate that ever stricter data protection laws have led to a legal framework in which the 
digital press can only be operated – let alone financed – with the consent of a large number of individual 
readers. The design and delivery of editorial content, website integrity, audience measurement, 
financing through advertising  – including safeguards against ad -blockers, the launch of new and 
innovative products and features , and sales to readers online (such as digital  subscriptions) are all 
essential conditions for the existence of the digital press. 
Most of these functions are not possible without data processing and, in most cases, consent.  
Contrary to a common misconception, the digital press does not rely on consent and data processing 
solely for advertising and financing. In other words, digital publishing is structurally dependent on the 
ability to request, obtain and implement consent for various purposes. 
Where individual consent is required by law  - and the Digital Omnibus does not change this 
requirement  in most cases - it must always be ensured that (i) publishers are able to freely and 
effectively request and manage consent , (ii) that consent given at website must be effectively 
implemented and not ignored, overridden or blocked by software settings, consent managers or other 
gatekeepers, add-ons or intermediaries, and (iii) that publishers can make access to their professional 
editorial content dependent on obtaining consent  for purposes necessary to finance their products , 
including targeted advertising and audience measurement.  
Against this background, we welcome that the proposal aims to acknowledge the role of the press and 
media in Article 88b(3) by exempting the media sector from the damaging centralised consent 
obligation, although this exemption remains insufficient in scope. At the same time, Articles 88a and 
88b raise serious concerns for publishers and their business models, which must be addressed if the 
Omnibus is to remain aligned with its stated goals.  
Moreover, a significant step for effective simplification for digital services would be to clearly legitimise 
and promote the use of other legal basis such as legitimate interest, releasing several practices and 
purposes from the boundaries of the consent criteria, without prejudice to specific DMA obligations 
for designated gatekeepers. If competitiveness and the Apply AI’s Strategy goal to promote AI in the EU 
are providing the justification to move to legitimate interest for data processing for AI traini ng,

competitiveness and fostering a pluralistic and flourishing press and media ecosystem in the EU 
should provide enough political incentives to move to legitimate interest for the press and media too. 
I. Digital Press and Media and Articles 88a and 88b  
1. Article 88b and the Media Exception 
The obligation under Art . 88b to accept centralised consent and the prohibition to make  individual 
consent requests will lead to a significant decline in consent rates for all services that depend on such 
consent. In this respect, Art . 88b contains precisely those elements that were, for good reasons, 
rejected during the negotiations on the ePrivacy Regulation and in the so-called “Cookie-Pledge”. 
Against this background, we see no justification for reintroducing in the Digital Omnibus without any 
impact assessment a proposal that has already failed and has proven harmful to the European digital 
economy as a whole (see also Section II). The deletion of this provision is therefore necessary. 
Nonetheless, we welcome and appreciate that Article 88b(3) at least aims at exempting media service 
providers from the centralised consent obligation under Article 88b. Should Art 88b not be deleted , 
this exception, which unfortunately is insufficient in scope, becomes indispensable for ensuring 
a minimum level of protection for the provision and financing of editorial press publications, so 
it must be clarified to address its shortcomings and upheld throughout the legislative process. 
As such, any effective media exemption must be designed in a way that takes market realities into 
account. Two critical points must be stressed on this front: 
First, it must be clear that browsers, standardisation organisations and similar intermediaries may not 
technically block or neutralise consent requests and signals by media services that fall under the 
exception. Recital 46 points in this direction, but legal certainty is essential and this protection is not 
sufficiently anchored in the operative provisions. Furthermore, it must be ensured that this exemption 
applies to all media services as defined in Art. 2(2) of the European Media Freedom Act (EMFA), without 
need for the media to prove their status via registries etc.   
Moreover, the exception must also reflect the realities that press publishers operate in. Unfortunately, 
according to paragraph 3, only media providers as controllers are allowed to ask for consent. This 
means that publishers can ask for their own consent, but not for their indispensable partners such as 
advertising vendors that act as independent controllers, audience measurement providers, etc. 
Should publishers not be allowed to ask for consent for their partners on their website, the media 
exception will in practice be largely meaningless. The existing exemption for audience measurement 
in Article 88a(3)(c) is insufficient for the purposes of the media, as the “own use” limitation does not 
reflect the collaboration between media and audience measurement companies. 
Publishing editorial content in the digital environment requires sustainable financing through different 
combinations of subscription and advertising revenue. As the audience and markets of each 
publication are different, there is no one-size-fits-all. Nonetheless, practice shows that financing from 
advertising can only be achieved if the advertising is targeted at the interests of the user, while purely 
contextual advertising does not yield sufficient revenues.  
This applies, on the one hand, to press outlets that are essentially advertising-funded and publish their 
editorial content online without a paywall. Such publications are of particular importance for those 
citizens who wish to inform themselves beyond the media they pay for, or who cannot afford or do not 
wish to pay for press content. It can be estimated that such purely advertising-funded editorial media 
generate—and would stand to lose—up to 90% of their revenues from interest-based advertising. 
6 
 
 
 
At the same time , additional advertising revenue is often also indispensable for press offerings that 
generate distribution revenues through subscriptions, both behind the paywall and in front of it, in the 
context of freely accessible articles that lead users to subscribe. For digital press offerings with paid 
content, revenues from interest-based advertising can amount to as much as 30% of total revenues. 
If access to professional press and journalism is to be guaranteed for as many people as possible while 
at the same time enabling the financing of the media, it is necessary to maintain the possibility of 
obtaining consent from a large number of readers, including in the context of “Consent or Pay” models. 
If centralised, automated consent rejections at browser level were binding on media services, it would 
become practically impossible to reach readers and obtain the necessary consent. In that scenario, 
the economic and operational basis for market-financed digital press would be severely weakened. 
Furthermore, despite the exception for Art. 88b, media service providers are still subject to the 
requirements of Art. 88a(4) for consent requests, that might also undermine the effectiveness of the 
exception in practice. As such, and also in case Article 88b is rightfully deleted, we recommend to 
uphold the spirit of the media exemption and the commitment to media financing and  use them as 
guiding principles to amend Art. 88a, including deleting paragraph 4 that would effectively undermine 
media financing  as described below. 
Lastly, we believe that it would be appropriate to consider a change in paradigm . In fact, the data 
processing to provide and support the digital press, including for targeted advertising, is not in itself 
harmful for users . On the contrary, it brings them attractive digital offerings and more relevant 
advertisements, thus reducing the overall amount of advertising displayed on the website. It is only the 
misuse of data processing and targeted advertising for other purposes that can be problematic. 
From this perspective, it might be appropriate to consider allowing publishers and cooperating 
vendors to process data, including for personalised advertising, without requiring consent and on the 
basis of legitimate interest, without prejudice to the possibility to ask for payment instead. If targeted 
advertising is carried out fairly and proportionately, including the right to opt -out, it poses no risk  to 
users. This would also be a more effective way tackle “consent fatigue” as it limits the situations where 
online services are required to obtain user consent, without undermining the level of data protection 
as users remain in control. This should also come without prejudice to the DMA and to more stringent 
solutions for designated gatekeepers, that have first -party access to an incomparable amount and 
detail of personal data due to the size and scale of their profile-based systems. 
2. Article 88a(4)(a): Mandatory “Reject All” Button 
Article 88a(4)(a) introduces an obligation to include a single-click “reject all” button in the first layer of 
consent requests. This requirement further restricts the GDPR framework, which deliberately does not 
impose such a rigid design obligation, and should not be imposed without an impact assessment. 
For digital press publications, this measure would have far -reaching negative effects. It would 
significantly reduce consent rates and thus directly impair financing, audience measurement and 
product development. Many digital editorial offerings already operate on extremely narrow margins or 
even with losses . Further reducing their ability to monetise digital audiences would increase the 
economic pressure and negatively impact the digital transition and development.   
Particularly problematic is – as mentioned above - the interaction with the well-established “Consent 
or Pay” models. Under these models, users can either consent to data processing for interest -based 
advertising (“ consent”) or pay a monetary fee for access (“pay”). These models are designed to 
reconcile data protection requirements with sustainable financing and user choice  and have been 
developed with national Data Protection Authorities (DPAs) and upheld in court both at national level 
and at the European Court of Justice.  
7 
 
 
 
A mandatory “reject all” button on the consent-based access path would effectively introduce a third 
option that undermines the logic of the binary Consent or Pay model. If users can reject all processing 
without choosing either consent or payment, Consent or Pay models cease to function. A de facto 
prohibition of Consent or Pay models cannot be the intention of an Omnibus that aims to boost 
competitiveness, yet Article 88a(4)(a) may produce exactly that effect.  
In any case, users should not be entitled to access professional editorial content without neither 
consent nor payment, so any new measure must come without prejudice for the possibility for media 
to make access conditional to a form of remuneration. In a digital economy, not being able to use 
certain user data represents a financial loss that must be compensated , hence the request to pay 
when a user declines consent. If this option were removed, press publishers would be forced, in order 
to survive, to inc rease subscription prices. In the current economic context, this could lead to a 
decline in subscriptions, with users increasingly turning to free online sources for information. These 
sources do not necessarily meet the same professional and editorial standards as established media 
outlets. Revenue generated through the “Consent or Pay” model also enables us to maintain high -
quality journalism, which in turn plays a crucial role in supporting democratic societies.  
Therefore, the obligation to include a one-click “reject all” button should be deleted.  
3. Article 88a(4)(b): Prohibition on renewed consent requests while consent remains valid 
This provision prohibits controllers, once consent has been given, from requesting consent again for 
the same purpose for as long as the original consent remains valid. Such a prohibition is not practically 
workable, as consent will in many cases remain va lid for long periods of time, while new processing 
partners, changes to services involving additional or partially modified consent requirements, and 
other circumstances necessitating renewed consent typically arise at much shorter intervals. 
In all such cases, it must remain possible to request consent again  as alternatives are not possible. 
For example splitting consent requests into user-specific, individualised banners depending on how 
many of the requested consents have already been given is neither technically feasible nor 
reasonable. The same applies to the alternative of having to offer users an individualised “old” version 
of a product, depending on the scope of consents already granted, until the relevant period expires. 
The prohibition should therefore be deleted. 
4. Article 88a(4)(c): Six-Month Ban on Renewed Consent Requests 
Article 88a( 4)(c) provides that, once a user has rejected consent, the publisher may not request 
consent again for six months. For press publishers, this raises severe practical and legal problems  
and, again, would lead to effects opposite to the intended objectives of the Omnibus proposal.  
The measure will have a significant negative effect on financing, innovation and readers’ access to new 
and innovative features. For example, the provision would deprive publishers of the flexibility to adapt 
to changing circumstances, to introduce new features, introduce new advertising partners, or updated 
processing purposes etc, because such changes or new features are mostly data -driven and would 
require new consent.  
If a reader declines consent but a publisher rolls out a new feature or service, it will then lead to a 
scenario where certain readers will be offered the new feature, while others would have to wait several 
months until a publisher may request consent again. This cannot be the intention of a proposal that 
aims to strengthen digital competitiveness and innovation.  
In case Art. 88b is not deleted, and even if the media could ask for consent on behalf of their advertising 
partners, this provision would be unworkable . To provide some examples: Vendors within the 
8 
 
 
 
Transparency and Consent Framework ( TCF) ecosystem change their data very frequently, and 
Consent Management Platforms (CMPs) are required to keep up with these changes. If they fail to do 
so, vendors withdraw their offerings. Under the proposed rules, this would mean that certain 
advertising offerings would have to be removed for up to six months, until CMPs are able to re-collect 
and centralize user consents. This would result in a substantial financial loss. 
Moreover, innovation within publishing houses would be significantly reduced. Advertising deals are 
negotiated on a continuous basis, but this framework would mean that new deals could only be 
implemented every six months , at different moments depending on when users are presented the 
consent request. Publishers would also be required to automate or manually verify which users have 
already given consent for these new advertising partners. Until consent is renewed, these users would 
not be eligible to receive the corresponding ads. For example, if a user gives their initial consent on 1 
January upon their first visit to a website, and new advertising deals are introduced on 1 February, how 
can publishers ensure that this user does not receive ads from those n ew partners? The operational 
burden would be enormous and, in practice, unworkable. 
In addition , the implications for Consent or Pay models  are concerning. A binary model based on 
consent or pay does not have a “decline” button in the sense of Art 88a(4). If a reader decides against 
giving consent and either leaves the website without completing the payment, or only pays for a period 
shorter than six months , may the publisher present the consent -or-pay choice again at a later time  
within six months from the initial request, or would only the pay option be allowed, as a choice to reject 
has already been made for that period ? If this is not clarified, publishers may have to exclude users 
from their offerings for months simply because no legally-viable financing option remains.   
Last, in most cases, publishers cannot reliably identify when a particular visitor has previously 
rejected consent. The majority of readers are not logged in and recognising them would itself require 
identifiers – which raises new challenges once consent has been refused.  
Therefore, this prohibition should be deleted.  
II. Other Digital Offers of Publishers and Article 88b  
As mentioned in section I.1 ., e ven with the media exception, Article 88b would have serious 
consequences for publishers, and in particular on their  non-editorial digital services. As part of the 
digital transformation of the press, many traditional offerings of the press such as job listings, 
classifieds, blogs, forums  and marketplaces have been separated from editorial products and 
developed into standalone digital services. In addition, publishers have built new commercial 
offerings based on their editorial expertise. 
Although these services are not “media services” in the narrow sense, they remain economically vital. 
They contribute significantly to the financing of publishing houses and thereby indirectly to  the 
financing of journalistic-editorial content. 
The proposed centralised consent management in Art . 88b would lead to a dramatic decline in 
consent rates for all services that rely on consent-based processing. 
Users typically refuse consent in abstract, general settings for unknown services, while granting 
consent in concrete situations to services they trust and value. The moment of accessing a specific 
service is therefore the  only appropriate point for an informed and meaningful decision. Preventing 
such individual requests in favour of abstract, centralised rejections amounts to a de facto prohibition 
of many consent-based purposes. 
9 
 
 
 
Moreover, Article 88b would elevate browsers, browser plug-ins and similar intermediaries to new 
consent gatekeepers. These actors, in some cases already Gatekeepers under the DMA, would steer 
which services receive data and which do not, replacing individual user choices at service level with 
platform-controlled defaults. Furthermore, the proposal does not address several related issues, such 
as prohibiting browsers / extension providers from asking websites to pay for the “intermediation 
service” of managing consent, prohibiting the preferential treatment of individual websites, etc. 
Past and current experiences with digital Gatekeepers demonstrate the strong economic incentives 
for such actors to steer data flows in their own interest. The result is likely to be further market 
concentration and data supremacy by already dominant Gatekeepers  and reduced innovation by 
smaller European providers. As such competition issues and general concerns for the functioning of 
the digital economy have already led to the rejection of centralised consent solutions in past proposals 
and initiatives, the attempt to r eintroducing centralised consent via the Digital Omnibus without an 
impact assessment is incomprehensible and would mark a significant and unjustified policy reversal. 
III. Conclusion 
Press publishers’ associations welcome the Commission’s intention to simplify regulation and 
strengthen Europe’s digital competitiveness. However, new Articles 88a (4) and 88b GDPR  would 
undermine these goals. They would make it  substantially harder, not easier, for publishers to obtain 
the consent that is legally required to operate, develop and finance digital press offerings.  This will 
further worsen the already tense financial situation of publishers at a time when the European 
Commission is trying to im prove their situation through other measures (e.g. European Democracy 
Shield). From the consumers' perspective, such solution s will lead to a reduction in the range of 
services available to them, or at least those that are accessible without payment. 
Without the suggested changes , the Digital Omnibus risks further weakening the already fragile 
economic foundations of independent, market-financed press in Europe – and thereby moving away 
from its stated objectives of simplification, competitiveness and fair digital markets. Therefore, we call 
on the co-legislators to consider our suggestions and allow sufficient time for a thorough assessment 
of this proposal and its systemic effects, and to strive for legislative quality and effective simplification. 
Ultimately, if the EU truly aims to foster innovation and competitiveness while maintaining a high level 
of data protection, it must move away from a system that formally relies on consent while making 
consent increasingly difficult to request and obtain in practice. Simplification cannot mean preserving 
consent as the default legal basis while gradually undermining it. Either consent must remain 
workable, or greater reliance should be placed on legitimate interest, combined with tra nsparency, 
balancing and effective opt -out rights. It is particularly difficult to justify tightening consent 
requirements for publishers’ limited and well -understood data processing, while the same Digital 
Omnibus proposal allows AI training – a far more intrusive and risk-prone activity – to rely on legitimate 
interest rather than consent.

 
 
 
ZVEI position 
 
Digital Omnibus on AI 
 
The AI Act hinders the innovation and competitiveness of European industry in key areas. There is 
an urgent need for reform in three areas in particular in order to reduce double regulation and bur-
dens on industry. Our key demands are: 
1. The industrial use of AI is already covered by sectoral regulation. The legal acts in Annex I (A), in 
particular the Machinery Regulation and the Radio Equipment Directive, should therefore be 
moved to Annex I (B) of the AI Act in order to avoid double regulation and inconsistencies. 
2. The deadlines should be extended as soon as possible, as industry cannot prepare for the imple-
mentation of the AI Act without the availability of harmonised European standards. From the ZVEI's 
point of view, 24 months is a realistic timeframe. 
3. The implementation of the transparency requirements under Article 50 is also causing a great deal 
of adjustment work for industry. The corresponding deadline should therefore be extended by 12 
months to 2 August 2027. 
 
The European AI Act has been in force since 1 August 2024. Initial experience with the new regulation al-
ready makes it clear that the goal of low-bureaucracy and legally secure regulation of artificial intelligence 
will not be achieved. The EU Commission has recognised this problem and presented a proposal on 19 No-
vember to simplify the AI Act ("Digital Omnibus on AI").1 In addition, on 16 December, the European Com-
mission presented a proposal to simplify the Medical Device Regulation and the In Vitro Diagnostic Regula-
tion, according to which these two legal acts would be moved from Annex I (A) to Annex I (B) of the AI Act.  
 
The ZVEI supports these plans because, particularly in the field of industrial AI, the AI Act creates serious 
hurdles and uncertainties that damage the innovation potential and competitiveness of European industry. 
From the perspective of the electrical and digital industry, ambiguities and inconsistencies arising from the 
interaction of the AI Act with other legal acts of the New Legislative Framework (NLF), such as the Machinery 
Regulation, the Radio Equipment Directive and the Medical Device Regulation, are particularly serious.  
 
The ZVEI therefore calls for the following points to be addressed in order to limit the AI Act to the essentials 
and harness the economic and innovation potential of industrial AI in Europe:  
 
Our position 
 
• Sector-specific regulation of industrial AI: MDR/IVDR review as a model (Art. 6) 
A key problem with the AI Act is its inconsistencies and unclear interaction with NLF legal acts. One ex-
ample of a lack of consistency with NLF terminology is the differing definitions of "safety component" in 
the Machinery Regulation and the AI Act. There is also uncertainty in the area of the Radio Equipment 
Directive, for example due to the overlap between "safety" and "security". As a result, it is still unclear to 
many companies whether their products fall under the high-risk AI requirements of the AI Act – even 
checking whether they might be affected involves considerable effort. At the same time, the AI Act does 
not contribute significantly to achieving the protection goals of safety and health, as these are already 
guaranteed in other NLF legal acts – with or without AI. Products that are already covered by NLF leg-
islation (Annex I, Section A) should therefore be exempted from the provisions of the AI Act. 
Where necessary and if not already done so, AI-specific requirements should be integrated into the rele-
vant NLF sectoral regulation without going beyond the requirements of the AI Act. Alternatively, it would 
be possible to integrate Annex I, Section A into Section B so that the products listed there are also cov-
ered by the exemptions in Article 2(2) of the AI Act. The EU Commission's proposal in the context of the 
MDR/IVDR review of 16 December 2025 can serve as a model here.

We propose the deletion of Article 6(1) and Annex I to avoid legal uncertainty in parallel application of le-

• Rapid legal certainty regarding deadlines, linked to the availability of hENs (Art. 113) 
Due to the short implementation deadline and delays in the provision of harmonised European standards, 
companies lack the time to prepare for the implementation of the AI Act requirements in a timely manner 
or to bring their products to market in compliance with the AI Act. However, all other actors involved in the 
implementation of a legal act (market surveillance authorities, notified bodies, national authorities) are  
also faced with the challenge that suitable standards are not yet available and they need sufficient time 
for implementation. The deadlines for the application of the AI Act should be based on the publica-
tion of harmonised European standards. Once all planned standards covering the essential require-
ments for high-risk AI systems have been published, a period of 24 months for the implementation of the 
standards by industry is realistic. 
In principle, ZVEI welcomes the postponement of the deadlines by at least 12 months. However, the cur-
rent draft from the EU Commission still allows for the possibility that these deadlines could be shortened 
to six months by a decision of the EU Commission. This creates legal and planning uncertainty. At the 
same time, there is a risk that an extension of the high-risk AI deadlines will only be decided shortly be-
fore the current deadline of 2 August 2026 – legal certainty should be created quickly here by decoupling 
the deadline extension from the rest of the omnibus procedure. However, it should be ensured that the 
time gained as a result is used for the urgently needed simplification of the AI Act. In this context, priority 
should be given to removing industrial AI from the scope of the AI Act, provided that it is already subject to 
sectoral regulation. 
Specific proposal: 
Deleting the reference to the "adoption of a decision of the Commission" in Art. 113 (d) and linking the 
deadline of 12 months to the availability of harmonised European standards. 
• Clarification of transparency obligations (Article 50 (7)) 
A clarification of Article 50 is urgently needed. The transparency requirements may have far-reaching con-
sequences for AI-generated programme code. Particularly – but not exclusively – in the B2B context, use 
cases must therefore be considered in which an unconditional labelling requirement for AI content would 
not be practicable. In addition, cases must be taken into account (e.g. in the Code of Practice currently 
being developed) in which the labelling requirement under Article 50(2) ends after review and confirma-
tion by a human user (analogous to Article 50(4), second paragraph). The implementation of the transpar-
ency requirements therefore entails a high level of adaptation effort, especially since the above-men-
tioned Code of Practice is not yet available. The corresponding deadline should therefore be extended by 
12 months to 2 August 2027. 
• Adaptability as a mandatory AI feature (Article 3 and Machinery Regulation) 
The Machinery Regulation already regulates artificial intelligence as "self-evolving behaviour" (Annex I, Part 
A, Points 5 and 6). This definition differs from the more comprehensive definition in the AI Act, which con-
siders "adaptiveness after deployment" to be optional ("may") (Art. 3 (1)). The AI Omnibus should also 
clarify that, in the interaction between the AI Act and the Machinery Regulation , the relevant definitions in 
the Machinery Regulation, including the definition of a safety component, are decisive. 
• Exceptions for additional safety functions (Art. 6) 
In our opinion, the term "safety component" also requires further clarification. In particular, our members 
have pointed out that additional safety functions should not fall under the high-risk AI requirements of Art. 
6 if sufficient protection is already provided by a safety component without AI functionality. Otherwise, 
there is a risk that optional AI-based safety functions will not be implemented if this would make a product 
a high-risk AI system. 
• No mandatory AI competence as a positive proposal (Article 4) 
The ZVEI supports the EU Commission's proposal to shift the obligations for teaching AI competence. It is 
crucial that the liability implications of the previous Article 4 are also clarified and that legal certainty is 
created for companies in this area.2

Specific proposal: 
To Art. 6(3) should be added: "e) the AI merely provides an additional layer of safety". If Art 6(1) remains 
unchanged, it should also be made clear that this provision also covers AI systems falling under Annex I 
of the AI Act. 
• Clarification of registration requirements (Article 6(4)) 
The ZVEI supports the streamlining of registration requirements and their corresponding deletion in the 
current omnibus draft. With regard to future documentation requirements, it should also be clarified that 
regular updates do not trigger a new documentation requirement. 
• Practical orientation in conformity assessment procedures (Art. 28) / Clarification regarding 
NANDO codes (Art. 30(2)) 
The ZVEI supports the simplification of conformity assessment procedures and, in particular, the simpli-
fied designation of notified bodies. However, it is not clear how the different code types in Annex XIV (e.g. 
AIP for products and AIH for technology) are to "interact" with each other and for what a notified body 
would be specifically accredited. It is crucial that existing notified bodies are quickly and unbureaucrati-
cally enabled to pragmatically incorporate the additional high-risk AI requirements into existing conformity 
assessment procedures without having to go through a comprehensive new accreditation process. The 
proposal in the draft bill for the national implementation of the AI Regulation to incorporate existing sec-
toral market surveillance structures is explicitly seen as a model for further European approaches. 
Specific proposal: 
Apart from removing the reference to the “option to opt out”, the reference to Annex VII should be re-
moved, in order to clarify that self-assessment is a viable route for the Quality Management System (An-
nex VI), i.e.: “The requirements set out in Section 2 of this Chapter shall apply to those high-risk AI sys-
tems and shall be part of that assessment. Assessment of the quality management system set out in Arti-
cle 17 and Annex VII shall also apply." 
• Internal production control should not be referred to as an "opt-out" option (Art. 43 (3)) 
The new version of Art. 43 (3) refers to an "option to opt out from a third-party conformity assessment". 
This wording contradicts the logic of the Blue Guide (OJ 2022/C 247/01), which does not provide for inter-
nal production control (Module A) as an "exception" to other forms of conformity assessment (e.g. Module 
H, conformity based on comprehensive quality assurance involving a notified body).  In this context, it 
would also be important to delete the reference to Annex VII in the newly worded Article 43(3) in order to 
clarify that internal production control is also possible for the quality management system.  
Specific proposal: 
Deletion of the phrase "option to opt out" as well as deletion of the reference to Annex VII in Article 43(3), 
first paragraph. 
• Clarification of grandfathering in the legal text (Article 111) 
The ZVEI supports the EU Commission's proposal to clarify the grandfathering provisions in Article 111. 
However, this clarification should be included not only in Recital 21, but also in the text of Article 111(2). 
Specific proposal: 
We propose to add some language from the clarification that the Commission provides in Recital (21) to 
Article 111(2) itself, to avoid inconsistencies with the Blue Guide and MDCG 2025-6 as regards the con-
cept of placing on the market.  
• Priority of clinical investigations in accordance with MDR/IVDR 
For medical devices, the priority of clinical investigations in accordance with the MDR (or performance 
studies in accordance with the IVDR) must be ensured, as well as the exclusive applicability of the rele-
vant provisions in the MDR (or IVDR) to AI systems that are themselves medical devices or accessories 
to medical devices or safety components of medical devices (or accessories). The proposed amendment 
to Article 60 of the EU AI Act and the proposed insertion of a new Article 60a into the EU AI Act do not en-
sure this. 
 
 
Background 
 
• Unclear interaction between the various NLF legal acts and the AI Act:  
During the drafting of the AI Act, ZVEI already pointed out that a clear limitation of Article 6 of the AI Act 
for so-called high-risk AI is necessary and that a close link to the NLF and consistency with other market 
 
 
access regulations must be ensured. Otherwise, this will lead to unnecessary and increased compliance 
costs. Of course, ensuring the integrity and robustness of ML models, functional safety, data security, etc. 
remains a key task. "Trustworthy and Ethical AI" could become a unique selling point for European com-
panies, as edge applications in the automotive and industrial sectors, sensor technology, functional 
safety, cyber security, etc. are core competencies of European industries. However, inconsistencies exist 
in the following legal acts, among others:  
‒ Machinery Regulation: The definition of a safety component in the Machinery Regulation differs from 
that in the AI Act, meaning that the same product can be classified as both a safety component and a 
"non-safety" component in the same risk assessment.  
‒ Radio Equipment Directive: An initial proposal by the European Commission on the interaction be-
tween the Radio Equipment Directive and the AI Act from July 2024 has still not been finalised after 
almost a year. The first draft contained significant contradictions and ambiguities, which were strongly 
criticised by stakeholders, as it would have unintentionally significantly increased the number of high-
risk AI systems affected.3 The current Digital Omnibus on AI also uses the sometimes highly problem-
atic term "opt-out option". 
‒ Medical Device Regulation (MDR)/In-Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR):  
▪ A preliminary draft of the European Commission's FAQs on the interaction between the AI Act and 
the MDR/IVDR contains around 40 questions that demonstrate the extent of legal uncertainty, even 
though they do not address all the open points of discussion. The problems of misalignment con-
cern the unresolved conflicting requirements for risk management and the management of signifi-
cant changes, clinical trials and diverging requirements for the management of placing on the mar-
ket/putting into service. 
▪ The risk of double regulation is already apparent in the standards that apply. For example, both the 
MDR/IVDR and the AI Act require a risk management system to be established. For medical de-
vices, EN ISO 14971:2019 is the harmonised European standard for this purpose. The parallel ap-
plication of legal regulations and the need to "find out" which requirements from the EU AI Act that 
go beyond the respective NLF legal acts must be taken into account create considerable additional 
work and legal uncertainty. The EU Commission has recognised this problem and on 16 December 
2026 presented a corresponding reform of the MDR/IVDR, which would move the MDR/IVDR from 
Annex I (A) to Annex I (B) of the AI Act. 
‒ The guidelines on the definition of AI systems published in February 2025 are still too vague for the 
industrial context.4 For example, possible contradictions arise from the term "inference", which is also 
enabled by mathematical optimisation systems that should actually be excluded from the definition of 
the AI Act. The individual characteristics of the definition of AI still lack the clarity needed to distinguish 
it from other products, meaning that classic software solutions could also fall under its scope. Key 
terms such as "basic data processing" remain undefined. 
• Sufficient transition periods5 : 
‒ Sufficiently long transition periods are essential to ensure that economic operators and all other parties 
involved in the implementation of a legal act (market surveillance authorities, notified bodies, national 
authorities) have enough time to implement new or amended requirements in a timely manner. Due to 
the short deadline for the provision of harmonised European standards, there is not enough time to 
prepare for the implementation of the requirements of the AI Act in good time. According to the original 
timetable, the first standards should be available by the end of 2024, before the high -risk AI regula-
tions come into effect in August 2026 and August 2027, respectively. Due to the expected delay in the 
provision of the standards (currently around two years), it is necessary to postpone the deadlines for 
the application of the AI Act accordingly in order to enable implementation. This should be based on 
the actual publication date of the harmonised European standards. This is the only way to ensure that 
AI products can be placed on the market in Europe.  
‒ If this is not done, it must at least be ensured that manufacturers are temporarily allowed to use Mod-
ule A (internal production control) as a conformity assessment procedure until the harmonised stand-
ards are available. If these standards are not available (or not available in time), a notified body (certi-
fier) must be involved in the conformity assessment of the product. This means that, particularly during 
the transition period (from an existing) to a new legal act, the number of assessment requests to

AI Act – Key concerns and examples 
 
 
1. Key demand: Exclusion of industrial AI from the AI Act  
 
From the outset, the AI Act has ignored the fact that the essential aspects of AI in industrial applications are 
already regulated in other legal acts, such as product safety requirements. This now leads to double regula-
tion and additional bureaucracy, but not to greater safety. Unfortunately, the Digital Omnibus draft does not 
adequately address this problem. In our view, it would make most sense to remove industrial AI, which is al-
ready regulated elsewhere, from the AI Act. The Commission has already recognised this approach as sen-
sible for medical devices and proposed moving the regulations on medical devices and in vitro diagnostics 
within Annex I of the AI Regulation from Section A to Section B. The same must also apply to the Machinery 
Regulation, at a minimum, in order to avoid significantly hampering the use of AI in European industry 
through uncoordinated double regulation. A "lex specialis" approach would not be helpful, as it would further 
increase the complexity between the Machinery Regulation and the AI Act instead of reducing it , as the fol-
lowing examples illustrate.

Application scenario 
AI systems already recognise when employees approach a running machine and automatically stop 
operation as soon as a hazardous situation arises.  
This AI application is already strictly regulated by the Machinery Regulation. However, the definition 
of "safety component" in the AI Act means that the application is also subject to the rules of con-
formity assessment by third-party bodies. This adds new obligations, risk analyses and documenta-
tion requirements that examine the same aspects, namely the safety of the AI application, without 
actually making the system safer. The AI Act adds additional requirements (Articles 8-15) whose 
interaction with the Machinery Regulation is unclear, for example due to differing protection objec-
tives. It would be better to integrate the requirements – insofar as they are not already covered – 
fully into the Machinery Regulation, focusing in particular on the concept of "self -evolving behav-
iour". 
Consequences  Double testing, double audits, double bureaucracy – with-
out any additional safety gains. 
The result: companies are considering whether to con-
tinue developing such systems at all. 
Proposed solution Exclusion of industrial AI from the AI Act if the application 
of artificial intelligence is already regulated in other legal 
acts (such as the Machinery Regulation).  A "lex specialis" 
approach would not be helpful, as it would not reduce the 
complexity between the Machinery Regulation and the AI 
Act for companies, since companies would still have to re-
view and evaluate both (inconsistent) regulations. In each 
individual case, it would be necessary to check whether 
the Machinery Regulation already covers the require-
ments of the AI Act. 
 
 
Application scenarios:  
AI-based object recognition is used in production lines, for example in robot cells, automated as-
sembly workstations or pressing and punching machines, to monitor danger zones in real time us-
ing cameras or sensor fusion and to detect unexpected intrusions by persons into the working area 
of a machine. In the event of an incident, safety-related measures such as emergency stop, safe 
torque cut-off or reduced operating modes are triggered immediately, significantly reducing the risk 
of damage while enabling flexible production processes with close human-machine interaction. 
These safety precautions are based on the strict safety and health requirements of the Machinery 
Directive. Although the AI Act adds additional requirements under Articles 8–15, some of these are 
not relevant in practice or are already covered by the Machinery Directive. For example, the draft 
risk management standard would require all 50 European fundamental rights to be checked for 
every industrial AI application, even if there is no substantive connection, such as to freedom of art 
or religion. However, the risk assessment under the Machinery Directive already ensures that each 
person is reliably identified in order to prevent injuries, and thus also includes checks for prohibi-
tions on discrimination, as provided for in the AI Act. To ensure safety, it is therefore sufficient and 
appropriate to regulate AI-based object recognition exclusively under the Machinery Directive, as it 
already standardises all relevant protection objectives. 
Consequences  The Machinery Directive and accompanying regulatory re-
quirements, such as data protection and occupational 
safety, are fundamentally suitable for regulating high-risk 
AI in industrial applications. The existing uncoordinated 
double regulation by the AI Act, on the other hand, does 
not increase safety, but creates additional legal uncer-
tainty, hinders functional technical solutions and causes 
high implementation costs, which significantly weakens 
European industry in the international competition for AI 
leadership positions. 
Proposed solution  Double regulation can only be avoided by moving the Ma-
chinery Directive from Annex I A to Annex I B; a lex spe-
cialis is not sufficient here, as this would increase rather 
than reduce complexity and testing costs.

Modern adaptive control systems for industrial robots independently optimise movement sequences

This AI application of adaptive industrial robots is already strictly regulated by the Machinery Regu-

For companies, this lack of clarity means costly legal opinions, lengthy consultations with authorities 
and uncertainty vis-à-vis customers. Instead of clear obligations, there is broad scope for interpreta-
tion, which costs time, money and innovation speed and unnecessarily delays the use of efficient 
technologies. While the Machinery Directive is based on a decades-old understanding of safety 
components, the AI Act is breaking new ground here, partly due to differing protection objectives. 
As a result, an AI system is not a safety component under the Machinery Directive, but may be un-

Application scenarios: 
A production facility repeatedly experiences night-time shutdowns because a safety light barrier re-
ports a protective field violation and the control system triggers a safe stop. There is no technical 
malfunction; the interruption is caused by an animal crossing the protective area. Since the system 
cannot classify the cause, production losses, repeated alarms and additional personnel deployment 
occur. To reduce false alarms, an AI-based camera is installed to classify objects and distinguish 
between people and other triggers. When movement is detected again, the model classifies the ob-
ject as a "person" with a certain degree of confidence, whereupon the safety logic triggers another 
stop and activates monitoring processes.  
Consequences The planned improvements (additional training data, re-
dundant sensor technology, multi-level logic and extended 
testing) increase robustness but lead to significantly more 
regulatory effort. Among other things, extended technical 
documentation, data protection impact assessments, bias 
evidence, penetration tests and post-market monitoring 
are required, possibly with the involvement of notified bod-
ies. 
 
This causes additional costs, delays market launch and 
increases organisational effort, which is a particular hurdle 
for SMEs. After weighing up the costs and benefits and 
assessing the liability risks, the AI solution is not pursued. 
Instead, the light barrier remains in place, as it is easier to 
verify and avoids the risks associated with AI-based deci-
sions. In this scenario, regular downtime would therefore 
continue to occur. 
Proposed solution The AI safety function of camera surveillance should be 
based exclusively on the requirements of the Machinery 
Regulation, as the sole protection objective here should 
be to ensure "safety of life and limb" (by avoiding misiden-
tification of animals).

Application scenarios:  
The AI Regulation stipulates that an AI system is to be classified as high risk if it is "intended for use 
as a safety component of a product, or if the AI system itself is a product covered by the Union har-
monisation legislation listed in Annex I." The specific application case of AI-supported food recogni-
tion (Smart Food ID) in connected household appliances raises the following problem: The AI sys-
tem operates in a device (e.g. an oven) which, due to its connectivity, is subject to at least the Radio 
Equipment Directive (RED) in Annex I. This raises the question of whether the legal classification 
must be independent of the distribution model. Although the digital product Smart Food ID is cur-
rently sold separately from the physical appliance, its functionality is intrinsically linked to the appli-
ance's networking interface. This raises the question of whether the formal separation between digi-
tal and physical products is relevant for regulatory classification, or whether functional dependency 
should be the decisive factor. Furthermore, a purely local implementation without the use of the net-
working interface raises another problem of interpretation: in this scenario, the AI system would 
function as an integral part of the physical product (device with digital element), which in turn is sub-
ject to one or more harmonisation regulations in accordance with Annex I.  
Consequences  Open question as to whether an AI system such as "load 
detection" should be classified as a stand-alone product 
within the meaning of Article 6(1)(a), even if it is only a 
product component. 
Proposed solution  Clarification.

Application scenarios:  
Given their cross-sector applicability across all B2C and B2B areas, the development of the relevant 
standards has been significantly delayed, as the breadth of content and semantic harmonisation 
across heterogeneous fields of application pose considerable challenges. There are already more 
than 1,300 comments on 347 pages for a single standard in the area of quality management, which 
illustrates the high level of coordination and consolidation required. Standards put to the vote are 
therefore often rejected, while the HAS consultants appointed by the EU repeatedly criticise quality 
deficiencies and rightly refuse to accept their suitability as harmonised European standards.  
Consequences  Accordingly, high implementation costs are to be ex-
pected for these horizontal standards, which, unlike in the 
classic New Legislative Framework, are not sector-spe-
cific but apply across markets. At the same time, notified 
bodies are already working at full capacity, so that addi-
tional certification requirements in the area of high-risk AI 
are likely to lead to further bottlenecks. In addition, indus-
trial product cycles, particularly in mechanical engineer-
ing, electrical engineering and medical technology, are 
long-term and require transitional arrangements that ena-
ble the sustainable and high-quality integration of the 
standards into product and service design. The implemen-
tation of the requirements already ties up considerable ad-
ditional resources, as companies have to review, imple-
ment and document up to 35 harmonised standards, and 
insufficient transition periods would further increase these 
costs at the expense of investment in research and devel-
opment, innovation, data integration and data use. 
Proposed solution  An extension of the deadlines by 24 months is necessary 
to give companies sufficient time to revise all affected

Application scenarios:  
Some of our member companies are part of the European Commission's working group that is de-
veloping the Code of Practice for Article 50. The Commission began its work in autumn 2025. The 
Code of Practice is not expected to be ready until June 2026 at the earliest, shortly before the regu-
lations come into force. This means that both users and providers run the risk of having to comply 
with obligations that cannot yet be implemented in practice. For this reason, the transition period 
should also cover the obligations under Article 50(1)-(4) and be extended to 12 months in order to 
give both providers and users sufficient time to implement the upcoming guidelines and code of 
conduct. The advantage of an extension would be a legally secure interpretation for com panies 
through the availability of the CoP and guidelines. 
Consequences  In our opinion, a postponement would not have any nega-
tive impact on the standards of protection for citizens, in-
cluding workers, as no substantive changes would be 
made to the article and the level of protection would not 
be lowered. 
Proposed solution  Extension of the deadlines for transparency obligations 
(Art. 50). Here, differentiation according to context may be 
necessary, as industrial contexts for the use of genAI dif-
fer significantly from social media and similar applications.

Area of application Industrial production 
Company size Large

AI Act, Art. 50 
Application scenarios:  
Industrial AI chatbots are based on a clearly defined, high-quality or curated database, for example 
from manuals, certificates and technical documentation. They are used exclusively in a profession-
ally qualified context, such as specific maintenance or service tasks. Unlike B2C systems with po-
tentially broad social implications, for example through the spread of fake news, B2B implementa-
tions take place in controlled and regulated environments with established risk, liability and occupa-
tional safety structures, as well as industry-specific standards such as ISO and IEC standards. 
Consequences  Simplified, risk-based transparency requirements and ex-
tended transition periods should be provided for closed in-
dustrial AI applications. Since industry-specific require-
ments have not yet been taken into account in the Code 
of Practice despite corresponding risk-based proposals, 
an implementation period of eight weeks is unrealistic. 
Transition periods that are too short would significantly im-
pair the legally compliant use of AI-supported applications 
in industry and thus weaken the innovation and competi-
tiveness of European industry in the international competi-
tion for AI leadership positions in application. 
Proposed solution  Exemption of B2B applications from Article 50 or adjust-
ment of the corresponding deadlines for the B2B sector

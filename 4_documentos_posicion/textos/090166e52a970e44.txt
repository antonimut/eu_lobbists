AI Sweden’s Comments on the Digital Omnibus on AIs AI Sweden is the Swedish national center for applied artificial intelligence. Our mission is to accelerate the use of AI for the benefit of our society, our competitiveness, and for everyone living in Sweden. We are broadly funded and not for profit, as a part of Lindholmen Science Park. We collaborate with speed and boldness in everything we do with our over 170 partners from the private and public sectors as well as academia. AI Sweden is driving groundbreaking projects such as building transparent, open-source foundational European LLM (OpenEuroLLM), developing a shared digital assistant for the public sector (Svea) and creating innovative decoy systems leveraging AI to enhance cybersecurity (AI-Powered Honeypots). It is essential that the proposed changes are swiftly implemented and further strengthened, particularly in light of the ongoing technological and geopolitical landscape. Technological sovereignty should not be a question of politics but a question of strategic necessity. 
Application Time and Harmonized Standards AI Sweden would primarily like to underline the importance of having harmonized standards and practical guidance in place before the rules enter into force. During the drafting of the AI Act, a number of stakeholders emphasized the substantial need for standards and guidance to clarify and interpret the many vague concepts and assessments contained in the legal act. For many stakeholders, legal uncertainty is more difficult, costly, and restrictive than legislation with clear compliance requirements. Common standards and guidelines are imperative for being able to apply the rules and implement them in respective operations.  AI Sweden therefore welcomes the European Commission's intention to link the time of application more closely to the availability of harmonized standards and practical guidance. However, the currently proposed drafting of the provision creates significant unpredictability regarding when the Commission will consider that 'appropriate measures in support of compliance with Chapter III' exist, and thus when the Commission will adopt its decision, which in turn triggers the start of the

AI Literacy Requirements AI Sweden is generally positive towards the Commission's approach to simplifying the rules in the AI Act and reducing the regulatory burden. Nonetheless, we foresee difficulties with the currently proposed amendment, whereby Member States and the Commission are to encourage providers and deployers to provide their staff with sufficient AI literacy. We question what this requirement entails in practice and how it is to be fulfilled. It is also worth pointing out that the stakeholders themselves are best placed to determine the required competence, as this can differ significantly across different types of operations. We share the view that AI literacy needs to increase; it is a fundamental prerequisite for being able to draw upon on the significant opportunities presented by AI, while simultaneously mitigating the risks.  AI Sweden believes that AI literacy in society needs to be strengthened generally, and not solely among providers and deployers of AI systems. AI Sweden therefore proposes that the provision be adjusted in accordance with the below, so that the objective is instead to increase AI literacy among the general public. Article 4  AI literacy  The Commission and Member States shall encourage providers and deployers of AI systems to take promote measures to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among the general public of their staff and other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on their behalf, taking into account their technical knowledge, experience, level of

Bias Detection AI Sweden wishes to underline the importance of being able to detect and correct bias in AI systems. Proactively managing such risks is crucial for ensuring that AI use can take place in a safe, lawful, and responsible manner, while at the same time utilizing the potential that AI brings. Consequently, it is central that AI systems are designed and used in a way that respects human rights, values, and cultural diversity, in accordance with the 2019 Ethics guidelines for trustworthy AI drawn up by the independent AI High-Level Expert Group appointed by the Commission.  In these guidelines, the AI expert group formulated seven non-binding ethical principles for AI that should help ensure that AI is trustworthy and ethically sound. This includes minimizing risks such as bias and discrimination. In order to enable the responsible design of AI systems, it must be possible to process special categories of personal data, which is why we strongly welcome the proposal to broaden the legal basis. It should also be emphasized that there are a number of safeguards linked to the processing of special categories of personal data, which is why the change from 'strictly necessary' to 'necessary' is a well-justified adjustment.  AI Sweden further believes that the risk to fundamental rights and freedoms is greater if bias is undetected and remaining than if personal data is processed for this purpose.

Real-World Testing AI Sweden is positive towards the proposed revisions regarding testing in real-world conditions. However, we would like to underline that the practical effects of these alleviations risk being limited, as the fundamental problem of access to high-quality datasets remains. The difficulty of obtaining access to data for the development, testing, and validation of AI systems under secure and lawful conditions constitutes one of the most significant obstacles for European stakeholders. AI Sweden sees a great need for national as well as European leadership and commitment to resolving these issues and is happy to contribute to finding common and well-balanced ways forward. Furthermore, AI Sweden shares the Commission's assessment that guidelines are needed for the practical application of the research exemptions in Articles 2(6) and 2(8), including how these exemptions are to be applied in concrete development and testing contexts, which the Commission intends to prioritize. Against this background, AI Sweden considers that the announced guidelines on the research exemptions should be drafted in close connection with the provisions on AI regulatory sandboxes and testing in real-world conditions. Such a coordinated approach can contribute to a more uniform, predictable, and practical demarcation between research activities, experimental development, testing, and the actual use of AI systems, without introducing new binding requirements beyond the current framework of the AI Act. In addition, the guidelines should clarify how the exemptions are to be applied throughout different phases of the AI systems' development and testing and be supplemented with a clear advisory mandate for competent supervisory authorities and common principles of interpretation for research-oriented pilot projects. This would reduce the risk of precautionary behavior and uneven application in practice, in line with the Commission's stated objective of a smooth, predictable, and innovation-friendly implementation of the AI Act.

Testing in real-world conditions also raises questions regarding the allocation of liability, particularly when testing takes place in partnership between providers and deployers. Although the regulatory framework clarifies that testing can be carried out collaboratively - the legal liability for damages mainly remains with the provider. AI Sweden sees a risk that the allocation of liability may in practice act as a deterrent for providers considering testing with deployers in real-world environments, especially in situations where the provider does not have full control over how the AI system is used in daily operations. We assess that these challenges can, in many cases, be managed through clear and well-balanced contractual solutions between the parties. The promotion of common or guiding standard agreements can contribute to increased clarity regarding roles, responsibilities, and risk allocation, thereby lowering the thresholds for testing in real-world conditions. 
Market Surveillance Responsibilities Regarding the Commission's extended market surveillance responsibilities, AI Sweden advocates for a model that counteracts the fragmentation of the internal market. Management within the national market surveillance authorities’ risks leading to divergent assessments among Member States and creating legal uncertainty. Through a more centralized organization, it becomes easier to develop best practices. It is also a question of where the requisite expertise resides, which is why AI Sweden generally advocates an extended mandate and responsibility for the AI Board. In this way, the influence of the Member States increases to a greater extent than if the responsibility is given to the Commission or the AI Office. Furthermore, the AI Board should have the power to issue written opinions on its own initiative regarding the effectiveness of the Regulation. It should be mandatory for the Commission to consult the Board when drafting delegated acts and implementing acts. The AI Board should also be tasked with producing an interim report, no later than two years after entry into force, evaluating the Regulation's impact on innovation, research, and fundamental rights. The Commission should be obliged to take the Board's conclusions into utmost account in future revisions of the regulatory framework.

Labeling of Deepfakes AI Sweden welcomes the proposal in Article 50 that it must be disclosed when content has been artificially generated or manipulated. In the Digital Omnibus on AI, a transitional period of six months is proposed for providers who have placed their systems on the market before 2 August 2026, with the labelling requirement entering into force on 2 February 2027. For image and video content, AI Sweden notes that this technology is already available, whereas it is currently more complex to develop corresponding solutions for text. A later date of entry into force may therefore be justified. It should be recalled that the AI Act focuses on product safety for AI systems and is therefore designed as product safety legislation, whereas the Digital Services Act (DSA) focuses on the content of various services. Any introduction of content requirements into the AI Act would entail adding an additional layer of regulation, in direct opposition to the ambition to reduce the regulatory burden. For innovative stakeholders, this would result in increased complexity, where it becomes unclear which legislation takes precedence in specific situations. Particularly for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), cumulative regulatory requirements constitute a real barrier to market entry, as the costs of compliance can become disproportionately high. 
A More Relevant Qualification Basis for General-Purpose AI Models In Article 51(2), a general-purpose AI model is presumed to have high-impact capabilities and thus be classified as a general-purpose AI model with systemic risk, if the cumulative amount of computation used for its training measured in floating-point operations is greater than 1025. Basing the identification of systemic risks in general-purpose AI models exclusively on the amount of computation (measured in floating-points) is misaligned for the purpose and risks becoming an outdated basis for classification. By using high-quality data, it is already today possible to develop AI models that possess equivalent capabilities using fewer floating-point operations than 1025. Given the rapid technological development, the threshold value of 1025 will quickly loose its relevance, rendering the legislation obsolete.

The qualitative and quantitative criteria in Annex XIII are significantly more relevant for assessing whether a general-purpose AI model has high-impact capabilities or not. Hence, AI Sweden advocates that Article 51(2) be deleted, whereby the criteria in Annex XIII would have a greater impact through Article 51(1)(b). This adjustment would result in a more technology-neutral and robust regulatory framework that does not need to be revised every time a new, more efficient training method is developed. The change out to be complemented by recommendations from the AI Board specifying benchmark values for the various criteria. In this way, both clarity and predictability are achieved for the relevant stakeholders, while the benchmark values can be adjusted by the Board with some regularity as technology evolves. 
Clearer Distinction Between AI Safety and Cybersecurity AI Sweden is positive about the inclusion of AI safety in the AI Act. However, we wish to underline the importance of distinguishing between AI safety and cybersecurity, where the latter is already regulated in the NIS2 Directive and the Cyber Resilience Act.  To avoid overlapping regulation in this regard, we propose that Articles 15, 42(2), and 55(1)(d) be streamlined to address only aspects specific to the functionality of AI systems.

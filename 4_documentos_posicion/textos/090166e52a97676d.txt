General remarks  
The Danish Chamber of Commerce welcomes the European Commission’s initiative to simplify the 
EU’s digital regulatory framework through the Digital Omnibus proposals covering data, cyberse-
curity and artificial intelligence. We support the Commission’s overa ll objective of ensuring that 
the EU’s digital rulebook remains clear, coherent and conducive to innovation while maintaining 
the Union’s high standards for fundamental rights, privacy and cybersecurity.  
 
Europe is currently facing a structural competitiveness challenge with important economic and 
strategic implications. Regulatory complexity and fragmented implementation across Member 
States continue to increase compliance costs for companies and make it more difficult to scale dig-
ital innovation across the Single Market. In this context, the Digital Omnibus represents an im-
portant opportunity to streamline the digital rulebook, improve legal clarity and reduce unneces-
sary administrative burdens while preserving the objectives of the underlying legislation.  
 
Over the past decade, companies have had to navigate a rapidly expanding digital regulatory land-
scape, including the GDPR, the Data Act, the Data Governance Act, the AI Act, NIS2, the Cyber 
Resilience Act, DORA and several sector-specific frameworks. While each of these instruments ad-
dresses legitimate policy objectives, their cumulative effect has in many cases led to duplication of 
compliance obligations. Companies frequently face overlapping requirements in areas such as risk 
assessments, incident reporting, documentation obligations and governance structures.  
 
The Danish Chamber of Commerce therefore strongly supports the Commission’s efforts to im-
prove coherence across the EU’s digital legislation. Simplification should focus not only on reducing 
administrative burdens but also on improving clarity regarding ho w different legislative instru-
ments interact in practice. Data protection rules represent one of the areas where businesses most 
clearly experience regulatory complexity. Many companies – particularly SMEs – face significant 
uncertainty when navigating the intersection between the GDPR and emerging technologies such 
as artificial intelligence. Clarifying the legal framework for the responsible use of data in AI devel-
opment will therefore be essential to ensure that European companies are able to innovate while 
fully respecting European values and data protection standards.

Similarly, the growing body of cybersecurity regulation has created situations where companies 
may need to report the same incident under multiple legal frameworks. This not only increases 
administrative burdens but may also divert critical resources away from operational incident re-
sponse during cybersecurity events. Initiatives that consolidate and streamline reporting obliga-
tions are therefore particularly welcome.  
 
Finally, the Danish Chamber of Commerce supports the Commission’s ambition to facilitate the 
effective implementation of the AI Act. The AI Act represents an important milestone in creating a 
trustworthy AI ecosystem in Europe. However, successful implemen tation requires legal clarity, 
proportionate requirements and the timely availability of practical guidance, standards and com-

Taken together, the Digital Omnibus provides an important opportunity to ensure that Europe re-
mains both a global leader in responsible digital regulation and a competitive environment for dig-
ital innovation. 
 
The Data Aquis: 
 
It is highly welcome that the data landscape is now being addressed, as it currently constitutes a 
significant challenge for companies.  
 
According to an analysis conducted by the Danish Chamber of Commerce, data protection legisla-
tion is perceived as the most burdensome regulatory framework for Danish businesses. Specifically, 
65 percent of the Danish Chamber of Commerce’s members consider legislation in this area to be 
their greatest compliance challenge. There is a particular need for clearer interpretation of the reg-
ulatory framework in order to ensure greater consistency and predictability in the handling of data.  
 
We therefore recommend the following:  
 
Exempt certain types of personal data from the scope of the GDPR  
In line with previous recommendations from the Danish Chamber of Commerce, we propose that 
the Commission adjusts the scope of the GDPR so that ordinary contact details of customers and 
suppliers in a B2B context are not subject to the same requirements as other types of personal data.  
 
In practice, this type of data presents a low risk and forms part of ordinary commercial relations. 
Maintaining full GDPR regulation in this area creates unnecessary administrative burdens for busi-
nesses, particularly SMEs, which already experience the legislation as complex and resource-inten-
sive. A more risk-based approach would reduce the administrative burden without compromising 
the rights of individuals.  
 
Raise the threshold for maintaining records under Article 30  
The threshold for maintaining records of processing activities should be raised and simplified so 
that SMEs, small and medium-sized enterprises and organisations with fewer than 500 employees 
are only required to maintain such records when their processing activities are likely to result in a

“high risk” to the rights and freedoms of data subjects. The condition relating to occasional pro-
cessing should therefore be removed.  
 
Simplify the rules on transfers to third countries  
Since the Schrems II ruling, our members have experienced an increased focus on transfers to third 
countries. This issue creates challenges for many companies and organisations because, on the one 
hand, it is difficult to comply with the very restrictive rules in this area, while on the other hand it 
is unrealistic to expect that personal data will not be transferred to third countries in a globalised 
world where businesses rely heavily on technology and cloud-based solutions.  
 
The rules governing third -country transfers apply without regard to the risk -based approach on 
which the GDPR is otherwise built. In practice, the rules are interpreted to mean that no level of 
risk can be accepted in connection with third -country transfers. Exporters of personal data must 
conduct an in-depth assessment of the recipient country’s legislation and practices regarding pri-
vacy protection, including by preparing a Transfer Impact Assessment. As a result, the same re-
quirements apply when transferring an IP address or B2B contact information to an unsafe third 
country as when transferring health data.  
 
The rules governing third-country transfers therefore require significant resources that are not nec-
essarily proportionate to the level of protection required by the data involved. Considering that 
most technical solutions today rely on sub -processors located in third countries, compliance with 
the rules on third-country transfers has become extremely challenging.  
 
We therefore recommend introducing a risk -based approach to third -country transfers. This ap-
proach is already used in the United Kingdom, where Transfer Risk Assessments (TRAs) are ap-
plied instead of Transfer Impact Assessments. The UK Information Commissi oner’s Office (ICO) 
has developed a template for TRAs in which the risk categorisation of personal data depends, 
among other things, on the type of data (general, confidential or sensitive), the categories of data 
subjects (adults, children or other vulnerable groups) and the volume of personal data involved. 
The approach also takes into account the size of the company.  
 
Based on the experience of our members, we expect that such a risk-based approach would lead to 
stronger protection of personal data in connection with third -country transfers overall while also 
reducing the significant compliance risks that most companies currently face due to the restrictive 
nature of the current rules. 
 
Establishing an anonymization feasibility assessment 
To ensure continued increased trust in technology, it is proposed to include an anonymisation fea-
sibility assessment. An anonymization feasibility assessment includes the assessment of privacy -
preserving measures such as anonymization and pseudonymization techniques that are applicable 
for the personal data needed to be processed for the purpose of testing or training of an AI model 
or system. If those measures are found objectively unsuitable for the intended purpose, the con-
troller shall record the reasons and residual risks and integrate the assessment in the data protec-
tion impact assessment, adopt additional safeguards, and be able to demonstrate these findings to 
the supervisory authority upon request.

Streamlining cybersecurity incident reporting obligations  
DANSK ERHVERV   
 
  Side 5/9 
 
The Danish Chamber of Commerce (continues to) support the Commission’s ambition to estab-
lish a common European reporting platform for cybersecurity incidents as part of the simplifica-
tion and harmonization of EU cyber legislation. An effective and well-functioning common re-
porting solution holds significant potential to reduce administrative burdens for companies that 
are currently subject to parallel and partially overlapping reporting requirements across direc-
tives and regulations. 
 
At the same time, the Danish Chamber of Commerce notes that extensive reporting requirements 
in an acute incident situation may be counterproductive to the operational handling of the inci-
dent. An intuitive and well-functioning digital reporting platform, where relevant information can 
be securely shared with only the relevant competent authorities, can therefore support knowledge 
sharing without unnecessarily hindering incident handling. 
 
However, it is crucial for the success of the platform that it is accompanied by real harmonization 
of the underlying reporting requirements. If companies must continue to report the same inci-
dents under different definitions, thresholds, deadlines and information requirements, a common 
technical platform alone will not lead to the desired reduction of burdens nor the desired effi-
ciency and quality of incident reports. 
 
In this regard, The Danish Chamber of Commerce urges the Commission to ensure: 
- Alignment of reporting timelines under NIS2 and CRA (and possibly the sector-specific 
personal data rules of the ePrivacy rules) with the GDPR model’s 72 hours, thereby allow-
ing a proper analytical process before the first report, which may eliminate overly hasty 
and hardly usable reports. 
- Clear delineation of which reports can be reused across regulatory frameworks, so that 
companies are not subject to double or parallel reporting. 
- Consistent/coherent definitions of incidents across relevant legal acts. 
- Harmonized requirements regarding content, format and level of detail in reporting. 
- Strengthened supervisory cooperation on incident reports applicable under NIS2, CRA, 
GDPR and other relevant legislation. If one must report multiple times to multiple differ-
ent authorities with different timelines, content etc. at a critical point in time, dispropor-
tionately much attention is removed from the actual securement and restoration of the 
company itself and its customers, partners, etc. 
 
The Danish Chamber of Commerce notes that these issues do not yet appear to be sufficiently ad-
dressed in the proposals presented. Without such harmonization, there is a significant risk that 
the common European reporting platform will not achieve its purpose and in practice will merely 
become a new layer on top of existing reporting obligations. 
 
The Danish Chamber of Commerce therefore urges the Commission to work towards ensuring 
that the establishment of a common European reporting platform is accompanied by ambitious 
and consistent harmonization of reporting requirements across legislation. 
 
The AI Act:

  Side 6/9 
 
Alignment of the timeline for the implementation of high-risk rules  
The rules for high -risk AI systems are scheduled to enter into force in August 2026 for the first 
category of systems (pursuant to Annex III of the AI Act). It is highly concerning and impractical 
that companies are expected to prepare for these requiremen ts while the Commission is still de-
layed in publishing guidelines on the practical implementation of high -risk AI systems, and while 
the Omnibus proposal may introduce further changes.  
 
The lack of alignment between these timelines creates unnecessary costs and may hinder innova-
tion. The Danish Chamber of Commerce therefore proposes that the entry into force of the rules for 
high-risk AI systems listed in Annex III be postponed so that th ey take effect at the same time as 
the rules for systems listed in Annex I. This would allow the Omnibus proposal to be better imple-
mented with regard to the requirements for high-risk AI systems and ensure that the forthcoming 
guidelines provide clearer and more accurate guidance for companies.  
 
General easing of high-risk obligations for providers of internal AI systems  
At present, the obligations for providers of high-risk AI systems specified in Chapter III, Section 3 
of the AI Act apply both to AI systems placed on the market and to AI systems that are put into 
service (Article 2 of the AI Act). Many of these obligatio ns are understandable and appropriate 
when an AI system is placed on the market.  
 
However, they appear disproportionate when a self-developed AI system is deployed solely for in-
ternal use within an organisation. The Danish Chamber of Commerce therefore proposes that the 
requirements for AI systems should generally differentiate between systems that are placed on the 
market and systems that are used internally.  
 
In particular, the obligations in Chapter III, Section 3 and Article 79 should be reviewed and eased 
for internal AI systems where organisations have developed the AI system themselves and therefore 
become the provider under Article 3(3) of the AI Act.  
 
In particular, the Danish Chamber of Commerce proposes removing the requirement for CE mark-
ing where an AI system is used exclusively for internal purposes within an organisation.  
 
Ensuring greater flexibility in post-market monitoring  
The Danish Chamber of Commerce welcomes the proposal to remove the requirement for a harmo-
nised post-market monitoring plan. This will provide greater flexibility for organisations to incor-
porate monitoring into their existing internal processes.  
 
It is also positive and necessary that the Commission provides practical guidance on what is re-
quired for post -market monitoring, taking proportionality into account. The Danish Chamber of 
Commerce proposes that either the guidance or the AI Act itself sho uld clarify whether the post -
market monitoring requirement under Chapter IX, Section 1 applies in the same way in all circum-
stances. 
  
Reduction of the registration burden

The Danish Chamber of Commerce welcomes the wording in Article 6(4) of the AI Act and the 
proposal to remove the registration requirement in Article 49(2) in cases where a provider deter-
mines that an AI system listed in Annex III does not qualify as high risk.  
 
However, it should also be considered whether the remaining registrations under Article 49 should 
be managed exclusively at the European level, so that national registrations are not required.  
 
A European-level registration system would promote harmonisation of registration registers and 
reduce disagreements between national registration authorities. It would also be simpler and more 
efficient for companies operating globally and across multiple EU Member States.  
 
European registration instead of national registration would therefore simplify administrative pro-
cesses and improve efficiency for companies operating across Europe. If the EU aims to become a 
global leader in AI innovation, administrative procedures should be simplified wherever possible, 
and registration is one area where this could be achieved.  
 
Promoting compliance with data protection legislation  
The initiative aimed at promoting compliance with data protection legislation should more clearly 
specify whether it provides a legal basis for processing special categories of personal data as de-
scribed, or whether such legal basis must still be found within data protection legislation.  
 
In addition, it should be considered whether it is proportionate to allow the processing of special 
categories of personal data in light of the rights of data subjects under the GDPR. Instead, it could 
be considered whether access should be limited to ordi nary personal data covered by Article 6 of 
the GDPR.  
 
Guidance for the practical implementation of the AI Act  
The Commission has stated that priority will be given to developing guidance on the practical ap-
plication of the research exemptions under the AI Act.  
 
However, the Commission should equally prioritise guidance on the practical implementation of 
AI systems classified as high-risk. A clear understanding of how Annex III of the AI Act should be 
interpreted is crucial for both companies and authorities when assessing the administrative and 
compliance costs associated with these obligations.  
 
For example, the sector-specific categories listed in Annex III require more detailed clarification. 
One example concerns the concept of safety components in critical infrastructure (Annex III, point 
2). There is a need for practical examples clarifying wh en systems are considered to protect the 
physical integrity of critical infrastructure or the health, safety and property of individuals.

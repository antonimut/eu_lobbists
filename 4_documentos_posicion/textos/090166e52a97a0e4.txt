COCIR Response to the European Commission Consultation on the AI Omnibus 
Proposal 
March 2026 
We welcome the European Commission’s initiative to simplify and improve the 
implementation of harmonised rules on artificial intelligence through the AI Omnibus 
proposal. Ensuring that the AI framework is workable in practice is essential to maintain 
high safety standards while enabling innovation and competitiveness in the EU. In 
particular, the interaction between the AI Act and established sectoral legislation such 
as the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) requires careful alignment to avoid regulatory 
duplication, legal uncertainty, and unintended barriers to innovation. 
Timelines for high-risk AI systems 
The proposed extension of the application timeline for high -risk AI systems is a positive 
step, but the currently envisaged deadlines remain unrealistic for the medical 
technology sector. The medical device ecosystem is still undergoing the MDR transition, 
with limited notified body capacity, incomplete national implementation, and 
uncertainty regarding the interaction between the AI Act and the MDR. In addition, key 
harmonised standards and implementation guidance are unlikely to be available before 
the end of 2026. 
Under these conditions, the current timelines do not sufficiently ensure legal certainty or 
effective implementation. Extending the application date for AI systems regulated under 
the New Legislative Framework, including those covered by the MDR, to 2 Augu st 2029 
would allow time to establish a functional regulatory ecosystem while ensuring that 
patient safety continues to be safeguarded under the MDR. Fixed and predictable 
timelines should also replace conditional compliance triggers. 
Transparency obligations (Article 50) 
The extension of the transition period for Article 50(2) transparency obligations is 
welcome. However, further alignment is needed with sector -specific timelines. Article 
50 was not designed with medical devices in mind and could create ambiguity for 
manufacturers. Because transparency requirements may apply earlier than the sector -
specific obligations for medical devices under the AI Act, manufacturers could 
effectively face shortened implementation timelines. 
The risks addressed by Article 50, such as impersonation or deceptive content, do not 
reflect the controlled clinical environments in which medical devices are used. The 
application of Article 50(2) should therefore be aligned with the timelines for high -risk 
obligations or with the broader timelines for products regulated under the New 
Legislative Framework.

Designation of Notified Bodies 
A clear and efficient pathway for the designation of notified bodies under the AI Act is 
essential. Uncertainty about whether MDR -designated notified bodies can also be 
designated under the AI Act, and whether this requires a fully separate procedure, risk s 
recreating the capacity shortages experienced during the MDR transition. 
Allowing a single application and assessment process for designation under both 
frameworks would avoid unnecessary duplication while maintaining regulatory oversight 
and building on existing expertise and infrastructure. 
Pre-market clinical testing 
The AI Act currently lacks a clear legal pathway for investigational medical devices 
incorporating AI. Under the MDR, clinical investigations are essential to generate 
evidence for CE marking. Without clarification, AI systems used in clinical investigations 
risk being considered as already “placed on the market” or “put into service.”  Clarifying 
that real-world testing provisions apply to investigational devices under the MDR would 
prevent premature conformity assessment requirements and ensure that manufacturers 
can continue to generate clinical evidence efficiently. 
Avoiding duplicative conformity assessments 
AI-enabled medical devices may fall both under Annex III of the AI Act and sectoral 
legislation listed in Annex I. Requiring separate AI Act conformity assessments in such 
cases would create unnecessary duplication. The conformity assessment procedures 
under the relevant sectoral legislation, such as the MDR, should therefore apply where 
AI systems are integrated into regulated products. 
Post-market monitoring 
Medical devices already operate under comprehensive post -market surveillance 
requirements under the MDR, including incident reporting, trend analysis, periodic safety 
updates, and corrective actions. Introducing additional AI-specific implementing acts for 
post-market monitoring risks duplication and inconsistency. 
Replacing such implementing acts with Commission guidance would allow AI -specific 
considerations to be integrated into existing MDR surveillance systems while 
maintaining efficiency and patient safety.

Conclusion 
The AI Act represents a major step in regulating artificial intelligence in Europe. To ensure 
effective and workable implementation, the framework must be better aligned with 
existing sectoral legislation, in particular the MDR. We support a sectoral appro ach for 
AI in medical devices to ensure a more streamlined and coherent regulatory framework, 
with medical devices regulated under a single legislation  - the MDR - in order to avoid 
disruption to established safety and performance requirements. 
Based on the challenges observed in the implementation of the AI Act, we recommend 
aligning the compliance approach for MDR -regulated products with the approach 
provided for Section B, Annex I products. This would streamline compliance 
requirements, reduce duplicative conformity  assessments, and address legal 
incoherences. In parallel, extending timelines, clarifying clinical testing pathways, 
streamlining notified body designation, and ensuring coherent post -market 
requirements will be essential to safegua rd patient safety while enabling innovation in 
AI-enabled medical technologies and strengthening EU competitiveness.

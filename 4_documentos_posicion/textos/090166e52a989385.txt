  
1 (5) Position paper 13.3.2026    Feedback on digital omnibus: GDPR GDPR & Scientiﬁc Research: Enabling Europe’s Competitiveness and Single Market for Research and Innovation  Scientific research, especially health and biomedical research rely on access to personal data, for example to develop AI tools for earlier diagnosis, personalised treatments, that enable cost-effective healthcare. The GDPR has strengthened privacy and data protection, but fragmented national interpretations hinder cross-border research, slowing innovation and reducing Europe’s competitiveness potential. CSC therefore welcomes the Commission’s effort to make the GDPR better accommodate data needs in research and innovation. Key challenges in the current situation, and which the omnibus package can remedy if the opportunity is seized, include the following: • Divergent national interpretations block data flows needed for joint EU research projects, causing delays and incomplete datasets. • Ambiguity around pseudonymisation and anonymisation limits data reuse and increases administrative burden.  • Narrow readings of data minimisation do not reflect the realities of data-intensive science and AI, where results and value emerge only after data analysis. • Limited understanding of research processes among data protection authorities can lead to fragmentation and overly cautious decisions. Enable a broad deﬁnition of research that supports and recognises scientiﬁc methods The Commission’s intention to include in the GDPR a definition of scientific research in order to better enable personal data use is welcome. However, it needs to be aligned with scientific methods and principles such as reproducibility and verification, which in turn underline the need for data access. Therefore, an amendment like the one below is suggested. Additionally, it is important to recognise scientific research (including innovation) as a legitimate interest and a generally compatible processing purpose with appropriate safeguards, as proposed in the digital omnibus package. Promote consistent, research-friendly implementation across Europe Strengthening the science and technology literacy of data protection authorities and organisation DPOs is crucial for research data access. The guidance by EDPB explicitly addressing research and AI data needs can further contribute to utilising Europe’s valuable data assets in fields like health research. A further amendment could help the achievement of this and a more uniform application of the regulation across Europe: Introduce new Article 89a: EU-level guidance for research and innovation Justification “The Commission and the European Data Protection Board, in cooperation with scientiﬁc stakeholders, shall develop EU-level guidance to support application of the GDPR in scientiﬁc research and innovation. This guidance shall address, inter alia: (a) pseudonymisation and anonymisation practices; (b) proportional application of data minimisation; (c) cross-border data transfers for research; and (d) the speciﬁc methodological needs of scientiﬁc research and AI development.” 
EU research is currently hindered by fragmented national interpretations of key GDPR provisions, leading to delays, uncertainty, and stalled cross-border data flows essential for health and biomedical research. Ensuring consistent, research-appropriate guidance across the Union, addressing persistent ambiguities around pseudonymisation, anonymisation, data minimisation, and transfers, can contribute to more and better-quality European research utilising more diverse data.  4. Interpret data minimisation proportionately It must be acknowledged that health research and AI development often require broad datasets. This is essential for discovery and accurate models, as well as in the case of AI, the models’ learning. Consequently, the data minimisation principle should be interpreted correctly in the research and innovation context. An amendment introducing this approach into the GDPR could help better reflect the current research and innovation needs in the digital era.

Towards a Single Market of research and innovation The Letta report on the future of the Single Market urges better integrating research, innovation, data, competencies, knowledge, and education into the EU framework as a fifth freedom complementing the free movement of goods, services, people, and capital. Data being the basis for scientific discovery and evidence, its availability is the keystone also for better cross-border research in the EU. EU flagship initiatives, such as the Cancer Mission, 1+ Million Genomes, European Genomic Data Infrastructure, AI Factories, depend on the ability to pool sensitive data across borders. Streamlined, research-friendly GDPR implementation would enable faster discovery, stronger innovation, and better health outcomes for citizens.  A more enabling GDPR is not about reducing protection – it is about ensuring that strong protection supports the free movement of data for the public good.

GDPR and health research data: from hindrance to catalyst of Single Market Key messages 1. Data is vital for scientific research and innovation. 2. Access to personal data is a precondition for biomedical breakthroughs, such as AI-based tools to better understand cancer progression, leading to faster diagnosis, tailored treatments, and cost-efficiency. 3. GDPR updates must introduce a broad definition of research, recognising research as a legitimate interest and generally compatible processing purpose, and ensure core scientific principles like reproducibility and critical evaluation are captured, making clear the necessity of responsible data access. 4. Pseudonymisation and anonymisation criteria should remain practical and researcher-driven to provide proportional safeguards that support, rather than hinder, research and innovation. 5. Policymakers and competent authorities should promote a consistent, research-friendly application of the GDPR across Europe and strengthen data protection officers’ understanding of research and innovation processes, including the role and value of data within them. 6. The data minimization principle must be interpreted in context — research and AI development often require broader data collection than other sectors.  7. GDPR has strengthened data protection; now it’s time to enable secure cross-border data flows to build a Single Market for research and innovation. The Letta report on the future of the Single Market urges better integrating research, innovation, data, competencies, knowledge, and education into the EU framework as a fifth freedom complementing the free movement of goods, services, people, and capital.1 Data being the basis for scientific discovery and evidence, its availability is the keystone also for better cross-border research in the EU. In essential disciplines like health and biomedical research, a lot of important data is personal by nature. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) has had a significant positive impact on the protection of personal data and individuals’ rights. It has clarified responsibilities in the processing by defining the roles of data controller and data processor. Protection and clear responsibilities should in principle create conditions for secure transfers of personal data within the EU and internationally, which is presumed to facilitate cross-border cooperation in scientific research and accerelate innovation. In a way, the point of data protection is indeed to enable its free movement. While the data protection aspect of the GDPR has gained emphasis in the early years of applying the legislation, finding the right balance so that research, innovation and data can flow across Europe for better health and economic benefits to citizens is ever more important for the Union’s goals of prosperity and competitiveness. The solution may lie in a right kind of implementation supported by right kinds of guidance and awareness-raising.

Consistent application – but in a research-friendly manner The Regulation intends to offer flexibility for data processing for research purposes, as long as appropriate safeguards are in place. However, its application has on the contrary posed challenges, increasing bureaucracy and uncertainty regarding the correct procedures particularly in the health and biomedical fields. Transfer of biomedical data in cross-border research has slowed down and even halted on many occasions due to fragmentation in national application, which mainly seems to stem from diverging interpretations of the regulation by data protection authorities. This interrupts research projects or makes them rely on less generous data, thus reducing their potential of discovery, inhibiting for example EU innovation in precision medicine and slowing disease diagnosis. In some cases, research might move outside European borders, causing brain drain.  The requirements pertaining to consent, data subject rights, and international data transfers seem to be the biggest sources of difficulty for the research community.2 
To address the situation, it is paramount to ensure that the GDPR is implemented so that it guarantees protection but allows free and secure movement of data across borders and boundaries. This requires that the nature of scientific research and the principle of open science are understood by data protection officers and authorities. It can be helped by more consistent interpretation and application of the Regulation across Europe. The guidelines and recommendations of the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) can be instrumental in this regard, but they must take into account the specificities of science and technology development, raise awareness about them and the central role data plays there. Clarity and reconsideration required for pseudonymised data - data minimisation in science and AI development must be reinterpreted Any GDPR amendment and implementation guideline should reiterate and reaffirm a broad interpretation of scientific research, as well as confirm scientific research as a legitimate interest and generally compatible secondary use when appropriate safeguards are in place.3 In this respect, the targeted amendments introduced in the recent digital omnibus package4 go in the right direction, also by including innovation in the definition. However, the proposed definition must better capture the scientific method and its aspects like reproducibility and critical evaluation. This would make evident the necessity of access to data. At its core, scientific research has the expansion of knowledge by enriching it with new information and integrating new data into broader contexts, while being able to verify previous findings, too. One persisting challenge faced by research relying on biomedical data is the ambiguities about pseudonymized data and the adequate security levels for it. The digital omnibus tries to clarify the situation by adding to the GDPR that “information relating to a natural person is not necessarily personal data for every other person or entity, merely because another entity can identify that natural person”. Clarifying the status of pseudonymized data can improve the reusability of datasets which is important for resource efficiency and scientific verification and research reliability.6 With the omnibus proposal, the Commission prepares to develop means and criteria to assess when pseudonymized data no longer constitutes personal data. Although anonymization is a demanding process, these must be feasible and practical, avoiding for example mathematically complex and overly precise anonymisation requirements that instead of simplification would risk becoming additional burden for the research and innovation process. For this reason, the researchers – who often best know their data – should have a major role in the pseudonymisation and anonymisation process, and in determining the means and criteria. As research becomes increasingly data-intensive, it is also necessary to critically examine the GDPR’s principle of data minimisation in the context of research, as it is not always possible to define in advance what data is useful for the research at hand or, conversely, what new insights are missed if the data is scarce. It is impossible to know how different data types are related to each other in explaining a phenomenon before the research has been completed. Strengthening data protection officers’ understanding of research processes would help ensure a more consistent and proportionate application of the data minimisation principle.  The same applies in the case of AI systems trained on vast amounts of data that are supposed to develop and evolve over time. For example, predictive machine learning is a promising technology to understand and explain disease progress, but the prediction remit is bound by the information content of available research data used for the models. The GDPR implementation indeed needs to be in line with the digital transformation and respond to changing needs7. This entails a need to improve science and technology literacy among authorities but in society overall.  Towards benefits of cross-border research, data flows and digitalisation Health and life sciences research lays the foundation for patient care and more effective and efficient treatment and cures. For example, sensitive health data must be made available in a more streamlined way for Europe to address increasing healthcare demands caused by its aging population. Overall, the competitiveness sought through significant joint European investments in digitalization of science and innovation, such as the AI Factories, will not materialize unless there is data. Understanding phenomena like cancer progression – from cell and molecule level events to clinical observations – is only possible by accessing large, multimodal datasets covering e.g. patient data, clinical, molecular, imaging, and genomic data. The data do not reside readily in one place but need to be pooled together from different countries for analysis, research, and development of AI models. Gathering metadata, anonymised or aggregated data is in principle uncomplicated, but accessing detailed datasets with discovery potential usually requires decisions by data protection authorities. Technologies and procedures to secure the data analysis are there, so this is where the research-friendly regulatory framework and clear guidelines come in. In the best case, a streamlined and enabling implementation of the GDPR generates pioneering European cooperation in research and innovation. Data could be the factor that bridges seemingly disconnected EU initiatives like the Cancer Mission8, 1+ Million Genomes9, and the AI Factories10 – and even the Single Market for research and innovation.  Moreover, secure and seamless research use of biomedical and other personal data can provide a competitive advantage for European research and thereby contribute to improving public health, well-being and prosperity throughout Europe. Therefore, implementing the GDPR in scientific research should focus on ensuring the widest and smoothest possible use of data across Europe, in order to reap the benefits from European research and innovation activities and investments.

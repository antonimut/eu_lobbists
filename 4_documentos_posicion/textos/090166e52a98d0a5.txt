Microsoft recommendations on the Digital and AI Omnibus proposals 
 
We welcome the European Commission’s ongoing work on Digital Simpliﬁcation. By embracing a 
forward-looking and ambitious approach to simplifying key digital rules, the EU can ensure a 
pivotal step-change towards strengthening Europe’s competitiveness and meaningfully 
contribute to achieving its AI continent ambitions. Europe needs a digital acquis adapted to the 
next decade, anchored in accountability, risk-based regulation and more consistent enforcement 
across the Union. The Commission’s proposal for the “Omnibus on AI” and the “Omnibus for the 
Digital Acquis” contain important improvements and clariﬁcations to that end and offer a 
strategic opportunity for the EU’s co-legislators to further shape Europe’s digital framework, 
improving its clarity and efficiency, fostering compliance for companies of all sizes. To contribute 
constructively to that process, please ﬁnd below our key considerations and recommendations 
to further support the successful implementation and impact of both Commission proposals.  
 
1. Recommendations on the Digital Omnibus Regulation 
 
GDPR / ePrivacy 
  
A constructive step to clarify and modernize Europe’s data protection framework –  Ahead of 
the Digital Omnibus Package, Microsoft’s simpliﬁcation recommendations focused mainly on 
ensuring more consistent and harmonized enforcement under the GDPR and across other digital 
regulations. In our view, the Digital Omnibus proposals on data protection mostly clarify, rather 
than rewrite, the GDPR framework that is largely ﬁt for purpose. We wish to share the following 
speciﬁc observations:   
- Acknowledging AI and Sensitive Data – We welcome the new provisions on special 
categories of personal data in an AI context (articles 9 (2)(k) & 9 (5)), which recognise that in 
developing AI systems, exposure to special categories of data is in some circumstances 
unavoidable. This approach aligns with the CJEU caselaw in the landmark Costeja case and 
with the CNIL’s guidance on sensitive data processing in an AI context.  
o While we acknowledge that such processing should be done under solid safeguards, it 
is challenging to capture such safeguards in a single and static legal provision, which 
could easily become too restrictive or outdated. We therefore recommend more 
ﬂexibility in the formulation of the safeguards.    
- New research deﬁnition – We support the new concept of scientiﬁc research (article 4(38)), 
which clariﬁes that the GDPR rules on academic research also apply to projects with 
commercial involvement. This is aligned with GDPR recital 159 and will help stimulating 
Europe’s innovative potential, while maintaining protective safeguards.  
o We see room to ﬁne-tune the text, which could now be read to only cover research 
carried out for the “sole” aim of contributing to the growth of society’s general

The ePrivacy simpliﬁcation does not go far enough – We however regret that the proposal 
misses an opportunity to efficiently simplify the ePrivacy framework. While we appreciate the 
intention of the Commission to integrate article 5 of the ePrivacy Directive (ePD) partially under 
the GDPR (in new articles 88a and 88b), the simpliﬁcation unfortunately does not go far enough 
and adds new complexities.  
- The ePrivacy Directive is over 20 years old and was designed for a different era; the Digital 
Omnibus is the ideal moment to modernize it comprehensively, by merging most of its 
provisions with the GDPR (e.g., those on traffic data processing), or other legal instruments 
such as the EECC/DNA.  
- Second, the newly proposed article 88a foresees only very narrow exemptions to the consent 
requirement for processing personal data on a device: the exemption for security purposes 
should at a minimum cover: security, fraud, and related software updates.  We  also 
recommend including contextual advertising as an exception to the consent requirement – 
in accordance with the suggestions made by the EDPB/EDPS.   
- Third, with regard to article 88b GDPR, we question the feasibility of imposing the consent 
mechanism on the browser community, and how this might impact a wide range of European 
online publishers and service providers.    
Opportunities for clarifying International Data Transfers – The digital omnibus package does 
not include any simpliﬁcation proposals in the data transfers space, where legal uncertainty is 
causing signiﬁcant transaction costs that are duplicated across all processors who export data. 
We would welcome greater legal certainty on extra EU data transfers, e.g. around when the 
Commission’s standard contractual clauses (“SCCs”) apply, or through greater involvement of 
public stakeholders in assessing the adequacy of data protection regimes in third countries, and 
guidance on when and which “supplementary measures” are truly necessary for transfers. These 
steps would signiﬁcantly reduce duplicative compliance efforts and anxiety for businesses, 
without lowering the level of protection for Europeans’ data.

Data policy 
Maintain proposed consolidation of the data policy framework – We welcome the 
consolidation of the different pieces of legislation into the Data Act. This is an important ﬁrst step 
in creating more consistency across the different scopes and deﬁnitions and improving 
readability for companies. The creation of a single and uniﬁed framework for public sector data 
re-use is also a positive step to minimize the overlaps and could help foster data sharing in that 
context. We also welcome the proposed integration into the Data Act of the prohibition on data 
localization requirements. International data ﬂows are a cornerstone of the data economy, and 
companies rely on them to enhance their global competitiveness, develop products, and gain 
insights on issues that are global in nature.  
Remove ability to allow differentiated conditions for “very large companies” in the public 
sector data reuse context (Article 32.r) – The provision runs counter to the regulation’s stated 
goal of simpliﬁcation, as it would ultimately limit choices, including through higher fees, and 
create unnecessary additional complexity in the data sharing value chain, which would be 
detrimental to reaching the EU’s goals to foster data sharing and data re-use. A likely net effect of 
such provisions will be to limit the ability of EU businesses to access cutting edge and innovative 
data analytics, especially in the context of AI development and deployment. Especially the 
proposed option for public sector bodies to introduce different license conditions for data reuse 
by very large enterprises instead of using standard open licenses will lead to license 
incompatibilities and thus limit everyone’s ability, including SMEs and civil society, to reuse 
public sector data and combine it with other sources into large data sets. Instead of removing the 
encouragement to use standard licenses, the Digital Omnibus should strengthen standard 
licenses such as Creative Commons CC 0 or CC BY. 
Clarify or remove language related to the prohibition on termination fees for IaaS in the Data 
Act that could be misinterpreted – We are concerned that the current language about limits on 
fees collected upon termination of IaaS services that has been introduced in Article 31 (1b) could 
limit EU customers’ access to ﬁxed term agreements and discounted pricing through bulk or 
minimum purchase agreements. This new term deviates from the original intent of Data Act 
(particularly Recital 89) that is not making any differentiation between types of data processing 
services. It also risks misinterpretation by eliminating existing contract structures relied upon by 
EU customers. Early termination fees enable providers to offer signiﬁcant discounts and added 
value to customers who commit to multi-year agreements, fostering predictability and long-term 
investment in innovative services. Eliminating recovery when a contract ends prematurely results 
in providers facing heightened ﬁnancial risk and discourages them from granting favorable pricing 
or investing in advanced features. This also creates an incoherent situation and a different regime 
with SaaS and PaaS provisions. We understand it was not the direct intention of the Commission 
to create such prohibition and would encourage policymakers to ﬁx this during the negotiation 
process by removing the IaaS exclusion reference at the end of the Article 31(1.b). 
Introduce further ﬂexibility and simpliﬁcation on Cloud Switching and interoperability 
provisions – We welcome the amendments to the Data Act cloud switching rules that better take 
into account situations of data processing services that are adapted for highly customised 
services (i.e. “custom-made”). Further ﬂexibility on some of the technical and contractual 
requirements would be helpful to better reﬂect feasibility of switching scenarios. In particular we

Cyber incident reporting 
Maintain and build upon the Single-Entry Point for incident reporting – We view the 
introduction of a Single-Entry Point (SEP) for incident reporting, to be developed and managed by 
ENISA, as a promising ﬁrst step toward streamlining reporting obligations across key EU 
cybersecurity and digital regulations. By allowing organizations to submit a single notiﬁcation 
automatically routed to relevant national authorities, the SEP has the potential to reduce 
administrative complexity, prevent duplicative or inconsistent queries, and ensure that critical 
information promptly reaches the appropriate authorities. In order for the SEP to realize its full 
potential to further materially enhance the simpliﬁcation and alignment of incident reporting 
across multiple digital domains, we recommend taking the following aspects into consideration: 
- Use the CRA Single Reporting Platform (SRP) as the foundation for the Omnibus Single 
Entry Point (SEP) – To ensure a truly uniﬁed and coherent EU-level reporting architecture, 
the SEP’s scope should be expanded to include incidents notiﬁed under the Cyber Resilience 
Act (CRA). While Article 4(1) allows ENISA to rely on the CRA S R P, without explicit alignment, 
there is a risk that the CRA SRP and SEP evolve as parallel systems. We recommend that co-
legislators designate the CRA Single Reporting Platform as the model and shared 
infrastructure for the Single-Entry Point (SEP), creating a coherent and uniﬁed EU-level 
incident reporting framework. 
- Integrate AI Act serious incident reporting into the SEP mechanism – Allowing providers 
to submit a single interoperable notiﬁcation that is automatically routed to the relevant 
authorities and national market-surveillance authorities (MSAs) would prevent up to 27 
parallel investigations and inconsistent or uninformed queries. It can also offer clear 
efficiency and oversight beneﬁts for governments and national authorities. By routing 
notiﬁcations through a single-entry point with national CSIRTs docked in, authorities can 
more easily identify cross-sectoral trends, detect cross-border risks, and coordinate 
responses. This approach also eliminates the need for Member States to manually 
disambiguate overlapping legislative requirements and accelerates information-sharing 
between various cyber, AI, and product-safety regulators. Over time, it could support a more 
coherent understanding of emerging risks, including clarifying the boundary between model-
level and system-level incidents. 
- Develop a secure and efficient mechanism within SEP to facilitate follow-up 
communications – We further recommend incorporating within this platform a secure and 
efficient mechanism for follow-up communications. A centralized system for responding to 
regulatory inquiries would allow competent authorities to share information, reduce

data is essential to design secure, interoperable, and user-friendly reporting mechanisms, 
strengthen shared cybersecurity responsibility, and mitigate inadvertent compliance 
burdens.

2. Recommendations on the AI Omnibus Regulation 
 
2.1 Provisions Microsoft supports and recommends strengthening 
In this section, we highlight the modiﬁcations that we welcome in the proposal while also 
recognizing that they require further modiﬁcations to gain clarity and effectiveness and fully 
enable their objective of legal certainty and simpliﬁcation. 
- “Grace Period” for requirements on disclosure of AI-generated content (Art. 11): We 
welcome the Commission’s intention to provide additional time for providers of generative 
AI systems to comply with any applicable marking obligations under Article 50(2) AI Act 
without disrupting the market. 
o However, the Code of Practice detailing the implementation of these requirements will 
not be ﬁnalized before June 2026. As the proposed six-month grace period applies only 
to AI systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026, systems placed on the 
market after that date would need to comply almost immediately on the basis of the 
ﬁnal Code, creating signiﬁcant legal uncertainty and practical engineering planning 
and implementation challenges. 
 We therefore recommend extending the grace period to 12 months and applying it 
to AI systems placed on the EU market between 2 Aug 2026 and 2 Aug 2027. 
 For text, a further grace period should be considered given heightened technical 
challenges and likely time -intensive implementation once an impactful, feasible 
solution is identiﬁed. 
o Similarly, the delayed application timeline proposed for Article 50(2) should be 
extended to the rest of Article 50, in particular Article 50(4), to ensure regulatory 
coherence and feasible implementation. As the forthcoming Code of Practice will also 
deﬁne transparency and provenance expectations for deployers under Article 50(4), 
the absence of any grace period for deployers creates the same implementation 
challenges that motivated the grace period for providers. 
 We therefore recommend also extending the timeline for Article 50(4) and the 
remainder of Article 50 to allow both providers and deployers sufficient time to 
translate the Code into robust engineering and operational solutions, enabling 
consistent, technically sound, and interoperable implementation aligned with the 
Commission’s guidance. 
- “Grace period” for High-Risk AI Systems (Recitals 21-22, Paragraph 31 amending Art. 113): 
We welcome the introduction of a grace period due to the unavailability of “adequate 
measures in support of compliance, ” as companies need sufficient time to assess such 
measures once available and adjust their compliance investments accordingly. However, as 
proposed, the transition mechanism is ambiguous and raises concerns about the existing 
timeline. 
o Given the short timeframe before high-risk AI obligations apply, and the uncertainty 
over whether and when the AI Omnibus will be agreed and enter into force, companies

2.2 Provisions Microsoft supports and recommends maintaining 
Aligned with the objective of simplification, we support the following measures proposed by the 
European Commission: 
 
- More Flexible Post-Market Monitoring (Recital 23 / Art. 72): We welcome the stated 
objective to offer “more ﬂexibility in the post-market monitoring by removing a prescription 
of a harmonized post-market monitoring plan” , thereby replacing the harmonized template 
with a post-market monitoring plan, on which the EC needs to publish guidance. This 
approach allows companies to develop plans tailored to their speciﬁc AI systems and risk 
contexts, and that fit their organizational structure and technologies rather than requiring them 
to follow a set, pre-determined, one-size-ﬁts-all template. 
 
- Legal Basis to Process Special Categories of Personal Data for Bias Mitigation (Art. 4a): 
We welcome the introduction of Article 4a, which provides a legal basis for providers and 
deployers to exceptionally process special categories of personal data for bias detection and 
correction under defined conditions. This enhances legal clarity for companies when 
processing such data. Article 4a(1d) provides that the exemptions would not apply where 
special categories of personal data are transmitted, transferred, or otherwise accessed by 
third parties. We would welcome clarifying language to explicitly recognize situations 
involving controlled disclosure to business partners under contractual arrangements that 
impose strict limitations on access, purpose, and data deletion. 
 
- Removal of Registration Obligation for Certain Non-High-Risk Systems (Art. 6 / Art. 49): 
We welcome the removal of the obligation to register AI systems in the EU database where 
systems listed in Annex III are exempted from high-risk classiﬁcation under Article 6(3), for 
example because they are used only for preparatory tasks. This provides greater legal clarity 
and aligns with the AI Act’s risk-based approach, whereby higher obligations correspond to 
higher risk, which preparatory uses do not meet.

2.3 New provisions Microsoft recommends introducing 
 
Apply a risk-based and proportionate approach to retroactive application of the AI Act 
- Retroactive application of AI Act obligations to AI systems or GPAI models placed on the 
market or put into service before the relevant application dates should be signiﬁcantly 
narrowed, guided by the level of risk those systems pose to health, safety, and fundamental 
rights. 
- Compliance expectations should focus on legacy systems with an especially elevated risk 
proﬁle and take into account technical feasibility, system maturity, and whether systems are 
actively maintained or materially modiﬁed. They should also account for signiﬁcant 
differences in how providers can engage retroactively with suppliers across the AI value 
chain. 
- Where retroactive compliance would require disproportionate re-engineering with limited risk-
reduction beneﬁts relative to the current context of their deployment, authorities should 
explicitly scope out systems or requirements or favor proportionate mitigation measures and 
phased enforcement, using supervisory discretion to support good-faith compliance while 
ensuring meaningful safety outcomes. 
o We therefore recommend clarifying this approach through an addition to Recital 197: 
“When exercising their powers under this Regulation in relation to AI systems and 
general-purpose AI models placed on the market or put into service before its 
application, competent authorities should act in a proportionate and risk-based 
manner. In doing so, they should scope out systems where re-engineering efforts are 
disproportionate to risk-reduction beneﬁts and prioritize cases involving risks to health, 
safety or fundamental rights. They should also take into account the feasibility of 
retroactive compliance, the maturity of the AI system or general-purpose AI model, and 
whether it is actively maintained or substantially modiﬁed. ” 
 
Interplay between the AI Act and EU Cybersecurity Legislation 
 
Clarify interplay with Cyber Resilience Act 
- We recommend reinstating the clarification, included in the leaked AI Omnibus draft, that high-
risk AI systems complying with the Cyber Resilience Act’s essential cybersecurity 
requirements and covered by an EU Declaration of Conformity under the CRA are deemed to 
meet the AI Act’s requirements on accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity (Art. 15). 
- Absent this clarification, providers face legal uncertainty and the risk of duplicative or 
inconsistent conformity assessments across overlapping EU frameworks. Reinstating it would 
avoid double testing, reduce administrative burdens for providers and notified bodies, and 
ensure the EU’s digital product safety framework operates as an integrated system rather than 
as parallel regimes, thereby supporting legal certainty and more efficient implementation.

AI systems used solely for cybersecurity purposes should not qualify as safety components 
- Recital 55 already provides an exclusion from qualification as a safety component for certain 
critical infrastructure uses. The AI Act should therefore apply a consistent approach to AI 
systems that are not considered high-risk. 
- We accordingly recommend clarifying that AI systems integrated into products or standalone 
AI systems listed in Annexes I and III, when used solely for cybersecurity purposes, should not 
be qualified as safety components. Extending this exclusion across Annexes I and III would 
enhance legal clarity and help prevent divergent interpretations of high-risk classification, as 
has been observed in discussions around the interpretation of the Radio Equipment Directive. 
 
Interplay with the GDPR 
 
Avoid duplicative or conflicting frameworks when applying overlapping AI Act and GDPR 
requirements (Art. 86 AI Act) 
- Article 15(h) GDPR grants individuals the right to be informed about solely automated 
decision-making, including meaningful information about the logic involved, signiﬁcance, 
and consequences of the processing. EU authorities and courts have clariﬁed that this 
obligation does not require disclosure of algorithms (Dun & Bradstreet ruling). Article 86 of the 
AI Act similarly provides a right to obtain meaningful explanations of the role of a high-risk AI 
system in a decision-making process and the main elements of a decision that adversely 
affects health, safety, or fundamental rights. While the two provisions differ in scope—GDPR 
focusing on automated decision-making and data use, and the AI Act on the role of high-risk 
AI systems and the resulting decision—there is signiﬁcant overlap in the information 
concerned. AI system providers should therefore not be required to respond to multiple 
requests covering the same information, nor to provide more granular explanations under the AI 
Act than are required under the GDPR. Given the higher-level framing of Article 86 AI Act, 
deployers should retain ﬂexibility in how explanations are provided. 
o Where the deployer under the AI Act is different from the controller under the GDPR, 
individuals may need to exercise (i) GDPR access rights against the controller and (ii) 
Article 86 AI Act rights against the deployer—risking fragmented processes or 
contradictory replies. We recommend endorsing a single, consolidated response 
workﬂow (and authority cooperation) so that one routed reply can satisfy both 
frameworks when requests concern the same decision and information, consistent 
with Article 86(3)’s anti-duplication clause and GDPR’s architecture of rights 
addressed to the controller. 
o To reduce uncertainty and compliance burden, we recommend Article 86 (or 
accompanying guidance) specify minimum elements of an explanation provided to an 
individual—using “in particular… ”—such as: (i) the AI system’s role in the decision 
pathway; (ii) the main input categories considered; (iii) whether/where human oversight 
occurred; (iv) key limitations or error sources etc. This would mirror the GDPR’s 
intelligibility and usability requirements and support consistent, person centric 
disclosures.

Clarify the interplay between AI Act human oversight requirements and GDPR automated 
decision-making rules 
- Article 14 of the AI Act requires high-risk AI systems to be designed and developed so that 
they can be effectively overseen by natural persons during their use. In parallel, Article 22 GDPR 
grants individuals the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated 
processing that produce legal or similarly signiﬁcant effects. 
- There is currently a lack of clarity as to whether, and under what conditions, the forms of human 
oversight required under Article 14 AI Act would be sufficient for a decision to be considered not 
“solely automated” for the purposes of Article 22 GDPR, including in light of the amendments 
currently proposed under the Digital Omnibus. 
o We therefore recommend that the Commission issue guidance clarifying the interaction 
between these provisions, including what types of human involvement would be relevant 
under GDPR, in order to provide legal certainty for providers and deployers. 
 
2.4 Additional Simpliﬁcation Opportunities to consider for the Fitness Check 
 
Avoid new horizontal or sector-specific AI legislation 
- The EU AI Act entered into force on August 2, 2024, but significant parts of the framework apply 
only after extended transition periods, with some provisions not applicable until August 2, 
2027. We therefore recommend that: 
o Until the AI Act is fully implemented and there has been sufficient time to assess its 
operation in practice, the Commission should refrain from proposing new horizontal AI 
legislation (such as the recently withdrawn AI Liability Directive) or sector-speciﬁc 
measures (for example, rules on AI in the workplace). 
o If and when new instruments are considered, they should provide regulatory clarity, avoid 
duplicating existing requirements, and be developed in close consultation with private 
stakeholders, in line with the Commission’s objective of engaging more effectively with 
stakeholders. 
 
Clarify cooperation between market surveillance authorities and authorities protecting 
fundamental rights 
- To ensure effective coordination, the legal framework should establish clear rules governing 
cooperation between MSAs and DPAs, while fully safeguarding the independence, mandate, 
and supervisory powers of DPAs and of MSAs. We therefore recommend: 
o Specifying information-sharing gateways, joint investigation possibilities, and conﬂict 
resolution mechanisms under the AI Office/Board umbrella. 
o Clarifying that MSAs should serve not only as administrative points of contact for 
executing and transmitting requests to providers and deployers, but also as essential 
counterparts to DPAs in situations involving both AI and personal data.

Adopt a “qualitative,” capabilities-based threshold to qualify as a GPAI model or a GPAI 
model with systemic risk 
- The GPAI Guidelines define GPAI models using a quantitative training compute threshold 
(exceeding 1023 FLOP), while the AI Act sets a 1025 FLOP threshold to identify GPAI models with 
systemic risk. 
- We welcome the AI Office’s acknowledgement that training compute is an “imperfect proxy” for 
a model’s generality and capabilities. While compute thresholds may offer short-term legal 
certainty, reliance on compute alone risks capturing an overly broad set of models. The need 
to frequently adjust such thresholds, given their limited suitability, undermines their claimed 
clarity and creates unpredictability for companies, particularly SMEs, when making 
investment and technical decisions. This runs counter to the Commission’s simpliﬁcation 
objective to keep pace with technological progress and make compliance easier for 
companies. 
- We consider capability-based evaluations, which directly assess a model’s generality and 
capabilities, to be an effective way to refine which models above a compute threshold fall 
within scope. As evaluation methods, tools, and standards mature, we recommend a 
combined compute- and capability-based approach to assess GPAI models. For example, a 
model exceeding the 10^25 FLOP threshold but performing significantly below leading frontier 
models on state-of-the-art benchmarks should be classified as a GPAI model unlikely to pose 
systemic risk. 
- Although benchmarks require periodic updating, increasing uncertainty around indirect 
proxies and growing investment in standardized evaluation tools make a combined approach 
more reliable and future-proof. The AI Office could also maintain an updated list of relevant, 
reliable benchmarks for providers to refer to in capability assessments. 
o We therefore recommend adopting a threshold focused on whether a model’s 
capabilities pose systemic risks covered by the Act, and on whether downstream 
modiﬁcations materially increase those capabilities and risks for the purpose of 
classifying providers. 
 
Establish a regular process to revise methods for assessing GPAI models with systemic risk 
(Article 51-52 AI Act) 
- Article 51 governs the classification of GPAI models with systemic risk, while Article 52 sets out 
the Commission’s procedure, including the obligation to publish and keep up to date a list of 
GPAI models with systemic risk (Art. 52(6)). 
- Consistent with the EU Simplification Agenda’s objective to keep pace with technological 
development, we recommend: 
o Introducing a regular review cycle (for example, annually) for systemic risk thresholds 
as model development techniques evolve. 
o Subjecting pre-training criteria to more frequent review (for example, every six months), 
in light of rapid gains in training efficiency and the emergence of techniques that 
enhance capabilities outside pre-training, including test-time compute. 
o Where a capabilities-based approach is adopted, establishing a regular process to 
update performance benchmarks as they become saturated or outdated.

Clarify the use of Annex XIII criteria for ex officio designations of GPAI models with systemic 
risk by the EU AI Office (Annex III AI Act) 
- For models below the compute threshold, the EU AI Office, with support from the independent 
scientific panel, may assess models ex officio to determine whether they have high-impact 
capabilities, using the criteria set out in Annex XIII (such as number of parameters, dataset size 
or quality, input and output modalities, capability benchmarks and evaluations, and market 
reach). However, the absence of quantitative indicators or thresholds for these criteria makes 
it difficult to anticipate how ex officio designations will be determined. We therefore 
recommend that the EU AI Office: 
o Provide guidance on how Annex XIII criteria will be applied when assessing models ex 
officio below the training-compute threshold. 
o Offer additional clariﬁcation on the application of Annex XIII, with a particular focus on 
capability-based criteria directly related to a model’s capabilities, such as 
benchmarks and evaluations of model performance, including task generality, 
adaptability, autonomy, scalability, and access to tools. 
 
Clarify the research exemption to enable responsible pre-release GPAI model testing 
(Article 2(8) and Recital 25 of the AI Act) 
- Article 2(8) excludes research, testing, and development activities conducted prior to placing 
a GPAI model on the market, except for testing in real-world conditions. Recital 97 and Article

before market placement fall outside the Act’s scope. However, the scope of “testing in real-
world conditions” remains unclear pending AI Office guidance, risking a chilling effect on 
legitimate pre-release testing that is essential for model safety and quality. 
- In practice, providers often test unreleased GPAI models through limited, non-commercial 
exposure on public or semi-public platforms to gather human feedback, benchmark 
performance, and identify safety or robustness issues. This established practice generates 
insights that cannot be achieved through internal testing or static benchmarks alone. Absent 
legal clarity, providers may avoid making such models available in the EU, undermining pre-
deployment safety testing and innovation. 
- To address this, we recommend a narrow interpretative clarification in Recital 25 confirming 
that pre-release testing of GPAI models involving limited, non-commercial exposure to real 
users for research, evaluation, safety testing, or benchmarking does not constitute “testing in 
real-world conditions” under Article 2(8), provided appropriate safeguards are in place. This 
would support rigorous pre-market testing while remaining aligned with the AI Act’s risk-
mitigation and safety objectives. 
- Suggested addition to Recital 25: 
o For the purposes of Article 2(8), the use of GPAI models prior to their placing on the 
market for research, development, prototyping, evaluation, or safety testing, including 
controlled or limited exposure to real users or researchers to collect feedback, 
benchmark performance, or identify risks, shall not be considered testing in real-world 
conditions where such use is non-commercial and subject to appropriate safeguards.

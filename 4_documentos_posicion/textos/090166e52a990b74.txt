EBU feedback on the Digital Omnibus on data 
 
The European Broadcasting Union is the world’s leading alliance of public service media (PSM). The EBU has 
115 member organizations in 56 countries who operate nearly 2,000 television, radio and online channels and 
services and reach an audience of more than one billion people in 160 languages.  
 
PSM organizations are entrusted with the performance of a service of general economic interest, which consists, 
inter alia, of the provision of high-quality content that fulfils the cultural and democratic needs of the society they 
serve. 
 
The European Broadcasting Union (EBU) welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on the 
European Commission’s proposal for a Digital Omnibus on data. 
 
As organisations operating at the intersection of media regulation, data protection and digital 
innovation, Public Service Media (PSM) are directly affected by the proposed adjustments to the 
EU data rules.  
 
We appreciate the Commission’s efforts to streamline and clarify existing laws. We stand ready 
to contribute constructively to this process, with a view to ensuring that simplification measures 
bring tangible benefits  to the European media industry and support the fulfilment of the public 
service remit. 
 
Summary of EBU recommendations 
- Extend the audience measurement exception in to better encompass existing audience 
measurement practices (Art. 88a).  
- Consider an adjustable list of additional consent exemptions for low -risk processing 
activities (Art. 88a). 
- Delete or significantly reduce the time limit for renewed consent request (Art. 88a). 
- Delete or significantly amend the provisions on consent automation (Art. 88b). 
- Support simplification measures regarding users’ right of access, data breach notification 
and data protection impact assessments (Art. 3(4), 3(8), 3(9)).  
- Maintain the exemptions for public service broadcasters from the Digital Governance Act 
and the Open Data Directive (now merged and incorporated in the Data Act) (Art. 1(18)). 
These recommendations are further developed in the following sections.

1. Consent exemptions (Article 88a) 
A. Audience measurement  
 
Audience measurement and data analytics are essential tools for PSM to fulfil their public 
service remit. They enable PSM to assess and demonstrate their reach, continuously improve 
their content offer, and adapt their services to the evolving needs and expectations of 
audiences. 
 
In several Member States, PSM  are legally mandated to report publicly on their performance 
and reach, in line with their remit. This is the case for example for the VRT in Belgium and RTÉ 
in Ireland. Similarly, Article 26a of the German State Media Treaty  (MStV) requires PSM to 
develop performance indicators and procedures, including both quantitative and qualitative 
analyses of the use of PSM services by specific target groups. 
 
Against this background, we welcome the European Commission’s proposal to introduce 
a consent exemption for audience measurement purposes . Such an exemption would 
represent an important step towards ensuring that PSM can continue to fulfil their statutory 
obligations.  
 
However, the current wording of the proposed provision appears too restrictive in scope 
and insufficiently flexible to accommodate existing audience measurement systems and 
industry practices. In particular, language such as “carried out by the controller (…) solely for 
its own use ” risks exclu ding measurement activities conducted by independent third 
parties acting on behalf of the media industry, such as Joint Industry Committees. These joint 
or industry -wide measurement systems play a critical role in ensuring transparency, 
comparability and credibility of audience data across the market. 
 
To address this issue , we recommend  amending Article 88a to enable a fully effective 
audience measurement framework while maintaining a high level of protection for end users’ 
personal data and privacy.  
 
B. Consent exemption for low-risk processing 
 
The objective of tackling consent fatigue and reducing the proliferation of cookie banners 
could be further advanced by introducing an additional consent exemption for a limited 
number of clearly defined use cases that do not entail significant risks for data subjects. 
 
In the media sector, certain data processing activities present no or only very limited risks to 
end users. Exempting such low-risk processing operations from consent requirements would 
reduce the volume of consent requests, thereby enhancing the overall e ffectiveness of the 
consent framework.

Examples of low-risk processing use cases include: 
 
- Improving the appearance or functionality of a service when displayed on, or accessed 
through, a user’s device, as well as adapting the way a service appears or operates in 
accordance with a user’s preferences.1 
 
- Displaying and measuring the performance of contextual advertising. As proposed by 
the EDPB and the EDPS2, certain trackers necessary for these purposes, such as frequency 
capping cookies, advertising audience measurement cookies, or cookies to combat click 
fraud, could be exempted from consent requirement s provided that they are strictly 
limited to these functions.  
 
More broadly, the introduction of a consent exemption mechanism based on a regularly 
updated whitelist of specific purposes would render the GDPR consent framework more 
responsive to technological and market developments. Such an approach would contribute 
to rationalising consent requests and reducing their frequency, while maintaining a high level 
of data protection. The European Commission, in cooperation with national regulatory 
authorities, could be entrusted with establishing and periodically updating this list. 
 
C. Limitations on consent requests 
 
Article 88a(4)(c) would prevent a controller from requesting consent again for the same 
purpose for a period of at least six months where the data subject has previously declined 
such a request. 
 
While the objective of reducing the frequency of consent requests and addressing consent 
fatigue is understandable, the proposed restriction would have unintended consequences for 
online media services, including Public Service Media.  
 
In practice, a rigid six -month prohibition would substantially limit the ability of service 
providers to introduce, test or refine new products and services that rely on user consent, even 
where these developments respond directly to evolving user expectat ions or technological 
innovation. 
 
The digital media environment is characterised by rapid  and iterative development/ 
improvement of services. Providers frequently adjust functionalities  and user interfaces in 
ways that may reasonably justify re -engaging users and inviting them to reconsider their 
choice. A blanket six -month ban on renewed consent requests  would constrain European 
media organisations’ capacity to innovate, compete effectively and remain relevant to their 
audiences in an increasingly competitive global market.

For these reasons, we recommend deleting this provision or at a minimum significantly 
adjusting it, by reducing the time limit to one month instead of six months, and  possibly by 
allowing for exceptions where there are material changes to the service. 
2. Consent automation and centralisation (Article 88b) 
Article 88b de facto  introduces a model of centralised  user consent management through 
browsers or other general setting  systems. While the objective of simplifying how end users 
manage their consent preferences is commendable, the broader implications of such an 
approach require careful technical and regulatory assessment 
 
It is important to recall that similar proposals have previously been considered and ultimately 
rejected in the context of the draft ePrivacy Regulation (Article 10), as well as under the 
European Commission’s “cookie pledge” initiative. On both occasions, significant concerns 
were raised by Public Service Media and the broader media industry regarding the practical 
consequences of centralised consent mechanisms: 
 
- Intermediation of user -service relationship : the centralisation of consent through 
browser settings risks creating friction by intermediating the relationship between end 
users and individual services. Users trust each individual service differently: a trusted 
local PSM is not perceived the same way as an unknown  or non-EU service provider which 
may not be subject to the same legal standards. Consent set at browser level is lik ely to 
reflect caution toward the least trusted services, undermining user choice for more trusted 
ones. 
 
- Lack of granularity and context : centralised consent tools often reduce complex data 
practices into "allow all/block all" options. This approach fails to capture important 
nuances, such as non -commercial or public -interest uses, or cookies that play a 
functional role without harming privacy. As a re sult, users may inadvertently block 
services they rely on or disable beneficial functionalities. Furthermore, a system based on 
broad, pre -set browser choices raises questions as to its compatibility with the GDP R 
standard of consent, which requires consent to be “specific” and “informed”.3 
 
- Gatekeeping risks: centralising consent enables browser providers to shape how consent 
is handled by imposing their own preferences or prioritizing business interests. For 
example, dominant browser providers may take an overly restrictive or overly permissive 
approach depending on their commercial priorities, which may not align with users’ 
interests or public service obligations.

We acknowledge that the Commission has tried to mitigate some of these risks by introducing 
a specific exception for media service providers in  Article 88b(3) . However,  it is unclear 
whether th e exception will be retained in the final legislative text , nor whether it would be 
sufficient in its current form to cover the relevant activities carried out by  media service 
providers. 
  
In light of these concerns , we recommend deleting Article 88b or, as an alternative, 
introducing sufficient safeguards to ensure that:  
 
- Controllers/media service providers  retain the ability to communicate directly with 
their end users  and, where appropriate, invite them to review or adjust their privacy 
settings. It is important in that regard that machine-readable indications of data subjects’ 
choices are developed on the basis of common standard s and industry guidelines, as 
proposed in Article 88 b(4), rather than relying on proprietary technologies or unilateral 
design choices. 
  
- Consent given at the level of an individual service prevails over general browser or other 
system-wide privacy settings, thereby preserving user autonomy, trust relationships and 
compliance with the GDPR standard of specific and informed consent. 
3. Data subjects’ rights 
Public Service Media support the proposed modification to data subjects’ rights in Article 
Article 3(4)  of the Digital Omnibus , which provides  a necessary clarification in situations 
involving manifestly unfounded or excessive requests. 
 
In practice, some PSM, like other large organisations with public-facing activities,  can receive 
requests that are repetitive, disproportionate or clearly unrelated to the purpose of data 
protection rights , such as  vexatious requests aimed at disrupting operations. While data 
subject rights must be fully respected, their misuse places a significant administrative and 
financial burden on organisations, diverting resources away from core public service 
activities. 
 
The proposed modification would facilitate a more effective handling of such situations by 
clarifying the circumstances in which controllers may decline to act on manifestly unfounded 
or excessive requests. This would enhance legal certainty and ensure tha t data protection 
rights are exercised in good faith, while preserving robust safeguards for individuals.

4. Procedural simplification 
Public Service Media support the procedural simplifications proposed in the Digital Omnibus, 
notably with regard to data breach notifications 4 and the harmonisation of Data Protection 
Impact Assessments (DPIAs). 5 These measures will contribute to reducing unnecessary 
administrative complexity while preserving a high level of data protection. 
 
For Public Service Media, which operate in a fast-evolving digital environment and increasingly 
rely on innovative technologies, such procedural improvements will enable more efficient 
allocation of resources, while maintaining full respect for fundamental rights and data 
protection standards. These adjustments therefore represent a proportionate and 
constructive step towards a more coherent and workable EU data framework. 
5. Exemptions for Public Service Broadcasters in the data acquis 
PSM have already underlined, in their contribution to the public consultation on the Data 
Union Strategy and the call for evidence  on a Digital Omnibus, that regulatory streamlining 
must not come at the expense of  safeguards protecting media freedom and the public service 
remit. 
 
Two key exceptions currently apply in the EU’s data rules: 
 
- Exception in the Data Governance Act (Regulation 2022/868/EU): data held by public 
service broadcasters and their subsidiaries for the fulfillment of a public service 
broadcasting remit are exempted from Chapter II provisions under Article 3(2)(b) of the 
Data Governance Act. 
 
- Exception in the Open Data Directive (Directive 2019/1024/EU): p ublic service 
broadcasters and their subsidiaries are exempted from the Open Data Directive under 
Article 1(2)(i). It is important that this exemption is maintained in any future revision, as the 
case of Public Service Broadcasters is fundamentally differe nt from that of the public 
service bodies which fall within the scope of the Open Data Directive. 
 
It is essential that the existing exemptions for Public Service Media (PSM) are explicitly 
maintained while the abovementioned laws are merged into the Data Act , for the following 
reasons:

- First, the definition and organisation of the public service broadcasting remit fall 
within the exclusive competence of the Member States , as recognised by the 
Amsterdam Protocol. PSM are already mandated to ensure that citizens have broad 
access to diverse content. Imposing horizontal data access or re -use obligations at EU 
level would risk interfering with the scope and modalities of that remit. 
 
- Second, the vast majority of data and materials held by PSM are protected by 
intellectual property rights . This includes not only broadcasters’ own rights but also 
third-party rights (such as music rights), which are embedded in virtually all broadcast 
content. Requiring systematic re-use or access would, in practice, conflict with copyright 
protection and cr eate significant legal and financial uncertainty. The clearance of rights 
for commercial re -use of archived materials often requires the consent of num erous 
contributors and may prove impracticable or disproportionate. The current exemptions 
appropriately recognise these structural constraints. 
 
- Third, and most importantly, PSM hold highly sensitive information linked to editorial 
processes and journalistic activity.  This includes confidential journalistic sources 
protected under Article 11 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights, unpublished editorial 
content, investigative research materials, and audience data revealing the consumption 
of potentially sensitive content.  Any obligation to provide access to such material would 
risk undermining editorial independence, source protection and media freedom.  
 
For these reasons, the existing exemptions are not mere technical carve outs but fundamental 
safeguards. Maintaining them in a simplified EU data framework is necessary to protect media 
freedom, intellectual property rights, and the integrity of the public service broadcasting remit. 
 
We therefore  welcome Article 1(18) of the Digital Omnibus on Data introducing a new 
32i(2)(d) in the Data Act, and we call upon co -legislators to ensure that this provision is 
maintained in the final text.

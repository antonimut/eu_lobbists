 
 
AI Chamber Submission on the European Commission’s “Digital Omnibus” package 
AI Chamber welcomes the direction the European Commission has taken with the proposed 
“Digital Omnibus” package. We see it as a necessary step toward reducing regulatory friction in 
Europe’s digital economy. At the same time, the package, at least as currently framed, does not 
go far enough to remove the practical obstacles that keep European companies from developing, 
deploying, and scaling data-driven and AI-enabled products across the Single Market.  
Europe’s competitiveness challenge is no longer abstract. If the Union aims to retain economic 
influence and credible strategic autonomy in the digital domain, it must be able to build and adopt 
foundational technologies domestically. That requires a regul atory environment that is workable 
in practice, especially for SMEs and start -ups and/or scale-ups while maintaining a high level of 
protection for fundamental rights.  
Our detailed commentary and proposed amendments for both the Digital Omnibus and the AI 
Omnibus are presented in the sections below. A summary chart is also included at the end of this 
document to provide a visual overview of the proposed amendments. 
We wish to draw attention to two overarching general concerns: 
Overlapping regulatory frameworks 
The primary concern for stakeholders is the significant and widely noted overlap of obligations 
between key legal acts - specifically the Data Act, the GDPR, and the AI Act. This overlapping 
regime creates an inconsistent and burdensome legal environment t hat threatens to stifle 
innovation. As we architect the future digital landscape, legislators must ensure this foundational 
issue of regulatory coherence is addressed comprehensively. 
We advocate for the introduction of clear priority rules and a "single compliance flow" to reduce 
administrative friction. Under this model, where data is shared under the Data Act, compliance 
with a harmonized set of requirements - such as standard clauses, safeguards, and 
documentation - should be recognized as sufficient also for GDPR and AI Act purposes, without 
additional, separate reporting and audit layers. 
Key definitions should be aligned across instruments and supported by joint, practical guidance 
in the form of a single coherent framework. Without such coordination, companies risk being 
forced to design multiple parallel compliance tracks, leading to dis proportionate costs that 
ultimately undermine EU competitiveness. 
A single fixed application date 
We call for a single, fixed application date for all high-risk AI systems, which is essential for legal 
certainty and effective compliance planning. Applying Chapter III, Sections 1 to 3, from 2 August 
2028 to all high-risk AI systems, whether classified under Article 6(1) and Annex I or under Article 
6(2) and Annex III, would create a clear and uniform implementation framework across the 
market.

I Digital Omnibus 
A. Regulation (EU) 2023/2854 - Data Act:  
 
1. Trade secret protection as an express ground for refusal (Arts. 4(8), 5(11) Data Act) 
Proposed provisions by the EC in the Digital Omnibus can harm trade secrets. Disclosure of a 
trade secret is effectively irreversible: once the information is released, confidentiality is lost and 
even expedited proceedings cannot restore its economic value. The amendment proposed by the 
AI Chamber therefore strengthens ex ante protection, i.e., before disclosure takes place. 
The purpose of the proposed amendment is to make explicit that trade secrets constitute a legal 
ground for refusing to share the requested data. Without such a clear legal basis, companies that 
seek to protect their know-how are, in practice, pushed towards urgent court action (e.g., interim 
measures), because once disclosure occurs the protection comes too late. 
The change also supports the competitiveness of EU firms in situations of heightened leakage 
risk, in particular where access is requested by entities linked to third -country control or subject 
to jurisdictions offering weaker or non -equivalent protection.  At the same time, it preserves 
proportionality by allowing partial refusal or provision of the data in a limited form, so that only 
what is strictly necessary to protect the trade secret is withheld, rather than imposing a blanket 
denial. 
2. Union-level methodology for data fees (Art. 32y(4) Data Act) 
From an internal market perspective, it is necessary to ensure that the criteria and methodology 
for calculating any fees are uniform at Union level, so that divergent national fee regimes do not 
emerge and create fragmentation and barriers to cross -border re-use. Where fees are charged, 
they should remain strictly tied to the closed list of “necessary costs” already set out in paragraph 
3, and the public sector body should provide a transparent breakdown of the fee for each specific 
request. This approach preserves cost-recovery for legitimate compliance and security -related 
expenses, while ensuring predictability and comparability of fees across the Union and preventing 
additional locally-created cost items. 
3.  SaaS legal clarity (Art. 31(1a) Data Act) 
This targeted clarification strengthens legal certainty by expressly confirming that the exemption 
in Article 31(1a) also covers individualised Software as a Service (SaaS) services provided on a 
bespoke basis. In practice, many customised SaaS offerings are adapted to a comparable extent 
 
 
as other tailored data processing services, yet the absence of an explicit SaaS reference creates 
interpretative uncertainty as to whether Chapter VI obligations (including switching -related 
requirements) apply. The proposed wording does not broaden the exemption materially; it simply 
clarifies that, where the majority of features and functionalities have been adapted to the 
customer’s specific needs, the exemption applies equally to bespoke SaaS. 
B. Regulation (EU) 2016/679 - GDPR 
1.  Workable safeguards for incidental special -category data in AI development (Art. 
9(5) GDPR) 
The objective of amending Article 9(5) is to make the safeguard regime operational and 
proportionate in real -world AI development and testing. As drafted, the requirement to “avoid” 
incidental collection of special category data implies an impossible obligation to pre-scrub large-
scale sources (e.g., open web data) and effectively blocks the use of large public datasets. It also 
creates a “privacy paradox”: forcing developers to scan for sensitive data in order to remove it 
can increase the amount of sensit ive processing and associated risks. Reframing the provision 
around “mitigating the risk” through technical and organisational measures preserves strong 
protection while enabling practical compliance and accountability. 
2. Legitimate interests in the AI context: preserving GDPR coherence (Art. 88c 
GDPR) 
The objective of the amendment is to preserve the Commission’s simplification goal by making 
Article 88c legally coherent with the GDPR’s own structure and workable across the Single 
Market. First, legitimate interest under Article 6(1)(f) GDPR explicitly covers the interests of both 
the controller and third parties; limiting Article 88c to the controller’s interests needlessly narrows 
the balancing test and reduces legal certainty for AI development and deployment. Second, the 
clause allowing “other Union or national laws” to require consent risks re-fragmenting the Single 
Market by enabling divergent national pathways that neutralise the clarification the Omnibus 
seeks to provide. Third, an “unconditional right to object” is not aligned with Article 21 GDPR; the 
right to object must operate under the GDPR framework to avoid legal inconsistency and 
enforcement uncertainty. The proposed edits therefore clarify, not rewrite, GDPR: they broaden 
the legitimate-interest test to its GDPR -native scope, remove an in ternal fragmentation trigger, 
and align the objection right with Article 21 GDPR, while maintaining strong safeguards 
3. Definition of “scientific research”  
We support the Omnibus amendment introducing an explicit definition of “scientific research” in 
the GDPR, Article 3 of the Digital Omnibus, amending GDPR Article 4 by adding a new point (38). 
This provides a clearer legal reference point for GDPR compliance in research and innovation 
contexts and helps reduce legal uncertainty in practice. 
4. Scientific research: safeguards and compatible further processing (Art. 89(1) 
GDPR) 
 
 
We support strengthening legal certainty for scientific research by maintaining a broad and 
innovation-friendly understanding of the concept, while preserving clear safeguards. The 
Omnibus proposal appropriately links scientific -research processing to appr opriate safeguards 
pursuant to Article 89(1) GDPR and clarifies purpose limitation by treating further processing for 
scientific research as compatible in accordance with Article 89(1). This improves predictability for 
research and innovation activities (including in industry settings) without lowering the level of data 
protection, and helps prevent divergent national interpretations and avoidable compliance 
disputes. 
5. Clarifying the contractual exception for solely automated decision-making (Art. 22 
GDPR) 
We support keeping the proposed change to Article 22 GDPR strictly targeted and business -
enabling. Clarifying the contractual exception for solely automated decision -making improves 
legal certainty by reducing divergent interpretations of “necessity” in co ntract-related processes 
and facilitates the scaling of lawful automated business operations. At the same time, this 
amendment should not be accompanied by additional conditions, tests, or further layers of 
obligations (including in recitals) that would, i n practice, create a new compliance regime and 
increase implementation costs without commensurate regulatory benefit. If further “safeguards” 
are proposed during negotiations, the preferred minimal -change approach is to rely on the 
protections already embe dded in the GDPR, rather than introducing new standards through 
recitals or guidance that effectively add new documentation or assessment duties. 
6. Definition of personal data (art. 4(1) GDPR) 
We strongly support bringing the definition of personal data more in line with the jurisprudence of 
the Court of Justice. While the proposed clarification does not address in an adequate manner 
the stifling effect and overbroad interpretation of personal d ata continues to have in the EU 
economy, it offers at least some clarity and lowers the level of legal risk for data processors and 
administrators. It would have been a far more useful move to remove the hypothetical aspect of 
the definition (“identifiable  person”) and create an unequivocal and clear definition of personal 
data as relating only to a concrete, identifiable natural person. If such a change is impossible the 
small, but positive change proposed by the EC must be maintained.  
C. Cybersecurity 
To make incident-reporting obligations genuinely simpler, the Digital Omnibus should also cover 
the AI Act within the “single entry point” approach. Incidents involving AI systems often have a 
clear security and/or safety dimension, and maintaining separat e reporting channels leads to 
fragmentation and duplicated compliance efforts. We therefore recommend that the Omnibus 
explicitly includes the AI Act within the “single entry point” mechanism and designates one 
common reporting platform (in particular, the platform developed under the CRA) as the primary 
place to submit reports, with appropriate onward routing of information to the competent 
authorities. 
 
 
II. AI OMNIBUS:  
C. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 – AI Act 
1. Research and development exemption covering commercial and collaborative R&D 
(Art. 2(8) AI Act) 
The research and development exemption in the AI Act should be applied in a manner that is 
consistent with the objectives of Union-carried out and Union-funded research activities, as well 
as with collaborative research projects involving public and private actors across Member States, 
including strategic initiatives such as OpenEuroLLM. In its current form, conditioning the 
exemption on the absence of market placement or putting into service risks limiting its practical 
applicability for companies engaged in research and development. In industrial and commercial 
research contexts, AI systems and models often need to be made available or deployed, including 
through formal market placement, in order to be used for research purposes, testing or joint 
development. As a result, the exemption may not be usable in situations where it was intended to 
apply. Clarifying that AI systems and models used for research and development purposes, 
including commercial research and development, fall within the scope of the exe mption ensures 
that legitimate research activities are not inadvertently subject to regulatory requirements 
designed for real-world deployment. 
2. Clarifying the supportive nature of AI literacy obligations (Art. 4 AI Act) 
Article 4 AI Act should remain a provision aimed at encouraging appropriate knowledge and skills, 
rather than being interpreted as imposing an obligation to guarantee individual outcomes. Greater 
legal certainty is needed to confirm that the role of providers and deployers is supportive in nature 
and relates to promoting appropriate AI literacy in light of the relevant context, rather than ensuring 
that any particular person reaches a specific level of understanding. This would help prevent over-
compliance and reduce the risk of inconsistent enforcement based on unrealistic expectations as 
to individual AI literacy outcomes. 
3. Special-category data for bias detection and correction under strict safeguards 
(new Art. 4a AI Act) 
The AI Act should provide a clear and workable framework for the exceptional use of special -
category data where this is strictly necessary for bias detection and correction. In limited cases, 
sufficiently representative and reliable bias testing cannot be achieved through other data sources 
alone without a material loss of effectiveness. Greater legal certainty is also needed to ensure 
that narrowly circumscribed and equivalently safeguarded cooperation with relevant third parties 
is not inadvertently prevented where this is strictly necessary for lawful bias detection, correction 
or verification. At the same time, retention should remain subject to strict limits, while allowing 
data to be kept only for as long as necessary to verify the effectiveness of cor rective measures. 
This would support effective and auditable compliance without lowering the level of protection 
afforded to special-category data. 
 
 
4. Limiting machine-readable marking to synthetic audio, image and video content 
(Art. 50(2)AI Act) 
The machine-readable marking obligation in Article 50(2) should remain focused on synthetic 
audio, image and video content, for which such measures can provide meaningful transparency 
value. Extending this requirement to text would be neither technically r eliable nor proportionate. 
Text is routinely edited, reformatted, excerpted, combined with human input and reused across 
different contexts, which makes persistent and effective machine -readable marking difficult to 
implement in practice. In these circumst ances, applying the same requirement to text would 
impose significant compliance burdens while offering limited additional transparency benefits. A 
more proportionate approach is therefore to maintain the obligation for synthetic audio, image and 
video content, while excluding text from its scope. 
5. Proportionate authority requests for Article 6(3) self -assessments (Art. 6(4)  
AI Act) 
Article 6(4) should preserve the existing “document and provide on request” approach, while 
ensuring that requests by competent authorities remain proportionate and limited to what is 
necessary to verify compliance with Article 6(3). Greater legal certainty is needed to confirm that 
providers are not required to disclose information beyond the minimum necessary to substantiate 
their self-assessment that an Annex III system is not high -risk. This would reduce unnecessary 
administrative burden, support more consistent supervisory practice across Member States, and 
maintain effective oversight without expanding information requests beyond their proper purpose. 
6. Earlier availability of the Union-level AI regulatory sandbox for SMEs and start-ups 
(new Art. 57(3a) AI Act) 
The Union-level AI regulatory sandbox should be made available at an earlier stage of the AI Act’s 
implementation, in particular for SMEs and start-ups that will face compliance obligations before 
2028. Regulatory sandboxes are most valuable when they are operational at an early stage of 
regulatory application, allowing businesses to test compliance pathways and engage with 
competent authorities before obligations become fully applicable in practice. Delaying the 
establishment of the Union-level sandbox would significantly reduce its practical utility for smaller 
market participants. The framework should therefore ensure that the Union -level AI regulatory 
sandbox becomes operational well before 2028, and no later than the date on which the AI Act’s 
obligations for high-risk AI systems begin to apply. 
7. Proportionate Union -level pre -market conformity assessments for high -risk AI 
systems (new Arts. 75(1a)–(1c) AI Act) 
Where the Commission organises pre-market conformity assessments and tests for high-risk AI 
systems, the applicable procedures should remain proportionate and workable in practice, in 
particular for SMEs and small mid -caps. Without appropriate procedural s implifications, 
centralised assessments risk creating disproportionate documentation and administrative 
burdens, which may delay market access and create avoidable compliance bottlenecks. The 
 
 
framework should therefore ensure SME - and SMC -friendly procedures, including simplified 
documentation and reduced administrative steps, while fully preserving the level of protection and 
regulatory objectives of the AI Act. 
8. A single and proportionate transition period for transparency obligations (new Art. 
111(4) AI Act) 
The transitional framework for the transparency obligations under Article 50 should ensure both 
sufficient implementation time and a single, clear date of application. Extending the transition 
period would allow providers to introduce the required technica l measures in a consistent and 
technically robust manner. It is also important that the transitional rule applies coherently across 
the relevant transparency obligations, including both Article 50(2) and Article 50(4), and to 
systems placed on the market u p to the end of the transition period. Such an approach would 
strengthen legal certainty, prevent unequal treatment and market distortion between earlier and 
later market placements, and support a more coherent application of the transparency framework. 
9. Moving the Machinery Regulation from Section A to Section B to avoid regulatory 
overlap (Annex I AI Act) 
The AI Act overlaps with existing sectoral legislation, in particular the Machinery Regulation for 
self-learning machines and software. The additional requirements introduced by the AI Act do not 
enhance safety, as the mere presence of an AI component does not automatically make a product 
unsafe, but they do increase administrative burden and compliance costs. The parallel application 
of the AI Act and the Machinery Regulation risks duplicating requirements and stifling innovation. 
To avoid these overlaps and simplify the regulatory framework, the Machinery Regulation should 
be moved from Section A to Section B, allowing the Commission to address AI -specific risks 
through implementing or delegating acts where necessary. The Commission has already 
recognised this approach by proposing to move the Medical Devices and In Vitro Diagnostics 
Regulations from Section A to Section B, and the same logic should apply to the Machinery 
Regulation. In addition, notified bodies assessing high-risk AI systems should comply with the AI-
specific requirements set out in Article 31 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. 
10. Ensuring the independence of the AI Office from policy -making and industrial 
functions 
The AI Office would simultaneously participate in shaping regulatory practice, interpreting the AI 
Act, and enforcing obligations on providers of AI models and systems. If it remains embedded 
within DG CONNECT, there is a risk of conflating political, legi slative and supervisory functions. 
DG CONNECT is responsible for developing digital policy and supporting the sector’s 
competitiveness, whereas a supervisory body should operate primarily on the basis of impartiality, 
equal treatment and compliance control. 
The lack of clear independence may create the perception that AI Office decisions are taken not 
solely on the basis of legal and technical criteria, but also under the influence of the Commission’s 
current political priorities. This undermines market confi dence, especially in cases concerning 
 
 
GPAI models, enforcement measures, requests for access to documentation, or assessments of 
systemic risk. 
From a business perspective, the independence of the AI Office would increase regulatory 
predictability. Businesses should be able to rely on the authority applying the rules in a stable, 
expert and politically neutral manner, free from pressures linked to the EU’s industrial, innovation 
or political agenda. This is particularly important where the Commission is simultaneously 
promoting the uptake of AI and supervising compliance for the same technology. 
In practice, the issue is therefore not only one of formal autonomy, but of avoiding a conflict of 
roles: the same branch of the Commission should not at the same time promote the development 
of AI, help shape policy and exercise quasi-enforcement powers over the market. 
11. Deleting the complaint mechanism to preserve proportionate and risk -based 
enforcement (Art. 85 AI Act) 
Proposed deletion of the AI Act complaint mechanism is justified on proportionality and 
simplification grounds. The AI Act already relies on market surveillance and enforcement powers 
(including the ability to act ex officio, request information, investigate and impose measures), so 
a stand-alone “right to lodge a complaint” risks creating a parallel intake channel that increases 
administrative workload without clearly improving enforcement outcomes. In practice, such a 
complaint system can incentivise stra tegic or vexatious complaints and drive procedural 
uncertainty and cost for providers and deployers (especially SMEs/SMCs), while the underlying 
allegations can still be raised through existing legal and administrative avenues at national level. 
Removing t he complaint mechanism keeps enforcement focused on risk -based supervision, 
reduces duplicative processes, and supports a more predictable and streamlined compliance 
environment. 
12. Limiting source -code access to protect cybersecurity and trade secrets (Arts. 
74(13), 92(3) AI Act) 
Articles 74(13) and 92(3) should be deleted or at least significantly narrowed, because access to 
source code creates disproportionate cybersecurity and intellectual property risks for providers. 
Even if intended as an enforcement tool, such powers expose highly sensitive trade secrets and 
increase the risk of leakage, misuse or security compromise. Regulators should rely on less 
intrusive means wherever possible, such as documentation, testing, audit trails and controlled 
technical interfaces, rather than direct source-code access. 
13. Aligning the fundamental rights impact assessment with the GDPR data protection 
impact assessment (Art. 27 AI Act; Art. 35 GDPR) 
The comment addresses duplication between the fundamental rights impact assessment under 
Article 27 AI Act and the data protection impact assessment under Article 35 GDPR. From a 
business perspective, the concern is that deployers may be forced to perform two overlapping 
assessments. However, a DPIA covers data protection risks only, while Article 27 covers broader 
 
 
fundamental rights risks. The better solution would therefore be alignment or a single integrated 
assessment, rather than simply replacing Article 27 with GDPR DPIA. 
14. Deleting the CE-marking requirement for high-risk AI systems (Art. 48 AI Act) 
Article 48 should be deleted. CE marking was designed for product compliance and is not well 
suited to standalone AI systems or software -based services that are dynamic, continuously 
updated and often not placed on the market in the same way as traditional  products. Extending 
CE-marking logic to high-risk AI systems creates legal fiction rather than meaningful assurance, 
while increasing compliance costs, formalism and market-entry barriers, especially for innovative 
European providers. In the software and AI context, compliance should be demonstrated through 
targeted documentation, risk management and ex post supervisory powers, rather than through 
a product-style marking regime that may mislead users and business customers as to the nature 
and reliability of the system. Deleting Article 48 would therefore reduce unnecessary regulatory 
burden without preventing effective enforcement under the AI Act.

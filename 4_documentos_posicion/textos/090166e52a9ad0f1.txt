1 INTRODUCTION 
Dedalus welcomes the opportunity to provide input to the European Commission’s Digital 
Omnibus proposal. As a European leader in Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) and 
AI‑enabled medical devices (MDAI), serving large hospital networks across the Union, Dedalus 
strongly supports the objective of creating a predictable, innovation‑friendly, and clinically 
safe digital regulatory environment. 
The Commission’s 2024–2029 mandate, shaped by the competitiveness imperatives 
highlighted in the Draghi Report, correctly identifies the EU’s “competitiveness deficit” in 
digital technologies as a strategic risk. In the healthcare sector, where digital tools are integral 
to patient safety and clinical workflows, regulatory simplification is not merely desirable but

essential. Rigid administrative burdens slow down innovation cycles, increase compliance 
costs for European manufacturers, and jeopardise the timely deployment of life‑saving digital 
systems. 
The Digital Omnibus proposal, as a legislative corrective introduced in November 2025, 
reflects a decisive shift toward innovation‑friendly implementation, improving coherence 
between horizontal and sectoral legislation and supporting a digital infrastructure where 
patient safety and technological innovation reinforce each other rather than collide. 
Conclusions and Final Recommendations  
The Digital Omnibus demonstrates strong alignment with Dedalus’s core objectives: sectoral 
coherence, pragmatic timelines, streamlined reporting, and strengthened IP protections. 
Remaining gaps should be addressed to ensure full digital fitness and eliminate residual 
regulatory friction. 
Dedalus Recommendations to the European Commission 
1. Complete harmonisation of definitions between AI Act and MDR/IVDR to remove 
classification grey zones. 
2. Standardise simplified documentation templates for SMCs across the Union. 
3. Accelerate designation of notified bodies under the integrated MDR/AI Act 
supervision system. 
4. Clarify the interface between ENISA’s Single-Entry Point and national health-
sector reporting, ensuring frictionless adoption for hospitals. 
5. Expand EU-wide criteria on trade secret exposure risk, ensuring uniform 
application of the Anti-Leakage Toolbox. 
6. Monitor national EHDS implementation, preventing fragmentation in secondary use 
and data access infrastructures.

2.1 Streamlining and harmonizing the evolving European 
Data legislation: from the GDPR to the AI ACT 
The EU Data legislation constitutes a regulatory mosaic of uncoherent, n on-harmonized and 
overlapping provisions, whose redundancy risks compromising their correct legal 
interpretation and practical implementation , thus exposing  the Organizations, in particular 
multinational companies, not only to disproportionate economic and administrative burdens, 
but also to potential regulatory infringement (and therefore to subsequent sanctions). 
Moreover, the stratification of European regulations is such that companies find themselves 
trapped in this legislative complexity, which negatively impacts their competitiveness, whereas 
the direction to pursue should be the creation and strengthening of a unified social market 
economy and EU tech sovereignty.  Hence, the Organizations welcome the EU strategy for 
harmonization and simplification of the existing Data landscape, stressing the important to 
enhance regulatory clarity to support innovation so to regard the EU Digital Omnibus as a “wind 
of opportunity” rather than a “missed opportunity”. To this end, Dedalus advocates the 
proposals detailed below. 
2.2 The implications of the new definition of personal data 
under art. 4 (1) GDPR  
• Draft of  detailed, clear and unambiguous delegated acts clarifying the 
implications of the new definition of personal data under Art. 4 (1) GDPR :  this 
measure is urged since the mentioned new definition of personal data reshape current 
data protection roles of the Entities processing personal data, with a significant 
repercussions on the contractual obligations listed under Articles 28, 32 and 33 GDPR.  
In particular, the delegated acts should clarify whether, based on the new definition: 
1. are the Parties still required to  continue regulating their contractual relationships 
through the execution of a Data Processing Agreement? 
In case a Data Processing Agreement is no longer required: 
2. is the Data controller obliged to mention, among the data recipients in the information 
notice, also the third-parties processing non-personal data?

4. should a security incident involving non-personal data still be notified by the third

• Draft of EDPB guidelines aimed at defining the GDPR obligations as revised in light

2.3 The simplification of international data transfer rules by 
avoiding compliance with redundant requirements  
A topic still unaddressed but crucial for Dedalus and , more generally speaking, for  
multinational corporate groups concerns the simplification of legal mechanisms for 
international data tra nsfers. This is indeed a stimulating and challenging topic, in 
particular from an operational standpoint, partly due to the monopoly held by some 
stakeholders on the economic market.  
 
• In the current GDPR framework, intra‑group data transfers are subject to the same legal 
regime as transfers to external third parties, even though their risk profile is 
substantially different.  
This approach does not reflect operational reality and creates an unnecessary 
administrative burden for multinational groups that already operate under  a common 
data protection corporate governance structure, uniform technical and organizational 
security measures, shared cybersecurity oversigh t. Notably, the legislator already 
considers the belonging to a corporate group when assessing the amount of 
administrative fines (Art. 83 GDPR). However, the positive compliance aspects deriving 
from the belonging to a same group of companies — such as identical policies, shared 
infrastructure, harmonized security standards and coordinated accountability — are not 
recognized nor valorized for data transfer purposes. As a consequence, intra‑group data 
flows remain over ‑regulated through mechanisms designed for transfers to unrelated

data transfers.  
To this end, it appears opportune to bring to the attention the approaches adopted by 
non-EU countries on this matter.  
• The Information Commissioner Office (UK) launched a public consultation on "Guide on 
international data transfers” and its “Tool on Transfer Risk Assessment (‘TRA tool’) ”. 
Following the results of the mentioned public consultation, stakeholders have 
suggested, among the other, a  streamlining of the assessment procedure through the

• Lastly, a current gap in the EU data protection framework is the absence of pre-defined 
(i.e., by law) supplementary technical and organizational security measures regarding 
data transfer to be adopted in case the data importer is established in a not adequate 
country, thus guaranteeing a secure data transfer.

2.4 Cybersecurity incident reporting 
Dedalus supports the Union's objective of strengthening cybersecurity resilience but submits 
that the current reporting obligations under the NIS2 Directive and the Cyber Resilience Act 
(CRA) require substantial clarification, harmonisation, and greater operational feasibility 
before they can be effectively implemented. Both frameworks impose strict initial notification 
deadlines — 24 hours for early warning and 72 hours for incident notification — yet neither 
instrument provides sufficiently precise definitions of what constitutes a reportable 
"significant incident", a "severe cybersecurity incident", or an "actively exploited vulnerability". 
This definitional gap generates legal uncertainty, encourages divergent interpretations across 
national competent authorities, and imposes administrative burden without commensurate 
improvement in security outcomes. 
Dedalus therefore urges the Digital Omnibus initiative to introduce EU-wide, operationally 
actionable definitions for the key concepts underpinning both frameworks, including "severe 
cybersecurity incident", "significant impact", "actively exploited vulnerability", and 
"exploitation in the wild" as used in the CRA. These definitions should be accompanied by a 
common severity classification schema applicable across NIS2 and the CRA, so as to eliminate 
ambiguity and prevent inconsistent national transpositions. This is particularly pressing given 
that the CRA introduces the novel reporting category of "actively exploited vulnerabilities" 
without establishing thresholds capable of distinguishing minor exploit attempts from 
confirmed exploitation, product-level reportable events from matters relevant solely to 
internal monitoring, or situations triggering concurrent obligations under both frameworks. 
Definition of mandatory content for initial and full reporting 
To ensure consistent, predictable, and operationally feasible reporting obligations under both 
NIS2 and the CRA, Dedalus recommends that the Digital Omnibus introduce a harmonised 
EU‑level definition of the mandatory content required for (i) the initial notification and (ii) the 
full incident report. Clear delineation between these two phases is essential to avoid 
premature reporting, reduce administrative burden, and promote high‑quality, actionable 
information. 
Initial notification (early signal): 
The initial notification — submitted within the prescribed early warning timeline — should be 
limited to essential, high-level indicators necessary for situational awareness. At minimum, 
mandatory fields should include: 
• a short description of the incident or vulnerability and its classification; 
• preliminary indicators of compromise or exploitation; 
• the affected product(s), service(s), or infrastructure components;

• an initial assessment of potential impact on continuity, data integrity, or security; 
• confirmation of whether exploitation is suspected or reasonably evidenced; 
• contact information for the reporting entity’s operational lead. 
This narrowly scoped dataset ensures rapid EU-level visibility while preventing speculation, 
incomplete data, or unnecessary noise. 
Full incident report (validated analysis): 
The full report — submitted once sufficient evidence has been gathered — should require a 
well-defined set of validated information, including: 
• a confirmed technical description of the root cause and exploitation mechanism; 
• validated impact assessment, including affected assets, data, and users; 
• severity rating aligned with a harmonised EU-wide taxonomy; 
• mitigation and containment measures applied; 
• remediation actions planned or implemented; 
• cross-framework relevance (e.g., whether the same incident triggers CRA or GDPR 
obligations); 
• lessons learned and long-term prevention measures. 
Establishing these two standardised content sets will significantly reduce interpretative 
divergence across Member States, ensure proportionality between reporting effort and 
available evidence, and improve overall coherence and usefulness of cybersecurity reporting 
at EU level. 
 
If the 24‑hour deadline for submitting the pre-notification to the Authority is to be 
maintained, it would be advisable to more precisely define what constitutes “becoming aware” 
of a significant incident. This clarification is essential to ensure that the reporting entity has 
the necessary evidence to know with certainty that the incident qualifies as “significant” and 
therefore triggers the notification obligation. Such a definition could be introduced through 
European guidelines, to promote uniformity in the behaviours and procedures followed by all 
obligated entities. In this regard, the ACN NIS Guidelines (published in December 2025) 
provide important operational direction: they explicitly distinguish between handling an 
“event” and having sufficient evidence that a “significant incident” has occurred, the latter 
being the moment when notification obligations are activated. The guidelines emphasise that 
organisations must be able to determine, based on concrete indicators, when an incident 
meets the thresholds defined for “significant incidents of base type" and therefore triggers 
the obligation to notify within 24 hours. Drawing on these guidelines, ACN clarifies that the 
24‑hour countdown begins when the organisation obtains evidence that reasonably confirms 
the incident's significance, not merely when a generic anomaly is detected. This interpretation

supports the principle of effective and timely incident management as required by the NIS2 
framework and reduces the risk of premature or unnecessary notifications. Providing a 
clearer statutory or guideline‑based definition at national or EU level—possibly aligned with 
ENISA’s operational guidance supporting the implementation of NIS2—would help ensure 
consistent practices, reduce interpretative uncertainty, and enhance the overall effectiveness 
of cybersecurity incident response across all obligated entities. 
With respect to reporting channels and processes, Dedalus welcomes the Commission's 
stated intent to streamline notification procedures and emphasises that manufacturers 
subject to both NIS2 and the CRA will, in practice, be submitting reports through the same EU-
level platform. It is therefore imperative that reporting criteria, data fields, and severity 
classifications be fully aligned to prevent duplicative or contradictory submissions. Dedalus 
recommends the introduction of a single unified reporting template for all cybersecurity 
notifications under both frameworks, supported by automated cross-population of 
information across related reporting streams and accompanied by clear guidance on the 
conditions under which a single submission satisfies obligations under both instruments. 
Regarding notification timelines, Dedalus acknowledges the policy rationale underlying the 
24-hour and 72-hour model, but considers it operationally unworkable in complex software 
environments, and in particular in the healthcare sector. Early indicators of a potential 
incident are frequently incomplete or inconclusive; premature reporting risks conveying 
inaccurate or misleading information to authorities; and coordinated vulnerability analysis 
routinely requires more time than current deadlines allow. Dedalus accordingly proposes 
extending the early warning deadline from 24 to 72 hours, and the incident notification 
deadline from 72 to at least 120 hours, and recommends replacing the current model with a 
structured, phased reporting approach. Under this model, an initial early signal would be 
submitted within 72 hours, containing limited metadata and indicators of compromise 
alongside a preliminary impact assessment; a substantive update would follow within five to 
seven days, incorporating validated impact data, affected assets, and exploitation status; and 
a final comprehensive report, including full analysis and remediation measures, would be 
submitted within 30 days. This approach is better aligned with real-world security 
investigation workflows and enables authorities to maintain continuous situational awareness 
without compelling organisations to draw premature conclusions. 
Risks of overreporting under excessively short timelines and the need for a structured 
recall mechanism 
Dedalus emphasises that the current 24-hour early-warning obligation risks generating 
substantial “noise” within the EU reporting ecosystem. In practice, the compressed timeline 
forces organisations - especially those operating complex, distributed software environments 
- to submit notifications before sufficient evidence is available to validate whether an event

truly constitutes a reportable incident or an actively exploited vulnerability. This dynamic 
substantially increases the likelihood of false positives and low-quality submissions, diluting 
the operational value of EU-level situational awareness and creating unnecessary 
administrative burden for both companies and authorities. 
To address these risks, the Digital Omnibus should introduce: 
• A formalised recall or withdrawal mechanism that allows organisations to correct, 
update, or retract an initial notification once further investigation confirms that the 
event does not meet the reporting thresholds. Allowing such recall within a defined 
window — for example, until the 72-hour mark — would ensure accuracy while 
maintaining timely alerts. 
• Guidance clarifying that early signals are provisional and may evolve into 
confirmed incidents, downgraded events, or non-incidents without penalising the 
reporting entity. 
• Protections against negative compliance implications for organisations that, acting 
in good faith under the 24-hour model, must adjust or retract a preliminary report. 
Establishing a structured recall framework, together with more proportionate timelines, will 
reduce unintended overreporting, enhance data quality, and ensure that EU-level platforms 
receive information that is both timely and reliable—ultimately strengthening the 
effectiveness of Europe’s cyber-incident reporting architecture. 
 
As regards the CRA's 24-hour obligation to report actively exploited vulnerabilities, Dedalus 
submits that this requirement is insufficiently defined to be operable. Manufacturers 
currently lack authoritative guidance on the evidentiary threshold for "active exploitation", on 
whether exploitation must be widespread, targeted, or demonstrated within their own 
product estate, and on how to proceed with vulnerabilities subject to coordinated 
vulnerability disclosure that have not yet been weaponised. Dedalus recommends 
harmonising CRA vulnerability reporting with ENISA's established threat taxonomy and 
coordinated vulnerability disclosure processes, introducing a risk-based reporting threshold 
informed by criteria such as CVSS score, exploitation likelihood, and affected product 
category, and extending the standard reporting window for actively exploited vulnerabilities 
to five days, reserving the shorter deadline for incidents posing an imminent systemic risk. 
Incorporating ACN’s operational guidance to support harmonised and practicable 
implementation 
To strengthen the practical operability of reporting obligations under NIS2 and the CRA, 
Dedalus encourages the Commission to draw explicitly on the approaches developed by the 
Agenzia per la Cybersicurezza Nazionale (ACN) in its 2025 NIS guidance. ACN provides a

clear, evidence-based methodology for distinguishing between generic security events and 
reportable “significant incidents”, thereby offering a pragmatic interpretation of when 
notification obligations are effectively triggered. Building on this foundation, Dedalus 
recommends: 
• Adopting ACN’s event–incident differentiation model at EU level, ensuring that the 
reporting clock starts only once organisations have obtained sufficiently corroborated 
evidence that an incident meets the defined significance thresholds. 
• Integrating ACN’s severity-based assessment criteria into a unified EU severity 
classification scheme applicable across NIS2 and the CRA, thereby supporting 
consistent evaluation of impact, criticality, and urgency. 
• Leveraging ACN’s staged investigative logic to align EU reporting requirements with 
real-world forensic and incident-response workflows, reducing premature submissions 
and improving the quality of notifications. 
• Ensuring that EU-level guidance reflects ACN’s emphasis on operational

require structured evidence collection before accurate impact determination is

Embedding these ACN-inspired principles into the Digital Omnibus implementation 
framework would promote coherent interpretation across Member States, reduce 
administrative burden, and significantly improve the reliability and usefulness of cybersecurity 
reporting across the Union. 
Taken together, these adaptations would reduce unnecessary administrative burden, improve 
the accuracy and quality of reported information, eliminate duplication across overlapping 
legal frameworks, and ultimately strengthen EU-wide cybersecurity situational awareness by 
ensuring that the shared reporting platform receives consistent, reliable, and actionable data. 
Dedalus strongly supports the Union’s objective of strengthening cybersecurity resilience but 
reiterates that deadlines of 24 and 72 hours are operationally unworkable in complex 
healthcare environments. Early indicators of potential incidents are often inconclusive; 
premature notification risks inaccuracies and unnecessary noise.

2.5 Simplification & harmonization of data regulations 
Dedalus strongly welcomes the Commission's commitment to streamlining Europe's digital 
regulatory environment. Nevertheless, the concurrent application of the Data Act, the GDPR, 
and the emerging data regulatory framework continues to generate significant legal 
uncertainty for healthcare software manufacturers, providers, and data processors. We 
therefore urge the Commission to provide concrete guidance on scope boundaries, the 
hierarchy of obligations, and harmonised implementation rules across these instruments. 
Clarifying the Scope of the Data Act in Relation to the GDPR 
A primary source of uncertainty for industry concerns the delineation between data governed 
by the Data Act and data remaining exclusively subject to the GDPR. We request that the 
Digital Omnibus Package explicitly confirm that the Data Act applies predominantly to non-
personal and industrial machine-generated data, while personal data continues to be 
regulated by the GDPR as the primary framework and provide clear guidance on how to 
coordinate obligations arising by both regulations. Clear rules are equally needed for mixed 
datasets — particularly in clinical environments where device telemetry, technical logs, and 
metadata may indirectly identify individuals. The Commission should further specify the 
conditions under which medical device-generated data falls within the Data Act's scope, and 
establish unambiguous definitions of "data holder" and "data user" for the healthcare sector 
and practical guidance in all cases where Data act, GDPR and sectorial regulations will apply. 
Inconsistent national interpretations of these concepts create operational risk and risk 
discouraging data-intensive innovation in clinical settings. 
Harmonising Secondary Use Processes Across Member States 
To prevent regulatory fragmentation, Dedalus encourages the Commission to establish a 
single European template for secondary use data access requests, standardise the risk 
assessment criteria applied by HDABs, and introduce a uniform EU-wide classification system 
for health data categories. Such measures would facilitate cross-border research and AI 
development while preserving patient trust and maintaining high data protection standards. 
The clarifications outlined above would substantially increase legal certainty for healthcare 
providers and manufacturers, reduce unnecessary administrative burden, and enable 
organisations to direct resources towards innovation rather than regulatory interpretation — 
ultimately supporting a consistent and trustworthy implementation of the EHDS across the 
Union.

Clarifying the interaction between EU-level rules and national sector-specific legislation 
(e.g., Germany’s GDNG) 
To ensure consistent and coherent application of the Digital Omnibus Package across the 
Union, Dedalus recommends explicitly addressing the interplay between EU-level data 
regulations and national sector-specific frameworks such as the German 
Gesundheitsdatennutzungsgesetz (GDNG). National laws governing digital health, 
secondary use of health data, or data-access governance can introduce additional procedural 
requirements, diverging definitions, or differing authorisation pathways that risk undermining 
the intended harmonisation of the Digital Omnibus. 
In particular, Dedalus encourages the Commission to: 
• Clarify the hierarchy of obligations where national health-data legislation (e.g., 
GDNG in Germany) imposes additional or more specific requirements for data access, 
data sharing, or secondary use that may differ from those foreseen under the Data 
Act, GDPR, or the EHDS framework. 
• Ensure alignment of definitions and procedural steps, such as the criteria for data 
access requests, technical safeguards for data provisioning, or the scope of data 
categories covered under sector-specific laws to prevent conflicting implementation 
across Member States. 
• Promote interoperable governance mechanisms by encouraging Member States to 
harmonise national access procedures and risk assessment criteria with the unified 
EU-level templates and standards proposed under the Digital Omnibus and EHDS. 
• Avoid duplicative administrative burdens by ensuring that national digital-health 
regulations do not introduce additional documentation, notification, or authorisation 
layers beyond the harmonised EU system—particularly for cross-border service 
provision and multinational healthcare software manufacturers. 
Explicitly integrating the role of national digital-health legislation—such as the GDNG—into 
the Digital Omnibus framework will strengthen legal clarity, support uniform implementation 
across the Union, and prevent the emergence of parallel national regimes that could 
reintroduce fragmentation into the European digital regulatory landscape. 
Dedalus welcomes the consolidation of the Data Act, the Data Governance Act, and the Open 
Data Directive into a unified framework that supports EHDS-scale interoperability. However, 
greater protection is needed for proprietary clinical algorithms, model architectures, and 
training datasets.

2.6 Targeted adjustments to the Artificial Intelligence Act 
2.6.1 Intersection between AI Act and GDPR 
A) Legitimate interest as legal basis for development and operation of AI 
systems/models 
To strengthen the proposed regulatory framework under the Digital Omnibus, it is essential to provide 
greater clarity and harmonisation regarding the use of legitimate interest as a legal basis for the 
development and operation of AI systems and models. While the current proposal introduces legitimate 
interest for personal data used to train and operate AI systems, the persistence of national specificities - 
particularly in Member States that still require consent for such processing  - risks undermining lega l 
certainty and creating a fragmented compliance landscape. In practice, if national regimes impose 
consent requirements that override legitimate interest, organisations operating across multiple 
jurisdictions will face inconsistent obligations, making the  legal basis unpredictable and ultimately 
discouraging innovation. A more uniform and directly applicable approach at EU level is therefore needed 
to ensure that the legitimate interest basis can function effectively, without being superseded by diverging 
national rules, while still ensuring strong safeguards for data subjects. 
 
B) Proposal of art. 9 (2) K GDPR 
Dedalus welcomes the introduction of the new legal basis under Article 9(2)(k), which permits the 
processing of special categories of personal data for the development and functioning of AI systems and 
models, subject to the conditions and safeguards set o ut in paragraph 5. This proposal represents a 
much‑needed and pragmatic evolution of the GDPR, acknowledging that limited exposure to sensitive 
data may be technically unavoidable during training, testing, or validation phases. At the same time, the 
strict minimisation requirements and the very narrow scope of this exemption demand a clear, 
harmonised, and operational interpretation across the EU. To avoid divergent national approaches and 
ensure consistent implementation, Dedalus considers it essential that the EDPB, the EDPS and the AI Office 
jointly develop detailed, practical, and technology‑aware guidance. This should include clarifications on 
what constitutes “appropriate technical and organisational measures” under paragraph 5 - for example, 
the expected standards for dataset curation, automated detection and filtering of special category data, 
documentation duties, model‑level protections, and safeguards required when removal of sensitive data 
would involve disproportionate effort. Such coordinated gu idance is critical to support organisations in 
meeting minimisation obligations, ensuring that inadvertently included sensitive data is either removed 
or - where removal is technically burdensome - rendered unusable for generating outputs or being 
disclosed, in full alignment with the legislative intent.

C) Strengthening Cross‑Authority Cooperation for Consistent and Predictable AI Governance 
In light of the increasing complexity and cross‑cutting nature of AI governance, Dedalus encourages a 
stronger and more structured cooperation framework between the AI Office, the EDPB, and the EDPS, 
complemented by the systematic involvement of both publi c and private stakeholders. As recent 
developments show, the AI Office is positioned to play a pivotal coordination role at EU level, but the 
effectiveness of this role depends on consistent alignment with data‑protection authorities and on 
maintaining open channels for stakeholder feedback throughout the interpretative process. At the same 
time, the EDPB has already underlined the importance of harmonised analysis and consultation with the 
AI Office and external stakeholders when addressing the data‑protec tion implications of AI models. 
Building on these foundations, Dedalus stresses that clear, unified, and operational guidance remains 
essential to ensuring legal certainty - particularly for organisations developing AI systems and models, 
which depend on p redictable and consistent interpretations across Member States. Strengthened 
cooperation among these bodies is therefore crucial to deliver coherent expectations, reduce 
fragmentation, and support responsible and innovation‑friendly AI development across t he European 
Union. 
 
D) Explicitly recognize the use of the legal basis of legitimate interest for the sole purpose of 
anonymising special categories of personal data.  
This would establish a dedicated legal ground for processing sensitive data exclusively to enable 
anonymisation, particularly in contexts such as algorithm training. Such a provision would strike a balance 
between fostering innovation and ensuring robust protection of data subjects’ rights, by clearly delimiting 
the scope and safeguards applicable to this specific processing purpose.

The interaction between the AI Act and the MDR/IVDR framework is one of the most critical 
alignment questions for medical software and AI-enabled medical devices. As a manufacturer 
operating across Europe’s most demanding clinical environments, Dedalus strongly supports 
the Digital Omnibus initiative to structurally embed AI requirements into the MDR/IVDR 
architecture. This approach avoids duplicated audits, contradictory obligations, and 
operational inefficiencies while preserving the highest standard of patient safety.

coherence — and where additional clarifications remain essential.

By relocating medical devices and IVDs into Section B of Annex I, the Digital Omnibus correctly 
acknowledges that AI systems embedded in clinical devices operate within a pre-existing, 
risk-based regulatory framework. This integration ensures that: 
• AI-related obligations are not applied twice through separate legislative routes. 
• Medical devices are not subject to parallel AI Act conformity assessments that overlap

• Manufacturers follow a single, coherent regulatory workflow for both the device and its

This shift is not administrative — it directly prevents regulatory divergence that would delay 
the availability of AI-enabled clinical solutions. 
 
2. Removal of Redundant Administrative Burdens 
2.1. Elimination of Duplicate Registration Obligations 
The deletion of Annex VIII Section B reporting obligations is decisive. Without this correction, 
manufacturers of medical devices would have been required to: 
• re-register MDR/IVDR-certified products under the AI Act, 
• supply duplicate documentation to parallel authorities, 
• maintain synchronisation across inconsistent data registries. 
Such fragmentation would have caused audit delays and increased the risk of inconsistent 
regulatory records across the Union. Consolidation into MDR/IVDR removes that risk. 
 
3. Notified Body Oversight and Conformity Assessment 
3.1. Single-Notified-Body Model 
Healthcare providers rely on seamless, predictable conformity oversight. Dedalus therefore 
welcomes the reinforcement of a Single-Notified-Body model: 
• One notified body reviews the technical documentation under MDR/IVDR. 
• The same body reviews the applicable AI Act obligations (post-Omnibus). 
• No second AI-Act-only assessment is required. 
This ensures consistency, accelerates conformity assessment timelines, and avoids the risk of 
conflicting interpretations between authorities. 
3.2. Avoidance of Dual Conformity Assessments 
Without Omnibus integration, manufacturers would need:

4. Technical Documentation Simplification and its Ecosystem Impact 
4.1. Simplified Documentation (Article 1(8)) 
The extension of simplified documentation requirements to Small Mid-Caps (SMCs) is 
strategically important for Dedalus. Many of our partners — algorithm developers, clinical 
decision support suppliers, module integrators — fall within the SMC definition. 
This provision: 
• reduces barriers to entry for innovative clinical AI SMEs/SMCs, 
• strengthens the EU innovation chain, 
• supports ecosystem-wide compliance without imposing disproportionate burdens. 
5. Post-Market Surveillance and Monitoring Activities 
5.1. Removal of Prescriptive Monitoring Templates 
Article 1(24) replaces the rigid Art. 72(3) plans with flexible guidance. This enables 
manufacturers to: 
• tailor post-market monitoring to actual clinical risk, 
• integrate monitoring into existing PMS systems under MDR/IVDR, 
• avoid unnecessary administrative reporting that does not correlate with safety 
outcomes. 
This is a major improvement, aligning regulatory expectations with the realities of large-scale 
hospital deployments. 
5.2. Integration With MDR/IVDR PMS Systems 
AI-specific surveillance (drift detection, model robustness, data drift signals) can be naturally 
integrated into: 
• PMS planning, 
• trend analysis, 
• PSUR cycles, 
• CAPA investigations. 
The Digital Omnibus avoids creating a second, parallel monitoring regime, which is essential 
for clinical safety and efficiency.

6.1. SME/SMC Fine Caps (Article 1(29)) 
Disproportionate penalties risk deterring smaller innovators whose algorithms may be 
integrated into Dedalus platforms. Extending proportionate fines to SMCs ensures: 
• financial sustainability across the ecosystem, 
• availability of niche clinical innovation, 
• predictable investment conditions for mid-sized health tech companies. 
7. Pre-Market Clinical Testing Pathways 
7.1. Clarifying the Status of AI in Investigational Devices 
The legal ambiguity surrounding investigational AI components arising from the original AI Act 
would have hindered clinical trials. Dedalus supports clear statutory confirmation that: 
• AI components within investigational MDR/IVDR devices are not considered placed 
on the market, 
• the MDR framework for clinical investigations governs their deployment, 
• AI validation can proceed without triggering premature AI Act conformity. 
This is essential to maintain Europe's capacity to generate clinical evidence for AI systems. 
8. Implementation Timelines Anchored to System Readiness 
8.1. Stability Through the Prerequisite-Based Model 
AI deployment in hospitals cannot depend on assumptions about the readiness of 
harmonised standards or notified body capacity. 
The Omnibus correctly anchors timelines to: 
• availability of harmonised standards, 
• release of guidance and templates, 
• readiness of conformity assessment infrastructure. 
8.2. Clear Timeline Anchors for Industry 
• December 2027 – horizontal Annex III rules for high-risk AI. 
• August 2, 2028 – MDR/IVDR AI components (Annex I products). 
• 16-month adjustment mechanism – additional flexibility if tools are delayed. 
These anchors are essential for predictable R&D planning.

The Digital Omnibus delivers structural improvements that the medical device sector has 
requested for years: 
• MDR/IVDR remains the single regulatory home for medical devices with AI.

2.7 GOVERNANCE CLARIFICATION: AI OFFICE AND SECTORAL 
AUTHORITIES 
The Digital Omnibus introduces a more coherent governance structure by: 
• positioning the AI Office as the supervisor of model-level obligations; 
• maintaining sectoral medical device authorities as the regulators of clinical 
application. 
Dedalus welcomes this division of responsibilities, which avoids overlaps that could otherwise 
impede hospital operations or introduce conflicting requirements.

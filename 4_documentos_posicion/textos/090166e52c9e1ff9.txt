1. Introduction 
 
The EU is at a critical turning point. While the United States and China surge ahead in artificial intelligence 
(AI) and digital innovation, Europe's share of cumulative global market capitalisation of the technology, 
media, and telecom sector has plunged from 30% in 2000 to 7% today. 1 Not a single one of the world’s 
ten largest companies is European. The EU has not produced a new company with a market capitalisation 
exceeding €100 billion in fifty years. In the same period, the United States has created six companies 
worth over €1 trillion.2 
The warning lights are flashing. More than 60 of the EU's largest companies, representing millions of 
workers and families, have publicly called on the EU to radically simplify its digital regulation, explicitly 
naming the AI Act (AIA) and General Data Pro tection Regulation (GDPR). Twenty Member States have 
demanded a systematic review of EU rules to identify what is superfluous, excessive or unbalanced, and 
to examine whether the digital acquis is fit for the Union’s competitiveness goals. The message from both 
industry and governments is unequivocal: EUs current digital rulebook is undermining its ability to 
compete. 
This message is not new. Since 2024, the EU Institutions have been warned of the consequences of not 
changing course. Former European Central Bank (ECB) President Mario Draghi’s Report made it 
abundantly clear. The EU's proliferation of digital regulators coupled with the accumulation of EU -level

In this context, the European Commission’s (EC) Digital Omnibus proposals of 19 November and the 
broader simplification agenda fall far short. Instead of the fundamental recalibration that is urgently 
needed, the Omnibus proposals offer only modest technic al fixes and incremental clarifications. Tweaks 
to the AIA, GDPR or ePrivacy Directive (ePD) cannot compensate for a structurally flawed approach to 
legislation and anti -innovation mindset that piles complexity on top of complexity, while other regions 
create conditions for scale, investment and innovation.  
What is required now is a paradigm shift. The EU Institutions must move the objective beyond the slight 
easing of regulatory compliance burden. The EU needs a thorough revision of the entire digital acquis to 
identify which rules still serve their purpose and which actively obstruct innovation and growth. This must 
be accompanied by a pause on the pipeline of new digital laws – including the AIA, Digital Networks Act 
(DNA), Digital Fairness Act (DFA) and any other planned initiatives.  
Central to this shift is the need for an explicit innovation mandate for regulators. Authorities responsible 
for AI, privacy and the single EU digital market ambition must be required – and held accountable – to 
protect fundamental rights (all of them) in a balanced manner, a balance enabling innovation, investment 
and economic opportunity. Without such a mandate, enforcement will continue to skew towards the 
punitive, discriminatory, and preemptive, accelerating EU’s technological and macroeco nomic 
marginalisation. The issue is not innovation vs fundamental rights. However, when only one fundamental 
right is considered in isolation — such as focusing solely on privacy — and the risk -based approach is 
absent, innovation becomes disconnected from  real-world needs. Absolutist approaches have led to 
conflicting regulatory actions that have stifled EU progress. 
The Digital Omnibus risks becoming a historic missed opportunity if changes addressing industry calls on 
the AI Act and structural failures in the data protection environment are indefinitely deferred. Introducing 
harmonised compliance templates across the EU is a welcome improvement but it is a procedural fix to a 
systemic problem, it does not address a deeply ingrained risk -averse regulatory approach. Real 
simplification needs to be about creating a regulatory framework that is fit for the future. That in cludes 
deconflicting laws, not simply easing compliance with overlapping rules. While the forthcoming Digital 
Fitness Check (DFC) must deliver a reassessment of not only the AI and privacy frameworks but the entire 
digital legal framework, that is not an a rgument for inaction today. The EU is already late. Every year of 
delay in resolving the foundational ambiguities of the data protection framework is another year in which 
investment flows elsewhere, AI features launch outside the EU first, and EU citizens  are denied the 
benefits of innovation that their counterparts elsewhere take for granted. The Digital Omnibus must 
deliver the best it can on its core promise now. 
2. Summary of recommendations  
2.1 EU AI Act  
● Stop the clock on the AI Act 
● Pause implementation of the full framework – including GPAIM rules – not only the 
high‑risk regime.  
● Align the grace period for the high‑risk obligations to a single application date and extend 
it to also include a grace period for the GPAIM and transparency obligations, to at least 
August 2028 to allow for the comprehensive competitiveness-driven review which should 
have been finalized when the Act was first proposed, to now be conducted. 
● Embed an explicit innovation mandate 
● Introduce a clear legal mandate and accountability framework for EU and national AI 
regulators to balance fundamental rights with innovation, competitiveness and 
investment. 
● Defend the risk‑based approach against inconsistent asymmetric obligations 
● Reject extensions of reduced penalties and privileges to selected categories of providers 
based on size (e.g. SMCs) when risk is comparable. 
● Ensure penalties and obligations are symmetric for equivalent risk levels, by revising 
Article 99(1) to prevent unjustified divergences between Member States and operators. 
● Strengthen centralised oversight by the AI Office 
● The EU AI Office should be designated as the sole authority responsible for supervising 
and enforcing compliance for all AI systems developed by GPAI model providers. 
Concentrating oversight within a single body would promote consistent and predictable 
application of the AI Act across the Union. 
● This centralised supervisory model should apply regardless of an AI system’s classification 
under other EU legal frameworks. In practical terms, the Omnibus should make clear that 
supervision of these AI Act obligations is distinct from, and not contingent  on, the DSA 
framework, in order to prevent overlapping mandates and divergent enforcement 
outcomes. 
2.2 GDPR & ePrivacy 
● Clarify the definition of personal data 
● Retain the Omnibus link between “personal data” and what a controller can reasonably 
do with available means. 
● Clarify that identifiability must be assessed from the controller’s perspective, taking into 
account costs, technology, time and safeguards, in line with CJEU case law and Recital 26 
GDPR. 
● Explicitly position the promotion of innovation as a core GDPR objective and introduce a 
primary mandate for Data Protection Authorities: 
● Innovation and proportionality: Explicitly recognise innovation and economic growth as 
EU objectives, and ensure balanced, risk -based, and practical interpretation and 
enforcement—especially for fast‑evolving technologies. 
● Innovation mandate for DPAs:  Require DPAs to balance fundamental rights with 
innovation and competitiveness, with annual KPI -based reporting and stronger EU -wide 
consistency (including clearer central coordination under appropriate EC/CJEU oversight). 
● Keep the Omnibus clarification that automated decisions are permissible when Article 22 
conditions are satisfied 
● Specify that Article 22 applies only to automated decisions determining legal status or 
having comparably significant effects, avoiding its misuse as a blanket restriction for any 
automated decision. 
● Harmonise AI and data protection (Article 88c) 
● Retain Article 88c, but streamline it by removing references to consent and eliminating 
additional caveats that would render the legitimate interest basis ineffective. The GDPR 
already sets out robust security obligations, and reliance on legitimate interests requires 
a documented assessment of risks and mitigating measures to support the applicable 
balancing test. 
● Reform joint controllership 
● Introduce a targeted amendment to Article 26 to clarify that entities should be regarded 
as joint controllers only where they actively participate in — and materially influence —

3. Deep Dive: AI Act 
The proposed Omnibus on the AIA is a missed opportunity. At a moment when the EU needs decisive, 
pro‑competitive reform, it offers only marginal adjustments and leaves the structural problems of the 
framework intact. Yes, there are some positive elements: a broader legal basis for sensitive data use, a 
move toward more centralised oversight, and a transitional period for certain high ‑risk obligations. But 
these are peripheral fixes. They do not resolve the core dysfunctions that AI innovators  already signalling: 
regulatory overreach, leading to persistent legal uncertainty, and overlapping, often  unclear obligations, 
with  a patchwork of staggered timelines that makes planning and investment increasingly risky. 
 
The handling of General Purpose AI Models (GPAIM) is a case in point. By blurring the distinction between 
model development, downstream use — and by failing to introduce a grace period that would pause the 
applicability of the GPAIM rules —the Omnibus clearly misses its mark. At the same time, regulators at EU 
and national level still lack an explicit mandate to support innovation and competitiveness. In practice, 
this means enforcement will skew towards preemptive, punitive, inconsistent, regardless of 
macroeconomic consequences. 
 
The EU  does not need more cosmetic tweaks. It needs a political decision to instill an innovation and 
economic growth mindset, pause the implementation of the AIA as a whole, confront these design flaws 
openly, and rework the framework so that it provides clear, predictable rules that enable and promote – 
rather than suffocate – innovation, investment and growth. 
3.1 Broadening the Grace Periods to Safeguard Innovation and Legal Certainty 
The current response to widespread calls from industry and Member States for a genuine “stop the clock” 
on the AIA is inadequate and counterproductive. Instead of providing a clear and predictable pause, the 
Omnibus introduces a patchwork of separate, narr owly framed and condition ‑based grace periods 
covering parts of the high‑risk regime and a limited subset of transparency obligations. Far from reducing 
complexity, this approach entrenches it. 
Postponing some high‑risk obligations and a small portion of Article 50 transparency rules is an implicit 
recognition that the original timelines and requirements are unrealistic. Yet the broader compliance 
burden and legal uncertainty remain untouched, pa rticularly for GPAIMs and the full transparency 
framework under Article 50. Moreover, the conditional nature of the high‑risk grace period – dependent 
on vague and discretionary EC “confirmations” – means businesses cannot rely on it as a genuine 
transition. The regime can effectively start at any time, depriving companies of the planning certainty a 
grace period is meant to provide. 
The current trajectory of the GPAIM regime represents a concerning departure from the original risk -
based approach of the AI Act. The GPAIM framework must be fundamentally reassessed to ensure it aligns 
with this foundational principle, rather than imposin g broad obligations that are inappropriately 
calibrated to actual risk levels. 
What is needed is a single, fixed, and credible new timeline, extending at least until August 2028, that 
applies coherently to the high -risk regime, the GPAIM provisions, and the transparency obligations. 
Without such a unified and predictable schedule, businesses will continue to face moving targets, unclear 
triggers, and staggered start dates, all of which undermine legal certainty, discourage investment, and 
weaken the EU’s ability to build a competitive AI ecosystem. 
This fragmented timeline only targeting limited parts of the AIA also departs from a coherent risk ‑based 
logic. GPAIM provisions already blur the line between model development and downstream use, shifting 
focus away from regulating risk at the point of de ployment. Starting enforcement of GPAIM rules earlier 
than the fully operational high ‑risk regime would further destabilise this architecture, increasing 
compliance costs without improving safeguards. Likewise, a six‑month grace period for only part of Article 
50, limited to systems marketed before a specific date, ignores the universal technical challenges of 
labelling and watermarking, the absence of mature standards, and the limited preparedness of 
enforcement authorities. 
The final AI Omnibus text should therefore:  
● To restore legal certainty and uphold a genuine risk -based approach, it is essential that all 
compliance timelines are established simultaneously and coherently. A clear, realistic, and unified 
application date for all high-risk systems should be introduced, set no earlier than 2 August 2028. 
Crucially, the grace period must be broadened to explicitly cover the GPAIM regime. In parallel, 
all transparency obligations under Article 50 must benefit from this same unified grace period, 
with a single compliance date — also set no earlier than 2 August 2028 — for all systems in scope, 
regardless of their market entry. Only through a coherent, synchronized timeline of this kind can 
European innovators be given a fair chance to comply and compete. 
3.2 Establishing a Regulatory Innovation Mandate 
The AI Omnibus proposal advances centralisation of supervision under the AIA, but fails to address a 
fundamental gap: the absence of a clear innovation mandate for all digital regulators. While Article 1 of 
the AIA refers to promoting human ‑centric, trustworthy AI and supporting innovation, this objective is 
not operationalised in the powers, duties or accountability mechanisms of the AI Office or national market 
surveillance authorities. In practice, this leaves regulators incentivised to replicate the innovation‑averse 
dynamics already seen in data protection enforcement, where fragmented, overly cautious and 
unbalanced interpretations have chilled investment and slowed deployment.  
The final AI Omnibus text should therefore:  
● Include explicit requirements on all the digital regulators to balance fundamental rights protection 
with the promotion of innovation, competitiveness and economic impact. All regulators shall work 
in a consistent manner, united by a common goal, i.e., to address the EU needs of economic 
growth and competitiveness in a single market. This mandate must be accompanied by 
transparency and accountability: regulators should be required to report annually on how their 
activities have enabled innovation and contributed to the EU's digital competitiveness, using clear 
key performance indicators rather than aspirational language. There is already existing regulatory 
precedent for this approach, both the UK and Singapore have adopted. Without such a regulatory 
innovation mandate, the EU will continue to self-sabotage its potential AI leadership and ability to 
attract talent, capital and cutting‑edge development that will continue to go elsewhere.  
3.3 Defending the Risk‑Based Principle Against Asymmetric Application 
The AIA is built on a central promise: obligations scale with risk, not with the identity or size of the 
provider. The AI Omnibus proposal’s extension of reduced penalties and regulatory privileges from SMEs 
to SMCs in Article 99 fundamentally erodes this principle. High‑risk systems can be—and routinely are—
developed by organisations of all sizes; the potential for harm is inherent in the system and its deployment 
context, not in the company’s headcount or balance sheet. Introducing differentiated penalties and lighter 
regimes based on company size re‑introduces arbitrariness into a framework designed to be objective and 
risk‑driven. 
Such asymmetry is not only conceptually flawed, it is practically damaging. It creates enforcement blind 
spots where smaller or mid‑sized providers operating high‑risk systems face weaker incentives to comply, 
despite posing equivalent risks to individuals and society. It also distorts competition by imposing unequal 
burdens on operators offering comparable services, and invites fragmentation as Member States interpret 
and apply unfair privileges inconsistently. Over time, this undermines the credibility of  the AIA, reduces 
protection for citizens and weakens the level playing field the Union seeks to foster. 
The final AI Omnibus text should therefore:  
● Ensure that the AIA’s obligations and sanctions apply symmetrically to all entities developing or 
deploying systems of comparable risk. Article 99 should be clarified to ensure that national rules 
and penalties cannot result in asymmetric regulation or unj ustified differences in treatment 
between operators engaged in equivalent activities. Reaffirming the primacy of the risk ‑based 
principle is essential both for the integrity of the framework and for the EU's ability to claim global 
leadership in trustworthy AI. 
3.4 Reducing Fragmentation in AI Act Oversight 
The AI Omnibus represents a pivotal opportunity to strengthen and streamline EU’s digital governance. 
To ensure coherent oversight and prevent regulatory fragmentation, the Omnibus should centralise 
enforcement by granting the European AI Office exclusive supervisory authority over all AI systems 
developed by GPAI model providers. 
When enforcement is dispersed across numerous authorities and legal instruments within Member 
States, the effectiveness of the regulatory framework is significantly weakened. The principal risk is 
inconsistent interpretation and application of the AI Act a cross national authorities. This fragmentation 
creates bottlenecks, legal uncertainty, and disproportionate compliance burdens —ultimately 
discouraging innovation. If the EU is to enhance competitiveness while maintaining robust safeguards, it 
needs a unified and predictable enforcement approach. 
The final AI Omnibus text should therefore:  
● The AI Office should be designated as the sole authority responsible for supervising and enforcing 
compliance for all AI systems developed by GPAI model providers. Concentrating oversight within 
a single body would promote consistent and predictable applic ation of the AI Act across the 
Union. This centralised supervisory model should apply regardless of an AI system’s classification 
under other EU legal frameworks. In practical terms, the Omnibus should make clear that 
supervision of these AI Act obligations is distinct from, and not contingent on, the DSA framework, 
in order to prevent overlapping mandates and divergent enforcement outcomes. 
4. Deep Dive: EU Data Protection and Privacy Framework 
While the Digital Omnibus proposal rightly seeks to modernize the GDPR and ePrivacy frameworks, the 
current text misses the opportunity to firmly embed risk -based proportionality and a clear mandate for 
innovation for the data protection regulators. Clarif ying the legitimate interests’ legal basis for AI 
development and the concepts of personal data and anonymisation in line with the Court of Justice of the 
EU (CJEU) case law are positive steps, but they remain insufficient. The proposal does not fully addr ess 
the legal uncertainty and fragmentation that continues to stifle EU competitiveness, and it falls short of 
achieving a true consolidation of ePrivacy rules into the GDPR. 
The Digital Omnibus also fails to repair a cookie regime that imposes friction without commensurate 
benefits and repeats past mistakes by introducing consent management tools through browsers that 
increase complexity, harm the economy and prevent the EU’s data strategy to boost the EU economy 
through the Internet of Things’ data. Full alignment of ePrivacy with GDPR legal bases, including bringing 
traffic data and e -direct marketing under the GDPR, is essential to ease consent fatigue, enable safety -
enhancing use cases, support growth through accurate measurement of ad effectiveness and ensure 
consistent enforcement. Without these changes, the regulatory environment will remain fragmented and 
unpredictable. 
4.1 Required Structural Upgrades Enabling Innovation 
Structural upgrades are needed to secure coherence, predictability and promote innovation. More 
specifically: 
Embed a risk -based approach as a core GDPR principle: The GDPR needs to explicitly acknowledge 
innovation and economic growth as central EU objectives, and must ensure  by matter of principle a 
balance, proportional (risk-based approach) and practical interpretation and enforcement of the GDPR to 
support practical compliance for fast-evolving technologies.  
Establish an innovation mandate for DPAs: The GDPR needs to introduce explicit requirements on all the 
Data Protection Authorities to balance fundamental rights protection with the promotion of innovation, 
competitiveness and economic impact to ensure a balanced and proportionate application of t he law.  
This mandate must be accompanied by transparency and accountability: regulators should be required to 
report annually on how their activities have enabled innovation and contributed to the EU's digital 
competitiveness, using clear key performance indicators rather than aspirational language. Without such 
a regulatory innovation mandate, the EU will continue to self -sabotage its potential AI leadership and 
ability to attract talent, capital and cutting‑edge development that will continue to go elsewhere. Building 
on the EC’s Digital Omnibus Proposal and centralizing wider interpretative powers  with the European 
Commission under direct CJEU oversight and safeguards to guarantee its accountability, could bring the 
uniformity, consistency, economic awareness and respect of the rule of law that citizens and businesses 
need. Only through comprehensive reform can the EU unlock the full potential of its digital economy and 
ensure its regulatory frameworks keep pace with technological advancement. 
The final Digital Omnibus text should therefore:  
● Codify proportionality and risk as central  principles of the GDPR, requiring that obligations, 
supervisory expectations, and enforcement measures scale to the likelihood and severity of risks 
to individuals’ rights and freedoms. 
● Establish an innovation mandate for DPAs: Introduce an explicit requirement for Data Protection 
Authorities to apply the GDPR in a balanced and proportionate manner —safeguarding 
fundamental rights while supporting innovation, competitiveness, and economic outcomes. The 
UK model demonstrates that regulators can preserve their core mandate while being accountable 
for the economic effects of regulatory decisions, as reflected in the Commission’s adequacy 
assessment.3 This mandate should be supported by enforceable transparency and accountability 
duties, including annual public reporting against measurable KPIs on how regulatory actions have 
enabled innovation and strengthened the EU’s digital competitiveness.

4.2 Embedding CJEU Doctrine on Personal Data and Anonymisation 
The EC’s Digital Omnibus proposal to amend Article 4(1) GDPR marks a necessary correction of the 
excessive and absolutist interpretation held by the data protection authorities in these last 40 years (since 
the Directive 95/46/EC) disregarding Recital 26 of the GDPR. In this case, the proposal builds on specific 
and recent CJEU’s jurisprudence - particularly the SRB and Scannia rulings - on identifiability. The proposal 
confirms the GDPR relative concept of anonymisation, expressly clarifying  that inform ation is personal 
data only if a person or entity is reasonably likely (i.e., Recital 26 GDPR) to identify the individual using 
realistically available means. By moving away from the broad, absolute interpretation often adopted by 
the European Data Protection Supervisor (EDPS) - which lost the SRB case - and national data protection 
authorities (DPAs), the proposal addresses the inconsistent enforcement, disproportionate compliance 
burdens, and uncertainty that have hampered innovative services and AI, and deviated from the GDPR’s 
original intent. 
A true risk -based approach must ensure that, if an entity cannot reasonably identify an individual, the 
data should not be subject to the full weight of GDPR obligations for that entity. In addition, the current 
text can benefit from further precision. The  statutory language must create clear operational criteria to 
secure a coherent and uniform interpretation of the definition of personal data, taking into account 
objective factors such as costs, technological resources, time required for identification, a nd the 
safeguards applied to processing. This is essential to prevent regulatory overreach and to ensure that 
information does not become “personal” for an entity merely because a third party might speculatively 
and theoretically have the means to identify the data subject, unless those means are reasonably likely to 
be used by the entity in practice.  
The final Digital Omnibus text should therefore:  
● Retain the Digital Omnibus amendment, but explicitly remind and clarify that personal data is a 
relative concept (and not an absolute one) and, therefore, identifiability must be assessed from 
the perspective of the specific entity holding the data, consis tently with Recital 26  GDPR 
(“reasonably likely”). This assessment must account for objective factors such as reasonably likely 
costs, existing (rather than speculative) technological resources, business motivations and the 
time required for (re)identification, as well as the s afeguards applied to the processing. The text 
must ensure that information is not or does not become "personal" for an entity merely because 
any potential third party might have, today or in the future, the means to identify the data subject, 
unless those means are reasonably likely to be used by the entity in practice. This approach, in 
conjunction with Article 41a, will harmonise pseudonymisation and anonymisation concepts as 
intended by GDPR, provide legal certainty and support EU digital competitiveness. Legal certainty 
should be established within the GDPR text itself, rather than relying on future guidance, in 
particular, if entrusted to the Data Protection Authorities. These authorities have had 40 years to 
provide workable guidance and they’ve failed ; in 2014, they issued anonymisation guidelines, 
which did not solve any issue and rather further complicated matters by promoting an 
unattainable concept of anonymity —contrary to the GDPR —which enabled them to justify 
regulatory overreach. 
4.3 Restoring the Exceptional Nature of Joint Controllership 
Joint controllership under GDPR Article 26 was originally conceived as an exceptional regime, intended 
only for situations involving genuine co -determination of purposes and means. However, CJEU rulings - 
such as Wirtschaftsakademie, Fashion ID, and Russmedia - have been broadly misunderstood. Along with 
expansive interpretations by national DPAs (notably the German BfDI), Article 26 has evolved into a de 
facto and sui generis liability framework, contrary to basic rules of liability by creating a regime wh ere 
responsibility is divorced from actual control. 
The expansive interpretation of joint controllership has led to a situation where entities with little to no 
factual influence over data processing are nonetheless held liable and creates a significant departure from 
the basic legal tenet that liability should be commensurate with fault and control. CJEU Advocate General 
Bobek, in his seminal opinion in the Fashion ID (CJEU) case, warned against a legal fiction where “everyone 
will be a [joint] controller of personal data!”. He argued for an approach where liability is tied to the 
specific processing operations over which an entity exercises genuine control. Bobek highlighted the 
absurdity of holding a party liable who has no access to the data and thus cannot fulfill data subject rights, 
a situation he described as a “judicially sponsored command to disconnect” from the modern internet. By 
detaching liability from the capacity to influence the processing, Article 26’s joint controllership 
framework not only creates an unworkable compliance burden but also fu ndamentally challenges the 
core principles of accountability and justice that underpin traditional liability rules. 
Article 26 and its interpretation have forced entities, including SMEs and public institutions, to enter into 
complex arrangements for minimal interactions, leading to unclear responsibilities, duplicated 
transparency notices, and disproportionate liabilit y. To resolve regulatory overlap and unnecessary 
complexity, Article 26 must be returned to its original purpose given the continued expansive 
interpretation of Article 26 also poses a significant barrier to AI deployment in the EU, including open -
source and API-based models. In modern, multi -layered AI supply chains, upstream providers often lack 
data access and operational control over downstream processing, yet risk being treated as joint 
controllers merely for providing general -purpose technology. Witho ut a targeted clarification that joint 
controllership attaches only where an entity actually co-determines the purposes and essential means of 
a specific processing activity, Article 26 will continue to create disproportionate liability and chill EU 
innovation, investment, and market access. 
The final Digital Omnibus text should therefore:  
● Introduce a targeted amendment to Article 26 clarifying that controllers are to be regarded as 
joint controllers only where they actively participate in, and actually influence, decisions on the 
purposes and means of processing. This change is essential to restore the original limited purpose 
of joint controllership, ensuring that liability and obligations are allocated where actual control 
resides. 
4.4 Defining "Legal Effects" in Automated Decision-Making 
The clarification that automated decisions are permissible under Article 22 when conditions are met is a 
step in the right direction. However, Article 22 uses the terms "legal effects" and "similarly significantly 
affects" without defining them. This lack of definition has been misused by the DPAs to build extreme 
interpretations that misconstrued Article 22 as a prohibition, contrary to the GDPR letter and, in addition, 
have equated almost any algorithm with an automated decision under Article 22 GDPR, eli minating de 
facto the high legal threshold that this article sets forth and was reconfirmed by the CJEU in SCHUFA. 
Article 22 GDPR was designed with a deliberately high threshold, intended to cover automated decisions 
with substantial gravity — such as the automatic refusal of an online credit application, denial of a social 
benefit like a pension or housing benefit, rejection at the border in an asylum process, exclusion from an 
educational placement, or the automatic termination of a contract — that is, determinations which 
directly affect a person's legal rights or have an equivalent level of seriousness, impacting t heir financial 
standing or access to essential services. For processing to "similarly significantly affect" an individual, the 
decision shall significantly influence the circumstances, behaviour, or choices of the individual concerned 
or, at its most extreme, to lead to the exclusion or unfair discrimination of that person. This is a demanding 
standard, and it is one that was deliberately calibrated to distinguish high -stakes automated 
determinations from the vast universe of data -intensive algorithmic proc essing that characterises the 
modern digital ecosystem.   
Notwithstanding this, a troubling pattern has emerged in the enforcement practice of several DPAs, who 
have progressively disregarded the threshold's limiting function by equating almost any algorithmic or 
data-intensive processing — particularly practices they personally disfavour, with the category of art. 22 
decisions, including those activities that are necessary to comply with other laws (e.g., EDPB draft 
guidelines on DSA).  As the Future of Privacy Forum's comparative analysis of over seventy DPA and court 
decisions documents, there are instances where enforcers have applied Article 22 or its associated 
transparency obligations to processing that, on any objective reading, does not produce effects of the 
requisite gravity, ther eby converting a targeted safeguard against automated decisions of the most 
serious kind into a generalised instrument for scrutinising any algorithm a regulator finds objectionable. 
This approach not only distorts the text and legislative history of Artic le 22, but it has rendered the 
provision's threshold meaningless: if every data -intensive algorithmic process is treated as potentially 
"qualifying ADM," the concept of a high threshold ceases to perform any filtering function, and the 
provision loses the precision that justifies the stringent obligations that it imposes. 
Without a clear definition, routine algorithmic processes  would continue to be treated as having “legal 
effects” by regulators. This regulatory overreach makes the development of standard AI processing 
impossible and hinders the EU's competitiveness. 
The final Digital Omnibus text should therefore:  
● Retain the Digital Omnibus amendment but add a specific definition clarifying that legal effects 
arise only where a decision decisively determines a data subject’s legal status, entitlement, or 
contractual rights. Similarly, "significant effects" should be  defined as having a prolonged or 
permanent impact comparable to a legal effect, to prevent regulatory overreach into low -risk 
automated processes.4 
4.5 Harmonising Legitimate Interest for AI Development 
Article 88c is a pivotal addition, expressly recognizing legitimate interest as a legal basis for AI 
development and aligning GDPR concepts with the AIA, as the CJEU did in the past for the search engines. 
This responds to the gaps in the GDPR regarding the particulars of model development and model training 
exposed by the rapid growth of AI, coupled with the inability of the data protection authorities to accept 
a relative concept of personal data/anonymity. 
However, the current draft introduces a certain number of caveats that would make this legal basis 
inoperable in practice, failing to provide legal certainty for AI development, because it (i) does not address 
the potential use of the special categories of data; (ii) makes references to “consent” requirements under 
"national laws" that undermine harmonization, and (iii) includes a list of undefined safeguards that are 
redundant at best and confusing at worst. This would include the references to children or to art. 32 GDPR 
measures that are already regulated in the GDPR as well as the fact that the GDPR balancing test inherent 
to the legitimate interests test shall take into account safeguards commensurate to the potential impact 
on the individuals’ data protection; adding prescriptive lists in the legal text creates confusion. 
The final Digital Omnibus text should therefore:  
● Retain Article 88c as a legal basis for AI development, but streamline it to align with the GDPR’s 
legitimate interests’ inherent operations and other GDPR existing duties, without any reference 
to (national) consent requirements: the provision should not introduce consent-related elements 
or any additional caveats that would dilute or undermine reliance on legitimate interests. The 
GDPR already establishes robust security and accountability obligations, and use of the legitimate 
interest basis is contingen t on a documented balancing assessment, including identification of 
risks and appropriate mitigating measures. Accordingly, overly prescriptive or model -specific 
safeguards (e.g., stage -gate data minimization, tracking “residually retained data,” or an 
effectively unconditional right to object) should be replaced with a reminder of the -already 
existing- general requirement to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures, 
preserving flexibility and legal certainty, while supporting state -of-the-art privacy protections 
tailored to the relevant AI system or model.

4.6 Rejecting a Parallel Cookie Regime and "Consent Fatigue" 
The Omnibus proposal’s introduction of Article 88a effectively reinstates a rigid consent -centric regime 
for terminal equipment ("cookies"), closely following the logic of the ePD and that has proved to be 
unworkable. This creates a complex sub-regime that restricts legal bases essentially to consent, with only 
narrow exemptions. 
This approach departs from the GDPR’s established risk-based system and from the claimed simplification 
goal. It ignores the viability of legitimate interests for low -risk activities like measurement and analytics 
as well as the activities that shall be reasonably be construed as part of a contractual relationship with the 
user, perpetuating "consent fatigue" through excessive banners.  
The Digital Omnibus proposal to establish a dual regime for cookies is fundamentally flawed for several 
reasons: 
● Firstly, by essentially duplicating the ePD's consent -centric model within the GDPR, it fails to 
acknowledge that a consent-only framework for cookies has proven to be unworkable and does 
not afford any special, additional protection beyond what the GDPR already provides.  
● Secondly, the proposal creates an unworkable division by mandating GDPR compliance for 
cookies that collect personal data while leaving those collecting non-personal data under the ePD. 
This forces companies into the unresolved debate of distinguishing per sonal from anonymous 
data, requiring them to build convoluted, complex and costly technical processes to sort the data 
gathered through the same cookie into two categories to apply a different regime —a direct 
contradiction to the goal of simplification.  
● Finally, subject to limited exceptions, it is illogical to limit the installation of a cookie to solely one 
legal basis in practice (consent) and another set of legal basis for using the data this same cookie 
collects. A cookie is never placed in a vacuum; its existence is intrinsically linked to the use of the 
data it gathers, which is governed by the six alternative legal bases in the GDPR, including, but not 
limited to, consent.

4.7 Deleting Mandatory Automated Consent Signals 
The proposal to introduce Article 88b, mandating browser-based machine-readable consent signals, adds 
a new layer of complexity on top of existing rules. Experience shows that top-down, EU-wide consent tool 
mandates—such as the "Cookie Pledge" or "Do Not T rack"—have repeatedly failed due to technical and 
political challenges. 
Imposing this requirement now would disproportionately burden all organisations with complex 
implementation costs while confusing users with yet another consent mechanism. No interoperable 
standard currently exists, and a mandatory infrastructure -level signal risks conflicting with the purpose -
based assessment required by Article 6 GDPR. 
The proposal to mandate that web browsers collect cookie consent is not new and has been discarded in 
the past because it faces significant legal, technical, and financial issues. First, legally, the current system 
of consent banners has already led to “co nsent fatigue,” where users click through without reading, 
rendering consent or consent rejection meaningless; a browser-based system would exacerbate this issue 
by creating a single point of failure. In addition, such a system would further entrench the m arket power 
of the major browsers, to the detriment of independent publishers. Technically, a universal, browser -
based consent mechanism would require a level of standardization that is costly and difficult to achieve 
and lead to a fragmented and inconsist ent user experience. Financially, the consequences of a browser -
based consent model could be severe for the digital advertising ecosystem, leading to a sharp decline in 
advertising revenue, threatening the viability of many online players at the expense of a diverse and open 
internet. 
The final Digital Omnibus text should therefore:  
● Delete Article 88b in its entirety. Cookies shall not be subject to a consent-only system and, even 
where consent would be required, the GDPR already offers a comprehensive framework for 
consent; a parallel mandatory technical regime would only render the consent a meaningless 
action, will create antitrust issues, be technically complex and costly and impact the economic 
viability of the digital ecosystem. 
4.8 Completing the Consolidation of ePrivacy into GDPR 
The partial consolidation of the ePrivacy framework leaves electronic direct marketing and traffic data 
subject to fragmented, overlapping regimes. Direct marketing is already regulated under the GDPR 
regardless of channel, so maintaining a separate ePriva cy regime (effectively 27 national 
implementations) solely because marketing is delivered electronically is difficult to justify. 
Much traffic data is generated by IoT devices and is intended to be further leveraged under the Data Act 
to support the EU digital single market. Keeping these data under a consent -only ePrivacy model is 
inconsistent with that objective, especially as the GDPR provides additional lawful bases beyond consent 
that are often necessary for scalable IoT and AI innovation. A cookies-only reform would therefore create 
an incoherent regulatory architecture, preserving fragmentation and barriers the Digital Omnibus aims to 
remove. 
Consolidating these areas into the GDPR adds no new obligations but relocates existing rights into a 
coherent framework. This aligns with the Digital Omnibus objective to streamline the digital acquis and 
ensures technological neutrality by applying equivalent rules to all operators. 
The final Digital Omnibus text should therefore:  
● Delete Articles 6, 9, and 13 of the ePrivacy Directive and move the regulation of traffic data and 
direct marketing entirely under the GDPR. This would subject these activities to a single, 
harmonised transparency and legal basis framework, reducing admini strative burdens and 
ensuring consistent governance. 
5. Deep Dive: EU Cybersecurity Framework 
5.1 Cybersecurity Incident Reporting 
The Digital Omnibus proposals surrounding cybersecurity incident reporting can be viewed as progressing 
towards harmonisation and simplification across cybersecurity regulations. The proposal contains important 
cybersecurity-related outputs and reforms designed to simplify and harmonise how cybersecurity and incident 
reporting work across the EU’s digital rulebook, such as: 
● Single-Entry Point (SEP) for Cybersecurity & Incident Reporting; 
● Alignment and Harmonisation of Reporting Thresholds & Timeline; 
● Common Templates & Harmonised Processes; 
● Strengthened Role of European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) 
These proposals are a necessary step toward reducing administrative burden and improving coherence across 
the EU’s digital and cybersecurity framework. In particular, the introduction of a SEP for incident reporting and 
the alignment of timelines represent tangible progress for organisations operating across borders and sectors. 
However, the Digital Omnibus remains primarily a procedural simplification, leaving significant legal, 
definitional, and supervisory fragmentation unresolved. To fully realise its objectives, further action is needed 
to move from coordinated reporting to genuinely harmonised compliance.  
5.2 Digital Omnibus Expansion - Where Could More Be Done 
The Digital Omnibus is strong on “how” but is cautious on “what”. The Digital Omnibus meaningfully 
simplifies the reporting mechanics, the timelines and the administrative burden. However, the EC could 
go further by harmonising legal triggers, unifying def initions and taxonomies, reducing national 
divergence and potentially extending the “once-only” principle beyond reporting.  
Based on the Digital Omnibus logic and objectives (simplification, burden reduction, coherence), there are 
several areas where the EC could go further which are not radical changes, but natural next steps that 
would deepen the Digital Omnibus’ impact. 
5.3 Beyond “Single Entry Point” to Single Legal Notification 
The Digital Omnibus focuses on procedural consolidation (report once, distribute many), but legal 
obligations remain fragmented with different “trigger points”, different legal tests and different liability 
consequences under each instrument. The EC could introduce a single, cross -regime legal notification 
standard for cyber incidents with one definition of “awareness”, “significant incident” and “high risk” with 
one unified legal act of notification that sat isfies the GDPR, the Network and Information Security (NIS2) 
Directive, the Critical Entities Resilience (CER) Directive, and the Electronic Identification, Authentication 
and Trust Services (eIDAS) Regulation simultaneously. Today, even with one portal, o rganisations would 
still have to perform multiple legal assessments and justify different thresholds in parallel. True 
simplification will also require legal, not just technical unification. 
5.4 Harmonise Incident Taxonomies and Severity Scales 
The Digital Omnibus foresees common templates, but still leaves sector-specific taxonomies intact and it 
allows divergent severity scoring. The EC could mandate a single EU incident taxonomy, including incident 
types (availability, integrity, confidentiality, supply chain), severity levels (e.g. 1 –4), impact dimensions 
(users, services, data, financial loss) similar to how aviation safety uses  a shared classification system 
across jurisdictions. This would result in easier internal classification, better EU -wide analytics and a 
reduced risk of inconsistent enforcement decisions.  
5.5 Align Substantive Risk-Management Requirements 
The Digital Omnibus is cautious whereby it avoids touching NIS2 security measures and sectoral 
cybersecurity obligations. More harmonisation is possible by identifying horizontal cybersecurity controls 
common across regimes in areas such as incident response, backup & recovery, supplier risk management 
and logging and monitoring for example. It is a possibility for the EC to create a baseline EU cybersecurity 
control set, allowing sectoral laws to add requirements, but not redefine the basics. 
5.6 Divergence by Member States 
The current reality, even with harmonisation would mean that Member States could add national 
reporting nuances, authorities could issue diverging guidance and enforcement expectations may be 
different or misaligned across jurisdictions. The Digital Omnibu s could more explicitly enforce limits on 
additional national reporting fields, and impose a mandatory mutual recognition of assessments. This 
would result in more binding EU level guidance, not just best practice which is sometimes not linear in its 
interpretation. Without this, simplification at EU level risks being undone nationally. 
5.7 Clarify Interplay with Product Cybersecurity 
The Digital Omnibus focuses on operational incidents, but does not necessarily address overlaps with the 
Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), the Product Liability Directive and the AIA cybersecurity duties. More clarity 
on incident attribution rules like when is a n incident an incident, when is an incident a product 
vulnerability issue, when is an incident an operational failure and when is an incident a service outage 
would be beneficial. The result of clarity here would be fewer parallel disclosures across reporting regimes 
and less legal ambiguity in supply chain incidents.  
5.8 Interim Solutions and Measures 
While the Digital Omnibus represents a significant step toward simplifying and harmonising EU 
cybersecurity obligations, its full implementation will depend on the completion of inter -institutional 
negotiations. In the interim, there is scope for the EC, ENISA, and national competent authorities to deliver 
meaningful simplification through non -legislative and operational measures that reduce duplication, 
enhance legal certainty, and prepare stakeholders for the future framework without prejudging the 
legislative process. 
As a first priority, the EC could promote the immediate use of a voluntary, cross-regime incident reporting 
package that organisations can prepare once and submit to all relevant authorities. This “single 
notification package” would not replace existing re porting channels but would harmonise the content of 
notifications across GDPR, NIS2, and other applicable regimes. Authorities would be encouraged to accept 
this package as fulfilling their information requirements and to refrain from requesting the same 
information in alternative formats. Such an approach would significantly reduce administrative burden 
while remaining fully compatible with existing legal obligations. 
To support this objective, the EC, in close cooperation with ENISA and relevant supervisory bodies, could 
publish a common interim incident reporting template covering all mandatory data points required under 
existing EU cybersecurity and data protection l aw. This template should be modular in design, allowing 
authorities to focus only on the sections relevant to their mandate, while enabling organisations to rely 
on a single, structured dataset. By standardising the information collected, this measure woul d deliver 
immediate harmonisation benefits even in the absence of a single technical reporting portal. 
To reinforce this structural harmonisation, ENISA should oversee the alignment of existing national portals 
by enforcing a common schema, identical forms, and unified field definitions. This approach draws on the 
GDPR model, where national breach portals u se structured fields to facilitate cross -border sharing and 
data interoperability. By standardising the digital interface of reporting, the EU can prevent the "digital 
fragmentation" that often occurs when Member States implement shared requirements throug h 
divergent technical systems. 
Furthermore, the EC could move to allow single common language submissions. To eliminate the latency 
and cost of translating complex technical reports into up to 27 national languages, the EC could mandate 
that authorities accept submissions in a single co mmon language. This would ensure that during the 
critical early hours of an incident, resources are directed toward remediation rather than administrative 
translation, significantly improving the speed of the European response. 
In parallel, the EC could facilitate coordinated interpretative guidance clarifying key concepts that 
currently drive fragmentation and over-reporting, in particular the notions of “awareness” of an incident 
and “significance” or “high risk.” Joint guidanc e developed with ENISA and the EDPB could establish a 
shared understanding of when reporting clocks start and how initial, intermediate, and final notifications 
could be sequenced. This would improve the quality and usefulness of incident notifications rec eived by 
authorities. 
Further simplification could be achieved through soft alignment of reporting timelines. Without altering 
legal deadlines, competent authorities could agree, through guidance and supervisory practice, to accept 
notifications aligned to the longest applicabl e timeframe where multiple regimes apply, provided 
organisations act in good faith and without undue delay. This would reduce pressure on organisations to 
submit incomplete or duplicative early -stage reports and would better reflect operational incident 
response realities. 
Beyond initial notification, authorities should also seek to minimise duplication in post-incident follow-up. 
Mutual acceptance of post -incident analyses, root -cause reports, and remediation plans across 
supervisory and enforcement bodies would significant ly reduce repetitive information requests. This 
could be achieved through administrative cooperation arrangements or coordinated supervisory 
expectations, without affecting authorities’ substantive powers. 
Finally, as a light-touch precursor to a future single-entry portal, ENISA could host a central online landing 
page consolidating interim templates, guidance, and Member State submission instructions. While not a 
reporting mechanism in itself, such a resource would provide a single point of reference for organisations 
and reinforce consistency in reporting practices across the Union. 
Taken together, these interim measures would deliver tangible simplification and harmonisation benefits 
in the short term, support both authorities and regulated entities in managing cybersecurity incidents 
more effectively, and ensure that practical experience gained during this transitional period directly feeds 
into the implementation of the Digital Omnibus once adopted.

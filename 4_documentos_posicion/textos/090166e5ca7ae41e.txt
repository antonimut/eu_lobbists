To European Commission 
 
 
Implementing the outstanding elements 
of the Basel III framework with adjust-
ments to take into account of EU speci-
ficities  
 
The EBA in its report on the implementation of the outstanding elements of the 
Basel III framework recommends an implementation in the EU that will result in an 
overall increase in capital requirements for EU banks of 23,6%.  Danish banks and 
mortgage banks would face a capital increase of nearly 40% of which the in-
crease stemming from the output floor accounts for 23%. These figures are in 
sharp contrast to G20 indication that the final Basel III revision should not bring 
about a significant increase in overall capital requirements. In Europe this view 
was reiterated by Member States during the ECOFIN Council meeting in July 
2016.  
 
Given the very large impact on the Danish banking and mortgage market it is es-
sential to modify the EBA recommendations for implementing the Basel 2017 re-
form package into European regulation. In this regard we highly appreciate that 
the European Commission as stated in the Inception Impact Assessment will con-
sider an implementation of the outstanding elements of the Basel III framework 
with adjustments to take into account of EU specificities. 
 
We strongly support the principle that capital requirements should reflect the risks 
of banks’ lending and trading book activity. A close link between capital require-
ments and risk will support sound business decision in banks and an efficient allo-
cation of resources in the economy. While improvements have been made to 
the risk sensitivity of the standardised approaches in the final Basel III reforms that 
are in scope for implementation in the EU we are concerned that especially the 
Standardised Approach for credit risk in some important areas does not appropri-
ately reflect the diverse conditions in the EU. More graduality is especially 
needed in the risk weighting of corporate loans and loans secured by real estate, 
including the possibility to adjust the prescribed risk weighting of mortgage loans 
for property in jurisdictions where there is long and solid evidence of low loss 
rates.

the standardised approaches will have an unproportionally large impact on Eu-

The output floor 
 
The main reason for introducing the output floor in the Basel framework was to re-
duce excessive variability of risk weighted assets and to enhance the compara-
bility of risk weighted capital ratios.  
 
A study from EBA finds1: 
• that 82% of the variability can be explained by observable factors, such as 
default status, country of the counterparty etc. The remaining 18% is either 
due to variability in credit risk within each portfolio or because of variability of 
the internal models. 
 
• Capital ratio based on observed defaults rates (rather than PD estimates) 
would only have a minor impact, i.e. the internal model-based capital ade-
quacy ratios seem in line with observed default rates.  
 
• Any model variability is not necessarily biased towards lower capital require-
ments. In fact: “estimated values for PDs and LGDs are, in general, higher 
than the observed default rates and loss rates, which suggests that banks 
are, on average, conservative.  
 
In Europe the issue of unwarranted variation in model outcomes is addressed by 
the very substantial work through the EBA’s IRB Roadmap and the ECB’s Targeted

Review of Internal Models (TRIM). This work aims at harmonizing, strengthening 
and verifying the use of IRB models. We have yet to see the results of this work, 
but we are confident that these initiatives will more effectively than the output 
floor address excessive variability of risk weighted assets from the use of models 
and enhance the comparability of risk weighted capital ratios.  
 
Moreover, the intention of the Basel III framework, including the output floor, is 
not to increase overall capital levels for banks. Yet, as shown in the EBA impact 
studies, the overall effect on the capital requirements for European banks is an 
estimated average an increase of 23,6%, which is very substantial. The output 
floor is the main driver of this estimated increase; with an average contribution to 
the increase of 8,6%. In several member states, including Denmark, Sweden and 
Germany, the increase in capital is far higher and even more significantly driven 
by the isolated effect of the output floor.    
 
The reason for these significant impacts on capital requirements of the output 
floor is that the standardized approach for credit risk is not calibrated to fit Euro-
pean specificities in important loans segments (e.g. mortgage loans and corpo-
rates, as explained in the sections below). The effect is magnified for IRB banks 
operating under well-functioning mortgage systems and in stable and solid eco-
nomic conditions. 
 
Households and corporates will face increased interest rates on their loans due to 
higher funding cost or more difficulty for obtaining loans due to banks having to 
reduce lending to fulfill increased capital requirements. Overall, this will have a 
negative effect on GDP growth in Europe and especially in the most affected 
countries, as for example Denmark. 
 
This is confirmed by the EBA study on the macroeconomic effect of the final Ba-
sel III implementation. They estimated an impact on average annual slowdown in 
GDP growth in the EU area in the first 4 years after implementation of 0,2%, corre-
sponding to an overall GDP costs of around 0.8 %. It will be more severe in the 
most affected Member States.  
 
The EBA study on the macroeconomic effects estimates an economic benefit in 
the longer run due to lower risk and lower costs of financial crises as well as a re-
duction in banks’ funding costs due to being considered safer when they have 
higher capital levels. We believe that such longer-term benefits are very uncer-
tain and difficult to estimate. Other studies show that an optimum capital ratio 
for banks is about 15% with only limited benefits by increasing capital ratios 
above15%. Finally, it should also be recalled that it was not the intention that the 
Basel III reform should increase overall capital levels.

Fundamentally, we believe that the argument for EU implementation of the out-
put floor is founded on the obligation for the EU to adhere to the international 
agreement. The output floor will not have a significant beneficial effect of Euro-
pean financial stability but would add to making European financing more ex-
pensive and less available for households and corporates. Moreover, it will pose 
a serious threat to the European banking model where low risk loans are kept on 
the balance sheet of banks and to a large extent funded through covered 
bonds; a model which historically has functioned well in Denmark.  
 
Solution: For these reasons we strongly recommend the European Commission to 
carefully consider the options for implementing the output floor in Europe in a 
way that minimize the potential negative effects of the floor in Europe. Our rec-
ommended option is to implement the output floor as separate additional capi-
tal requirement for banks that use internal models. This output floor capital re-
quirement should be based on the specific minimum capital requirements set at 
international level, and not include specific EU capital buffers and institution spe-
cific capital requirement set by supervisors. 
 
Mortgage loans 
Risk weights 
The output floor will as stated above particularly affect mortgage loans in Den-
mark where the actual level of losses and impairments from mortgage lending 
are low in both a European and global context. This is to a large extent due to 
that the foreclosure processes in Denmark are highly efficient and borrowers are 
subject to full recourse liability. 
 
Loan impairments have been less than 1% of total lending by Danish specialized 
mortgage banks, including commercial lending, even during the worst crisis in 
the 1930s. During the recent financial crisis – following considerable declines in 
property prices and GDP – the loan loss level, including commercial lending, has 
been modest at 0.2% annual. Further details on loan impairments are shown in 
the Annex. 
 
Generally estimated risk weights for Danish retail mortgages calculated using the 
IRB formula (including the LGD floor of 10% at portfolio level and LGD downturn 
add on) show average risk weights in the area of 13-17%. This is below the risk 
weights under the Standardised Approach which start at 20% in the lowest LTV 
bucket.

Solution: It is essential in the EU framework to include further refinements to both 
the whole loan approach and the loan splitting approach to make the ap-
proaches appropriately reflect the European conditions. Room for calibration of 
lower risk weights in jurisdictions based on clear and objective documentation of 
very low loss rates. Further refinement to the loan splitting approach is also 
needed as the loan splitting approach as proposed in the Basel standard com-
pletely ignores any effect of the real estate collateral above LTV 55%. A specific 
refinement is to reduce the risk weight in the 55-80% LTV band based on the risk 
weight of the counterparty reduced by a factor of [X%] to reflect that the loan is 
fully secured by the real estate property. We propose to apply a reduction factor 
of 25%, whereby mortgage loans to a retail counterparty would be risk weighted 
at 50% in the 55-80% LTV bracket. This would imply approximately the same over-
all risk weight for an 80% LTV mortgage loan under the whole loan and the loan 
splitting approaches.

Current value of real estate 
The Final Basel III framework includes a principle of “value at origination”. A gen-
eral requirement to keep the value of the property constant as measured at orig-
ination in the calculation of the LTV would oblige lenders to ignore variations in 
property values over the longer term. 
 
We recommend that lenders should as today have the option to update the 
value of the property, also upwards, when justified by market developments. This 
is in line with option 2 in the EBA advice to maintain the current approach of CRR. 
 
Unrated corporates 
 
In the Basel Committee’s finalisation of the Basel III framework from December 
2017 unrated corporates under the Standardised Approach for credit risk are as-
signed a risk weight of 100% in jurisdiction where external ratings are allowed for 
regulatory capital purposes. In jurisdictions where external ratings are not al-
lowed for regulatory capital requirements, a 65% risk weight may be assigned to 
corporates that the bank has assessed to have a credit quality that corresponds 
to the “Investment Grade” definition, provided the corporate (or its parent com-
pany) has securities outstanding on a recognised exchange. 
 
In Europe corporates normally fund themselves through the bank loans. Only very 
large corporates fund themselves through the market. One of the consequences 
of this funding structure is that only very large corporates in Europe have a need 
to acquire an external rating. In Denmark only about 10 corporates are rated, 
and the share of corporates loans granted to rated counterparties is very mod-
est, and near zero for all but for the largest banks. The unrated mid-size corporate 
segment in Europe, that comprises by far the largest share of corporates, will 
practically all be assigned a flat, risk insensitive 100% risk weight.

Promoting the use of external ratings for corporates that rely on bank funding 
would not be an economically rational option. In a bank funding model, external 
ratings are fundamentally not needed in the credit allocation process. Banks typ-
ically have more credit risk information on their small and midsize corporate cus-
tomers than external credit rating agencies have. External ratings would only 
serve for regulatory purposes, at a considerable cost for banks and their corpo-
rate customers, without adding value for the credit allocation process. 
  
On the other hand, applying the SCRA approach in Europe and dismissing all to-
gether the use of external rating for regulatory purposes is not a recommended 
option. Ratings are important for market funding as well as for regulatory pur-
poses in several exposure classes (sovereigns, institutions, including covered 
bonds and securitisations).  
 
Danish IRB banks face a high impact on the risk weighting of their non-SME cor-
porate portfolios from the Basel output floor. We estimate that this could increase 
the risk weighted exposure amount for an average non-SME corporate counter-
party by over 60%. Such a large impact on the required capital for corporate 
lending will increase the costs of corporate funding significantly.  
 
Solution: To ensure a minimum level of risk sensitivity for corporates under the 
ECRA approach comparable with the treatment of corporates exposures un-
der a market funding model, it is essential that a mechanism is implemented 
in the EU regulation whereby corporates that are assessed by institutions against 
the definition of “investment grade” may be assigned a risk weight of 65%. 
 
Allowing banks to apply the SCRA risk weight for investment grade corporates at 
65%, and non-investment grade corporates at 100% would be consistent with the 
treatment of unrated banks in the revised Basel Standards2.   
 
The application of the 65% risk weight under the Basel SCRA approach requires 
the corporate (or its parent company) to have securities listed on a recognised 
exchange. In a European bank funding model this requirement would be unduly 
restrictive, and should not be a requirement in Europe, because unrated coun-
terparties typically do not issue securities. Furthermore, having securities listed is 
not necessarily an indication of superior credit quality.

Other important adjustments to account for European speci-
ficities 
The EBA report on the implementation of the outstanding elements of the Basel III 
framework recommends that the EU specific SMEs supporting factor, which has 
recently been adopted in the CRR2 in a revised form, and the current European 
exemption from Pillar 1 capital requirements to cover credit valuation adjustment 
(CVA) risk for certain counterparties, including non-financial corporates, should 
be removed. 
 
The EU specific SME supporting factor 
We recommend retaining the EU specific SMEs supporting factor. It is an im-
portant element for reducing the cost of financing for small businesses in Europe 
and in Denmark. 
 
Capital requirements for CVA risk

nificant effect om the capital requirements for European banks, partly because

Unlisted equity  
In the finalisation of the Basel III framework the risk weight for unlisted equity in the 
standardised approach for credit risk has been increased from 100% to 250% for 
holdings in corporate entities where the institution intends to establish a long-term 
business relationship. In Denmark small and midsize banks together own shares in 
entities dedicated to providing ancillary IT-services for their owners. It is important 
that the current risk weighting of shares in such entities is retained at 100%. Other-
wise such arrangements would be unduly penalised compared to having in-
house IT-services.  
 
Exposures to institutions 
The Basel text for the revised risk weighting for unrated institutions based on the 
classification of institution counterparties into grades should be clarified as re-
gards the inclusion of the Pillar 2 capital requirements. The text seems to suggest 
that the Pillar 2 capital requirements, and other requirements imposed the super-
visor, should be included in the definition of “Grade A” depending on whether 
these requirements are disclosed. Internationally, and within Europe also, supervi-
sory practises differ in setting Pillar 2 capital requirements and the disclose of the 
Pillar 2 requirements. Before such practises are harmonised further, we recom-
mend that Pillar 2 capital requirements should not be included in the EU definition 
of ”Grade A”. 
 
The simplified alternative to the new standardised approach for market risk 
We welcome the simplified alternative to the new standardised approach for 
smaller institutions with limited trading book activity. The new standardised ap-
proach for market risk is complicated to apply and will be a significant adminis-
trative and financial burden to implement for banks with simpler trading books, 
even though the size of the trading book exceeds the threshold set in CRR2 to 
qualify for the simpler alternative to the standardised approach for reporting pur-
poses.  Especially the threshold in CRR2 based on the relative size of the trading 
book compared to the bank’s total assets is constraining for many smaller banks 
in Denmark with a simple trading book activity. The Basel standard does not set

The PD floor in IMA for market risk for sovereigns and EU covered bond  
The Final Basel III framework for markets risk includes 0.03% PD floor to be applied 
to all exposures for the default risk capital requirement under the Internal Model 
Approach. For high quality EU Sovereign bonds and EU covered bonds this floor 
would be disproportionally penalising. We proposed that EU sovereign are ex-
empted from the PD floor and that the PD floor for AA or higher rated EU cov-
ered bonds is set at 0,01% in the EU implementation.

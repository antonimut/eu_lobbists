Preliminary remarks 
The Italian Banking Association (ABI) welcomes the opportunity to comment on the EU Commission 
“Public consultation document implementing the final Basel III reform in the EU”.  
In ABI’s view, the EU legislative action should foster equitable and sustainable economic growth, as well 
as employment and competitiveness of the EU economy. The prudential framework for banks should 
not be intended, in principle, as pursuing a theoretical idea of financial stability. It should rather 
ultimately serve the purpose o f ensuring the EU a sound financial sector supporting the real economy 
and the growth.  
Indeed, ABI would highlight that the new regulations introduced, along with the supervisory action, as 
well as the commitment on the part of the banks themselves, resulted in a huge increase of banks’ 
capitalisation over the last decade. Therefore, the reform is not intended to address major concerns 
about the resilience of the banking sector, by increasing its capitalisation. The real challenge, that the 
upcoming legislation will face, concerns rather the credit supply to the economy.  
The regulation that will transpose in the EU the so called “Finalisation of Basel III” package (henceforth, 
“Basel III", “BCBS framework” or “BCBS standards”) should be assessed against this background. 
The importance of this piece of legislation goes well beyond its impact on the banking sector. It is likely 
to affect the real economy, and ultimately European citizens. Before transposing such a comprehensive 
package, its impact should be carefully assessed, to ensure that its benefits in terms of improved 
financial stability outweigh the effects on the real economy via restrictions in the size and conditions of 
the credit supply. 
Needless to say, this is key for Europe, due to the predomina nt role of banks in financing corporate, 
SMEs and households.  
A well-suited implementation of the BCBS standards in the EU is crucial to ensure a genuine level playing 
field vis-à-vis other jurisdictions, and namely the US. In this respect, it is worth re minding the G -20 
mandate not to significantly increase capital requirements. 
Indeed, figures from the BCBS assessment show that, while this target seems to be met for other 
regions, a major increase in capital requirements is expected in the EU following to a strict 
implementation of the standards. 
The impact assessment should be based on reasonable assumptions. The need for additional capital will 
not be limited to the shortfall to minimum capital requirements – including the effects of increased RWA 
on MREL/TLAC requirements for eligible liabilities – but should also consider realistic market 
expectations about banks’ capitalisation. As a matter of fact, banks need voluntary capital buffers to 
preserve business flexibility and the capacity for exploiting new business opportunities.  
A balanced implementation of the international standards, taking into account the specificities of the 
European financial landscape, is therefore essential. For this purpose, it is crucial to preserve tools which 
proved effective in fostering the access to bank financing for certain categories of borrowers, such as 
the SMEs, otherwise penalised under the international regulatory framework. 
As the commitment to the abovementioned objective - not to significantly increase capital requirements 
- was endorsed by the European Parliament and the EU Council, it is necessary that some refinements 
be introduced when implementing the BCBS package in the EU.

removing the requirement of “being listed” on recognised stock exchange.

1.1.2.2. Identification of short-term exposures to institutions 
10) In your view, what are the relative costs and benefits of using the original maturity as opposed 
to the residual maturity for identifying short- term interbank exposures? Please provide relevant 
arguments and evidence to substantiate your views. 
ABI deems the appropriate choice to be the use of the residual maturity. Indeed, the lower risk weight 
recognized for short-term exposures seems to be warranted in light of the very limited probability that 
the credit risk profile might worsen over a short- time horizon. This is true even in case the original 
maturity was longer, but the remaining life of the exposure is short. The preferential risk weight should 
therefore be granted accordingly. 
More precisely, for short-term exposures to banks, up to 1-month maturity, ABI recommends keeping 
the current 20% risk weight for all counterparties, instead of the differentiated treatments according to 
the BCBS standards. This is to account for the very limited probability of default of ban ks in such a 
short-time horizon, given that they are subject to strict prudential regulation and supervision.

13) Views are sought on the definition of ‘investment grade’ provided by the Basel III sta ndards 
(paragraph 42). In particular, would you deem further refinements or clarifications necessary in order 
to ensure a consistent application across the Union? Please elaborate. 
As referred above (see the response to question 2), with respect to corpora te exposures, ABI 
recommends introducing in the EU, the 65% risk weight (RW) to be applied to unrated corporates 
identified as “investment grade”.  
Among other things, the definition of “investment grade” provided by the Basel III standards (paragraph 
42) stipulates that “the corporate entity (or its parent company) must have securities outstanding on a 
recognised securities exchange”.  
ABI deems not well-suited to the EU market that identification of corporate as “investment grade” be 
conditional upon issuing listed securities.  
The “investment grade” definition is supposed to be linked to creditworthiness, since it is used to identify 
counterparties for the purpose of the application of a lower risk weight for credit risk. Moreover, since 
most of unrated counterparties are not either large companies which issue securities or subsidiaries of 
groups where the parent company issues securities, this more favourable treatment risks being de facto 
not applicable in most cases. We would therefore recommend that this requirement is eliminated. 
 
14) What other measures, if any, could be taken to increase the risk-sensitivity of the standardised 
RW treatment of corporate exposures which currently have no external rating? Please elaborate and 
provide relevant evidence. 
 
15) In your view, which other aspects, if any, should be considered in the context of revising the 
standardised treatment of corporate exposures? Please elaborate. 
ABI welcomes the progress made by the Basel Committee, as the new credit risk framework 
acknowledges the specificities of SME financing and introduces a specific treatment for unrated SMEs 
(85% instead of 100% risk weight) within the corp orate asset class. Nonetheless, ABI considers this 
measure not to be sufficient, as a large amount of banks’ exposures to SMEs falls out of its scope. In 
order to avoid that the new framework results in penalising credit supply to SMEs, ABI therefore 
recommends maintaining the so called EU SME Supporting Factor,  as amended by Regulation (EU) 
876/2019 (CRR2), i.e. applied to exposures towards eligible SMEs, even if assigned to other asset 
classes, and irrespective of the approach (standardised or IRB) under which they are capitalised (thus 
preserving the level playing field between banks applying the standardised and the IRB approach).

Keeping the SMEs Supporting Factor is deemed essential, in light of the role of SMEs in the EU economy 
and their funding essentially based on bank financing, across all member States – which makes this the

Moreover, ABI also recommends retaining and enhancing the so-called EU Infrastructure SF, introduced

supported. 
In addition, ABI recommends introducing in the EU a capital relief for sustainable finance (see also the

Within the retail portfolio, ABI recommends maintaining the specific treatment (35% r isk weight)

Moreover, this kind of loans should be incentivised as they grant access to finance to clients with lower

• there is a 32.6% return to performing status within one year; 
• the effective loss rate (weighted-average LGD rate) is 5.8% 
• the expected loss (EL) is 0.16% 
Accordingly, the theoretical risk weighting (RW) factor, calculated on the retail curve, is 8.4%. 
ABI would also recommend introducing a preferential treatment for exposures to professionals and 
micro-firms, characterized by auxiliary services of assistance and monitoring of the financed subjects, 
able to reduce the probability of credit default.

1.1.6. Real estate (RE) exposures 
1.1.6.1. Implementation of loan splitting (LS) approach vs whole loan (WL) approach 
34) Views are sought on the relative costs and benefits of the LS approach and the WL approach 
provided by the final Basel III standard. In particular, how do the two approaches compare in terms of 
risk-sensitivity, impact on RWAs and operational burden? Please provide relevant evidence to 
substantiate your views. 
We recommend applying the “loan spl itting approach -  LS” that is currently applied in the CRR. In 
particularly, the LS approach is a sensible solution to the high capital charge deriving from residential 
real estate and has the following advantages: 
(i) it avoids important differences in terms of RW when a loan moves into the next LTV band;

1.1.6.4. Prudently conservative valuation criteria 
41) Views are sought on the costs and benefits of the valuation criteria provided by the Basel III

ABI recommends keeping the current option in CRR to apply market value or market lending value to

requirements of prudential, conservative and sustainable valuation.

the valuers; (iv) a possible mortgage credit crunch due to the new LTV assessment.

4.1. CONVERTING THE REPORTING REQUIREMENT INTO AN OWN FUNDS REQUIREMENT 
146) What considerations should be taken into account regarding the implementation of the revised 
trading book boundary? Please specify and provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views 
Some refinements are deemed necessary as to the scope of the trading book.  
First, mandatory inclusion in the trading book should n ot be required for short positions in own credit 
spreads – typically arising from own liabilities issued out of the banking book (whose purpose is funding 
rather than trading). The prescription that instruments giving rise to net short credit position should be 
assigned to the trading book will produce the unintended consequence that own issuances will have to 
be originated from the trading book, unless explicit derogation is specified for short positions in own 
credit spread.  
Indeed, own liabilities issued typically for funding purposes generate a short position in the own credit 
spread and should hence be assigned to the Trading Book according to 25.6(2). This however would 
have at least two undesirable consequences:  
1. Attribution to the trading book of a position without any trading intent (i.e. funding purposes)  
2. Expected Shortfall ( ES) being affected by simulated trading losses generated by scenarios of 
own spread tightening. Depending on the relative magnitude of the own issuances compared to the size 
of the genuine trading activities this could generate a left tail that is completely dominated by own credit 
tightening scenarios, dwarfing any other risk factor. 
A derogation for short positions in own credit spread is therefore essential. 
Some aspects of the Internal Risk Transfer (IRT) framework should be reviewed as well. The final FRTB 
standard is still very restrictive with respect to IRT. In particular, Equity  and Credit IRT can only be 
recognized in case they are directly externalized using a perfectly matching trade with a third party. The 
requirement appears over-reaching in that it should suffice that the IRT to the t rading book is exactly 
matching the banking book pay-off so that all risk is transferred from the banking into the trading book. 
How such risk is then managed/externalised is a trading book management issue and any mismatches 
will attract own funds requirements. It should suffice that a bank has processes in place that 
demonstrate that IRT is exact and rigorously preserved. 
POSITION PAPER  
 
Page 45 of 71 
The point is particularly relevant in the case of issuance of structured notes from the banking book with 
embedded equity (Certificates) or credit (CLN) derivatives. The request that an IRT is only valid if the 
derivative is exactly externalised with the Street through an OTC transaction would severely impair the 
business model, generating an in -natural request for OTC transactions to back-to-back risks that the 
bank is typically managing via macro-hedges. 
In Europe, the e quity-linked notes business amounts to a several hundred billion notional. Having to 
hedge these issues with external counterparties in a perfect way (1:1) would severely impact the sales 
margin. A very large part of existing issues would need to be (perfectly) hedged retrospectively with an 
external party. This would lead to serious operational challenges and very high additional transaction 
costs. Consequently, the  rules put in question the profitability of the whole business. Furthermore, 
issuing notes out of the trading book may be problematic for many banks, because this can result in a 
trading book VaR/ES dominated by un-hedgeable own credit spread exposure.   
Moreover, capitalisation of the IRT Desk for internal risk transfer of general interest rate risk on a stand-
alone basis, without diversification with other risk positions -  once all risk transfers and their 
effectiveness are documented - seems to be unduly penalising. 
ABI therefore recommends, at the very least, to remove or substantially relax the requirement to 
"perfectly" (i.e. 1:1) offset the internal hedge/IRT. Ideally, banks should be allowed to account for IRT 
where they can prove that the latter are part of a sensible business model (as opposed to a capital 
arbitrage scheme).

2. Fall-back approaches 
 
a. The hypothetical portfolio approach 
The ‘hypothetical portfolio’ approach for trading book funds under the SA -MR suffers from several 
inconsistencies and its use would result in very limited gain in terms of capital compared to the approach 
based on the ‘single equity’ approach even for funds that are yet diversified and regulated such as UCITS 
funds: 
- the capital requirements must be computed on a stand -alone basis which does not reflect 
sufficiently the diversification effects; 
- it can be overly complex in practice as the fund’s mandate allows many degrees of freedom: 
o long/short positions with potentially some derivatives without any description, which 
made this approach impossible to use on most of funds in the trading book; 
o broad categories of investments that, even under the assumption that there is no 
position on derivatives (contrary to the mandate), would lead to hypothetical portfolios 
composed of only few assets penalised under the SA-MR. 
Thus, regarding the ‘hypothetical portfolio’ approach: 
o to consider diversification effects, it is suggested to remove the requirement which would oblige 
banks to calculate capital requirements on a stand-alone basis under this approach; 
o to make the use of the ‘hypothetical portfolio’ approach feasible when the fund’s mandate 
mentions long/short positions, derivatives positions or when the fund’s duration or leverage 
effect may be ambiguous, it is recommended to provide additional guidance. 
 
b. The single equity approach 
The ‘single equity’ approach is particularly impactful given th e magnitude of shocks (-/+70%) and the 
absence of diversification between funds in the other sector bucket.  
Moreover, BCBS d457 defines a 100% LGD to calculate jump-to-default (JtD) for non-look-through funds 
(and non -look-through indices) under the Defau lt Risk Charge (DRC). This LGD does not reflect 
diversification effect within a fund and should be low to reflect the limited fund (or index) losses resulting 
from one of its numerous underlying. 
Under this approach, funds can be expected to attract a charge that easily bypasses the full deduction. 
To reduce the highly punitive impact of the ‘single equity’ approach on equity investment in CIU, ABI 
proposes to assign funds meeting certain diversification requirements (see the response to question 
149) to the equity index bucket (15% RW) and the other funds to the “Other bucket”.  
Finally, to account for diversification effects under the DRC, we propose to define a low loss given default 
(i.e. LGD = 5%), to reflect the fact that fund losses are limited when one of its components defaults.

159) Views are sought on the cost and benefits of implementing the  revised CVA framework in the 
EU. In particular, how do the approaches provided by the final Basel III standards compare with the 
current approach of the CRR in terms of impacts on RWAs and operational burden? Please provide

The improved risk sensitivity of the revised CVA framework comes to a much higher cost as compared 
to the current approach, both in terms of RWAs and operational burden. 
The scope of transactions subject to the new requisite would be much larger if the current exemptions 
are not preserved in the EU transposition. In the following responses we elaborate on the impact and 
highly undesirable consequences in case of removal of  the existing exemptions from the scope of the 
CVA charge. 
We would also highlight that a  huge impact comes from the review of the exposure calculation 
methodology (SA-CCR instead of CEM). In addition, while being conceptually the same, the capital 
requirement increases significantly due to the increased risk weights. The increase in risk weights is 
caused by a less granular approach (IG/HY and NR versus credit rating steps), where the risk weights 
for counterparties with the highest credit quality are comp arable to those assigned to counterparties 
with lower credit quality in the currently applicable Standardised Method for CVA. 
As to the operational effort, it should be noted that t he use of FRTB sensitivities for the calculation of 
the SA-CVA capital charge will be operationally complex as long as the calculation of the capital charge 
of the two frameworks will not be concomitantly in force. 
A large and very granular set of CVA sensitivities are required (up to 100k), even for margined 
counterparties where they are expected to be almost negligible. Tight CSAs should be indeed exempted 
from the calculations of SA -CVA since the cost for computing their sensitivities is disproportioned 
compared to the risk they embed.  
A very granular hedge of a regulatory (as opposed to accounting) CVA is required. Benefit of this hedging 
(especially for index hedging of counterparty credit spread) is however very limited. 
The misalignment between the accounting CVA and the regulatory CVA maintains an operational burden 
for banks. Wrong-way risk is required to be reflected in the CVA calculation, even if accounting CVA 
does not.

8. SUSTAINABLE FINANCE  
191) In your view, which  further measures, if any, could be taken to incorporate ESG risks into 
prudential regulation without pre-empting ongoing work as set out above? Please elaborate and provide 
relevant evidence to substantiate your view. 
We think the integration of ESG risks in banks’ risk management, whenever possible, is part of a sound 
and prudent management, while the development of solutions and products enabling the climate change 
transition is a key factor of growth and development. 
Including climate-related risk in the general risk management represents material challenges, in terms 
of methodology (e.g. time horizon, risk materialisation, scenario(s)), data granularity or risk 
governance. The measures will require major adjustments to banks business strategies, risk 
management, corporate culture and the way banks communicate, collect, process and analyse data with 
significant investments in the resources, processes and IT systems. Relevant, reliable and comparable 
data on rather granular level are also a necessary to ensure compliance with the envisaged regulation.  
We are proposing measures that we believe can be incorporated within the existing EBA mandates and 
the upcoming work of EBA , and bringing to the  attention of the Commission some considerations that 
in our view merit further discussion.  
 
1. Preferential prudential treatment of sustainable exposures and revision of the timeline of the 
EBA mandate  
As stated in a recent keynote speech of Vice- President Dombrovskis, prudential rule s could favour 
'green' investments and loans, while keeping prudential considerations in mind.  The European Banking 
Authority (EBA) has been mandated by the Capital Requirement Regulation (Article 501c)  to assess 
whether a dedicated prudential treatment of exposures related to assets or activities associated

substantially with environmental and/or social objectives would be justified, including by analysing 
methodologies for the assessment of the effective riskiness of those exposures compared to the riskiness 
of another exposure.  
Sustainable assets, where a lower prospect of financial risk related to the ESG factors can be 
demonstrated, should benefit from a preferential prudential treatment. 
Maintaining a link between risk and capital is crucial to avoid any financial stability concerns. Pending 
the development of the methodologies for incorporation of the ESG factors into IRB/ AIRB models and 
in the standard supervisory framework, and in line with the objective to maintain the link between long 
term risk considerations and capital, we suggest that the European Banking Authority (EBA) explores,

Using forward looking methodologies, it is suggested that the EBA investigates whether there are groups 
of assets/activities under the EU taxon omy that show a lower financial risk, and, specifically, a lower 
credit risk profile by virtue of their sustainability. Please see the details of our proposal for a Sustainable 
Finance Supporting Factor in the EBF report “Encouraging and Rewarding Sustaina bility” proposed in 
the context of broader range of incentives that can accelerate sustainable finance   
The supporting factor would only apply to such eligible assets after the RWA requirement has been 
computed as usual (using the standard ised or the IRB/AIRB approach). The SFSF(s) would apply both 
to SA-CR and IRB/AIRB approaches but once banks applying IRB/AIRB approach will have embedded 
the sustainability profile in their validated internal rating model, the SFSF can no longer be used. Given 
the temporary character of the supporting factor and its potential to contribute to the acceleration of 
sustainable finance, the timeline of the EBA mandate should be revisited and moved forward.   
Examples of potential eligible asset classes whose RWAs, could be multiplicated by a single SFSF or by

sustainable assets, could be reasonably demonstrated. We support the recommendations of the 
Technical Expert Group on Sustainable Finance (TEG) suggesting that the EBA, as part of its 
mandate, also assesses the possibility of developing a segment of green bonds that would define 
the conditions to be met by the EU -Green Bond Standard in order to benefit, potentially, from 
a preferential prudential treatment. 
c. Green securitization. 
d. Green lease.  We believe that leasing plays an important role in achieving sustainable growth in 
Europe by helping European businesses access productive and greener assets. Leasing is useful 
in supporting investments for climate-neutral activities and to permit the efficient consumption 
of scarce natural resources. Lease allows to replace old or polluting equipment with cleaner and 
more efficient assets. The identification of the green assets leased and/or the green activities 
(as taxonomy requires) financed through leasing could be clearly disclosed in lessor/lessee 
reporting. Lease operation features for sustainable goals lead to a higher value of the asset as 
a guarantee and to a better maintaining of the guarantee value over time for a lower LGD.

Banks will also be increasingly steering complete portfolios towards a decarbonisation path, thereby 
reducing both transition-related and systemic risk created by climate change.  At portfolio level, banks 
will have to demonstrate the extent to which the lending of each institution and per sector contributes

2. Priority should be given to “climate risks”  
The prudential rules, in the Capital Requirements Directive and Regulation, are aimed at enhancing 
banks’ risk management in order to strengthen their resilience and the soundness of the financial 
system. Whereas climate-related risk has been acknowledged by academics, regulators/supervisors and 
the industry as a source of financial risk, this has not been clarified for the other risk categories in the 
scope of the question, namely the other than climate- environmental risk types (e.g. biodiversity), and 
social risk, which haven’t been clearly defined yet in the regulatory work. 
It  is essential to consider a phased implementation of the requirements for the various risk categories 
(starting with climate related risk) in line with the increasing knowledge on such risk categories and 
with standards setting at international level, in order to ensure a consistent and effective 
implementation, in the same vein as the actions of  the Network for Greening the Financial System 
(NGFS) or the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) for example.  
 
3. The suitability of the EU taxonomy also for risk management purposes should be assessed, 
including for climate related stress tests  
The risk management framework needs to cover the whole balance sheet in terms of risk analysis, 
management and control at bank level and needs to take into account all the elements in relation to the 
counterparty. The current EU taxonomy was not developed for climate risk assessment at 
client/counterparty level. It has been designed so far for investment purposes - financing of specific 
projects and is not adapted to general purpose loans which represent circa 80% of total loans. It also 
does not fully take into account the corporate strategy / commitments in favour of carbon issuance 
reductions within a transition time path, w hich should be a key driver of climate risk mitigation. The 
suitability of the taxonomy for risk management purposes should therefore be carefully assessed as part 
of the ongoing work of EBA.   
Supervisors’ and banks’ own climate risk related stress test a re still premature. Before proceeding to 
own stress- test, banks need firstly to assess their risks (which require the definition of common 
methodologies for transition and physical risks assessment) and also to build their base line scenario.  
Running a ba seline scenario and measuring portfolios sensitivity with a limited number of scenarios 
would be a much more pragmatic approach both at the supervisory and banks level.   
In addition, climate risks related stress tests should not be included in the existin g EBA stress test 
exercise as they raise many key concerns, such as: 
- Mismatch between the time horizon of traditional risk models (3 years), on one hand, and the 
speed and time frame (that can be very long term around 15- 20 years) of climate risks 
materialization, on the other hand. 
- Difficulties, and sometimes impossibilities for obtaining data from clients (especially geolocation 
data for physical risks)

- Impossibilities to include Sectoral d etails needed for climate stress -testing as they are too

4. ESG Risk Disclosures and Data availability  
Relevant, reliable and comparable data on rather granular level are necessary, not only to comply with 
the envisaged regulation, but also for a proper and efficient risk management as well as for development 
of further products and scaling up on a voluntary basis. Quality climate risk related disclosure in a form 
of standardized enhanced disclosures is a prerequisite for an effective bank climate risk management 
framework. Relying on market incentives or direct engagement from financial market participants with 
corporates is not enough. It also underestimates the costs of obtaining data from companies that do 
not publish ESG information in their usual disclosures, nor indirectly through third parties. While it is 
important to avoid imposing unnecessary burdens on companies, particular the smaller ones, a balance 
needs to be found to ensure data availability and quality by requiring mandatory and harmonized 
disclosure by economic actors.  
The availability of ESG data, if not dealt with appropriately, will be a clear source of competitive distortion 
between the largest corporates and the small- and medium-sized companies. It should therefore be the 
priority of the EU legislators to ensure the symmetry between the objectives to mainstream sustainable 
finance, screening criteria and proper risk management on one side and the reporting requirements 
from companies on the other side. Data quality and effectiveness of the EU Taxonomy as well as risk 
management will largely depend on the availability of reliable and con sistent data. While banks are 
working together to extend available metrics on a sector and borrower basis to banking lending, more 
statistical data is needed to reliably measure in a science -based way the progress towards the Paris 
goals of limiting climate change. Manual assessment, data collection and processes represent a major 
obstacle to acceleration of the sustainability agenda.  
Currently, little is envisaged in terms of upgrading, standardising or requesting improved data disclosure 
from corporates. The legislation affecting the reporting of non -financial information does not cover a 
large part of the European business sector and may constitute a barrier to the objectives of accelerating 
market transition, as it does not make it possible to collect c omprehensive data on ESG criteria and 
associated performance.  
We therefore suggest for consideration the following possible measures:  
- The EU Commission could put in place or support infrastructures to collect periodically, with the

available relevant   information such as related to physical risk or, at least, identify the sources. 
A centralized database would facilitate building of ESG disclosures and the access to relevant 
and reliable data at the EU level. 
- The EU Commission could launch a project to identify the information gaps of relevant climate 
change mitigation and adaptation information that are not in the scope of the EU Non-Financial 
Reporting Directive (NFRD) with respect to the Taxonomy. 
- In terms of sustainability, transparency is expected. Therefore, open source information should 
be favoured. The publ ic sector (national banks, local authorities, utility companies, …) could 
publish their data (energy efficiency, air pollution, …) on a statistical basis, protecting the private 
information for individuals. The EU should open its databases that collect env ironmental 
reporting data and make those re-usable for finance providers. 
- Engagement and coordination between EU Commission’s DGS on the data availability and 
disclosures.

Other issues 
As stated in its “Inception impact assessment”, the Commission is taking the opportunity of this CRR 
review to address inconsistencies or mistakes in the existing rules. In this regard, the Commission has 
identified three areas for possible interventions and has included them in the present consultation.  
ABI would respectfully suggest that the Commission takes the opportunity of the legislative proposal to 
address also other aspects of the regulatory framework  where refinements are deemed appropriate . 
Indeed, in ABI’s view it is time to conclude the wave of reforms of prudential regulation and to achieve 
a stability of the framework. Hopefully,  the forthcoming CRR3/CRD6 will therefore represent the last 
occasion, for many years, to address shortcomings of the existing regulations.  
Reference is made to the following areas (even if further points might arise in the future): 
- Definition of default. The implementation of the harmonised definition of default is still burdensome 
for banks and customers. The revision of the CRR would be the chance to address some issues, especially 
regarding the application of the definition of default in respect to exposures to public sector entities. As 
highlighted in the recent past by ABI, such exposures deserve more flexibility, since delays in payments 
by public entities often occur for other reasons than  repayment difficulties (such as length of 
administrative procedures) and should therefore not trigger the definition of default. ABI would therefore 
recommend excluding exposures to public sector entities from the application of the materiality 
thresholds for past-due set out in Article 178 (1) (b) CRR .
 We consider important to take into account 
that in some EU Countries: 
a) insolvency of public entities is treated by peculiar provision. Even in the case of temporary financial 
difficulties or even distress, for such entities the regulatory framework provides special procedures 
that cannot end until each creditor has been satisfied. Consistently, the experience of many 
factoring companies which are specialized in purchasing public sector debts is that risk of losses is 
non-existent.  
b) any payment by public entities is subject to rigorous, articulate and strictly formalized administrative 
procedures. Such procedures respond to the need to assure transparency and traceability of any 
cash outflows from public entities, thus pursuing virtuous purposes of public interest protection. On 
the other hand, the practical effect of these procedures is that voluntary payments by the public 
entity is inhibited until the whole process is complete, resulting in a non-observance of the payment 
schedule. Any delay in payment is therefore consequence of lengthiness, frictions and 
organizational inefficiencies and by no means of concrete insolvency of the entity. 
- Minority interests. ABI would ask for some refinements of the framework with respect to the recognition 
of minority interests as consolidated CET1. A wider recognition of minority interests would entail positive 
effects, since, in general, the availability of capital backing assets, such as loans to households and 
enterprises, allows banks to enhance the support to the real economy -  and consequently growth and 
employment. More specifically, a wider eligibility of minority interests would also encourage parent 
companies to enlarge the capital base of subsidiaries. This could represent an incentive for strengthening 
subsidiaries’ capital therefore increasing financial stability. It should be also taken into account that the 
regulation should be, to the extent possible, neutral with respect to the organizational structure of 
groups. 
- “Danish Compromise”. Current Article 471 of CRR (as amended by CRR2) allows non -financial 
conglomerates not to deduct from CET1 their holdings in insurance undertakings over a 5 -years 
transition period. ABI recommends maintaining the exception set by CRR2 on a permanent basis. The 
rationale lies in the need to avoid measures penalis ing banks based on business models and strategic 
choices.

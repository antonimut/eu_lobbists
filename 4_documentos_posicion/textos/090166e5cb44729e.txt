1.1.7.1. New credit conversion factors (CCF) 
 
 
57) What are the costs and benefits of the new CCF introduced by the Basel III standards? 
In particular, how does the Basel III treatment of OBS items compare to the current 
treatment in terms of risk -sensitivity and impact on RWAs. Please provide relevant 
evidence to substantiate your views. 
 
In the actual CRR, the CCF which applies to Performance Guarantees is 20%.  
 
The Basel Committee’s position on Performance Guarantees has been that “the CCF 
expresses the likelihood of an off-balance sheet position to become an on-balance 
sheet exposure, i.e. it is not related to the riskiness of a counterparty which is 
expressed by the position’s probability of default.”  
 
In 2011, the Basel Committee considered some early data from the ICC and 
suggested that at that time the ICC Trade Register did not provide “sufficient 
analytical evidence for reducing the CCF in the risk-based approach below 20%”. 
 
In 2019, the ICC in conjunction with the Global Credit Data Consortium (GCD) 
organised a special collection of data covering most recent years from 12 of its 
largest member banks. This resulted in a large number of observations of 
performance guarantees issued, claimed, paid or extended and financial guarantees 
being analysed to determine CCF. To be clear, the transaction types covered were the 
same as the GCD facility types below, covering both financial and performance 
guarantees. 
 
Ref. Ares(2020)361627 - 20/01/2020
 
 
 
For performance guarantees, the level of claims is very low at 2%, and only 14% of 
these are paid out. This leaves an “ultimate drawing rate” of only 0.25% of issued 
performance guarantees being paid. This is higher than the default frequency 
observed in the ICC Trade Register Report of 2018, consistent with the scenario that 
not all guarantees paid are connected to defaulted borrowers and not all defaulted 
borrowers with guarantees issued have them called and paid. 
 
Based on the discussion of the special nature of contingent facilities in the previous 
section, we need to be careful when establishing exactly what method and what data 
to use for calculation of the historical LGD. Note that the LGD calculations are made 
using GCD standards with discount of cash flows at the risk free rate, see method 
notes to the 2019 Large Corporate LGD paper1.  
 
Firstly, to be clear, we are using the calculation method called by GCD as “LGD2” 
where the starting default outstanding amount is increased by any drawings which 
happen after default. This method is more appropriate for contingent facilities which 
often have 0 cash outstanding at date of default as all risk is in issued instruments 
which may or may not be drawn at a later date. The alternative method “LGD1”, 
which nets all post default cashflows, often results in incalculable LGD because the 
divisor of outstanding at default is zero. 
 
Secondly, we have chosen to cap the maximum LGD at 150% and floor it at 0%. There 
do exist real cases above and below these points, normally smaller size cases, due to 
legitimate reasons such as workout costs higher than recoveries or payment by the 
borrower of penalty fees and interest above the discount rate. 
 
Thirdly, we need to factor into our averages what we do with the cases where there 
is no drawing of the contingent instruments. One method is to exclude these cases as 
they have the same problem of incalculable LGD due to the divisor of outstanding at 
default being zero. A second method would be to include these cases as observations 
of 0% LGD.

The LGD2 results for most of the cash type facilities are well below the 20% average 
we have seen in our Large Corporate studies. Receivables Financing at around 21% is 
very similar to normal corporate financing, as might be expected, as the recovery of 
receivables is integral to most liquidation scenarios. The high level of LGD2 for 
Transactional Traded Finance is unexpected at 38%, however this is a mixed group of 
both cash and contingent lines, so only the drawn contingents are driving up the LGD 
here. 
 
The results for contingent and mixed cash and contingent facility types differ starkly 
dependent on whether the never drawn cases are included or not. For example, 
when examining only the 7.4% of Payment Guarantees and Standby L/Cs which ever 
become drawn, then the outcome is a very high 46.8% LGD, however when including 
the full 100% of these cases the LGD drops to around 3%. 
 
For contingent facilities this supports the arguments and data from the ICC Trade 
Register study. If the substantial number of undrawn cases are used to support a very 
low CCF rate, then a normal to high level of LGD results for the remaining cases. If 
these undrawn cases are included in the LGD calculation then exceptionally low LGD 
is observed. Clearly it would be methodologically unsound to use these undrawn 
cases to calculate both a low CCF and a low LGD at the same time. 
 
ICC data shows drawing rates for performance guarantees of less than 1%, for the 
total population of defaulted and non-defaulted borrowers. 
 
GCD data shows a higher drawing rate of 8% on the same facility types but only for 
defaulted borrowers. Clearly this indicates that there is some connection between 
the borrower’s default and the claim and drawing of commercial guarantees issued 
on their behalf. 
 
Both data sets support the case that a 20% CCF is acceptably conservative and might be 
used by the BCBS, considering its definition of a CCF. Hence, 20% should remain the CCF for 
Performance Guarantees in the CRR.

A) We consider that a revised CRR should not include any reference to Equivalent 
Countries for Trade Finance instruments, in particular for Letters of Credit.   
 
When a Corporates exports goods or services and questions the capacity of its 
customer to pay him, it can ask its customers to open a Letter of Credit through its 
local bank and then request its core bank to confirm the payment to be made by the 
local bank. Hence the core bank is taking a risk on the local bank.    
In the final version of Basel III, in the Standardized Approach, exposures to banks are 
weighted according to their rating.

It was agreed in 2017, that exposures to banks linked to movements of goods 
supported by LCs opened for 6 months or less can be dealt as exposures shorter than

In the EU, with the present CRR, other rules apply. Firstly, the EU published a list of 
countries whose supervisory and regulatory authorities are considered as equivalent 
to the ones of the EU; this list includes 21 jurisdictions (most but not all OECD 
countries). Secondly, if an exposure refers to a bank established in an Equivalent 
Country, risk weights are calculated according to the Article 120 of the CRR which 
refers to Financial Institutions. If the bank is not established in an Equivalent Country, 
then Risk Weights will be calculated according to Article 122 which refers to 
Corporates.

LCs are mostly opened by banks which are established outside of the OECD (non-
Equivalent Countries) and which have ratings often ranging at the “BBB” level or 
below. As a consequence, the confirming EU bank uses a risk-weight factor which can 
be 5-times lower than a non-EU bank. This risk-weight has to be priced by the bank 
and this can penalize an EU exporter vs a non-EU one. 
We urge the European Authorities to delete this reference to Equivalent Countries 
for Trade Finance as it does not exist in the last version of Basel III (d 424).

1.4. CREDIT RISK MITIGATION – IRBA 
 
 
109) In your view, which other aspects, if any, should be considered in the context of revising 
the CRM framework under the IRBA? Please specify and rank your answers from the most 
important to the least important aspect. 
 
In the paragraph 34 of the consultation paper on the Guidelines on CRM published in March 
2019, the EBA suggests that, in the presence of partial covers, cash flows coming from the 
guarantor should be allocated to the guaranteed part of the exposure. As regards the cash-
flows coming from the obligor, two options were considered  
 in Option 1, these cash-flows are allocated to the part of the exposure not covered 
by the unfunded credit protection, which means to the unguaranteed exposure;  
 in Option 2, these cash-flows are proportionally allocated to the outstanding amount 
of the guaranteed and unguaranteed exposures (of the bank) 
 
In the paragraph 409 of its Policy Advice on Credit Risk published in August 2019, the EBA 
suggests to adopt Option 1. However, neither Option 1 nor Option 2 reflect the way cash-
flows are normally allocated. 
 
As a reminder, in case of partial covers, the guarantor normally shares the risks on a pari-
passu basis with the bank. And, if an instalment is not paid by the original obligor, the 
guarantor pays for the percentage it agreed to cover at the beginning.  
 
Option 1 considers that all cash-flows coming from the obligor are allocated to the 
unsecured tranche and that all cash-flows coming from the guarantor are allocated to the 
secured tranche. Several consequences appear :  
1. If the obligor pays normally its first instalments, the unsecured portion will be fully 
amortized before any reduction of the secured portion, which means that the 
insurer is junior to the bank.  
2. It might appear on the last instalment date that the secured portion is not fully paid 
which means a default of the obligor although it indemnified the bank as scheduled.  
 
3. Finally, the amount of the commitment of the guarantor to indemnify is not always 
the amount of the secured tranche (and it can be lower, which questions the 
qualification of secured tranche).   
This Option 1 does not reflect the normal practices of partial guarantees. 
 
Option 2 considers that the cash-flows stemming from the guarantor are allocated to the 
secured tranche and that cash-flow stemming from the obligor are shared according to the 
remaining balances of the secured and the unsecured tranches of the bank’s loan. Several 
consequences appear : 
1. The commitment of the guarantor does not necessarily evolve as the amount of the 
secured tranche   
2. At the end of the repayment period, the secured tranche might not be fully repaid  
3. At the end of the repayment period, the final percentage of the unsecured tranche 
for the bank vs the total remaining debt of the obligor is not always the original 
percentage of the unsecured tranche, which means a breach of the pari-passu 
clause.  
This option does not consider that if the guarantor has to cover one instalment, he becomes 
a lender and is then entitled to participate in the allocation of cash-flows coming from the 
obligor. 
 
Hence, it might be appropriate to consider a third option which would reflect normal 
market practices with  
- Allocation of cash-flows stemming from the obligor to the unsecured portion in 
proportion with the percentage of uncovered debt.   
If the payment is made on an instalment date, this cash-flow is allocated on a-
prorata basis the unsecured tranche and the secured tranche of the bank 
If the payment is made on another date, which means that the obligor is covering 
sums which were due before and that the guarantor had to indemnify the bank, this 
cash-flow is allocated on a-prorata basis to the unsecured tranche of the bank and 
the debt assumed by the guarantor    
- Payments made by the guarantor, in case of a default of the obligor, in proportion to 
its rate of cover 
On the last instalment date, it appears that 
- There is no amount left in the secured tranche, which means that the bank was 
covered as scheduled by the insurer. 
- The percentage of the unsecured tranche versus the total remaining debt of the 
obligor (versus the bank and the insurer) is the original percentage of uncovered 
debt, which means that the pari-passu applied.  
 
To illustrate these 3 options, let’s consider a loan of 100 with 5 consecutive instalments of 
20 in principal and a guarantee of 90%. For any reason, the obligor pays the first instalment 
in full, half of the third one and half of the fifth one; it also makes a payment of 5 between 
the third and the fourth instalments. Hence it reimburses 45 out of 100 and still ows 55 at 
the day of the last instalment. 
 
The final LGD on the unsecured tranche goes from 0% (Option 1) or 10% (Option 2) to 55% 
(Option 3). The final LGD on the secured tranche goes from 0% (Option 2 or 3) to 1% (Option 
1).

General statement 
The European AVM Alliance (EAA) is a European federation consisting of leading 
providers of Automated Valuation Models (AVMs). The EAA's main aim is to bring 
about a consistent approach to automated valuations for residential real estate 
enabling the mortgage lending, investor, rating and regulatory communities to 
operate in a more transparent and effective way. In that vein the EAA has published 
the first ever “Standards for statistical valuation methods for residential property in 
Europe” in September 2017. 
Against this background our response to the Commission’s consultation focusses 
entirely on the revisions to the Standardised Approach to Credit Risk (SA-CR) with 
regard to the real estate exposure class, specifically on the calculation of the loan-to-
value (LTV) ratio, where we strongly recommend the use of the current value which is 
essential to measure credit risk exposures accurately.

1.1.6.4. Prudently conservative valuation criteria 
 
Question 41  
Views are sought on the costs and benefits of the valuation criteria provided by the Basel 
III standards. In particular, how does this approach compare with the current 
approaches available under the CRR (MV and MLV) in terms of simplicity, comparability, 
risk-sensitivity, impact on RWAs and operational burden? Please provide relevant 
evidence to substantiate your views.  
 
EAA Response 
Market Value 
The EAA holds that from a risk-based point of view the market value (MV) should 
always be determined. In the CRR and other existing EU legislation the MV is a defined 
and well-understood concept, whereas that of a ‘prudent value’ based on some 
general valuation criteria as provided for in paragraph 62 is not.  
One of the resulting concerns is that a ‘prudent value’ by itself cannot be 
benchmarked; benchmarks and unambiguous objective assessments of valuations 
only exist for MVs. Therefore, in order to ensure that the ‘prudent value’, which 
cannot be measured objectively, does not (as required in paragraph 62) exceed the 
MV, which can be measured objectively, the latter must be determined using 
appropriate valuation methods.

1.1.6.5. (Re-)valuation: value at origination vs. current value 
 
Question 45  
Views are sought on the costs and benefits of capping the property value at loan 
origination. In particular, how does the approach provided by the final Basel III 
standards compare with the current approach of the CRR in terms of possible cyclical 
effects on RWs, risk-sensitivity, impact on RWAs and operational burden? Please provide 
relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
 
EAA Response 
Loan-to-value (LTV) ratio as principle risk driver 
The EAA welcomes the fact that the revisions to the SA-CR in the final Basel III 
standards follow the BCBS’s own assessment, expressed on its second consultative 
document on the SA-CR of 15 December 2015, that ‘one of the key weaknesses of the 
current SA is the lack of granularity and risk sensitivity in a number of exposure 
classes’, and introduce and cement the LTV ratio as the main and principal risk 
driver for weighting purposes. 
 
Current value in LTV ratio essential to measure credit risk exposures accurately 
While the EAA fully agrees that the LTV ratio is the best indicator for setting risk 
weights in general and therefore supports this revision proposed by the BSBC, the 
EAA does not agree with the approach provided by the final Basel III standards of 
capping the property value at loan origination as provided for in paragraph 62.1 
In contrast, the current approach of the CRR in Article 208, which requires frequent 
monitoring, reviewing and re-valuation of residential property, provides a much 
higher level of prudency and risk assessment, taking into account the cyclical 
effects on RWs and the impact on RWAs, and is thus to be preferred. In order to add 
to prudency even further, the EAA would recommend to increase the frequency of

the monitoring of residential property from every 3 years to every 1 year at a

Justification  
One of the major costs of capping the property value at origination as proposed by the 
final Basel III standards, would be the lack of comparability, both from mortgage to 
mortgage and from lender to lender. This is due to the fact that the point of 
origination is a different point in the cycle for each mortgage and hence each property 
valuation would be capped at a different point in the cycle. 
Compared with the current approach of the CRR this approach suggested in the Basel 
III standards would have pro-cyclical effects on RWs, on risk-sensitivity on impact on 
RWAs and operational burden, and should therefore be avoided.  
The lack of comparability would also mean that some mortgages are treated more or 
less prudently than others and therefore are more and less lucrative to lenders. As a 
result, lending at the top of the market would hold comparatively less RWA and 
would be more lucrative for lenders which is the opposite of what the Basel 
rules intend to achieve.  
In addition, there is a risk that lending that has been on the books for a long time, and 
holds a property value many years out of date and therefore perhaps significantly 
below the current market value, is less lucrative for a lender which 
disincentives lenders to keep customers on their books.  
For accurate risk management and capital allocation, and prudence with regard to the 
LTV ratio it is therefore of fundamental importance to monitor the value of the 
property frequently and to use – except in specific circumstances – the current value 
rather than keeping the value measured at origination, precisely in order to avoid the 
lack of risk sensitivity that the current SA-CR shows, and independent on whether the 
current value is higher or lower than the original value.  
Credit institutions should therefore have at least the choice whether to use the 
current rather than the originally measured value, since the use of current value in the 
LTV ratio is a much more accurate measure of the current risk and thus allows for a 
more prudent and sensitive management of that risk and capital allocation by the 
banks. In particular:

• Over time the LTV ratio evolves, not only due to the repayment of the loan, but 
also – and primarily so – due to house price movements, both up or down. To 
reflect this fluctuation in property values the setting and annual monitoring of 
risk weights should always be based on the current LTV ratios as these 
accurately mirror the current risk in the housing market. For instance: If house 
prices go down, the lenders face a large problem in a foreclosure sale and the 
effect of a slump of value might be underestimated or even pass unnoticed if 
the bank relies on the original value. This should be addressed early in the 
bank's books and therefore the current value (i.e. the market value – see 
below) is necessary. 
• The general exceptions for basing the LTV ratio on the property value at 
origination proposed by the final Basel III standards (i.e. if required by a 
national supervisor or in case of an extraordinary, idiosyncratic event), are 
nowhere near enough and may not have the intended impact. For one thing, 
they only consider a downward revision of the value of the property. Second, 
house price fluctuations – even extraordinary ones – do not always occur 
nationwide and can differ greatly by geographic region or even locally. These 
geographical and local differences are very difficult to perceive and monitor for  
a national supervising authority and they will largely go unnoticed by house 
price indices, which in most jurisdictions typically apply a national or regional 
average. 
• While some regions might show a positive market price trend, other regions 
can see prices declining at the same time. Hence, the value of a bank’s property 
portfolio has to be taken into account on an individual property basis wherever 
possible (e.g. for residential properties), and this can only be achieved by 
frequently monitoring the individual property values with appropriate, 
geographically granular, accurate and reliable valuation methods that take into 
consideration individual property characteristics. 
• Home owners should be incentivised to modernise their property, e.g. to 
increase its energy efficiency and thus reducing its negative impact on climate 
change. Updating the LTV ratio with the increased value of a modernised 
property works as such an incentive, while keeping the value of a modernised 
property at origination may have the opposite effect.   
Furthermore, the EAA is concerned about the fact that the new rules seem to restrict 
any possible revision of the original value in the LTV ratio to a downward direction 
only. If the intention of the new rules is to assess credit risk more accurately then 
banks must be allowed to use the current value – independently of the requirements 
of a national supervisor or extraordinary events – also and in particular if the current 
value is higher than the original value, since this in turn would identify and 
appropriately quantify a decrease of risk for the bank.

Issues with Accounting Standards (IFRS 9) 
Finally, the principle to keep the value at origination and only allow a downward 
revision displayed in these new rules seems to run contrary to a general regulatory 
move in the market towards basing accounting standards and balance sheet 
reporting obligations on the current value of assets, be it residential property or 
other assets, such as stocks. This is the case, e.g., in the new IFRS 9 rules where for 
financial risk assessment purposes an asset such as a lease has to be measured at fair 
value, rather than its original value.  
 
Question 47 
In your view, which other aspects, if any, should be considered in the context of revising 
the requirement for re-valuation of RE collateral? Please elaborate and provide relevant 
evidence to substantiate your views.  
EAA Response 
Use of Advanced Statistical Methods for Re-valuation 
The ever-accelerating progress in information technology, in particular in the fields of 
AI and machine learning, and the development of large property and transaction 
databases have led to the development and increased accuracy of advanced statistical 
models for property valuations. Restrictions on the use of current value would reduce 
the necessity for accurate and effective (both time-wise and cost-wise) valuation 
solutions and thus hamper the development in this market and the overall progress of 
the valuation market. Inhibiting such developments and progress would seriously 
reduce the availability of tool for prudent risk management. 
The EAA therefore recommends to the Commission, when revising the requirements 
for re-valuation of RE collateral, to include the use of advanced statistical models for 
re-valuation of residential real estate, as long as these models adhere to 
internationally recognised standards3 and are: 
• property-specific; 
• valid and accurate, and subject to robust back-testing; 
• based on a sufficiently large and representative sample; 
• based on up-to-date data of high quality; and 
• including a confidence measure as output with each valuation result, thus 
providing a reliable indication of accuracy at a property-by-property level. 
The use of advanced statistical models for re-valuation which are used in several EU 
jurisdictions and which adhere to the above criteria demonstrably contribute to 
better risk management, while avoiding additional cost pressure.

Question 2.1 If yes, what would be their prudential rationale?

The volume of covered bonds (Pfandbriefe) issued in Germany that are not rated by an external 
rating agency is substantial. For these unrated Pfandbriefe, the risk weight is derived from the 
rating of the issuing institution, in accordance with the revised Basel III standards. However, 
many institutions that issue Pfandbriefe do not have an external rating.  
In the EU exposures to unrated institutions are currently subject to the country of 
incorporation principle (Article 121 CRR). Institution exposures are assigned a risk weight in 
accordance with the credit quality step to which exposures to the central government of the 
jurisdiction in which the bank is incorporated are assigned. The risk weight of unrated German 
banks is currently 20%. 
The revised standardised approach for credit risk includes a new procedure for calculating the 
risk weight of exposures to institutions with no external rating: the standardised credit risk 
assessment approach (SCRA). Where all grade A criteria are met, a risk weight of 40% may be 
applied. For grade A, application of a risk weight of 30% is also possible, provided that the bank 
has a CET1 ratio of 14% or higher and a leverage ratio of 5% or higher. Unfortunately, most 
banks do not meet these criteria, so that the reduced risk weight will rarely be applied. 
Consequently, the risk weight for exposures to unrated German banks will usually double from 
currently 20% to 40%, which would not be appropriate to the risk.  
4 
For covered bonds such as Pfandbriefe, this also meant, from a risk standpoint, that there was 
an unjustified increase in the risk weight for the Pfandbrief derived from the risk weight of the 
issuing institution, equivalent to a doubling of the current rate of 10% to 20% in future. 
The country of incorporation principle currently applied should therefore be retained in the 
EU.

The proposed requirement to carefully assess external ratings, on top of internal bank analysis

external ratings with a separate internal credit analysis for each individual borrower in the form

In general: 
It should be noted that the institutions have the option of using one of the two approaches. 
Likewise, in order to ensure uniform application across all member states, an interpretation-

Commercial: 
Calibration of the risk weights for commercial real estate finance where repayment of a loan is 
either dependent on or not dependent on cash flows from the collateral is not granular enough 
in the standardised approach for credit risk. The economically motivated distribution of risk is 
not nearly appropriate enough particularly in the low LTV buckets. For example, finance with an 
LTV ratio of ≤ 50% is assigned the same risk weights as finance with an LTV ratio of 60% (risk 
weight of 60% where independent of cash flows and 70% where dependent on cash flows). The 
specified risk weights need to be made more risk-appropriate. 
Risk weights should therefore be fixed within a granular LTV bucket structure to reflect the 
specific risk profile of different LTV ratios and ensure a consistent risk-appropriate increase in 
risk weights. In the case of commercial real estate finance where repayment of a loan is not 
dependent on cash flows from collateral, further differentiation could be achieved by, for 
example, an additional LTV ≤ 50% bucket with an appropriate risk weight of 50%. Like the whole 
loan approach, the loan splitting approach would also have to be adjusted. This could, for 
example, be achieved by lowering the risk weight also to 50% for the portion of finance that is 
regarded as secured (LTV ratio ≤ 55%). 
Low, risk-appropriate risk weights in the low LTV range are suitable, as low actual loss rates 
have been demonstrated for Germany through the hard test since 1988. 
We welcome it that the hard test for commercial real estate exposures may be used as a 
substitute for the criterion that repayment of a loan should not be dependent on cash flows 
from real estate collateral. As European banks are already required by the CRR to include in 
COREP reporting loss data on real estate finance for all countries in which they provide loans, 
the necessary hard test data is available for all countries. 
Residential: 
We believe it would be risk-appropriate if the criterion whereby repayment of a loan should not 
be dependent on cash flows from real estate collateral were substituted in European 
implementation by the hard test for residential real estate exposures as well. This also complies 
with the European Banking Authority (EBA) Recommendation (CR-SA 24), as promulgated in its 
policy advice report of 5 August 2019. As indicated in footnote 46 to paragraph 67, with the 
retention of the hard test, the criterion of cash flow dependence is defined in greater detail. 
This approach is justified since lower risk weights are appropriate for Europe compared, for 
example, with America. For example, it is also clear from the March 2019 Basel III Monitoring 
Report that IRBA institutions in America report average risk weights of 22%, whereas in Europe 
values of only 13% are calculated. The risk weights determined using the standardised approach 
are therefore a better fit for American real estate financing than for European real estate 
financing.

Question 36. What would justify implementing both approaches in parallel from a risk 
perspective? 
If both approaches were to be implemented and made available on discretionary basis, how would 
comparability across institutions be ensured and how would regulatory arbitrage as well as undue 
complexity be prevented in this case? 
Europe’s property markets are not homogeneous but differ in various respects. In Germany, for 
example, it is standard market practice for institutions to agree an expanded collateral clause 
with borrowers. This clause means that the collateral covers all existing, future and conditional 
claims arising from the entire business relationship between the bank and the borrower. Banks 
in Germany therefore usually apply the loan splitting approach to determine the risk weight for 
loan exposures. In other member states, on the other hand, this may not be necessary because 
of the lending culture prevailing there. 
Given these differences, we believe that banks should be permitted to decide which approach 
to use to allow as smooth implementation of the rules as possible. We believe it would be 
appropriate if each bank were to adopt either of the two approaches once only and irrevocably 
for its real estate finance portfolio. 
By choosing the suitable approach, the institution concerned can reduce the individual effort 
and expense involved in applying the new approaches at the institution. Because the two 
approaches to choose from have been similarly calibrated, they will lead to comparable risk 
weights from the standpoint of the overall real estate portfolio. Based on an analysis of the data 
on residential real estate portfolios of [individual] member institutions, the unweighted average 
of the risk weights under the loan splitting approach deviates from the unweighted average 
under the whole loan approach by [●] percentage point(s), while the median risk weight under 
the loan splitting approach deviates from the median risk weight under the whole loan 
approach by [●] percentage point(s). No significant deviations are expected. No incentives are 
provided for regulatory arbitrage, especially as this would mean uniform exercise of the option 
for the entire institution. This option does not entail an unjustifiably high degree of complexity, 
either for regulators or for other stakeholders. Another supporting factor is that, once the 
institution has chosen an approach, it will remain unchanged over time and the approach 
chosen should be clear from the information disclosed.

Question 37.1 If no, please elaborate on your response to question 37. 
To determine whether real estate financing is materially dependent on cash flows generated by 
the property serving as collateral, it is necessary to determine the volume of cash flows 
generated by the real estate being financed (CFRE) and the total volume of cash flows available 
to the borrower (CFTotal). If CFRE is equal to more than 50% of CFTotal, this is a case of cash-flow-
dependent real estate financing.  
When calculating CFRE, only cash flows generated by the real estate being financed should be 
considered, and not also the cash flows the borrower generates from other properties that are 
not being financed by the loan. In contrast, to calculate CF
Total, all of the borrower’s cash flows 
should be included. 
A different approach would unduly overstate the risk of the underlying real estate financing, 
because the cash flows from the non-financed properties in the real estate portfolio are 
unrelated to the cash flows from the real estate financing. Therefore, the cash flows from the 
real estate portfolio reduce the dependence on the real estate being financed. These 
diversification effects reduce risk, rather than increasing it.

1.1.6.3. Eligibility of property under construction 
Question 39. What are your views on the costs and benefits of implementing the preferential 
treatment for certain properties under construction as provided by the Basel III standards? 
Please provide relevant evidence supporting your view. 
The requirements under Basel III standards to grant a preferential risk weight only to financing 
for residential properties under construction for which 
 the borrower is a natural person, 
 the residential building contains a maximum of four residential housing units and 
 the borrower’s main place of residence must be in the building under construction, 
will restrict the preferential treatment of financing for residential properties to an excessive 
degree. Instead, the previous requirements laid out in Article 125(1)(a) CRR should be retained. 
That means: no restriction to natural persons, no limit to a maximum of four residential housing 
units, option of letting the property instead of using it oneself (main place of residence). This 
would ensure that the very tight housing situation in many metropolitan areas would not be 
aggravated even further. 
As for the main residence criterion, the institution likewise has no influence over whether the 
borrower has to relocate his/her main place of residence – for work-related reasons, for 
example. In this case, the institution would have to reclassify the financing. This would result in 
a significantly higher risk weight, although the financing risk would not necessarily change 
because of the change in the main place of residence. In addition, the higher risk weight would 
no longer be reflected in the agreed lending rate, which would further reduce the institution’s 
profitability. 
Further, the change in requirements would cause banks to incur substantial costs to make the 
adjustments. Because housing loans (especially in this environment of persistently low interest 
rates) usually have very long maturities, the vast majority of today’s portfolio business would be 
affected as well.

1.1.6.4. Prudently conservative valuation criteria 
Question 41. Views are sought on the costs and benefits of the valuation criteria provided by the 
Basel III standards. 
In particular, how does this approach compare with the current approaches available under the 
CRR (MV and MLV) in terms of simplicity, comparability, risk-sensitivity, impact on RWAs and 
operational burden? 
Please provide relevant evidence supporting your view. 
The terms “market value” and “mortgage lending value” currently enshrined in the CRR are 
concepts of value that have been in use and standardised for many years at the international, 
European and domestic levels. For the domestic real estate markets, extensive data are 
12 
available based on these terms that serve as the basis for valuation. Certifications at 
international and domestic level guarantee that qualified valuers reliably apply the individual 
valuation standards. 
Introducing a new concept of value based on the remarks in the finalised Basel III standards 
would lead to massive changes in valuation practice. First, it would require an unambiguous 
definition of value that would have to be supported by transparent valuation standards that 
could be applied in all the European domestic real estate markets. In addition, the necessary 
data would have to be generated in the European real estate markets – and ideally on a 
retroactive basis over a longer period of time. Valuers would have to be trained in the new 
methodology and it would be necessary to gain practical experience. In the area of private 
housing loans, in particular, the valuation process involves a considerable level of IT support. 
This would require extensive adjustments. In addition, these values would no longer be 
comparable with the “old values” in the bank portfolios. 
Introducing a new definition of value and a new valuation method would therefore entail 
substantial and tedious changes to bank processes and substantial costs for the banking 
industry. In addition to banks’ lack of experience using the new valuation method, in our view 
another big problem is that initially, the valid foundation of data essential for valuations would 
be lacking or at least incomplete. 
The option to choose between market value and mortgage lending value currently enshrined in 
the CRR should be retained in order to ensure that valuations will be based on tried and proven 
standards, implemented by qualified valuers and based on a data foundation built up over many 
years.

1.1.6.5. (Re-)valuation: value at origination vs. current value 
Question 45. Views are sought on the costs and benefits of capping the property value at loan 
origination. 
In particular, how does the approach provided by the final Basel III standards compare with the 
current approach of the CRR in terms of possible cyclical effects on RWs, risk-sensitivity, impact 
on RWAs and operational burden? 
Please provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
Article 208(3) CRR contains extensive requirements for monitoring and reviewing property 
values. In addition, these regulations today already require credit institutions to have clearly 
regulated procedures that describe the specific effects of changes in value on real estate 
collateral. With this background in mind, there is no discernible advantage to an upper limit, 
defined as “value at origination”. However, after the fixed-interest period has expired, this rule 
provides a very clear incentive for the borrower to switch banks if real estate values increase. In 
such cases, therefore, a bank should be able to refer to the current real estate value so that it 
will have the same conditions as a competitor wishing to finance this collateral for the first time. 
From a competitive standpoint, any “value at origination” upper limit must be rejected. 
Furthermore, in its report (policy advice on the Basel III reforms: credit risk, paragraphs 189-
190), the EBA rightly determined that on the date when the loan is granted, an upper limit could 
have adverse effects in terms of providing incentives for shorter loan maturities. Moreover, the 
EBA rightly notes that fixing the “value at origination” for long-term mortgage loans presumably 
will not adequately reflect the banks’ risks over the loan term. By contrast, the existing CRR 
provisions – under which the “value at origination” can also be exceeded – would better reflect 
the actual credit risks.

1.1.6.6. Land acquisition, development and construction (ADC) exposures – general treatment 
Question 48. What are your views on the costs and benefits of replacing the existing treatment of 
‘speculative immovable property financing’ with the treatment of ADC exposures as provided by 
the Basel III standards? 
Depending on the individual financing arrangement, the new provisions mainly lead to higher 
risk weights than currently required under the CRR. 
For properties in which land acquisition, development and construction (ADC) are financed by 
loans to companies and special purpose vehicles, the new Basel III standards call for a risk 
weight of 150%. 
Pursuant to CRR requirements, no allocation is made to the “speculative immovable property 
financing” exposure class with the same 150% risk weight if the properties will be owner-
occupied or let. Financing for future owner-occupied or let residential property loans can be 
assigned a preferential risk weight of 35%. Future owner-occupied or let commercial property 
financing must be assigned a risk weight of 100% (if the risk weights for the borrower are lower, 
they can be applied). In both of these cases, the new Basel requirements lead to unreasonably 
higher risk weights (residential: 329%; commercial: at least 50%). Even in cases where the lower 
Basel ADC risk weight for housing loans of 100% can be used (in the case of pre-letting or a 
substantial equity stake), this would still be 186% above the current CRR risk weight. Therefore, 
the “ADC financing” exposure class should be limited to financing in which there is an intention 
to sell at a profit, similar to the CRR exposure class “speculative immovable property financing”.
Currently, real estate financing in which there is an intention to sell at a profit can only be 
allocated to the “speculative immovable property financing” exposure class with a risk weight of 
150% if no irrevocable sales agreements yet exist. In this case alone, there is no difference 
between the new Basel risk weights and the current CRR risk weights. 
15 
If irrevocable sales agreements have been signed, the CRR risk weight in the real estate 
exposure class is 100%. (If the borrower’s risk weights are lower, they can be applied.) However, 
the new Basel risk weight of 150% is significantly higher. It is only for residential property 
financing that the Basel risk weight can be reduced to 100% as well. This should be expanded to 
include commercial property financing as well, so that risk weights can be used that are 
comparable to the CRR risk weights. 
In order to achieve the aforementioned reduction in the future risk weight from 150% to 100%, 
certain requirements (pre-sales, pre-letting or a substantial equity stake) have to be met. 
However, these requirements should apply only to cash-flow-dependent ADC financing within 
the meaning of footnote 48 to paragraph 73 of the Basel III standards. In addition, the 
requirements for cash-flow-dependent financing should adequately reflect the risk. Within the 
meaning of footnote 52 to paragraph 75 of the Basel III standards, for pre-sales, an advance 
payment for a percentage of completion of 30%, and for pre-letting, a rate of 30% of the space 
earmarked for letting or, alternatively, in view of the borrower’s substantial equity stake, a 10% 
percentage of completion amount would be appropriate from a risk standpoint. 
For cash-flow-independent ADC financing, reference should be made to the risk weights for 
the “corporates” exposure class. This will ensure that the agreement for the property to serve 
as collateral for the loan continues in force and there is no waiver of this collateral, so that any 
lower risk weight can be used in accordance with the “corporates” exposure class.

1.2. Internal ratings based approaches (IRBA) 
1.2.1. Reduction of the scope of internal modelling 
Question 62. What are your views on the costs and benefits of reducing the scope of internal 
modelling as described above? 
In particular, how would this reform impact the robustness and levels of RWAs for the affected 
portfolios? 
Please provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
The Basel III standards disallow the use of the advanced IRBA (AIRBA) for exposures to large 
corporates and institutions. 
Financing that no longer qualifies for the AIRBA but instead must be handled in accordance 
with the new Basel III standards under the foundation IRBA (FIRBA) will have significantly higher 
risk weights than currently under AIRBA pursuant to the CRR. Based on an analysis of the data 
from [individual] member institutions, the unweighted average of RWAs for exposures to large 
corporates and institutions increases by [●]%. 
We believe it is neither necessary nor in any way sensible in Europe to reduce the scope of the 
AIRBA. The European approach of reducing undesired RWA variability by targeting the 
underlying causes is both efficient and effective. This approach aimed at reducing undue 
variability in the results delivered by models essentially comprises three elements: 
 IRBA repair: EBA work on IRBA-related standardisation through regulatory technical 
standards (RTSs) and guidelines (GLs), e.g. on the definition of default, on PD and LGD 
parameter estimation along with uniform treatment of model uncertainties by way of 
margin of conservatism (MoC) requirements, downturn add-ons, comprehensive new 
acceptance criteria for approval to use the IRBA, 
 EBA benchmarking: annual internal model benchmarking in accordance with CRD IV 
Article 78, 
 ECB banking supervisors’ exercise of targeted review of internal models (TRIM) 2017-
2019. 
We therefore believe that the Basel Committee’s envisaged restrictions on using the AIRBA 
should not be introduced in the EU. 
It is undoubtedly challenging to reliably estimate LGDs for exposures in low-default portfolios. 
Since comparatively few defaults are recorded in these portfolios, there is little data available 
for the statistical analysis on which estimates are based. Despite the scarcity of available data, 
we take the view that reliable internal LGDs can still be estimated for low-default portfolios. 
A distinction needs to be made between reliability in individual cases and mean reliability. In 
individual cases, the loss in the event of default cannot be expected to occur exactly as 
predicted. When an individual borrower defaults, the proceeds from restructuring, resolution 
or the sale of assets depend on a number of factors that are difficult to predict before the 
18 
default even in high-default portfolios (exception: collateral). When it comes to mean values, by 
contrast, LGD can also be reliably estimated for low-default portfolios. Even in segments with 
very few data points, it is possible to derive a mean value of realised loss rates which will 
subsequently prove robust even when compared with recent loss experience. From a statistical 
methodological point of view, this is because the mean value can be reliably identified even 
when sample sizes are small. In low-default portfolios, too, these mean value estimates are 
further differentiated by risk drivers of the loss rate (collateral, seniority, type of debtor). As a 
result, sound predictive power can be achieved even with a small number of data points. 
The reliability of LGD estimation is further supported by measures over and above the mere 
statistical analysis of a bank’s own loss data: 
 Comprehensive data quality assurance to ensure the predictive value of the bank’s own 
historical data with respect to future defaults (representativeness), as well as individual 
analysis of historical losses and of their different mechanisms and realisation channels 
following default.  
 Additional plausibility checks with evaluation by specialists from the restructuring units, 
particularly regarding effects that it has not (yet) been possible to observe in the data.  
 Safety margins to take adequate account estimation uncertainty. 
 Extension of the data basis through the use of pool data from companies such as Global 
Credit Data or CredaRate, which are maintained and constantly expanded specifically 
for the purpose of LGD estimation. 
In addition to estimation methods for deriving mean values, collateral can also be taken into 
account as a major transaction-specific risk driver of the loss rate in low-default portfolios. The 
small number of realised losses notwithstanding, the stability of the collateral’s value can be 
reliably demonstrated using alternative data (such as market values). This gives these LGD 
estimates more predictive value for individual cases.

1.2.2. PD – increase of the input floor 
Question 65. Views are sought on the costs and benefits of increasing the PD input floor 
to 0.05%. 
In particular, how does the increased floor compare with the current floor in terms of achieving 
the aim of decreased RWA variability? What is the impact of this change on RWA levels? 
Please provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
Input floors envisaged in the Basel III finalisation package will have adverse effects. Input floors 
for PDs make it considerably more difficult to analyse and manage portfolios. Differentiation 
between borrowers/risk positions below the floors will be severely impaired. The “equalisation” 
of borrowers and risk positions will, in addition, lead to less risk analysis (in the rating process, 
for example), which cannot possibly be the desired objective. 
 
Question 66. In your view, how does the increased floor compare with the current floor in terms 
of achieving the aim of increased conservatism? 
Would you consider a floor that implicitly assumes that a default occurs once every 2000 years to 
be sufficiently prudent? 
Please explain. 
The CRR specifies an input floor of 0.03% for the probability of default (PD) for the exposure 
classes “corporates”, “institutions” and “retail business”. The new Basel III standards increase 
the input floor by 67% to 0.05%. In practice, relatively few borrowers are assigned a PD of less 
than 0.05%. 
However, it can be shown that there are specific types of borrowers whose internally calculated 
PD is below the input floor. The models on which this estimate is based are carefully reviewed 
by the regulators (e.g., acceptance test, TRIM) using numerous specifications (e.g., EBA 
guidelines) and add-ons may be applied if the estimates are uncertain (margin of conservatism). 
Increasing or introducing the input floor would mean that the risk sensitivity of such borrowers 
would no longer be reflected in a differentiated capital requirement. In this segment, incurring 
20 
higher risks would no longer be associated with an increase in capital requirements. Incentives 
to enter into less risky business, which is associated with lower margins, also decrease. 
In the question, the input floor of 0.05% is interpreted as one loan default every 2,000 years. 
This is from the standpoint of exposure to a single borrower. At the same time, however, it 
means that one of every 2,000 loans will default within one year. In our view, this order of 
magnitude inherent in the PD input floor of 0.05% is too conservative.

estimation to achieve a minimum level of conservatism and/or reduce RWA variability? 
Please provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
Various measures have already been implemented or will shortly be implemented in the EU that 
will result in a sufficiently conservative estimate of the PD and which will lead to an extensive 
reduction in the unexplainable variability in RWAs: 
 IRBA repair: EBA work on IRBA-related standardisation through regulatory technical 
standards (RTSs) and guidelines (GLs), e.g. on the definition of default, on PD and LGD 
parameter estimation along with uniform treatment of model uncertainties by way of 
margin of conservatism (MoC) requirements, downturn add-ons, comprehensive new 
acceptance criteria for approval to use the IRBA, 
 EBA benchmarking: annual internal model benchmarking in accordance with CRD IV 
Article 78, 
 TRIM ECB banking supervisors’ exercise of targeted review of internal models 2017-
2019. 
Currently there is no expectation that additional measures might still be required. Rather, after 
the new requirements are fully in force, it will be necessary to review where such measures 
exceed the desired target and, for example, promote herd behaviour by standardising internal 
procedures and undermine competition for best practices. 
 
Question 68. In your view, which other aspects, if any, should be considered in the context of 
revising the PD input floor? 
Please provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
The PD input floor reflects mistrust towards the quality of internal models. However, this is 
unjustified in the case of Europe due to the measures initiated (EBA’s IRBA repair, EBA’s 
supervisory benchmarking, ECB’s TRIM). The advantages of a conservative PD are already being 
reaped through the measures initiated. The disadvantages of the PD input floor (reduced risk 
sensitivity of capital requirements, negative incentives for low-risk transactions) would 
therefore not be offset by any further advantages.

1.2.3. LGD – input floors under AIRBA 
Question 69. Views are sought on the costs and benefits of exposure-level LGD input floors. 
In particular, how do the floors compare with the current treatment in terms of achieving the aims 
of conservatism and RWA variability? 
What is the impact of this change on RWAs? 
Please provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
Currently, the CRR does not provide for any LGD input floors at individual exposure level. Only 
for retail real estate financing is there a requirement to comply with an LGD input floor of 10% 
(residential properties) or 15% (commercial properties) at portfolio level. In contrast, the new 
Basel III standards introduce an LGD input floor at individual transaction level: for exposures 
secured by real estate, the LGD input floor is 10%. Here, it should be noted that the secured 
portion of the exposure is limited to 60% of the property value, as an additional haircut of 40% 
must be applied to the property value. An LGD input floor of 25% is applied to the exposure 
amount exceeding 60% of the property value. 
The introduction of LGD input floors is the main driver behind the increase in risk weights in 
AIRBA portfolios, which can remain in the AIRBA under the new Basel III standards. Based on an 
analysis of the data from [individual] member institutions, the unweighted average of RWAs 
increases by [●]% and the median by [●]%. 
It is notable that the new LGD input floor leads to extreme increases in the risk weight, 
especially where collateralisation is particularly good (with an LTV of < 60%). For example, due 
exclusively to the introduction of the LGD input floor of 10% at individual loan level, the risk 
weight increases from 3% under CRR (with a probability of default [PD] of 1.7%, maturity of 2.5 
years and an LTV < 60%) to 24% under the new Basel III standards. With increasing LTVs, i.e., 
with increasing risk, the risk weights under CRR and the new Basel III standards quickly 
converge and in this example each amount to 35% with an LTV of 73%. From an LTV of > 73%, 
the risk weights under the new Basel III standards are slightly lower than today’s risk weights 
under the CRR.  
As a result, financing low risk will be in a significantly worse position due to the new 
requirements, whereas financing with higher risk will be in a better position in some cases. 
Introducing the LGD input floors at individual exposure level will merely lead to a particularly 
conservative treatment of low-risk transactions. Risk-adverse Pfandbrief banks therefore 
unjustifiably receive significantly higher capital requirements.  
With the LGD input floors, the Basel Committee is striving to limit the unexplainable variability in 
RWAs. The LGD input floors, however, inherently affect only particularly low-risk business. 
Therefore, the LGD input floors not only limit any existing unexplainable variability, but also 
accept a capital requirement that does not appropriately reflect risk. The course already 
adopted in the EU for limiting the unexplainable variability in RWAs is more effective. For 
22 
example, various measures have been implemented or will shortly be implemented that will 
result in a sufficiently conservative estimate of the LGD and which will lead to an extensive 
reduction in the unexplainable variability in RWAs: 
 IRBA repair: EBA work on IRBA-related standardisation through regulatory technical 
standards (RTSs) and guidelines (GLs), e.g. on the definition of default, on PD and LGD 
parameter estimation along with uniform treatment of model uncertainties by way of 
margin of conservatism (MoC) requirements, downturn add-ons, comprehensive new 
acceptance criteria for approval to use the IRBA, 
 EBA benchmarking: annual internal model benchmarking in accordance with CRD IV 
Article 78, 
 TRIM ECB banking supervisors’ exercise of targeted review of internal models 2017-
2019.  
Specifying LGD input floors also leads to significantly higher complexity in modelling, data 
storage and processing. The separate LGD input floor parameters for individual types of 
collateral and the unsecured portion of the exposure hugely increase institutions’ expenses. 
Under the CRR, such a separation was not necessary for every single exposure. Rather, to date, 
collateral has been included directly in the LGD for the entire exposure amount. These new 
allocation requirements for determining the LGD input floors would lead to a wave of re-
modelling.  
All of these new models would have to be reviewed and accepted by the competent authorities. 
In addition, there would be massive increases in IT expenses for data storage and processing, as 
data would have to be provided at the granular level of the various types of collateral, instead of 
at exposure level. On the one hand, this increases the complexity of preliminary processing. On 
the other hand, there will be a huge increase in storage costs, and processing times for 
preparing reports will also increase. This must be classified as a conflict of objectives, given the 
requirement for continuous compliance with the requirements and the concomitant daily 
monitoring. 
 
Question 70. As regards the different types of exposures and collateral, to what extent do you 
consider that the LGD input floors maintain an adequate level of risk sensitivity with respect to the 
wide range of practices of EU institutions? 
As demonstrated in the note to question 69 above, introducing LGD input floors at individual 
exposure level would mean that financing with low risk would be in a significantly worse 
position, whereas financing with higher risk would be in a better position in some cases.  
Therefore, if the new requirements for low-risk real estate financing (i.e., in the lower LTV 
range) are actually implemented, the LGD input floor should be lowered. This would mitigate 
the disproportionate increase (compared to the higher-risk business) in risk weights for low-
risk business.

70 > LTV in percent > 60, 60 > LTV in percent > 50, etc.). This would ensure that different capital 
requirements are provided for different risks, even in the low LTV range (< 60%).

No regulatory requirement for an output floor in the EU: 
Basically, we are convinced that in Europe the output floor is superfluous. One of the primary 
reasons for revising the Basel Framework was the critical view of internal bank models (e.g., 
IRBA), which in particular is attributable to the observed variability of risk estimates in Pillar I. 
We believe the goal of reducing the variability of risk estimates – which cannot be explained by 
differences in the banks’ portfolios – to be legitimate, sensible and worthy of support. 
However, there are various opinions on the best way to achieve this goal. Europe has long 
followed a cause-based approach that begins with the model estimates themselves in order to 
adequately address the variability problem. We believe this is the right approach and an 
appropriate European response. Unlike the output floor, it avoids limiting the models’ 
informative value. This European approach to reducing unreasonable variability of results 
essentially consists of three elements: 
 EBA work on IRBA-related standardisations through Regulatory Technical Standards 
(RTSs) and guidelines (GLs), among other things to define default, to estimate the PD 
and LGD parameters together with uniform treatment of model uncertainties by 
specifying a security buffer for expected estimation errors, add-ons for economic 
downturns, extensive new acceptance criteria for IRBA approval;  
 The annual regulatory comparative analysis of the internal models (so-called 
supervisory benchmarking) pursuant to Article 78 CRD IV; 
 The targeted review of internal models (TRIM) by ECB Banking Supervision for the years 
2017-2019. 
The output floor was essentially introduced with the intention of limiting the variability of the 
results from internal models in the simplest way possible. And the tool does achieve this effect, 
at least in one direction – but at a disproportionately high price. Finally, the output floor will 
significantly reduce the risk sensitivity of the capital requirement. The risk-sensitive risk 
estimates made by the models (after applying the output floor) will only be fully reflected in the 
capital requirement in cases involving worse risks. 
The problems with the output floor become clear from the way it interacts with the 
standardised approaches (e.g., the standardised approach for credit risk, SA-CR). The SA-CR is 
34 
not sufficiently risk sensitive. Among other things, the SA-CR’s lack of risk sensitivity is 
attributable to the fact that, e.g., for exposures to borrowers without any external rating, the 
credit standing of the borrower plays no role in the risk weighting, or the depiction of the 
degree of collateralisation through real estate that is reflected in the LTV is not sufficiently risk 
sensitive. Regarding the latter aspect, additional LTV buckets with lower risk weights would be 
required in the low LTV range. Based on an analysis of the data from [individual] member 
institutions regarding income-producing real estate and retail residential mortgage portfolios, 
the RWAs in the SA-CR exceed the RWAs in the IRBA on average by more than [●]%. 
Using an insufficiently risk-sensitive standardised approach in conjunction with the application 
of the output floor leads to significant bank mismanagement. This is primarily attributable to the 
fact that capital requirements calculated using standardised approaches show a weaker 
response, or no response at all, to any change in the risk of a position. As part of the IRBA, by 
contrast, the ratings always have to be adjusted if the institution receives new information 
about a borrower.  
Internal rating procedures in particular should be capable of precisely capturing the 
institutions’ risks – especially as the institutions have developed adequate data histories which 
also encompass a financial crisis. If the output floor is triggered, i.e., if risk estimates produced 
by the internal model are significantly lower than the level specified by the output floor, an 
institution could incur higher risks without that being reflected in a higher capital requirement 
(e.g., in the case of RWAs in the SA-CR that are not dependent on the credit rating). This effect 
is caused mainly by the risk exaggerations that are typical of the standardised approaches, 
combined with the fact that the output floor is set far too high at 72.5%.

entitled “Implementing Basel III: Challenges and Impact” on 12 November 2019 in Brussels

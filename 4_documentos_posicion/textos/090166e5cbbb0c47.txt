Dear Sir/Madam, 
 
Thank you for the opportunity to comment on the Commission’s consultation on the implementation 
of Basel III in the EU . We would like to share with you the following reflections that we hope will be 
considered by the Commission. 
 
General remarks: 
 
▪ SME supporting factor 
 
As is widely recognised, SMEs are key drivers for growth and job creation in the EU1 representing a clear 
European market specificity. On top of this they consistently pose lower systemic risk2 compared to large 
corporates, which should redound in lower capital requirements. Given the above, the recent CRRII has 
not only maintained, but extended the scope and intensity of the supporting factor early introduced in 
CRRI. Confirming the fact that the measure incentives channelling capital flows to SME financing by 
institutions. As stated by the European Commission, ESBG supports that the high er SME discount in-
troduced in the EU through CRR II should be maintained in CRR III , given the role of SMEs as key 
drivers for growth in the EU. Increasing cost of capital would otherwise increase funding costs and/or 
stricter collateral requirements, both discouraging and hindering SMEs financing. 
 
Questions: 
 
Question 1: Views are sought on the relative costs and benefits of the ECRA provided by 
the final Basel III standards and the SCRA? In particular, how do the two approaches compare 
in terms of risk-sensitivity, impact on risk-weighted assets (RWAs) and operational burden? 
Please specify the relative costs and benefits of the two approaches for exposures to i) institu-
tions, ii) covered bonds and iii) corporates. Please provide relevant evidence to subs tantiate 
your views. 
 
We welcome the Basel Committee’s decision to continue allowing the use of external ratings. A rating by 
a recognised rating agency contains much more information than the parameters proposed in the original 
Basel Committee’s consultation paper on the standardised approach for credit risk could actually deliver. 
One main advantage of external ratings is that they consistently take certain forward-looking components 
into account. As a result, such ratings reflect the risks of an exposure much more accurately than 
supervisory parameters. Furthermore, uniform use of external ratings ensures comparability of capital 
requirements. Supervisory arrangements should therefore continue to be based on external ratings in the 
EU as well. 
 
On the one hand, the ECRA is more risk-sensitive than the SCRA. On the other hand , the application 
of the SCRA would also regularly lead to a significantly higher investigation effort, insofar as the financing 
to be valued is not "classic" lending business. While the latter types of fina ncing involve personal 
customer contact and the availability and evaluation of the borrower's key figures, this is often not the 
case, for example, when securities are purchased. Here, the information provided by external rating 
agencies together with mark et indicators is largely sufficient for the decision on the acquisition. In our 
opinion, the use of external ratings allows a practicable and sufficiently risk-sensitive valuation for these 
securities, provided that rating agencies are strictly monitored. This is adequately ensured by the current 
European regulation.

Question 3: Views are sought on the costs and benefits of implementing the various clarifica-
tions and specifications provided by the Basel III standards (paragraph 4) in relation to the due 
diligence to be performed by institutions. Please provide specific answers on each of the clari-
fications /specifications and support your view with relevant evidence. 
 
We believe that current European banking legislation does provide for adequate regulation on due dili-
gence. In our opinion the general intention of para. 4 has already been implemented in Art. 79 CRD. 
However, para. 4 also propos es to carefully assess external ratings by means of an internal analysis  for 
the borrower. We believe this imposes an inconsiderable administrative burden on banks. Smaller banks 
in particular are likely to quickly reach the limits of their capacities should they have to perform such due 
diligence themselves. The due diligence process where external ratings are taken into account (exposures 
to development banks, banks, covered bonds and corporates) should not consist of comparing external 
ratings with a separate internal credit analysis for each individual borrower in the form of mapping, for 
example. 
 
Question 6: Views are sought on the costs and benefits of implementing the definition of 
grades under the SCRA provided by the Basel III standards (paragra phs 22-29). Please pro-
vide relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
 
Many banks in one of our members’ Country have no external rating issued by a rating agency. Exposures 
to unrated banks are currently subject in the EU to the country of incorporatio n principle (Article 121 
CRR). Bank exposures are assigned a risk weight in accordance with the credit quality step to which 
exposures to the central government of the jurisdiction in which the bank is incorporated are assigned. 
The risk weight of unrated banks in this Country is currently 20%.  
 
The revised standardised approach for credit risk includes a new procedure for calculating the risk weight 
of exposures to banks with no external rating (standardised credit risk assessment approach (SCRA)). 
Where all grade A criteria are met, a risk weight of 40% may be applied. For grade A, application of a 
risk weight of 30% is also possible, provided that the bank has a CET1 ratio of 14% or higher and a 
leverage ratio of 5% or higher. Unfortunately, most banks do not meet these criteria, so that the reduced 
risk weight will rarely be applied. Consequently, the risk weight for exposures to unrated banks in our 
member’s Country will usually double from currently 20% to 40%, which would not be appropriate to 
the risk. The Country of incorporation principle currently applied should therefore be retained in the EU.

Question 13: Views are sought on the definition of ‘investment grade’ provided by the Basel III 
standards (paragraph 42). In particular, would you deem further refinements or clarifications 
necessary in order to ensure a consistent application across the Union? Please elaborate. 
 
We consider the definition of "investment grade" in para 42 of the Basel Paper to be appropriate in 
principle. However, the criterion "must have securities outstanding on a recognized stock exchange" has 
to be replaced by an objective criteri on. Europe has a large number of companies without an external 
rating, which nevertheless have "an adequate capacity to meet its financial commitments in a timely 
manner and its ability to do so is assess ed to be robust against adverse changes in the econo mic cycle". 
For many reasons, however, these companies do not finance themselves via the capital market. 
Accordingly, they would not meet the criterion "listed on a recognised stock exchange". Nevertheless, 
their creditworthiness is so good that a risk weight of 65 % would be justified. 
 
Question 14: What other measures, if any, could be taken to increase the risk -sensitivity of the 
standardised RW treatment of corporate exposures which currently have no external rating? 
Please elaborate and provide relevant evidence. 
 
In the EBA qualitative questionnaire on the implementation of the final revision of Basel III, the question 
of using a combination of the two methods ECRA and SCRA is addressed and banks are asked to eval-
uate if such an approach would increase the degree of risk sensitivity. It can be implicitly deducted from 
the EBA’s questions, that the combined approach would let banks use external rating for those corporate 
exposures where the company has an external rating. For those corporates which do not have it, but can 
be assessed to correspond to investment grade, a risk weight of 65% could be applied, on the condition 
that the client has issued securities listed on an exchange. Here, the only criterion in the Basel rules text, 
which would require ada ption to EU specificities , is to not apply the criterion that the corporates have 
to be listed on an exchange. The condition that the company has to have securities listed on an exchange 
could at EU level be replaced with another criterion which can be app lied by banks using data from 
external sources, and not relying on banks ’ internal models. Such a decision at EU level would have to 
be taken against the backdrop that much fewer European corporates are listed on an exchange compared

Question 16: Views are sought on the costs and benefits of implementing the specific treatment 
of SL exposures provided by the Basel III standards (paragraphs 44-48). In particular, how does 
this treatment compare with the current treatment in terms of risk-sensitivity, impact on RWAs 
and operational burden? Please provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
 
We underline that there are almost no externally rated SL transactions. By consequence, the revised SA 
does not provide any risk sensitivity for SL as there is only one single RW level for object finance and 
for commodities finance, three levels for project finance, but mainly two as high-quality criteria are too 
restrictive. In comparison, unsecured corporate loans have 5 levels of RW. The unintended consequences 
are to push banks to choose the most risky transactions that will have sufficient margins to support these 
overstated RWs and/or increase margins, which will increase the cost of using the infrastructures for end 
users. 
 
Question 17: Would you d eem further refinements or clarifications concerning the structure or 
calibration of the treatment for SL necessary? 
 
Amendments to the high-quality criteria are necessary in order to avoid failing to identify the main part 
of HQ transactions. Regarding the operational phase, economically comparable financing should be in-
cluded, i.e. providing thanks to the structure put in place, the same degree of control as if it was with an 
SPV, and cash flows over the whole asset life should be taken into account. High  quality should cover 
the operational phase, but also the pre-operational one when the construction contracts provide a good 
mitigation of construction risk (like in the infrastructure SF), and it should also include projects with part 
of revenues contracted as long as they are resilient to downside sensitivities (like in the SF) . Finally, the 
termination amount in case of termination of the off-take contract should be required only when the off-
taker is not replaceable. 
 
Question 18: In your view, what other measures should be taken to better reflect the particular 
characteristics of SL exposures (as compared to general corporate exposures) thereby increasing 
the risk-sensitivity of the SA-CR and improving consistency with the IRBA? 
 
A more granular SA table should be defined for SL. The EBA recognizes that SL should not be treated 
like unsecured exposures. Hence RW should be positioned on an average level lower than unsecured , 
unrated corporates. W e propose that RW from 50% to 110% with an intermediate 75% level applies, 
taking into account the quality of the transaction and the LTV (please see the paper ESBG submitted to 
DG FISMA in April 2019). This would be justified by loss rates observed (please see the slides presented 
by AFME to BCBS on public hearing regarding SA), with loss rates twice lower than those of unsecured 
corporates and also justified by the loss data collection done by 7 Dutch and French Banks showing loss 
rates on portfolios lowe r than 0.20% , i.e. similar to High investment grade corporates or investment 
grade ones.

Question 21: Views are sought on the costs and benefits of the revised standard treatment for 
equity exposures under Basel III (paragraph 49 -50). In particular, would you consider any fur-
ther differentiation among equity exposures (apart from “speculative unlisted equity exposures” 
and “national legislated programmes” – see 1.1.4.2. and 1.1.4.3.) warranted, and if so, how should 
this differentiation be made and what would be its prudential rationale? Please elaborate and 
provide relevant evidence. 
 
First of all, more in general terms, according to Article 133 (2) CRR, equity exposures shall be assigned 
a risk weight of 100%, unless they are required to be deducted, assigned a 250% risk weight or treated 
as high risk items. However, under the final Basel III reform, banks will be required to assign a risk 
weight of 400% to speculative unlisted equity exposures and a risk weight of 250% to all other equity 
holdings. In our opinion this treatment lacks risk sensitivity since banks will assign a risk weight of 250% 
to all equity holdings (or 400% to speculative unlisted companies), penalising long term strategic holdings 
maintained by banks in other banks or companies. 
 
For strategic reasons, banks may in fact have holdings in other entities or central institutions within their 
group. These holdings are of a long -term nature and do not serve to earn profits from increases in the 
value of such entities or institutions. The proposed increase in capital requirements by the 2.5 factor for 
all strategic investments would raise capital requirements significantly without any justification for this 
from a risk perspective. We, therefore, believe that an adjustment would be appropriate. In our view, the 
current 100% risk weight could continue to be  applied to such  a strategic investment. We, therefore, 
propose to implement a new category called strategic, long-term equity. 
 
Moreover, equity exposures should be further differentiated, dependent  on if they are within an IPS 
(institutional protection scheme) or to a counterparty which is its parent undertaking, its subsid-
iary, a subsidiary of its parent undertaking.  We trust that the intragroup exposure bears limited risk and 
should beneficiate from a derogatory reduced RW of 100%. 
 
National supervisors may allow banks of the same solidarity scheme (such as IPS or any other mechanism 
used by mutual, cooperatives or savings institutions) to apply a lower risk weight in their jurisdictions 
than indicated to their intra-group or in-network exposures. This is only possible if both counterparties 
to the exposures are members of the same effective solidarity scheme that is a contractual or statutory 
arrangement set up to protect those institutions and seeks to ensure their liquidity and solvency to avoid 
bankruptcy.  
 
In addition to the removal of the IRB approach for equities and the new risk weights determined for a 
standardised approach, it would also be necessary to implement rules for intragroup participations . 
Under the current European framework, such intragroup participations might be subject to a 100% RW, 
provided the credit obligations to such counterparty can be assigned a risk weight of 0%. So the allowance 
of lower risk weights for members of an IPS or to a counterparty which is it s parent undertaking, its 
subsidiary, or a subsidiary of its parent undertaking mentioned above, should be enlarged to the partici-
pations in the form of a risk weight of 100%. A change to 250% RW for intragroup equities would 
conflict with the principle of  proportionality, as the impact is seen on solo -level only. The cumulated 
RWA increase of all the different single entities on solo level would amount approximately to 10% of the 
total group consolidated credit RWA.

Question 23: Do you agree that speculative unlisted equity exposures such as investments in 
private equity or venture capital firms should be subject to a relatively higher RW than other 
equity exposures? 
 
No. The Basel Committee has justified the increased RWs mainly by the speculative and thus short-term 
component. In principle, however, an investment in an equity exposure with the intention of making a 
profit should not lead to a higher risk weight, especially if t he investment initially aims at the further 
development of the company before a profit is to be achieved through a sale (medium to long -term 
investment horizon). Beyond the passage of short-termism, the criterion of the intention to make a profit 
is introduced, which, however, can never be denied. In particular, we do not consider the relevant criteria 
to have been met in cases in which such investments are held in the investment portfolio under commer-
cial law. In our opinion, the previous risk weighting of 150% as a "high risk item" is still appropriate.  
 
Question 24: Views are sought on the definition of ‘speculative unlisted equity exposures’ pro-
vided by the Basel III standards (paragraph 51 and footnote 31). In particular, would you deem 
further refinements or clarifications necessary and if yes, what should those be and what would 
be their prudential rationale? Please elaborate and provide relevant evidence. 
 
In our view further refinements to clarify the definition of speculative unlisted equity exposures are nec-
essary as we do not see long-term investments in venture capital / investment funds or even short-term 
investments in diversified funds, as ‘speculative’. Therefore, the nature (type of investment, diversifica-
tion level) and maturity (short-term vs. long-term) should be considered in the definition for ‘speculative 
unlisted equity exposures.

Question 33: In your view, is the current CRR sufficiently clear to ensure a harmonised applica-
tion of the “granularity criterion” or do you consider further guidance necessary? If yes, what 
are your views as to what this further guidance should entail? 
 
The definition of the " granularity criterion" as a relative criterion ("no aggregated exposure to one 
counterparty can exceed 0.2% of the overall regulatory retail portfolio"), if checked on the single institute 
level, would affect small institutes with relatively small retail portfolios (e .g. under EUR 500 million ex-
posure), so that in many cases a significant share of their retail portfolio would not be treated as "regula-
tory retail" anymore. We therefore welcome the EBA's assessment in Recommendation CR -SA 22 that 
the proposed granularity criterion of 0.2% of the total regulatory retail portfolio is not necessary. How-
ever, we consider the current CRR rules to be sufficient to ensure adequate diversification of institutions' 
regulatory retail portfolios. 
 
Moreover, the “granularity criterion” would have a particularly negative impact on lending to SMEs. 
Loans to SMEs would be significantly more expensive and/or the loan volume would be significantly 
reduced. We therefore recommend that the diversification of a retail portfolio can be demonstrated using 
methods other than the mandatory application of the granularity criterion. The condition would conflict 
with the principle of proportionality.

approach provided by the final Basel III standard. In particular, how do the two approaches

Question 39: What are your views on the costs and benefits of implementing the preferential 
treatment for c ertain properties under construction as provided by the Basel III standards? 
Please provide relevant evidence supporting your view. 
 
The preferential treatment should be extended to loans for projects that are defined in paragraph 
68, i.e. a) residential real estate property which is built by associations or cooperatives of individuals that 
are regulated under national law and exist with the sole purpose of granting its members the use of a 
primary residence in the property securing the loans; and b) residential real estate property which is built 
by public housing companies and not-for-profit associations regulated under national law, that exist 
to serve social purposes and to offer tenants long-term housing. 
 
Rationale: Loans to Real Estate companies that are regulated under national Law specifically to serve social 
services like offering long -term housing have, like loans to the sovereign or to PSEs, a different risk 
profile and a higher probability of being finished since the national legislation normally provides 
for strict supervision and enforcement powers for the public authorities.  This should be reflected 
in their treatment as property under construction. 
 
If Non-profit or Low-profit Housing Associations , as are existing in various Member States , would be 
treated like any other property developer, the risk -weight of 150% as foreseen in paragraph 67 would 
lead to materially higher housing costs especially for young, poor and immigrant families that 
depend on publically subsidized housing schemes . Compared to the actual risk weight of 35% for 
Residential Real Estate, a risk weight of 150% would result in credit margins that are app. 50 basis points 
higher than the actual ones.

In ESBG’s view, both the commercial real estate and the residential real estate exposure classes could be

using an actual value. One obvious issue is that a valuation that could be 30 years old hardly represents a 
reasonable indication of the risk of a mortgage.

Even the very simple case of only on e loan being secured by a certain property becomes problematic. 
Take the case with two in practice identical properties (next to each other in the same street) and the

Question 48: What are your views on the costs and benefits of replacing the existing treatment 
of ‘speculative immovable property financing’ with the treatment of ADC exposures as provided 
by the Basel III standards? 
 
If we correctly understand, the “speculative immovable property” financing is only targeting “sales pro-
jects” (“intention of reselling for profit”) and as such has in scope projects with a short -term horizon. 
Furthermore, the definition of ADC broadens and includes property for lease. While we deem the attrib-
ution of higher risk to projects in development phase positive , from our perspective, the definition of a 
necessary occupancy rate “prevailing in that geographic market for that type of real estate” is too restric-
tive and should be changed to a level that is supporting the acceptance of a real estate on the particular 
market. Based on our current approach, the ADC-concept would have the potential to increase our costs, 
but even more would result in significant cost-increase for the customer. 
 
According to Article 128 (2) CRR, ‘speculative immovable property financing’ is considered as an expo-
sure with particularly high risk requiring the application of a 150% risk weight. However given the super-
visory interpretation, all ADC exposures are being considered as ‘speculative immovable property financ-
ing’ and consequently, credit institutions are required to assign a 150% risk weight to these exposures 
regardless of the counterparty credit quality. In our opinion, this treatment lacks of risk sensibility.  
 
Therefore, we consider very positive the replacement of the existing treatment of ‘speculative immovable 
property financing’ with the treatment of ADC exposures , provided by the Basel III standards , since it 
introduces a more granular approach that differentiates high risk exposures from those that do not pose 
a higher risk  by allowing the assignation of a risk weight of 100% if certain conditions are met (e.g. 
significant amount of pre-sale contracts).

2/ We believe that the definition of ‘speculative immovable property financing’ in Article 4 (79) CRR 
(“loans for the purposes of the acquisition of or development or construction on land in relation to immovable property, or of 
and in relation to such property, with the intention of reselling for profit ”) has to be clarified due to the debate

In our opinion, from the perspective of the literality of the standard, the connection of the term “specu-
lative” with the process of transformation , linked to a normal real estate development , is called into 
question since this process incorporates added value to the land acquired over several years. Regarding 
the term “reselling” the same can be said since it is an expression hardly applicable when the nature of 
the property bought (land) and that of the property sold (flats, buildings) are completely different. More-
over, applying a very high risk weight, as 150%, to all exposures to immovable development (without 
discrimination) seems disproportionate , particularly in such transactions soundly guaranteed and/or 
based in low risk entrepreneurial projects ( like developments with high level of sales off -plan, projects

Question 123: How would exercising the discretion affect the link between capital incentives and 
management of operational risks? Please elaborate. 
 
Exercising the discretion may have a link with proper operational risk management for those banks using 
capital measures as a tool for operational risk management. On the other hand, for other banks having 
developed internal alternative processes and measu res, setting ILM=1 does not override incentives to 
properly manage operational risk. Even though from a quantitative perspective this would imply capital 
neutrality in terms of LC , this is in turn would be consistent with the conception of BI as a proxy of  a 
size business bank which has a positive correlation with operational losses.  
 
On top of that, one of the issues at the heart of the shortcomings that ended with the suppression of the 
Advanced Model Approach (AMA) was its heavy use of historical-event data, which makes many AMAs 
by definition backward-looking. Once a significant loss enters into an institution’s data set, capital num-
bers rise significantly and permanently unless institutions are allowed to somehow mitigate the impact of 
such loss events  on their regulatory capital calculations. In practice, without adjustments, past losses 
lingered on the capital numbers long after the causes of those losses have been remediated, exactly the 
same effect an ILM larger than one would have. Consequently, the ILM will not create any incentive to 
improve banks risk management capabilities as operational risk control and improvements will only start 
to have a positive impact on capital a lot of years after having been put in place. 
 
Incentives’ alignment is preserved through other mechanisms other than increasing capital requirements: 
from an internal perspective losses are per se in banks objective function to be minimized but from an 
external/market perspective disclosure requirements force entities to compete  for a better operational 
risk profile within competitors. Stakeholders ’ picture of a deficient operational risk profile may prevent 
them to invest or either operate with a financial institution. Moreover, supervisory requirements to re-
mediate identified operational vulnerabilities contribute to maintain a proper management of operational 
losses. In that regard, it is clear that Pillar 2 as well as Pillar 3 frameworks provide enough incentives to 
reinforce the use of best practices in the management of operational losses

Question 177: What are your views on the relative costs and benefits of including in the calcula-
tion of the OF more own funds requirements than those explicitly mentioned in the Basel III  
standards? In particular, how would such broader material scope compare to the scope required 
by the Basel III standards in terms of impact on RWAs, risk-sensitivity, comparability, complex-
ity and operational burden? Please provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
 
Compared to IRB based capital requirements, capital requirements based on standardised approaches 
lack risk sensitivity. Typically, fo r banking sectors with low historical losses, REA calculated based on 
standardised approaches (calculated as 72.5 % of SA risk weights) is significantly higher than the REA 
calculated with internal models on historical loss data. The floored capital requir ements will thus not be 
risk-based for these banks. This is a major concern considering that banks in the Member States with the 
lowest ratio of NPLs (see Chart 4 on page 11 of the ESRB report “Resolving non -performing loans in 
Europe” of July 2017) are al so the most impacted according to the EBA impact study (see Figure 4 on 
page 41 in the EBA Report).  
 
For such banks, the output floor will become a binding restriction, meaning that they will:  
 
- face incentives to lend less to low-risk clients and take higher risks; 
- be forced to push low-risk asset funding to capital markets competitors; 
- need to choose between higher prices, reduced activity or reduced profitability; 
- have to decide whether capital allocation, should be risk -based or based on regulatory capital con-
sumption: the former entails the risk of low returns, while the latter would risk building up excessive 
risk on the balance sheet. 
 
Interest rates will be higher for a majority of customers due to higher capital requirements, and the effect 
will be most significant for the clients in the best performing risk categories. Effectively, banks’ capacity 
to finance the real economy will deteriorate, and many customers will have to use alternative sources of 
funding. Contrary to the objective of the revised Basel framework, the financing of the real economy, as 
well as financial stability, will be negatively affected because of non-risk based capital requirements.  
 
It is important that the implementation of the output floor is preceded by careful consideration in order 
to avoid it becoming a binding constraint for low -risk banks: without such consideration , the capital 
requirements for these banks will increase significantly compared to what is the case under IRB ap-
proaches. 
 
According to the Basel standard, the output floor requirement should consist of 72.5 %*RWA-SA (min-
imum requirement + capital conserva tion buffer + countercyclical capital buffer + GSIB buffer). In 
order to avoid the output floor becoming a binding restriction for banks with low risk weights, and in 
order to ensure comparability across the EU, this calculation should not include buffers or requirements 
related to the O-SII Buffer, Systemic Risk Buffer or Pillar 2 (both hard P2R and soft P2G). This approach 
would help maintain both risk-sensitivity as well as comparability.

ESG rating agencies. This poses a series of issues like a lack of comparability among ESG 
products due to the lack of transparency of the methodologies used by data providers. 
We hence recommend to enhance the supervision of those stakeholders in order 
to make sure they rightfully use the common classification system described 
above (once determined) and that their methodologies are comparable by inves-
tors. 
 
Finally, several risk-related initiatives are being developed by the market in terms of stress testing 
or portfolio analysis . We would like to highlight that due to the lack of hindsight available on these 
topics, the markets are currently in a phase of experimentation and there is a gro wing trend of best 
practice sharing. Although there would at some point be a need to harmonize all the initiatives taken in 
those regards, we think that the work achieved by the NGFS is sufficient from now, and that no regu-
latory framework should be established before the market can use common scenarios and meth-
odologies in order to stress their portfolios. 
 
Question 192 : What would be the benefits and drawbacks of including the requirement for 
competent authorities to perform a fit and proper assessment of  at least some key function 
holders in the CRD? 
 
We would like to point out, that a widening of the requirement for competent authorities to perform a 
fit and proper assessment of key function holders for all institutes is to be rejected. For this we see no 
need. It should remain a responsibility for institutes to check whether an applicant and / or holder of a 
key function fulfils the institution -specific requirements for a position. Key function holders are not 
members of the management body but employees of institutions. The appointment of these functi ons 
falls solely within the area of responsibility of the executive board, which manages the institute on its 
own responsibility. 
 
As a result, a fit and proper assessment of key function holders for all institutes would only lead to an 
immense additional bureaucratic burden on the institutes, which faces no discernible supervisory benefit. 
All institutes are aware of the special importance of the key functions, so that it is in their own interest 
to ensure that the function holders have the necessary expertise and reliability. 
 
We would also like to point out that, even after the recent revision of Article 91 CRD by Directive (EU) 
2019/878, only members of the management body are subject to fit and proper assessments. The decision 
of the European legislator should be respected. 
 
Irrespective of our substantial concerns, for reasons of proportionality, the requirement should in any 
case be limited to significant institutions. Similar to the obligation to identify risk takers, the "limit" for 
classification as a significant institution should be total assets of more than 15 billion euros. 
 
It is true that the CRD does not provide expressly for the assessment of key function holders by 
competent authorities, but in our view, this is not even necessary since ke y function holders are integral 
part of the internal governance arrangements of an institution provided by Art. 88 CRD. According to 
this provision the internal governance arrangements of the institution fall into the accountability of the 
management body.

Furthermore, the concrete regulatory framework (including assessment) for key function holders is laid 
down by the Joint ESMA/EBA GL on the Suitability of members of the management body and key 
function holders (EBA GL Fit & Proper). The EBA GL Fit & Proper state in this respect that assessing 
the suitability of key function holders is the responsibility of the institutions. At the same time, the EBA 
GL on Internal Governance stipulate that the management body is responsible for setting, approving 
and overseeing the implementation of the selection and suitability process for key function holders. At 
the moment we do not consider  it necessary to change this approach, neither by expanding the role of 
supervisory authorities in t he assessment of key function holders nor by including new provisions for 
this matter in the CRD. Whilst it is perfectly justifiable from a regulatory point of view for the 
management body to have a CRD framework on the top of regulatory requirements since  the 
management body is on the highest accountability level in the institution, key function holders should be 
treated differently. Key function holders are strong ly connected to the organizational, strategic and 
business-related particularities of an inst itution. According to the current approach of the EBA GL Fit 
& Proper key function holders are persons having a significant influence over the direction of the 
institution but are not members of the management body, including heads of internal control func tions 
and other positions identified on a risk-based approach by the institutions. This definition is wide enough, 
in order to give the institutions, the necessary flexibility in taking sufficiently into consideration the above 
mentioned particularities when defining their key function holders. As outlined by the current definition, 
having internal control functions defined as key function holders within an institution regardless of its 
strategy and internal organization is appropriate from a supervisory point of view, however, going beyond 
this and laying down by a legal requirement further positions as key function holders could be in conflict 
with the internal organization and strategy of one institution. For example, depending on the business 
strategy and customer portfolio, in one institution the head of retail may be a key function holder, in 
other institutions not; furthermore, this differs even in group structures where the administration of 
customer portfolios is allocated across the group in different group entities.  
 
Interfering with the organizational and strategic framework of an institution is a significant drawback in 
including the requirement for performing fit and proper assessments for key function holders in the 
CRD. For this reason, we do not consider  it necessary to change in any way the current approach 
regarding key function holders.

Question 196:  Should the key function holders be subject to fit and proper assessments by

The EBA guidelines for internal governance were published in September 2017, and the EBA -ESMA 
guidelines on the assessment of the suitability of members of the management body and key function 
holders were published in March 2018, after a full consultation process with relevant stakeholders. Within 
these texts there are many criteria and requirements applied to European banks.  The supervisory

be beneficial. 
 
Question 197:  Please explain what you consider to be the advantages and disadvantages of 
competent authorities conducting ex ante and ex post approval, respectively, of suitability of 
members of the management body. 
 
The ex-ante approval of members of the management body could, in many EU Member States, slow 
down the process to appoint new members. In some banks, members are approved in the General 
Assembly or other occasions where many people are n eeded. If a bank must wait until after the 
assessment is carried out, this would cause severe delays in appointments leaving gaps in the management. 
 
However, ESBG understands the need for members of the management body and board members to be 
able to carry out their tasks. We therefore propose that an assessment is carried out at a specific point in 
time, to allow banks the chance to train their board members (i.e. six months after appointment). This 
would be particularly helpful in the case of ex-officio board members. 
 
In our view the current approach which allows national competent authorities to choose between the ex-
ante and ex-post approval system is the right one since the approval system is strongly connected to the 
particularities of the national law of the member states. Please consider that the potential introduction of 
the ex-ante approval system as mandatory in the CRD will have implications not only on the supervisory 
law but also on further legal areas, like company/corporate law which are not  supposed to be affected 
by the CRD.  
 
We are aware, that the reason behind the introduction of an ex -ante assessment is that competent 
authorities in some member states may have problems with replacing unsuitable members of the 
management body appointed b y the institution. However, deficiencies or weaknesses in national law 
should primarily be addressed in the context of national law. The CRD IV anyhow enables competent 
authorities to take necessary measures when an institute does not meet the requirements in the directive. 
Regarding the possibility to remove members of the management body this supervisory power is already 
provided by the new Art. 91 (1) CRD V and for SSM institutions by Art. 16 (2) lit. m of the SSM

Question 198:  If, in your jurisdiction, institutions are required to request approval for the 
appointment of members of the management body only after they take up their position, please 
explain what, if anything, would make it difficult for you to adapt to an ex ante system. 
 
It will be difficult to implement the ex-ante evaluation in some Member States.  
 
In two tier systems (separation of executive board and supervisory board) it is necessary to differentiate 
between executive board members on the one hand and members of the supervisory body on the other. 
For executive board members an ex-ante assessment in  our members’ Country is already taking place in 
the form of an informal pre-inquiry, which should also be retained in the existing form. 
 
The previous assessment practice of the members of the management board in its executive function 
only after the appointment has also proved itself. If the supervisory authority subsequently criticizes the 
fact that there are doubts regarding the knowledge, experience etc., seminars can also be attended after 
the appointment to eliminate any deficits. In the case of a dismissal of the direct or at the request of the 
supervisory authority, a substitute is initially available. 
 
In particular, an ex-ante assessment for members of the management body in its supervisory function of 
public-law institutes in our member’s Country  is impracticable due to the existing legal regulations for 
the election procedures : the members are elected partly by the representative body of the local 
government (municipality), partly by the employees of the institutes. Lists are drawn up with a large 
number of persons, w hereby it is not possible to foresee in advance - as is customary with elections - 
which persons will ultimately actually be elected to the management body in its supervisory function. 
 
If one wanted to carry out an ex -ante assessment here, on the one hand  all candidates on the election 
lists would have to submit their documents from the CV to the extract from the central trade register 
and the certificate of good conduct to the supervisory authority, although only a fraction of these election 
candidates wo uld then actually become board members. In order to allow the assessment authority 
sufficient time for the assessment, notifications would probably have to be sent well before the start of 
the Board's activities. Especially after the local elections, which  naturally generate an increased number 
of assessments, there would be a long period of time between the political vote and the decision. In our 
opinion, this is hardly possible and not necessary. This would result in expenses and costs that would be 
disproportionate to the expected benefits. 
 
On the other hand, the question would be when the documents would have to be submitted and what 
effects this would have on the election procedure. In order to achieve an ex-ante assessment, every person 
who is considering running for the management body in its supervisory function in the first place would 
have to have their suitability assessed by the supervisory authority at this point in time, as there will not 
be enough time between drawing up the election lists and holding the election. Alternatively, election lists 
would have to be drawn up and the election then "delayed" until all candidates have been assessed, in 
order to make any necessary adjustments to the election lists and then carry out the election. Both 
procedures are neither practicable nor compatible with the existing legal regulations for election 
procedures. 
 
In one of our members’ Country, the renewal process of some banks’ Boards of Supervisors takes place 
every six years (reference was made to Artic le L. 512-90 of the Monetary and Financial Code), meaning 
that the administrative burden of ex -ante evaluation would be very difficult to carry out within a 
reasonable time.

o the final step for their appointment according to the Stock Corporation Acts is their election by the

an ex-ante assessment by supervisory authorities.  
 
In case of members of supervisory boards of non-listed companies 
o It is the shareholders responsibility, at the shareholders meeting, to decide who should be part of the 
board. To provide the shareholders with a real opportunity to choose and decide  if the institutions 
would, in an ex -ante regime, have to designate and assess, a vast number of candidates and well in 
advance andsubmit all th e candidates for the approval of the supervisory authority. The decisions 
would be public which could negatively affect individuals who have been nominated but not chosen, 
and would reduce the readiness of suitable candidates to take up the function.  
o Also, the process for the institution would not be predictable in terms of timing. Even if the time 
limit is set for three to four months, there is no guarantee that the supervisory authority would keep 
such deadline. If the time limit is not kept, it would re sult in the institution having to hold an 
extraordinary shareholders meeting. In these circumstances the institution must take into account the 
legal requirements for notification etc. In addition, one or several members of the board may resign 
prematurely, which in the worst case, could lead to non-compliance with the statutory provisions or 
the legal requirements regarding the minimum number of members of the Supervisory Board due to 
a non-completed ex ante assessment. To avoid this state, the institution has to hold a “pool” of “spare 
board members” with different skills and knowledge, who ha ve been assessed by the supervisory 
authority. This will create practical problems, among other things by the fact that it is difficult to find 
suitable candidates who are also willing to act as “back -up” over a longer period of time. Above all, 
it is not in line with the law in our of our members’ Country that the Shareholders’ meeting in advance 
appoint members of the board in the event that one or several directors should resign prematurely.

could have influence on an institution’s choice of management etc. In critical situations an institution 
has to choose and appoint the persons most qualified without delay, and not persons whom have 
already been fit and properly approved by competent authorities, to avoid long case handling periods.

Question 204: Should the scope and format of fit and proper assessments be adapted to take into

ESBG is of the opinion that the Principle of Proportionality should be  basically taken into account due 
to the scope of the text – this should include subsidiaries within a group. Differentiation is required. The 
lack of differentiation does not meet the requirements of proportionality requirements and different 
situations (dualistic / monistic system).  Should the European Commission insist on subjecting key

Question 207: What would be the benefits and drawbacks of designing an accountability regime 
whereby the management body of each institution would be required to draw up a statement of 
responsibilities of each of its members clearly identifying the activities for which they are 
responsible, beyond t he sole responsibilities linked to their membership of specialised 
committees (e.g. risk committee, remuneration committee)? 
 
The accountability regime of the board of directors is defined in European jurisdictions in its Corporate 
Codes and, in global terms, it is defined as a collective accountability. In addition, European and national 
regulation, including both EBA Guidelines, on Internal Governance and on the assessment of the 
suitability of the members of the management body, provide a sufficient desc ription of the collective 
tasks and responsibilities of the board of directors and of its specialized committees. No further 
identification of responsibilities at individual level is needed within the board of directors as it is defined 
and acts as a collective body, subject to a collective accountability.  
 
A special accountability regime for each member of the management body is not necessary and would 
contravene the applicable legal requirements regarding collective liability and due diligence obligations of 
management and supervisory board members. 
 
Following the requirement of the EBA GL on Internal Governance (par. 21) management board mem-
bers institutions have already  put in place a clear allocation of duties for the management board in a 
written document. According to this allocation of duties every management board member has his re-
sponsibility areas for which he is accountable (for example, CFO is responsible for finance, budgeting or 
liability management; CRO is responsible for risk control and man agement, CIO for information and 
communication technology). The responsibility areas are clearly stated already in the job description of 
the respective management board position and are further subject to the suitability assessment of the 
candidates. Despite this allocation of duties management board members are still responsible collectively 
for the overall functioning of the institution and must fulfil their obligations as collective legally respon-
sible body. 
 
For supervisory board members there is a strong connection between the expertise area of every member 
and their committee membership: in fact, the set-up of the committees is based on expertise area of the 
members and out of this set-up any further delimitation of responsibilities going beyond the c ommittee 
membership would be detrimental for the functioning of the supervisory board and its specialized com-
mittees.

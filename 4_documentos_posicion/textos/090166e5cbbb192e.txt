Comments on the European Commission’s consultation:  
 
Implementing the Final Basel III Reforms 
in the EU 
 
Finance Denmark is a business association for banks, mortgage banks, asset 
management, securities trading and investment funds in Denmark. We are 
pleased giving the possibility to comment on the up-coming implementation of 
the finalization of the Basel III reforms in the EU. 
 
General comments 
The EBA in its report on the implementation of the outstanding elements of the 
Basel III framework recommends an implementation in the EU that will result in an 
overall increase in capital requirements for EU banks of 23,6%. Danish banks and 
mortgage banks would face a capital increase of nearly 40% of which the in-
crease stemming from the output floor accounts for 23%. These figures are in 
sharp contrast to G20 indication that the final Basel III revision should not bring 
about a significant increase in overall capital requirements. In the EU this view 
was reiterated by Member States during the ECOFIN Council meeting in July 
2016. 
 
Given the very large impact on the Danish banking and mortgage sector it is es-
sential to modify the EBA recommendations for implementing the Basel 2017 re-
form package into European regulation. In our answer from 18 December 20191 
to the European Commission’s inception impact assessment we have explained 
the need for modifications in the areas of high importance from a Danish per-
spective.  
 
We strongly support the principle that capital requirements should reflect the 
risks of banks’ lending and trading book activity. A close link between capital re-
quirements and risk will support sound business decision in banks and an effi-
cient allocation of resources in the economy. While improvements have been 
made to the risk sensitivity of the standardised approaches in the final Basel III re-
forms that are in scope for implementation in the EU we are concerned that es-
pecially the Standardised Approach for credit risk in some important areas

1) Views are sought on the relative costs and benefits of the ECRA provided by 
the final Basel III standards and the SCRA? In particular, how do the two ap-
proaches compare in terms of risk-sensitivity, impact on risk-weighted assets 
(RWAs) and operational burden? Please specify the relative costs and benefits 
of the two approaches for exposures to i) institutions, ii) covered bonds and iii) 
corporates. Please provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views.

Answer: 
Applying the SCRA approach in Europe and dismiss all together the use of exter-
nal rating for regulatory purposes is not a recommended option. Ratings are im-
portant for market funding as well as for regulatory purposes in several exposure 
classes (sovereigns, institutions, including covered bonds and securitisations). 
 
However, given the reliance of the EU’s corporate market on bank financing, we 
would urge regulators to be mindful of this in the implementation of the Stand-
ardised Approach (SA) alongside the output floor. Compared to the current 
framework, the implementation of Basel III will have a significant impact for banks 
that currently use IRB to calculate capital requirements for unrated corporates 
which are deemed investment grade. While the 100% risk weight (RW) for un-
rated corporates under the SA remains 100%, per the ECRA, the introduction of 
the output floor raises the effective RW for banks using IRB. As most EU corporate 
exposures are unrated this could, all other things being equal, result in financing 
becoming more expensive for unrated investment grade corporates. Our view is 
that the same capital requirements should be applied to borrowers of equivalent 
risk regardless of the rating approach used. Failing this, companies of equivalent 
risk potentially face different financing costs. 
 
Please see our answers to question 2 and 12-15 regarding unrated corporates.

13) Views are sought on the definition of ‘investment grade’ provided by the 
Basel III standards (paragraph 42). In particular, would you deem further re-

Answer:  
Danish IRB banks face a high impact on the risk weighting of their non-SME cor-
porate portfolios from the Basel output floor. We estimate that this could increase 
the risk weighted exposure amount for an average non-SME corporate counter-
party by over 60%. Such a large impact on the required capital for corporate 
lending will increase the costs of corporate funding significantly.   
 
To ensure a minimum level of risk sensitivity for corporates under the ECRA ap-
proach comparable with the treatment of corporates exposures under a market 
funding model, it is essential that a mechanism is implemented in the EU regula-
tion whereby corporates that are assessed by institutions against the definition of 
“investment grade” may be assigned a risk weight of 65%.  
  
Allowing banks to apply the SCRA risk weight for investment grade corporates at 
65%, and non-investment grade corporates at 100% would be consistent with the 
treatment of unrated banks in the revised Basel standards. 
  
The application of the 65% risk weight under the Basel SCRA approach requires 
the corporate (or its parent company) to have securities listed on a recognized 
exchange. In a European bank funding model this requirement would be unduly 
restrictive, and should not a requirement in Europe, because unrated counter-
parties typically do not issue securities. Furthermore, having securities listed is 
not necessarily an indication of superior credit quality. 
  
We propose that supervisory guidance is developed in the EU to ensure a harmo-
nized assessment methodology for EU banks to determine if a corporate qualifies 
as “Investment grade equivalent”. This should be based on an assessment, in line

Yes.

102) In your view, which other aspects, if any, should be considered in the 
context of revising the CRM framework under the SA-CR? Please specify and 
rank your answers from the most important to the least important aspect. 
 
Answer: 
Regarding level playing field between the standardised and IRB-approaches in 
the credit risk mitigation framework (CRM) it is important to adapt the rules in a 
way that leads to eligibility of the same physical collateral under both ap-
proaches.  
 
In the Standardised Approach, the two only types of collateral, eligible to the 
credit risk mitigation, are financial collaterals and immovable properties. The 
other types of physical collateral are not recognized to reduce credit risk sensitiv-
ity. 
 
For instance, physical collaterals (i.e. loan collateralized by automobiles) are not 
considered as eligible collateral under the Standardised Approach while they 
are eligible under IRB approaches. Consequently, a loan secured by a physical 
collateral will be treated as an unsecured loan under the Standardised Ap-
proach while it may receive a lower RW as a secured loan under IRB ap-
proaches.  
 
This difference in CRM will also play a central role in the calculation of the NPL 
prudential backstop for Non-Performing Loan since the provision path will be 
more stringent for unsecured exposures than secured ones. 
 
To maintain the proper incentives for banks to use CRM, the eligibility of physical 
collateral under the Standardised Approach should be reviewed and widened 
and aligned with the IRB rules.

148) What are your views on the introduction of the simplified SA-MR, in parti-

particularly affected by the revised calibration? Please provide relevant evi-

Answer: 
We welcome the simplified alternative to the new Standardised Approach for 
smaller institutions with limited trading book activity. 
 
The new standard approach for market risk is complicated to apply and will be a 
significant administrative and financial burden to implement for banks with sim-
pler trading books, which, however, exceeds the size thresholds set in CRR2 to 
qualify for the simpler alternative to the Standardised Approach for reporting pur-
poses.  Especially the threshold in CRR2 based on the relative size of the trading 
book compared to the bank’s total assets is constraining for many smaller banks 
in Denmark with simple trading book activity. 
 
The Basel standard does not set specific thresholds for the size of the trading book 
that banks must be below to qualify for the simplified alternative to the Standard-
ised Approach. Instead national authorities are mandated to allow banks that 
maintain smaller or si mpler trading books to use the simplified alternative Stand-

Output Floor (OF) 
 
177) What are your views on the relative costs and benefits of including in the 
calculation of the OF more own funds requirements than those explicitly men-
tioned in the Basel III standards? In particular, how would such broader mate-
rial scope compare to the scope required by the Basel III standards in terms of 
 
 
 
Finance Denmark  |  Amaliegade 7  |  DK-1256 Copenhagen K  |  www.financedenmark.dk 17 
 
 
January 3, 2020 
Doc. no. FIDA-119627797-22-v1 
 
impact on RWAs, risk-sensitivity, comparability, complexity and operational 
burden? Please provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
 
Answer: 
We strongly support the principle that capital requirements should reflect the 
risks of banks’ lending and trading book activity. A close link between capital re-
quirements and risk will support sound business decision in banks and an effi-
cient allocation of resources in the economy. 
 
The diverse conditions in Europe also mean that the new output floor based on 
the standardised approaches will have an unproportionally large impact on Eu-
ropean banks that operate in regions in Europe where the Standardised Ap-
proach for credit risk is poorly aligned to the underlying economic conditions. For 
IRB banks operating in these regions, the Basel output floor would not serve as a 
back-stop requirement affecting outlier banks, but instead be the constraining 
capital requirement for the great majority of IRB banks. The overall result will be a 
significant weakening of the link between risk and capital. Moreover, the output 
floor would, if implemented with no adjustments, be responsible for the largest in-
crease in the overall capital requirements in Europe over and above “a signifi-
cant increase”.  We strongly encourage the Commission to investigate the ef-
fects of the Basel output floor on banks risk taking. 
  
The main reason for introducing the output floor in the Basel framework was to re-
duce excessive variability of risk weighted assets and to enhance the compara-
bility of risk weighed capital ratios. 
  
In Europe the issue of unwarranted variation in model outcomes is addressed by 
the very substantial work through the EBA’s IRB Roadmap and the ECBs Targeted 
Review of Internal Models (TRIM). This work aims at harmonizing, strengthening 
and verifying the use of IRB models. We have yet to see the results of this work, 
but we are confident that these initiatives will more effectively than the output 
floor address excessive variability of risk weighted assets from the use of models 
and enhance the comparability of risk weighed capital ratios. 
  
Moreover, the intention of the Basel III framework, including the output floor, is 
not to increase overall capital levels for banks. Yet, as shown in the EBA impact 
studies, the overall effect on the capital requirements for European banks is an 
estimated average increase of 23,6%, which is very substantial. The output floor is 
the main driver of this estimated increase; with an average contribution to the in-
crease of 8,6%. In several member states, including Denmark, the increase in 
capital is far higher and even more significantly driven by the isolated effect of 
the output floor. 
 
 
 
Finance Denmark  |  Amaliegade 7  |  DK-1256 Copenhagen K  |  www.financedenmark.dk 18 
 
 
January 3, 2020 
Doc. no. FIDA-119627797-22-v1 
 
  
The reason for these significant impacts on capital requirements of the output 
floor is that the standardized approach for credit risk is not calibrated to fit Euro-
pean specificities in important loans segments (e.g. mortgage loans and corpo-
rates). The effect is magnified for IRB banks operating under well-functioning 
mortgage systems and in stable and solid economic conditions. 
  
Households and corporates will face increased interest rates on their loans due 
higher funding cost or more difficulty for obtaining loans due to banks having to 
reduce lending to fulfilled increased capital requirements. Overall, this will have a 
negative effect on GDP growth in Europe and especially in the most affected 
countries, as for example Denmark. 
  
This is confirmed by the EBA study on the macroeconomic effect of the final Ba-
sel III implementation. They estimated an impact on average annual slowdown in 
GDP growth in the EU area in the first 4 years after implementation of 0,2%, corre-
sponding to an overall GDP cost of around 0.8%. It will be more severe in the 
most affected Member States. 
  
The EBA study on the macroeconomic effects estimates an economic benefit in 
the longer run due to lower risk and lower costs of financial crises as well as a re-
duction in banks’ funding costs due to being considered safer when they have 
higher capital levels. We believe that such longer-term benefits are very uncer-
tain and difficult to estimate. Other studies show that an optimum capital ratio 
for banks is about 15% with only limited benefits by increasing capital ratios 
above15%.  Finally, it should also be recalled that it was not the intention that the 
Basel III reform should increase overall capital levels. 
  
Fundamentally, we believe that the argument for EU implementation of the out-
put floor is founded on the obligation for the EU to adhere to the international 
agreement. The output floor will not have a significant beneficial effect of Euro-
pean financial stability but would add to making European financing more ex-
pensive and less available for households and corporates.  and pose a serious 
threat to the European banking model where low risk loans are kept on the bal-
ance sheet of banks and to a large extent funded through covered bonds; a 
model which historically has functioned well in Denmark.

211) Do you consider that corporate culture could and should be taken into con-
sideration as part of the fit and proper assessment? If yes, please explain how this 
could be most effectively achieved. 
 
Answer: 
No, this assessment falls out of scope of the individual fit and proper assessment. 
The referral to art. 98 (7) in CRD is not part of the fit and proper assessment but an 
assessment of the institution. Furthermore, this assessment would be hard to per-
form as corporate culture is collectively achieved and the corporate culture dif-
fers from institution to institution. 
 
Documenting this for both institutions and NCAs will be impossible.

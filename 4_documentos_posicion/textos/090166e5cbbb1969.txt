EU IPU – MiFID-authorised asset managers 
 
Background 
 
Under the currently in-force (but not yet transposed and applied) version of CRD5 Article 21b, 
the EU IPU requirement does not extend to MiFID -authorised asset managers. This is 
because the current CRR2 Article 4(1)(2) definition of “investment firm” specifically excludes 
MiFID-authorised asset managers.  
 
We believe this should be  the correct outcome, and has always been the policy intention.  
Asset managers, whether authorised under MiFID, UCITS or AIFMD, were never intended to 
be in scope of the EU IPU provisions.  
 
Asset managers are very different businesses to broker-dealers and other investment firms – 
they are not at all “bank like”. MiFID -authorised asset managers, or UCITS/AIFMD asset 
managers with MiFID “top up” permissions, have a limited range of MiFID permissions. These 
limited permissions correspond to MiFID II Annex I Section A points (1), (2), (4) and (5): 
 Reception and transmission of orders in relation to one or more financial instruments 
 Execution of orders on behalf of clients 
 Portfolio management 
 Investment advice 
 
The above activities encompass the range of acti vities that an asset manager may typically 
perform. 
 
The EU IPU requirement must be transposed by EU member states by 28 December 2020. 
In-scope third-country groups will need to implement the requirements by 30 December 2023.  
 
 
Effect of IFD/IFR amendmen ts to CRD/CRR, and implications for MiFID -authorised asset 
managers 
 
However, recent amendments to CRD/CRR via IFD/IFR bring MiFID -authorised asset 
managers into scope of the EU IPU requirement. This is for two main reasons. 
 
Firstly, IFD Article 62(9) amends CRD5 Article 21b by adding CRD5 Article 21(b)(5)(b): 
 
“For the purposes of this Article [21b], … the term “institution” shall also include 
investment firms.” 
 
Secondly, IFR Article 62(3)(b) amends CRR2 Article 4(1)(2) – the definition of “investment 
firm” for CRR purposes – by deleting the carveouts for local firms and MiFID-authorised asset 
managers: 
 
‘investment firm’ means an investment firm as defined in point (1) of Article 4(1) of 
Directive 2014/65/EU [MiFID II] which is authorised under that Directive but excludes 
credit institutions;  
excluding the following: 
(a) Credit institutions; 
(b) Local firms; 
(c) Firms which are authorised to provide the ancillary service referred to in point (1) 
of Section B of Annex 1 to Directive 2004/39/EC [MiFID I], which provide only one 
or more of the investment services and activities in points 1, 2, 4, 5 of Section A of 
Annex I to that Directive, and which are not permitted to hold money or securities 
 
4 
 
belonging to their clients which for that reason may not at any time place 
themselves in debt with those clients. 
 
In isolation, these two amendments made by IFD/IFR make sense. But in combination, they 
have the effect of bringing MiFID-authorised asset managers into scope of EU IPU, when this 
was never the intention and never originally proposed.  
 
The amendment made by IFD Article 62(9) is intended to ensure that the investment firms that 
would have been subject to the EU IPU requirements under the CRD/CRR regime, will remain 
subject to the EU IPU requirements when they move to the IFD/IFR regime which applies from 
26 June 2021 . The amendment made by IFD Article 62(9) was necessa ry because the 
CRD/CRR definitions of “institution” and “credit institution” were being changed under IFD/IFR. 
Had the amendment in IFD Article 62(9) not been made, the EU IPU requirements would only 
have applied to credit institutions and “class 1 investm ent firms” of in -scope third country 
groups – a narrower range of firms than originally envisaged.  
 
The amendment made by IFR Article 62(3)(b) essentially deletes the “local firms” and “MiFID-
authorised asset managers” carveouts from the CRR2 Article 4(1)(2) definition of investment 
firms. This reflects that local firms and MiFID-authorised asset managers would generally no 
longer be subject to the CRD/CRR regime and would move across to the IFD/IFR regime. 
Therefore, for almost all CRD/CRR purposes (capit al requirements, governance, etc), it was 
no longer necessary to maintain the local firms and MiFID -authorised asset manager 
carveouts. 
 
However, when the effect of these amendments are combined, it means that for the first time, 
MiFID-authorised asset managers would be in scope for the EU IPU requirements. 
 
Inclusion of asset managers in the EU IPU framework creates an unlevel playing field between 
asset managers that are part of a group subject to EU IPU, and asset managers that are not. 
It also creates a n unlevel playing field between MiFID -authorised asset managers, and 
UCITS/AIFMD authorised asset managers. And as mentioned above, asset managers are not 
“bank like” in their activities.  
 
Furthermore, including asset managers into an EU IPU structure int o the same corporate 
structure, making such structures more complex, when third -country groups have made 
significant efforts over the last ten years to simplify their corporate structures and align legal 
entities and groups of legal with business activities. Corporate governance and management 
would become more complex, as groups of unrelated business activities would be bundled 
under the same EU IPU structure. In this sense, this goes against the direction of travel 
regarding the “governance” element of ESG.  
 
Bringing asset managers into the scope of EU IPU would significantly complicate recovery 
and resolution planning for in-scope third-country groups, when significant efforts have been 
made to enhance the resolvability of such third -country groups since the introduction of the 
Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive.

Drafting Solution 
 
In order to recalibrate the scope of the EU IPU provision so as to exclude MiFID -authorised 
asset managers, as originally intended and drafted, we suggest the following amendment to 
CRD5 Article 21b(5)(b). This should restore the application of EU IPU to credit institutions and 
MiFID authorised investment firms but excluding asset managers. 
 
In marked-up format (marked up from the version as already amended by IFD Article 62(9)): 
 
Article 21b 
 
Intermediate EU parent undertaking 
 
… 
 
5. For the purposes of this Article [21b]: …  
 
(b) the term “institution” shall also include investment firms , but excluding 
those “investment firms”: 
 
(i) which are not authorised to provide the ancillary service referred 
to in point (1) of Section B of Annex 1 to Directive 2016/65/EU 
[MiFID II] 
 
(ii) which provide only one of more of the investment services and 
activities in points (1), (2), (4) and (5) of Section A of Annex I to 
Directive 2016/65/EU [MiFID II] 
 
(iii) which are not permitted to hold money or securities belonging to 
their clients and which for that reason may not at any time place 
themselves in debt with those clients.

Internal TLAC - scaling 
 
CRR2 Article 92b (Requirements for own funds and eligible liabilities for non -EU G -SIIs) 
became applicable on 27 June 2019.  
 
CRR2 Article 92b sets the internal TLAC scalar at 90% of the external TLAC requirement that 
would have applied (under Article 92a) if the material subsidiary was a resolution entity. 
 
The FSB TLAC Term Sheet (9 November 2015) recommended an internal TLAC range of 75-
90% (ultimately to be determined by supervisors).  
 
Our concern is that setting the internal TLAC scalar at 90% may result in extensive pre -
positioning of capital requirements in several jurisdictions, if other jurisdicti ons take the EU’s 
90% approach into account. In addition, this approach does not align with an overall approach 
of adhering to international standards. 
 
This can be addressed by some drafting amendments to CRR2 Article 92b(1): 
 
In marked-up format: 
 
Article 92b 
 
Requirement for own funds and eligible liabilities for non-EU G-SIIs 
 
1. An institution Institutions that is a are material subsidiary subsidiaries of a non-EU 
G-SII G-SIIs and that is are not a resolution entity entities shall at all times satisfy 
requirements for own funds and eligible liabilities equal to a percentage (as 
determined by the institution’s resolution authority)  90 % of the requirements for 
own funds and liabilities laid down in Article 92a. The percentage determined by 
the resolution authority shall be between 75-90 %. 
 
Clean version: 
 
Article 92b 
 
Requirement for own funds and eligible liabilities for non-EU G-SIIs 
 
1. An institution that is a material subsidiary of a non -EU G -SII and that is not a 
resolution entity shall at all times satisfy requirements for own funds and eligible 
liabilities equal to a percentage (as determined by the institution’s resolution 
authority) of the requirements for own funds and liabilities laid down in Article 92a. 
The percentage determined by the resolution authority shall be between 75-90 %.

8. SUSTAINABLE FINANCE 
 
Q191: In your view, which further measures, if any, could be taken to incorporate ESG 
risks into prudential regulation without pre -empting ongoing work as set out 
above? Please elaborate and provide relevant evidence to substantiate your 
view. 
 
Policymakers may wish to consider introducing a "green supporting factor" (GSF) into 
the EU's prudential framework, in order to support sustainable finance and the 
transition to a net-carbon-zero economy.  
 
Arguments in support of introducing a GSF include: 
 
 the European Green Deal, launched by the Commission on 11 December 2019, is 
one of the six Commission priorities for 2019-24. 
 
 successful implementation of the European Green Deal will require action from 
both the public and the private sectors. In particular, it will require private -sector 
financing. Enhancing the CMU can boost private-sector financing in this regard. 
 
 dealing with climate change is generally viewed both as an urgent issue, and an 
issue of magnitude. Many policy levers will need to be deployed in order to address 
climate change, including but not limited to regulatory and fiscal levers. The GSF 
could be an additional, complementary policy lever. 
 
 introducing a GSF serves as a signal to the wider market that dealing with climate 
change is a top priority of policymakers. 
 
 the need for any transition to be a "just transition", providing suitable impetus for 
the financial and non -financial sectors to make the necessary changes to a net -
carbon-zero economy in the timeframes required, whilst "leaving no one behind". 
 
Arguments against introducing a GSF tend to centre around a view that the prudential 
framework should not be used to implement wider policy/societal issues; that the 
primary purpose of the prudenti al framework is to protect the stability of the financial 
system, and should not be used for other purposes.  
 
Other arguments against introducing a GSF include that there is insufficient evidence 
and data at this stage to demonstrate that "green lending" is less risky than "non-green 
lending". The EBA Action Plan on Sustainable Finance, published on 6 December 
2019, indicates a roadmap for the prudential treatment of assets from a sustainability 
perspective, consisting of a discussion paper in 2022 -24, and a final report in 2025.  
(However, some policymakers may view this timetable as lacking sufficient urgency.) 
 
In addition, the prudential framework is underpinned by standards set at the global 
level (Basel/BCBS), and at this stage , a GSF has not been intr oduced at the global 
level. (However, some policymakers may be concerned that the BCBS process may 
take too long to deal with a topic generally viewed as urgent.) 
 
 
 
 
 
 
12 
 
If a GSF is to be introduced, its exact features would n eed to be designed . 
Considerations include: 
 
 the need for a clear delineation between what is "green" and what is "not green" 
for GSF purposes. The EU's sustainable finance taxonomy  may be useful in this 
regard. Indeed, there is merit in using  the sustainable finance taxonomy (once in 
place) for the purposes of the GSF , in order to ensure horizontal consistency  
between prudential legislation and other legislation. 
 
 the GSF calibration (assuming it would be similar in concept to the SMF supporting 
factor). If the GSF coefficient is set too low, this may cause concern that prudential 
requirements will generally be too low , relative to the risks that the prudential 
framework is seeking to address. However, if the GSF coefficient is set too high 
(ie, too close to 1), then it may not provide sufficient impetus for a rapid transition 
to the green economy. 
 
 whether the GSF should be a single coefficient, or a set of coefficients 
differentiating between the extent to which an investment or exposure is 
considered "green" (i .e., a lower GSF for highly -green investment) . H owever, 
unless the sustainable finance taxonomy makes these distinctions, then separate 
decisions by policymakers on where to place the boundary  between “green”, 
“highly-green”, etc, would be needed. 
 
The introduction of a GSF into the prudential framework is more than a technical issue; 
it clearly has political dimensions. Therefore, it is important that any decision to 
introduce a GSF has political backing . P olicymakers would need to expla in to the 
electorate w hy it is being implemented, and its role as part of a broader range of 
measures to deliver a top policy priority. 
 
We would also note that if a decision is made to introduce a GSF, it should not be 
accompanied by any "brown penalising factor". In order to achieve a "just transition" 
and to ensure that the necessary financing is available to enable “brown” industries to 
transition to the green economy, it would be counter -productive to disincentivise the 
necessary finance that would enable them to move towards a sustainable economy. A 
“brown penalising factor ” may also have a disproportionate impact on some EU 
member states, where there has been greater dependency on “brown” industries. 
Accordingly, we would encourage policymakers to focus on the benefits of the GSF in 
order to effect change.

9. FIT AND PROPER PERSONS 
 
Q192: What would be the benefits and drawbacks of including the requirement for 
competent authorities to perform a fit and proper assessment of at least some 
key function holders in the CRD? 
 
The UK’s Senior Managers and Certification Regime (“SM&CR”) has been in place for 
banks since March 2016, insurers since December 2018, and all other firms subject to 
FCA regulation since 9 December 2019. 
 
Although the regime is now becoming part of the “ business as usual” environment in 
the UK, its implementation was challenging . One reason for this was because the 
introduction of SM&CR raised questions regarding the interaction of SM&CR with other 
areas of law, including: 
 corporate law (including directors’ duties, and the collective responsibility of the 
board) 
 employment law 
 privacy and data protection 
 regulation of professions (where relevant) 
 individual suitability under other legislation, eg, MiFID 
 
Many of these issues had to be considered as SM&CR was rolled out. In some cases, 
the outcome of interaction has not yet been fully tested, for example, by way of judicial 
decisions that clarify which area of law has precedence in the event of conflict between 
them. And this challenge relates to introducing the SM&CR in one jurisdiction only – 
the UK. 
 
We therefore think that there may be practical challenges in seeking EU harmonisation 
of laws related to fit and proper assessments, in the context of introducing harmonised 
SM&CR-style regimes, when t he same challenges as found by the UK would also 
apply in the other EU member states when considered individually.  
 
The challenge is then multiplied further by the fact that EU member states have not yet 
harmonised their corporate law (e.g., some jurisdictions have a unitary board system, 
others have a supervisory board and a management board), employment law, laws 
relating to regulation of professions – among other things. 
 
It may therefore be useful for the Commission to consider the extent to which 
harmonisation of some of the other areas of law would be desirable or necessary in 
order to achieve the objectives of a harmonised fit and proper persons regime.  
 
 
Q201: Considering a scenario in which at least some fit and proper assessments were 
to be conducted by competent authorities ex ante, what would be, for you, the 
costs and benefits of a deadline for the assessment of proposed board members 
being set in the CRD? What would you consider a reasonable time for the 
assessment? 
 
BNY Mellon would support a maximum 30 day timeframe for completion of ex-ante fit 
and proper assessments, in regard to prospective board members or others who would 
be subject to the fit and proper requirements.  
 
The timeframe should run from the time the relevant forms are s ubmitted to the 
competent authority. In the event that the assessment is not completed within this time 
 
14 
 
(ie, no communication either way from the competent authority), then it should be 
deemed approved. If the competent authority needs more information or time to make 
an assessment, the competent authority should have the ability to extend the 
assessment period by an additional 30 days, with notice of extension being provided 
before the end of the original 30 day deadline. However, there should be no more 
extensions beyond this – at the 60 day limit, the assessment should be deemed 
approved unless it is specifically rejected by the competent authority. 
 
The need for tight timeframes is important. Firstly, the EU operates in a globally 
competitive employment market, especially in regard to the more senior functions, as 
people at those levels tend to be more geographically mobile. An inefficient process 
for fit and proper assessments may make it difficult for institutions in the EU to attract 
and retain the tal ent they need, and those adversely impacted may look at other 
jurisdictions. Also in this regard, those in senior functions may also have skills that are 
transferable to other economic sectors. So in addition to geographic mobility,

Secondly, although the competent authority should “have a say” on recruitment via this 
process, the ultimate responsibility must rest with the institutions concern ed. 
Competent authorities can take action via ex-post processes. 
 
Thirdly, it is also important to consider the impact upon the individuals involved. 
Individuals will want certainty in this area, not only for themselves, but for their family 
members, especially if relocation decisions are also involved. Individuals may be 
reluctant to give notice in regard to their current role, if their next role is subject to a 
contingent process. In particular, many individuals would rather have their application 
rejected in a maximum 60 day period – knowing that they can then consider other 
opportunities (or stay where they are), rather than have this type of process extended 
over many months. 
 
Fourthly, it is undesirable to have key roles unfilled for long periods of time. Whilst it 
may be possible to have interim appointments, this is not desirable as a long -term 
strategy. 
 
Having strict timelines will help to reduce unnecessary barriers to an efficiently 
functioning labour market. 
 
 
Q212: What do you consider woul d be the benefits of, and/or difficulties encountered 
in, including the ability to create and promote the organisation’s desired culture 
as part of the “fit and proper” assessment of members of the management body? 
 
The practical difficulty is that it is d ifficult to “legislate for” good culture  (as many 
regulators would acknowledge) , and there is no “one size fits all” approach to what 
constitutes good culture. This may make it harder for supervisors to assess whether 
the individual’s ability to create and promote a particular corporate culture aligns with 
the “fit and proper” assessment. It is, however, easier to observe the outcome of 
conduct, and there are many rules and codes dealing with conduct.  
 
Ultimately, cultural change needs to be driven by orga nisations themselves. 
Nevertheless the implementation of a “fit and proper” regime  can help to create the 
conditions in which good culture is emphasised as a priority.

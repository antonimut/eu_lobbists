A. Introduction  
Deutsche Börse Group (DBG) welcomes the opportunity to comment on DG FISMA Public Consultation 
Document ‘Implementing the finalisation of Basel III in the EU’ published on 11 October 2019. 
DBG operates in the area of financial markets along the complete chain of trading, clearing, settlement 
and custody of securities, derivatives and other financial instruments and acts as such as a provider 
of highly regulated financial market infrastructures (FMIs). 
Among others, Clearstream Banking S.A., Luxembourg and Clearstream Banking AG, Frankfurt/Main, 
acting as (I)CSD1, as well as Eurex Clearing AG as a leading European Central Counterparty (CCP), are 
authorised as credit institutions within the scope of the European Capital Requirements Directive 
(CRD) and Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR). The Clearstream subgroup is supervised on a 
consolidated level as a financial holding group.  
Moreover, Eurex Clearing AG and European Commodities Clearing AG are both authorised as a CCP 
under Regulation (EU) No. 648/2012 (EMIR) and Clearstream Banking AG, Clearstream Banking S.A. 
as well as LuxCSD S.A. are CSDs, which will be authorised according to Regulation (EU) No. 
909/2014 (CSDR) in the future.  
Any banking services performed by our FMIs are only ancillary to their core functions and clearly limited 
by law to support their role to secure financial markets stability. 
Across all our group entities, the respective balance sheets contain mainly exposures towards credit 
institutions (collateralised), towards central banks and sovereign debt. Our businesses are due to the 
function as financial market infrastructures risk-averse. As such, financial exposures to our entities 
resulting from credit, counterparty or market risks are in general low. Contrary, operational risks are 
the driving risk factor and receive appropriate attention. 
For determining the credit risk own funds requirements, all our group entities are using the 
standardised approach. As none of our entities is performing trading on own account calculation of 
CVA risk as well as market risk results only in marginal own funds requirements compared to the other 
risk capital charges. 
The Clearstream subgroup, containing Clearstream Banking S.A., Luxembourg and Clearstream 
Banking AG, is currently using the Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA) while Eurex Clearing AG, 
European Commodities Clearing AG as well as LuxCSD S.A. are applying the Basic Indicator Approach 
(BIA) for determining the operational risk capital charge.  
Part B of the document at hand contains our general comments on the prudential standards developed 
by the Basel Committee to finalise the Basel III reforms and EU Commissions thoughts to implement 
them into applicable law. Dedicated responses to the questions raised within the consultative 
document, which do affect our businesses are included in part C of this document.  
B. General comments 
While we generally consider the finalized Basel III rules and their potential implementation in EU law 
as largely appropriate, we would like to highlight the importance of a proportionate implementation 
within the EU to account appropriately for the diverse financial market and particularly banking sector.

The transposition of the final Basel III rules into EU legislation needs to follow the principle of 
proportionality and account for the various differences resulting from business activities, size and 
complexity in an appropriate manner. In this regard different business models and their inherent risk 
profiles need to be considered. The treatment of FMIs in scope of the rules either based on the status 
as credit institution resulting from banking services being ancillary to their core activities or based on 
the usage of parts of the banking framework within the FMI legislation (i.a. the capital framework) 
should be appropriate.  
As such, we clearly reject the introduction of a 10% credit conversion factor (CCF) for unconditionally 
cancellable commitments (as described in paragraph 84 of the revised BCBS ruleset), as a positive 
CCF might have adverse effects on the provision of money transmission including the execution of 
payment, clearing, settlement and custody services in any currency and correspondent banking or 
financial instruments. In this field, unconditionally cancellable commitments are used to foster efficient 
interbank operations during the day and only exceptional overnight. Hence, the introduction of a 
positive CCF for such commitments might limit or negatively affect interbank operation.   
While we fully support the introduction of one standardised approach for operational risk that replaces 
the existing approaches, we would like to highlight, that the calculation method adds complexity in 
comparison to the current Basic Indicator Approach (BIA) for operational risk. We are furthermore of 
the opinion that selected components of the Business Indicator (BI) might not capture institutions’ 
operational risk profile adequately.  
The determination of the BI and necessary definition of interest income, interest expenses and interest 
earning assets might be subject to inconsistencies resulting from differences in accounting frameworks. 
As such, the determination of the BI should be accounting standards neutral. We believe the EU 
supervisory banking framework should follow definitions as well as categories of the EU directive on 
the annual accounts and consolidated accounts of banks and other financial institutions (Council 
Directive 86/635/EEC). We support a carefully developed mapping table, as mentioned in the 
consultative document, as it could provide clarification and unburden institutions from developing own 
mapping templates. This is of particular relevance, when using any other accounting standards (e.g. 
IFRS) or national accounting standards of a non-EU country. 
Moreover, the implementation of rules on centralised supervisory reporting and disclosure but also the 
requirements related to data on operational risk losses might constitute challenges for institutions as 
a high quality of data cubes or automatically generated data might not be ensured as of today.

C. Response to selected questions raised in the consultative document  
 
1. Credit risk 
1.1. Standardised Approach (SA-CR) 
Question 2: Would you deem refinements or clarifications necessary concerning the approach that you 
generally prefer, and if yes, what would be their prudential rationale? Please elaborate and provide 
relevant evidence. 
We would like to seize this question to highlight one point of uncertainty not directly relating to changes 
on the standardised approach for credit risk outlined within the consultation document at hand.  
The finalised Basel III rules do not yet contain changes on the treatment of exposures on sovereigns. 
The rules applicable for these exposures continue to allow deriving the respective risk weight from the 
Country Risk Classification (CRC, s. paragraph 9 of the finalized Basel III ruleset 2, d424). In this 
context, it is to be noted that the OECD since 2013 does not give CRCs to High Income OECD Countries 
(nor to High Income Euro Area Countries). Considering OECD rating as baseline, High Income OECD 
Countries are currently regarded as unrated. Consequently, exposures towards sovereigns would be at 
the lower end of the risk scale although in fact being viewed as less risky that countries with a top 
classification of ‘0’. We suggest to formally clarify that High-Income OECD Countries are being treated 
like countries with a CRC/minimum export insurance premium (MEIP) of ‘0’. 
Question 6: Views are sought on the costs and benefits of implementing the definition of grades under 
the SCRA provided by the Basel III standards (paragraphs 22-29). Please provide relevant evidence 
to substantiate your views. 
The introduction of the SCRA for ‘unrated’ bank exposures will lead to substantial efforts. Monitoring 
and assessing the ‘published minimum regulatory requirements’ of each unrated counterparty 
constitute an extensive workload. By implementing the technical details of the BCBS ruleset, an 
adequate transitional period must be ensured. 
Question 9: Would you deem any other or further clarifications necessary to perform the classification 
into the three grades? Please elaborate and provide relevant evidence. 
Depending on the respective institutions’ size and complexity, frequency of disclosure of own funds 
subject to Article 447 CRR will vary between annual and semi-annual disclosure. Moreover, respective 
disclosure dates will vary across institutions.  
Clarification is requested regarding the expected frequency and timing of re-classification of exposures 
to institutions into grades. As disclosure dates might differ considerably between institutions, obliging 
institutions to keep track of changes in adherence of the respective institutions to minimum 
requirements will create a considerable additional administrative burden potentially disproportionate 
to the risks mitigated. How often will institutions have to update grades and by which date will changed 
grades have to be considered in determining exposures’ risk weights? Expected frequency and timing 
should be set proportionate to potential risks.

Question 57: What are the costs and benefits of the new CCF introduced by the Basel III standards? 
In particular, how does the Basel III treatment of OBS items compare to the current treatment in terms

We clearly reject the introduction of a 10% credit conversion factor (CCF) for unconditionally

This is particularly true for such commitments in relation to the provision of money transmission

and the efficient exchange of funds between banks especially for the purposes named above.

3. Operational risk 
3.1. Discretion to set the ILM equal to 1 
Question 123: How would exercising the discretion affect the link between capital incentives and 
management of operational risks? Please elaborate.  
As we have outlined further below, we consider some of the elements of the Business Indicator (BI) 
as not fully adequate to mirror operational risks appropriately. Particularly the financial as well as the 
interest component might increase the capital requirements without relating to underlying operational 
risks. As such, we consider the determination of an appropriate and institution specific Internal Loss 
Multiplier (ILM) to account for potential inadequate tensions of the BI as of high importance, for all, 
bucket 1-3, institutions.  
Under consideration of the aforementioned, we are of the opinion that competent authorities should 
be free to set an institution or country specific ILM, including neutralising the effects of the ILM by 
setting it to 1. Such decision should be based on transparent and comprehensible criteria and justified 
by the respective competent authority respectively.  
Competent authorities should consider that the new standardised approach for operational risk might 
increase the incentive to reduce the occurrence of operational risk loss events and therefore encourage 
institutions to improve their operational risk management. In the first place this applies for institutions 
in bucket 2 and 3, where the ILM reflects past operational risk loss events and constitutes an important 
component of the capital charge calculation. Likewise, institutions in bucket 1 (at least at the upper 
end of the bucket) may increase their effort in operational risk management as the ILM might be 
relevant within the next years. Irrespective of this, we think that even with an ILM equal to 1, incentives 
to improve the operational risk management are provided, as the new approach requires complete and 
high-quality operational risk loss history independent of the bucket and ILM.

Question 124: Would you deem it necessary to mitigate possible cliff effects that might derive from 
the introduction of an institution-specific ILM? If so, which measures should be considered, for how 
long should they be applicable, and what would be the prudential rationale to implement them? Please 
elaborate. 
Mitigation of possible cliff effects might be necessary for institutions expecting a major capital increase 
due to the new approach. For institutions with largely increasing requirements, the new approach 
could be gradually phased-in (depending on the expected increase in capital requirements) e.g. during 
a period of up to three years by increasing the ILM annually to its defined level. Otherwise, 
implementation of the institution specific “correct” ILM, at the very beginning, might lead to an abrupt 
major capital increase. 
3.2. Discretion to increase the loss data threshold to EUR 100,000 
Question 125: What are your views on how a loss data threshold that is increased for some institutions 
may affect the soundness and risk-sensitivity of the operational risk framework, the volatility of the 
ILM, its comparability between institutions, and the incentive to carefully manage small to medium-
sized losses? Please specify your views. 
We consider a loss data threshold which corresponds to the size and complexity of the institution in 
combination with a fixed estimate for lower operational risk loss events as expedient to avoid 
uncomplete and unqualified loss history. In case a higher loss data threshold is considered appropriate 
for the respective institution, a fixed lump estimate for lower operational risk loss events is of 
importance, as otherwise institutions with lower thresholds might be relatively disadvantaged 
compared to institutions with higher threshold, as the latter mentioned might benefit from excluding 
lower operational risk losses. 
Generally, institutions should be free to apply a lower threshold (e.g. EUR 20,000) or a higher 
threshold (e.g. EUR 100,000). For setting the appropriate threshold, relation to the BI might be 
considered.   
Question 126: If the discretion was retained, which conditions and criteria should be introduced in 
order to ensure a level playing field in its application by supervisors? Please elaborate. 
As already mentioned above, we consider a threshold corresponding to the size and complexity of the 
institution to be useful. The BI could be used as a transparent and comparable criterion for seize in 
addition to a criterion representing the complexity of the institution.  
Question 127: Which threshold (EUR 20,000 or EUR 100,000) would better reflect the current 
threshold used for your loss data collection? Please elaborate and provide relevant evidence. 
We would like to point out that clarification is demanded on whether the mentioned thresholds are 
referring to gross or net loss data. The BCBS paper on the finalisation of Basel III 3 requires banks to 
identify gross loss amounts, whereas banks should also use net losses in the loss dataset (paragraphs 
22-23). Alignment with the current Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2018/959 (AMA RTS) on 
the use of the Advanced Measurement Approaches (AMA) for operational risk, which is generally 
referring to gross loss amounts, might be considered. Moreover, we would appreciate explicit 
clarification on the handling of positive operational risk events, leading to a gain instead of loss, within 
the new standardised approach.

As we currently consider a loss data collection threshold of EUR 10,000 net, we assume that the 
mentioned threshold of EUR 20,000 will reflect our current threshold better compared to EUR 
100,000.  
3.3. Discretion to use the ILM for bucket 1 institutions 
Question 128: What are your views on how this discretion might affect the overall level of own funds 
for operational risk of bucket 1 institutions and the comparability within bucket 1? Please elaborate 
your views. 
While introduction of a binding institution specific ILM for bucket 1 institutions would create a 
disproportionate operational burden for those and probably decrease comparability across bucket 1 
institutions, we consider allowing for reasonable discretions as useful.  
Particularly, bucket 1 institutions being close to the threshold for bucket 2 would benefit from such 
discretions, as it might decrease potential cliff effects. As cliff effects would be reduced, potential 
incentives to remain within bucket 1 (e.g. through artificial transactions) might be reduced as well. 
The potential use of ILM for smaller institutions might moreover incentivise those to build high quality 
loss databases or further improve management of operational risks, as it would have a direct effect on 
their minimum capital requirements for operational risk.  
With a given high quality of the institution’s internal loss data as well as an adequate loss data 
collection process in place, we see no reasons to generally prohibit bucket 1 institutions to use an 
institution-specific ILM although it might lead to lower (but probably more adequate) capital 
requirements.  
Question 129: If the discretion was retained, which conditions and criteria should be introduced in 
order to ensure a level playing field in its application by supervisors? Please elaborate. 
Most important, strong criteria on the quality of the internal loss data as well as the overall loss data 
collection process should be required to make use of such discretion.  
Question 130: If the discretion was retained, do you consider this could help smoothing the 
transitioning of institutions from Bucket 1 to Bucket 2? Please elaborate. 
Yes, this is expected as elaborated above. 
3.4. Discretion to request institutions to use less than five years when the ILM is greater than 1 
Question 131: What are your views on the discretion for supervisory authorities to request the 
institutions to use less than 5 years of loss data (when the ILM >1)? In which circumstances would 
such a request be justified? Please elaborate and provide relevant evidence. 
We consider such exception as generally useful, whereas an exception should be linked less to the 
currently used approach (e.g. 10 years for an institution using the AMA approach) but rather to the 
ensured quality of the internal loss data and the corresponding loss data collection process in place.

3.5. Exclusion of certain operational risk loss events 
Question 132: What would you consider to be the appropriate thresholds for allowing a request for 
exclusion of loss events from loss data history, for current and divested activities? Please explain and 
provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
Decisions to exclude specific loss events from the calculation should be based on clear and transparent 
qualitative criteria to be specified by the competent authorities or EU standard setting institutions and 
should be independent from a given materiality threshold.  
Question 133: What would be in your view an appropriate minimum retention period for the losses 
that will be excluded from the loss dataset? What would be an appropriate starting point of this period? 
Please explain and provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
We consider a retention period of three years, as suggested, as sufficient.  
3.6. Other operational risk topics 
Question 134: What are your views on retaining the aforementioned CRR provisions and adapting the 
corresponding CDR provisions with a view to maintain their binding status? 
To ensure a smooth transposition from the existing approaches to the new standardised approach for 
operational risk, we clearly support a close alignment with existing and currently applicable 
requirements. At least for AMA institutions, an alignment between the specifications outlined in 
Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2018/959 (RTS on AMA) and the new standardised approach 
for operational risk (e.g. EUR 20,000 gross data loss as threshold) is desired to reduce the operational 
burden for implementing new requirements to the extent possible. For institutions, currently using the 
Basic Indicator Approach, alignment regarding the accounting figures to be used for calculating the BI 
should be ensured.  
Question 135: Does your institution already comply with the relevant requirements? Please list the 
requirements that are not currently applicable to your institution and whether there is any additional 
operational burden associated with achieving compliance. 
Although the AMA is used for calculating capital requirements for operational risk within the 
Clearstream Group, selected aspects of the new standardised approach will constitute new 
requirements, such as the accounting date as well as the data collection threshold (we currently 
consider EUR 10,000 net as data collection threshold). To ensure compliance with the upcoming 
requirements related to the standardised approach, we are running a SA-Readiness initiative which is 
planned until end of 2020. 
Question 136: Are there any concerns in terms of proportionality that you would consider important 
to raise? Which threshold would you consider appropriate for the applicability of the governance and 
organisational requirements? Please elaborate. 
There are no concerns in terms of proportionality yet. We plan to implement the regulatory threshold 
of EUR 20,000 gross which is not that far away from our currently used internal threshold.

Question 137: What are your views on requiring the inclusion of the abovementioned elements 
(internal loss data, scenarios, external loss data and key risk indicators) in the ICAAP for operational 
risk? Please explain your reasoning in case of disagreement (separately for each element). 
The internal loss data is assumed to be a reliable source of information for the normative perspective 
of the ICAAP. Other elements should be considered in the economic perspective of the ICAAP, in 
contrast to the normative perspective as they currently don’t allow for comparability between 
institutions.  
Question 138: Would you deem further refinements or clarifications necessary concerning the ICAAP 
for operational risk, and if yes, what would those be and what would be their prudential rationale? 
Please elaborate and provide relevant evidence. 
We currently see no need for further refinements or clarifications necessary concerning the ICAAP for 
operational risk as we have an internal quantification model in place which is used in the economic 
perspective. 
Question 139: What threshold would you consider appropriate for the applicability of the 
aforementioned ICAAP requirements for Pillar 2? Please elaborate. 
Generally, we do not consider a set threshold as appropriate. A potential threshold should depend on 
the size and complexity of the institution as well as the quality and scope of the operational risk 
management framework in place. 
Question 140: What are your views on the costs and benefits of using FINREP templates as a reference 
for a harmonised identification of BIC items in the EU? Please substantiate your views with relevant 
evidence. 
While we are generally of the opinion that institutions would benefit from using available structured 
and comparable data like FINREP data, we would like to express our concern regarding the current 
determination of the financial component (FC) and the interest, leases and dividend component (ILID) 
in the BI and their appropriateness for capturing operational risk.  
Efficient trading strategies, irrespective of existing appropriate and efficient controls, might lead to high 
charges for operational risk through increasing the FC. In contrast to this, less sophisticated trading 
strategies leading to a close to zero net P&L on the trading book or the banking book do not receive 
any relevant capital charge at all (though missing controls could be the reason).  
Further, while we generally understand Basel’s intention to capture operational risk stemming from an 
institution’s interest-bearing business through including interest figures in the ILDC, we question a 
relationship between interest income and potential underlying operational risk. The magnitude of the 
interest income is depending on the interest rate level and the margins, though neither the interest 
rate level nor the margins are an adequate indicator of the operational risk of a bank. E.g. given an 
environment of low or negative interest rates resulting in a bank’s negative interest income, the absolute 
amount of the bank’s net interest income seems not to be the appropriate figure for the ILDC. 
We strongly encourage the EU to consider the abovementioned points to adequately account for 
potential unintended effects in implementing the new standardised approach for operational risk. As 
considered appropriate, potentially unintended adverse effects resulting from calculating the BI might 
be compensated through an adequate ILM.

Question 141: What are your views on introducing a mapping table via Level 2 measures to allow for 
timely updates in case the corresponding FINREP standards change? Please elaborate. 
We support consideration on providing a mapping table through Level 2 measures to account for 
differences in accounting standards used as it will contribute to transparency and increase 
comparability across institutions.  
Particularly, the determination of the Business Indicator (BI) and necessary definition of interest 
income, interest expenses and interest earning assets might be subject to inconsistencies resulting 
from differences in accounting frameworks. As such, the determination of the BI should be accounting 
standards neutral. The envisaged mapping table should follow definitions as well as categories of the 
EU directive on the annual accounts and consolidated accounts of banks and other financial institutions 
(Council Directive 86/635/EEC) to carefully account for differences.  
3.8. Implementation challenges and administrative burden  
Question 144: Which elements of the revised SA-OR, if any, would you deem particularly challenging 
to be implemented? Please elaborate and rank your answers from the most challenging to the least 
challenging revision. Please provide relevant evidence on the one-off costs to substantiate your views. 
We expect building of an adequate internal loss database for determining an adequate ILM meeting

In addition, regarding the BI, the new SA-OR criteria of relevant income components differ from the

7. Centralised supervisory reporting and disclosures 
Question 188: Once EUCLID is fully implemented, would you support that the EBA, on the basis of 
the collected supervisory data from all institutions established in the Union, centrally discloses the 
information of all those institutions that are subject to disclosure requirements under CRR/D, thereby 
relieving institutions from mandatory disclosures? 
The trend to request raw data or direct access to data cubes instead of edited data with explanations 
is increasing across different areas and regulatory topics.  
Centralised disclosure through EBA on the basis of collected supervisory data might increase 
comparability across institutions and reduce operational processes for disclosing such data by 
individual institutions while it would most probably also erase institutions possibility to provide 
explanation on selected figures. This is of particular importance for specialised institutions like 
Page | 11  
 
institutions operating with a banking license and providing banking services with a highly limited 
scope. Intended objective comparability might therefore result in perceived comparability.  
While we generally do not object centralized disclosure by EBA as it might further incentivise 
institutions to implement efficient and reliable structures for data processing, erasing the necessity to 
manually adjusted data, we assume that the data quality of most institutions, particularly smaller 
institutions, is not yet sufficiently sophisticated to refrain from manual adjustments or explanations. 
Moreover, implementation of respective high-quality data structures might result in an unproportionate 
implementation burden for small institutions. Institutions should in any case get the opportunity to 
comment on selected data when disclosure is ensured through EBA.

9. Fit and proper 
9.1. Key function holders  
Question 192: What would be the benefits and drawbacks of including the requirement for competent 
authorities to perform a fit and proper assessment of at least some key function holders in the CRD? 
As already outlined within the consultative document, existing inconsistencies in applying the Joint 
ESMA and EBA Guidelines (EBA/GL/2017/12) would decrease through including requirements on fit 
and proper assessment of defined key function holders into the CRD. Notwithstanding this, the existing 
variations in applying the Joint EBA and ESMA Guidelines on the assessment of key function holders 
reflect the existing diversity in national particularities and can be regarded as a proportionate 
application of the guidelines. Through including the requirements on key function holders into the 
CRD, national competent authorities would have less freedom to apply the requirements in a 
proportionate manner, balancing costs and benefits effectively.  
While we generally support a common and harmonised application of regulatory requirements across 
the EU and acknowledge the pivotal role of key function holders in an institution, we follow the 
Page | 12  
 
argumentation of selected national competent authorities4 and doubt that a mandatory assessment of 
key function holders will by default considerably contribute to financial stability and risk reduction. 
Institutions’ processes for selecting key function holders and assessing their suitability (as part of the 
internal governance arrangements) are subject to regular reviews within the Supervisory Review and 
Evaluation Process such that a binding suitability assessment by competent authorities might create 
additional administrative burdens disproportionate to its benefits.  
To account for a balance between potential benefits and drawbacks resulting from a mandatory 
supervisory suitability assessment we suggest considering a broad and comprehensive application of 
proportionality, whereas significance of an institution shouldn’t be the only criterium for requiring 
mandatory supervisory assessments of key function holders.  
Question 193: In your view, would it be useful to identify key function holders in a descriptive manner, 
and/or to specify certain roles as belonging, by default, to the set of key function holders? Please 
consider the practical implications of each option and the need for clarity and consistent application 
across institutions and competent authorities. Please elaborate and provide evidence. 
Identification of key function holders should correspond to the risk profile of the respective institution, 
its business model as well as rights, obligations and powers related to the respective position, such 
that potential impacts from inadequately filled positions can be assessed comprehensively. While we 
acknowledge that selected positions might probably always be considered as key function holders (e.g. 
Head of Internal Audit) and that specification of certain roles as belonging by default to the set of key 
function holders will reduce inconsistencies, foster harmonisation and transparency and might reduce 
uncertainties, we primarily support a descriptive identification of key function holders. A descriptive 
identification of key function holders could take better account of institution specific risk profiles.  
9.2. Competent authorities’ assessment of the suitability of members of the management body 
Question 197: Please explain what you consider to be the advantages and disadvantages of competent 
authorities conducting ex ante and ex post approval, respectively, of suitability of members of the 
management body.  
As already stated within the consultative document, ex-ante approval of members of the management 
body would mitigate the risk of unsuitable candidates (temporary) within the management body. The 
biggest disadvantage of mandatory ex-ante suitability assessments we see is the risk of vacant position 
over a potentially long period of time. As the search and selection of suitable candidates for position 
in the management body can take some time, an ex-ante supervisory approval process taking several 
months might create or extent periods of vacant positions.  
As institutions have a strong intrinsic interest in filling open positions in the management body (as well 
as open positions of key function holders) with adequate candidates following guidance provided by 
competent authorities, we consider the risk of (ex-post) rejections by competent authorities as tangible 
but limited. This is particularly true, as competent authorities tent to be involved in selection processes 
already in an early stage trough e.g. discussions during supervisory visits or supervisory jour fixes.

Question 201: Considering a scenario in which at least some fit and proper assessments were to be 
conducted by competent authorities ex ante, what would be, for you, the costs and benefits of a 
deadline for the assessment of proposed board members being set in the CRD? What would you 
consider a reasonable period of time for the assessment? 
We consider a clear deadline as well as clear assessment criteria as of utmost importance for 
institutions to reduce uncertainties regarding such important aspects as the assignment of new board 
members to the extent possible. Without a clearly defined deadline as well as clearly defined criteria 
upon which the supervisory assessment will take place, uncertainties might arise, impacting 
transitional as well as regular ongoing processes within the institution.  
As long assessment periods are likely to create or increase uncertainties that might negatively impact 
ongoing business or processes, while short assessment periods might lower competent authorities’ 
ability to assess suitability of selected board members, maximum time periods should be carefully 
balanced. We believe that maximum time periods should be set proportionally to institutions’ risk 
profile, size and complexity. For systemic, interconnected or large institutions, the regular assessment 
period should not exceed three months in addition to a possible extension, whereas extensions should 
be communicated as early as possible to the respective institution and clearly correspond to open 
points identified. 
Question 204: Should the scope and format of fit and proper assessments be adapted to take into 
account the principle of proportionality, including in relation to any new provisions such as those 
discussed in Sections 9.2.1.1. and 9.2.1.2.? Please elaborate on your reply and provide examples.  
As already outlined in our answer to question no. 201, we consider the application of proportionality 
as reasonable and expedient. Both, the decision on mandatory ex-ante or ex-post assessment of 
members of the management board as well as the appropriate maximum time for assessing proposed 
board members, should be subject to proportionality.

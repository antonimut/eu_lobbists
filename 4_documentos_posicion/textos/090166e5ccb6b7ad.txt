All corporate financing activities 
Considering the mostly intermediated financing of the European economy, companies (among which 
small and medium-sized corporates) make very little use of rating agencies. Commitments to non-rated 
companies therefore represent more than 75% of European bank s’ portfolios (60% for French banks 
which take benefit from the Banque de France rating).  
 
Leasing 
Leasing is used by more European SMEs than any individual category of traditional bank lending 
taken altogether (around 45% of all European SMEs make use of leasing which is more than any 
other individual form of lending)1  
For example, for an ASF member which is a big player in leasing in the EU, retail SMEs represent 2/3 of 
its outstanding. For this retail SME portfolio, the current RWA calibration is 56% (75% X 76%). Moving 
to a 85% calibration would represent a raise by + 31% of corresponding RWA, which is a 2 BN € impact. 
Preventing retail SMEs from the benefit of the current CRR SME supporting factor would have a direct 
impact on their capacity to accede to financing solutions.

Q13  
All corporate financing activities 
In the EU, an investment grade quality corporate, but non-rated by external rating agencies recognised 
by the regulators would be penalised under the standardized approach regime , that would  not 
adequately reflect the risk of this corporate . S uch solid EU corporates would be disadvantaged 
compared to their US equivalent companies. This is detrimental to the financing of European small and 
mid-cap corporates. For French banks, internal studies show that the vast majority of unrated 
corporate exposures could be qualified as investment-grade exposures under SCRA. 
 
Q14 
Factoring 
Factoring is an asset-based lending activity that presents a very low risk profile.  In 2017, according to 
a survey made by the EUF (EU Federation of factoring), the loss expected by the factoring industry, 
measured by the average provision made for credit risk, was 0,09% across the EU. 
This is due to the fact that purchased receivables represent an efficient collateral for the amount 
advanced to clients (recourse factoring) in case of debtor default, with high recovery capabilities when 
the exposure is directly to the purchased receivables (non-recourse factoring). 
The ASF supports the EUF proposition to recognize the low risk profile of purchased receivables also 
under the ECRA through: 
i. the possibility to consider receivables as eligible guarantees under the Standardized Approach, 
thus applying the risk weight of the debtor, even in the case where the factoring agreement 
provides partial or full recourse to the client; 
ii. the possibility to apply, for purchased receivables to corporates, the definition of default at the 
facility level (invoice) 
iii. the application of a lower risk weight for exposures to purchased receivables to unrated corporate 
(or other entities), i.e. 50 to 75%. 
 
Q15  
Leasing 
ASF shares the following Leaseurope (EU federation of leasing) position: 
Within the process of implementing Basel III agreement, we suggest introducing in the European 
regulation a specific risk weight for leasing in the Standardized Approach. 
Risk sensitivity would much benefit from a differentiated prudential capital treatment for leasing. 
Credit risk weightings under prudential standards should reflect the real underlying risks, without 
adding undue complexity. Failure in this could  lead to otherwise healthy, beneficial lending being 
disincentivised in terms of capital allocation and cost of funding. With this in mind, we suggest there 
is a strong case to be made for differentiating lease finance (where the asset is owned by the finance 
company during the life of the agreement) with a specific risk weight. 
 
The unique feature of a lease is the lessor’s ownership of the leased asset. These ownership rights 
provide lessors with a valuable and efficient form of in -built security which makes leasing extremely 
low risk. Asset ownership represents a major advantage for lessors compared to other financial 
products such as traditional loans, which are typically not secured on physical assets but rather with 
financial collateral or personal guarantees. 
 
Loss rates within the leasing activity are low because the lessor is funding a physical asset crucial to 
the client’s core business activities. Businesses therefore prioritise lease payments because they need 
these assets to run their business. As the asset is a key working tool for the lessee, many defaulted 
4 
 
leases regrade back to a healthy situation with a zero loss. Additionally, ownership of the asset makes 
repossession relatively fast and straightforward for the lessor (if it is necessary at all). The lessor can 
then sell or re-lease the asset in order to decrease any losses on the default, resulting in low loss rates. 
If the value of the asset exceeds the amount outstanding at default, the lessor can actually make a gain 
in the case of a default. 
 
In Europe, Deloitte undertook extensive research on our behalf which demonstrates that the leasing 
business model leads to significantly lower risk compared to traditional lending. 2 The graph below 
shows the results of the research, which was based on a portfo lio of 3.3 million lease contracts in 15 
European countries. The graph below shows that default rates and LGDs for leasing Retail and 
Corporate exposures are significantly lower compared to bank lending averages. These leasing LGD 
figures are for stressed conditions, average loss rate figures are even lower. European capital 
requirements under the SA are also shown to be 10 times higher than the real risks for SME leases 
within the Retail class. 
 
  
 
This result is consistent with data for other equipment finance markets, for example in the US and 
Canada, confirming that businesses across any jurisdictions will prioritise paying for equipment finance 
because they need these assets to continue to run their businesses. 
 
As far as international standards are co ncerned, we think that attention should be paid to the 
appropriate calibration of capital requirements and to the need for implementing international 
standards in a way that makes sense for Europe’s diverse financial landscape. 
 
The above- mentioned Deloitt e findings on losses of lease transactions have been brought to the 
attention of the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) and of the European Banking 
Authority (EBA) in the context of their work on, respectively, “the Review of the Standardised Approach 
for Credit Risk” and the “Future of the IRB approach”. We have also shared the findings with the 
European Commission in our response to the consultations on “the finalisation of Basel III”, “the 
Possible Impact of the CRR and CRD IV on Banking Financing of the Economy” and “the Call for Evidence 
on the EU Regulatory Framework for Financial Services”.

considering suggesting any changes, we consider that the Eur opean Union can, and should, take the

Consumer credit : 
The concept of transactor is a US concept (broadly defined in the US literature but is not perfectly 
fitted to European specificities). “Transactor” does not constitute a specific class of customers for 
which a specific monitoring or treatment exists currently in the bank. Thus no specific identification of

provide correct identification of this new class. For instance EU transposition of this category should 
clarify that a revolving retail credit facility would be considered as ‘transactor’ if it has been repaid in 
full in the last 12 months. Moreover, the notion of “ each scheduled repayment date” can be 
interpreted in different ways in case of specific repayment contractual repayments schedule 
associated to a credit card utilization and then should also be precised.

1.1.6. Real estate (RE) exposures 
1.1.6.1. Loan splitting (LS) vs Whole Loan (WL) – (Q 34 à 36) 
 
Q34  
Real estate financing : 
It is widely recognized that there are different credit policies for home loans: the two main ones are 
distinguished by their diametrically opposed characteristics: one is “Loan -to-value / variable rate” 
model (e.g. UK, US) and the other the “Loan-to-Income / fixed rate” model. 
Thanks to its characteristics and the obligations to subscribe compulsory death and disability 
insurances, the “Loan -to-Income / fixed rate” model applied in France notably generates very low 
default rates. It is also risk sensitive, particularly to the risk of non -repayment (main risk in the RRE 
portfolio).  
However, the Basel standardized approach was calibrated on the basis of the Loan -to-Value model, 
with customers having a higher risk profile.  
8 
 
In Europe, this will result in risk weighted assets calibration totally disconnected from the approach 
adopted in “Loan -to-Income / fixed rate” banks  : it will, at least partially, undergo an increase in 
interest rates that is very likely to happen within the next 15 years.  
 
For example, if applied to a major player of real estate financing in France (30% of the residential 
estates credit outstanding), the Whole loan and Loan splitting approaches would lead to RWA 
calibration of respectively 34% and 33%, which is much above th e corresponding real risks observed 
on the French market over the past years.  
 
Real estate leasing: 
The revised risk weight treatment of real estate financing exposures will raise capital requirement for 
real estate leasing activities: in general, the inc reasing use of the “loan to value” parameter in the 
revised framework is detrimental to leasing compared to classical banking loans, since banking loans 
granting processes require usually a higher initial self- financing part than leasing. Then leasing is 
penalized by the new LTV parameters, whereas it usually presents a lower risk profile - due the direct 
ownership of the asset by the lender – than usual loans. 
 
Most real estate asset financed through leasing are buildings dedicated to corporates own activities: 
factories, workshops, sales spaces, offices etc… They do not generate revenues in the sense of the 
Basel III agreement: they are not considered as “property generating cash flows”.  Strictly applying the 
new Basel III classification, those exposures would be treated as “user estate”. The new loan to value 
Basel approach would result in a significant raise in required capital for real estate lessors. 
For instance, some ASF members specialised in real estate leasing estimate RWA would increase by 16 
bp which would increase prices of financing by 18 bp for corporates.  
One of them, which is a major pan-european player on the real estate leasing market, estimate a raised 
by +7% RWA if  applying the LS approach, and by + 22% if applying the WL approach. These figures 
illustrate the poor relevance of LTV criteria for the real estate leasing business, but the LS approach is 
to be prefered.

1.1.8.1. Definition of commitment (Q 54 à 56) 
 
Q55  
Leasing and factoring: 
Specialised credit activities -  such as leasing or factoring -  require at the least national discretion 
exempting them from the definition of commitments and would even require a direct exemption in 
the European regulation. 
For example: in the case of the Factoring, receivables or trade letter of credit businesses, whatever the 
credit granted to any entity (be it debtor or client), any financing operation is backed by the provision 
of an underlying document. This means that the part of the credit agreement (whatever its limit) that 
is not used would never be utilised without any prior assessment by the institution  of the underlying 
document provided.  
Therefore, a 0% CCF should apply in those specific cases.  
 
1.1.8.2. New credit conversion factors (CCF) – (Q 57 et 58) 
 
Q57 
All financing activities: 
The revised framework of “off- balance sheet items” will raise capital requirements  for specialised 
credit institutions (consumer and auto loans, leasing, factoring, guarantees). 
For example: for a significant French institution specialised in consumer credit  and representative of 
the French consumer credit market, setting the calibration of CCF from 0% to 10% for UCC and from 
20% to 40% for other CCF would lead to an RWA increase by +230M€. 
 
Q58 
Factoring: 
Article 82 specifies that a 40% CCF is to be used, but Article 83 specifies that a 20% CCF is to be used 
for self-liquidating letters of credit. Factoring credit commitments should also be applied a 20% CCF, 
i.e. the same as proposed for letters of credit. 
CRR clearly define the scope of the trade finance operations,  of which trade letters of credit are a 
subcategory, which underlying documentation is primarily a supporting invoice. There is no case for a 
difference to be made between trade letters of credit and factoring, since factoring is also defined in 
CRR as a subcategory of trade finance, and also totally relying on underlying invoices.  
Considering these points, we call for applying a 20% CCF also to factoring.  
Next to commitments mentioned above, limits on debtors exist in factoring. Those limits represent the 
amount u p to which a factor could accept to buy receivables on a given debtor. This is a simple 
assessment and in no way a commitment to buy. Consequently, those amounts should in no way be 
considered as commitments and suffer any CCF, even at 0%.

ASF shares the following Leaseurope (EU federation of leasing) position: 
Within the process of implementing Basel III agreement, we suggest introducing in the European 
regulation a specific risk weight for leasing in the Standardized Approach. 
Risk sensitivity would much benefit from a differentiated prudential capital treatment for leasing. 
Credit risk weightings under prudential standards should reflect the real underlying risks, without 
adding undue complexity. Failure in this could lead to otherw ise healthy, beneficial lending being 
disincentivised in terms of capital allocation and cost of funding. With this in mind, we suggest there 
is a strong case to be made for differentiating lease finance (where the asset is owned by the finance 
company during the life of the agreement) with a specific risk weight. 
 
The unique feature of a lease is the lessor’s ownership of the leased asset. These ownership rights 
provide lessors with a valuable and efficient form of in -built security which makes leasing ext remely 
low risk. Asset ownership represents a major advantage for lessors compared to other financial 
products such as traditional loans, which are typically not secured on physical assets but rather with 
financial collateral or personal guarantees. 
 
Loss rates within the leasing activity are low because the lessor is funding a physical asset crucial to 
the client’s core business activities. Businesses therefore prioritise lease payments because they need 
these assets to run their business. As the asset is a key working tool for the lessee, many defaulted 
leases regrade back to a healthy situation with a zero loss. Additionally, ownership of the asset makes 
repossession relatively fast and straightforward for the lessor (if it is necessary at all). The lesso r can 
then sell or re-lease the asset in order to decrease any losses on the default, resulting in low loss rates. 
If the value of the asset exceeds the amount outstanding at default, the lessor can actually make a gain 
in the case of a default.

business model leads to significantly lower risk compared to traditional lending. 4 The graph below

Basel III may lead to a large reduction in the volume of funds allocated to the most penalised 
activities by the output floor with a detrimental effect on the real economy. The most impacted

This is the case for institutions specialised in specific credit activities (leasing, consumer credit, 
factoring) which have invested significant amounts of money and time over the past years to 
implement internal models, and to have them validated by supervisors, mainly because their types of 
activities were already highly penalised under the SA approach. 
Against this background, t he ASF exorts the Commission  to apply the output floor at the highest 
consolidated level.  
An application at the ‘solo’ level of banking groups’ subsidiaries -  where low risk specialized credit 
activities are to be found –  would have a disproportionate prudential impact disadvantaging th e 
financing of EU consumers and SMEs. 
The ASF considers that for certain consumer credit groups, among the European leaders, the 
application of the output floor at the solo level would, on average, increase Risk Weighted Assets 
(RWA) by 5 to 8%. This average hides an increase in RWAs that could reach +30% to +50% for certain 
subsidiaries of these groups if the output floor were to be applied individually. Such an increase, which

Leasing: 
The output floor has a major impact on a ctivities already highly penalised under the SA approach, as 
for leasing for example, not recognised as a specific type of exposure under the Standardized approach 
whereas the property of the asset leads to a much lower risk profile  
The proposed outfloor to IRB models, based on the new Standardised Approach calculations, will still 
be more punitive for lessors, unless the SA calibrations are adjusted to reflect the real risks of leasing 
exposures.  
Therefore, we call for a more relevant treatment of leasing within the SA. 
 
ASF shares the following Leaseurope (EU federation of leasing) position: 
Within the process of implementing Basel III agreement, we suggest introducing in the European 
regulation a specific risk weight for leasing in the Standardized Approach. 
Risk sensitivity would much benefit from a differentiated prudential capital treatment for leasing. 
Credit risk weightings under prudential standards should reflect the real underlying risks, without 
adding undue complexity. Failure in this could lead to otherwise healthy, beneficial lending being 
disincentivised in terms of capital allocation and cost of funding. With this in mind, we suggest there 
is a strong case to be made for differentiating lease finance (where the asset is owned by the finance 
company during the life of the agreement) with a specific risk weight. 
 
The unique feature of a lease is the lessor’s ownership of the leased asset. These ownership rights 
provide lessors with a valuable and efficient form of in -built security which makes leasing extremely 
low risk. Asset ownership represents a major advantage  for lessors compared to other financial 
products such as traditional loans, which are typically not secured on physical assets but rather with 
financial collateral or personal guarantees. 
 
Loss rates within the leasing activity are low because the lessor is funding a physical asset crucial to 
the client’s core business activities. Businesses therefore prioritise lease payments because they need 
these assets to run their business. As the asset is a key working tool for the lessee, many defaulted 
leases regrade back to a healthy situation with a zero loss. Additionally, ownership of the asset makes 
repossession relatively fast and straightforward for the lessor (if it is necessary at all). The lessor can 
then sell or re-lease the asset in order to decrease any losses on the default, resulting in low loss rates. 
If the value of the asset exceeds the amount outstanding at default, the lessor can actually make a gain 
in the case of a default. 
 
In Europe, Deloitte undertook extensive research on our behalf which d emonstrates that the leasing 
business model leads to significantly lower risk compared to traditional lending. 6 The graph below 
shows the results of the research, which was based on a portfolio of 3.3 million lease contracts in 15 
European countries. The graph below shows that default rates and LGDs for leasing Retail and 
Corporate exposures are significantly lower compared to bank lending averages. These leasing LGD 
figures are for stressed conditions, average loss rate figures are even lower. European cap ital 
requirements under the SA are also shown to be 10 times higher than the real risks for SME leases 
within the Retail class.

Credit risk 
1) Views are sought on the relative costs and benefits of the ECRA provided 
by the final Basel III  standards and the SCRA? In particular, how do the 
two approaches compare in terms of risk -sensitivity, impact on risk -
weighted assets (RWAs) and operational burden? Please specify the 
relative costs and benefits of the two approaches for ex posures to i) 
institutions, ii) covered bonds and iii) corporates. Please provide relevant 
evidence to substantiate your views. 
The use of ECRA is currently allowed and widespread practice in  the EU for 
determining the applicable risk weights (RWs).  For large corporates, institutions 
and sovereigns (including debt instruments and covered bonds), the ECRA has clear 
advantages as compared to SCRA as it is operationally easy to use, more granular 
and more uniform. It is also clearly more risk sensitive as - in general - ECAI rating 
methodology is rigorous, transparent, updated periodically if needed (often after 
public consultation). Moreover, the external ratings as determined by ECAI's, are 
subject to specific regulation on recognition of ECAI's, whereby adeq uacy of credit 
ratings issued by credit rating agencies for regulatory purposes is subject to 
continuous monitoring by the EBA in cooperation with the other European 
Supervisory Authorities. Therefore, we are of the view that ECRA should be 
maintained with in EU in its cu rrent form, where possible and applicable 
complemented by an appropriate method for unrated exposures.   A special 
treatment for unrated corporates in the EU , that does not depend on the use of 
external ratings, as for example envisaged in paragraph 42 of t he Basel standard, 
is justified and a sufficiently prudent approach in bank -based economies like the 
EU, where banks have extensive information on the creditworthiness of their 
corporate consumers. Therefore, the absence of an external rating for corporate s 
is not necessarily an indicator for higher risk and the information collected by banks 
on their corporate customers can adequately substitute the information that would 
otherwise be provided by an ECAI (and this is especially the case for mid -sized 
companies), in order to identify the counterparties eligible for the abovementioned 
special treatment.

In addition to our more general considerations in the response to question 1 , for 
rated corporate exposures ECRA is evidently more risk sensitive than SCRA. The 
opposite holds true for unrated corporate exposures as SCRA differentiates 
between “investment grade” corporate (RW = 65%) and “non -investment grade” 
corporates. We feel it i s appropriate that for unrated exposures SCRA (or some 
other, even more approp riate method) adapted to the European specificities (see 
our general answer to question 2) should be available in the EU. 
 
2) Would you deem refinements or clarifications necessary concerning the 
approach that you generally prefer, and if yes, what would be t heir 
prudential rationale? Please elaborate and provide relevant evidence.  
Yes. In general, for reasons explained in detail in our answer to question 1, we are 
of the view that ECRA should be maintained within EU in its current form, where 
possible and applicable and complemented by an appropriate method for unrated 
exposures. 
A necessary refinement would be on the treatment of unrated corporates under 
ECRA. Banks’ lending to unrated corporates which are the vast majority in Europe 
is extremely penalized ra ther than being promoted under  the standardised 
approach for credit risk. Indeed, corporates make very little use of rating agencies 
and have therefore t o be considered as unrated corporates which are assigned a 
high-risk weight of 100% under the framework . Final Basel III Reforms allow an 
alternative identification of investment grade exposures for which it is envisaged 
to apply a 65% risk weight. But thi s treatment can only be used in jurisdictions 
where external credit ratings are not eligible for the calculation of prudential capital 
requirements. Therefore, an issue of level playing field with other jurisdictions, 
namely the US, arises. A pragmatic sol ution would be to use this investment 
grade/non-investment grade approach as a fall -back approach to th e external 
rating approach for unrated corporates in Europe. 
The application of the 65% risk weight requires the corporate (or its parent 
company) to hav e securities listed on a recognised exchange. This requirement 
increases the operational burden while i t does not add any value to the credit 
assessment of the corporate. For these reasons we suggest removing the 
requirement of “being listed” on a recognised stock exchange. 
For the sake of simplicity, when banks use an internal definition of investment 
grade consistent with the regulatory definition, they should be authorize d to base 
the determination of the investment-grade condition on the internal PD. Therefore, 
we propose to establish a threshold on the PD of unrated exposures below which 
exposures would be considered as investment grade and risk weighted at 65%.  
This proposal is supported by the fact that banks use internal models which are 
reviewed and approved by the ECB and EBA.

Implementing this additional step of explicitly assessing whether ratings are 
appropriate and prudent would only bring additional complexity into the process 
without adding additional clarity into the creditworthiness of the counterparty. 
External ratings are used by a large portion of the industry and make it possible to 
easily compare between parties. Individual interpre tation will erode this 
comparability of external ratings. Furthermore, as mentioned on page 6 of the 
consultation document, the adequacy of the credit ratings issued by credit rating 
agencies for regulatory purposes is already subject to continuous monitoring by the 
EBA in cooperation with the other European Supervisory Authorities which further 
enhances their reliability. 
Costs of implementing clarification would not just be direct expenses (one -off as 
well as recurring) and workload, but also that the different approaches in individual 
institutions render each institutions’ reporting opaquer and more difficult  to 
compare or benchmark. In comparison, the expected increase in risk sensitivity is 
hardly material. 
Furthermore, since, as a result of the due dil igence, the risk weights could never 
improve, the due diligence requirements will drive up the impact of the new 
standardized approach.  
 
4) If you are of the view that the CRR/D should be amended to clarify/specify 
the rules on due diligence requirements, wh at would constitute an 
appropriate approach in your view? Please specify and provide relevant 
evidence. 
The EBF  recommends a proportionate implementation of the due diligence 
requirements in case of use of external ratings. Comparison of external ratings with 
internal assessment of the creditworthiness of single borrowers should not be 
required when the external ratings are allowed. High -level analysis per 
homogeneous groups of borrowers or asset classes should be sufficient to confirm 
that the risk weights  assigned on the basis of external ratings are appropriate to 
the risk profile and characteristics of the borrowers. Bank s should also be allowed 
to delegate this task to centralised entities. The objective should be to ensure that 
compliance costs are not disproportionate, also in light of the action undertaken at 
the EU level to foster the reliability of external ratings. 
 
6) Views are sought on the costs and benefits of implementing the definition 
of grades under the SCRA provided by the Basel III standards (paragraphs 
22-29). Please provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views.  
The grades under the SCRA will ensure a  closer correspondence between risk and 
the capital treatment compared to the government ratings derived method which 
is positive. However, we question the need for having increased risk weights for EU 
institutions compared to the existing framework.

10) In your view, what are the relative costs and benefits of using the 
original maturity as opposed to the residual maturity for identifying short-
term interbank exposures? Please provide relevant arguments and 
evidence to substantiate your views. 
We are of the view that from a risk perspective, a short -term interbank exposure 
should be determined by the residual matur ity, rather than the original maturity. 
For example: the risk of a 2 month original and residual maturity interbank deposit 
can be expected to be similar to the risk of a 2 month residual interbank exposure 
that had an original maturity of 1 year. Therefor e, we would like to propose to 
refine paragraph 19 and to keep the current possibility under CRR to use the 
remaining maturity instead of original maturity for short term exposures to 
institutions. Moreover, the current treatment of the CRR is preferable o ver the 
differentiated treatments introduced by the BCBS standards. 
 
11) What are your views on the extension of  the scope of the 
preferential treatment for short-term interbank exposures under Basel III 
from three to six months for exposures to institutions t hat arise from the 
movement of goods across national borders? To what extent would the 
change in definition change the amount of exposures benefitting from the 
preferential treatment? Please provide relevant evidence to substantiate 
your views. 
We support extending the maturity. 
Moreover, we support extending the scope of the preferential treatment. Letters of 
credit and guarantees (‘trade finance’ products) are commonly used for payment 
and financing of international trade. Trade finance provides exporters  with risk 
mitigation and financing options, allowing them to safely transact a cross borders, 
also with les s-trusted counterparties. As such trade finance is a critical banking 
product and fundamental to the growth of international trade. Moreover , trade

finance is often used to support imports into less -developed countries of essential 
commodities, such as wheat, sugar, oil and fuels. 
The tenor of trade transactions is short, usually well below one year. The very short 
tenor of trade was already recognised  by the Basel Committee in 2011 (refer to 
BCBS 205). 
In contrast to the very short tenor of trade transactions, the Foundation IRB applies 
an effective maturity of 2.5 years, unless the national supervisor allows to use the 
actual maturity.  
We would recommend to allow the use of actual maturity in level 1 regulation. 
Under current CRR Article 120.2, there is a differentiation for the short term (below 
3 months) exposures to Financial Institutions. In our view, this is a reasonable 
measure to reflect that by nature shorter exposure should bear a lower risk.  
We consider t his case we consider as particularly significant for short -term trade 
finance self-liquidating exposures. As shown by ICC Trade Default Register, these 
assets are very short term and they al so have lower default rates for both, 
Corporate and Financial Institutions Debtors. As an example, Import L Cs have an 
average default rate of only 0,07% (2008 to 2017) or 0,19% for Trade Loans. In 
our view, this justifies for the treatment of short term co rporate exposure to also 
have a differential treatment, at least for Trade Finance related transactions. 
Regarding the extension of the differentiated treatment for 3 months to 6 months 
where there is an underlying flow of goods or services, we consider th is is a 
reasonable approach as we think it is reflective of the nature of the product than 
the difference between 3 to 6 months (that is still considered short term) and where 
there are some Letters of Credit or similar transaction with tenors close to 120 days 
or similar that in our view deserve the same treatment. 
 
12) What is the share of your institution’s/(mem ber) institutions’ 
exposures to rated and unrated corporate SMEs and to non-SMEs? What is 
the share of exposures to unrated corporates whose parent companies are 
externally rated? Please provide relevant evidence (e.g. underlying

It is worth mentioning that the share of institution’s exposures to rated corporates 
often stems from a small amount of very large loans. Regardin g corporate SME’s, 
some member states do not acknowledge the EBA analysis that 20% of these 
exposures are rated.  
Looking at the rated vs. unrated question through the number of counterparties, 
rather than through the size of total exposures, would result in a much smaller 
amount than the 20-25% range indicated by EBA. In individual member states, the 
amount of rated non-SME counterparties may lie below 2%, whereas the amount 
of rated SME counterparties can be z ero. As a result, more than 99% of total 
corporate counterparties would be penalised as unrated corporates.

As for corporate exposures, the EBF  recommends introducing in the EU, in the 
context of the ECRA approach - thus recognizing ratings assigned by ECAI, if 
present - the 65% risk weight (RW) to be applied to unrated corporates identified 
as “investment grade”. Among other things, the definitio n of “investment grade” 
provided by the Basel III standards (paragraph 42) stipulates that “the corporate 
entity (or its parent company) must have  securities outstanding on a recognised 
securities exchange”.  
EBF considers it to be  not well -suited to the EU market that identification of 
corporate as “investment grade” be conditional upon issuing listed securities.  
The “investment grade” definition is supposed to be linked to creditworthiness, 
since it is used to identify counterpar ties for the purpose of the application of a 
lower risk weight for credit risk.  Moreover, since most of unrated counterparties 
are not either large companies which issue securities or subsidiaries of groups 
where the parent company issues securities, this  more favourable treatment risks 
being de facto not applicable in most cases. We would therefore recommend that 
this requirement is eliminated. 
Furthermore, when assessing  whether or not an exposure is considered to be of 
investment grade, banks should be allowed to use their internal PD. It has to be 
noted that in the EU the EBA Repair programme and the ECB’s TRIM have improved 
the reliability of the internal PD, therefore  making them usable for internal 
purposes. 
 
14) What other measures, if any, could be tak en to increase the risk -
sensitivity of the standardised RW treatment of corporate exposures 
which currently have no external rating? Please elaborate and provide 
relevant evidence. 
A plausible solution would be to use this investment grade/non -investment grade 
approach as a fall -back approach to the external rating approach for unrated 
corporates in Europe. 
Furthermore, in case of corporates that are subsidiaries within a g roup, the use of 
the external rating of the parent company should be allowed. Subsidiaries within a 
corporate group, rarely have their own external rating and will therefore be deemed 
as unrated under the new Basel framework.  This is recognized by the Base l 
framework for the SCRA approach where a corporate entity is allowed to use the 
listing of its parent. Allowing use of parent external rating for integrated 
subsidiaries (next to guaranteed) would align this framework with the CRSA 
approach. Another option that should be considered  in order to im prove the risk 
sensitivity is to revise the definition of corporate exposures . Subsidiaries of large 
corporate groups should not automatically be included in the category of exposures 
to corporates.

the context of revising the standardised treatment of corporate 
exposures? Please elaborate. 
The EBF welcomes the progress made by the Basel Committee, as the new credit 
risk framework acknowledges the s pecificities of SME financing and introduces a 
specific treatment for unrated SMEs (85% instead of 100% risk weight) within the 
corporate asset class. Nonetheless, the EBF  considers this measure not to be

sufficient, as a large amount of banks’ exposures t o SMEs falls out of its scope. In 
order to avoid that the new framework results in penalising credit supply to SMEs, 
the EBF  therefore recommends maintaining the so called EU SME Supporting 
Factor, as amended by Regulation (EU) 876/2019 (CRR2), i.e. applied to exposures 
towards eligible SMEs, even if assigned to other asset classes, an d irrespective of 
the approach (standardised or IRB) under which they are capitalised (thus 
preserving the level playing field between banks applying the standardised and the 
IRB approach). 
It is also important to continue to support SMEs in  the housing sector as well and 
thus it should be made sure that for the part of counterparties referred to in section 
68 (pages 22-23) also similar national housing entities with exposures secured by 
residential real estate property , such as e.g. companies under the Finnish Act on 
Housing Companies (1599/2009, as amended) . The actual risk related to these 
housing entities is very limited especially when a loan given to those entities have 
the first lien to the property securing the loan. At the same time, it is imperativ e 
to make sure that investments for example to energy efficiency are incentivized 
and not impeded by unnecessarily strict regulation. The energy efficiency of 
housing is for its part imperative in the European combat against climate change. 
The EBF also recommends retaining and enhancing the so-called EU Infrastructure 
supporting factor, introduced by the Regulation (EU) 876/2019, with appropriate 
refinements aimed at extending its scope and consequently maximising the related 
benefits. 
The EBF recommends introducing in the EU a capital relief for sustainable finance , 
which takes into account prudential considerations. 
Pending the development of the methodologies for incorporation of the ESG factors 
into the supervisory framework, and in line with the objective to maintain the link 
between long term risk considerations  and capital, it is suggested that the 
European Banking Authority (EBA) explores the possibility of introducing a 
supporting factor for certai n assets (Sustainable Finance Supporting Factor)  that 
are classified as sustainable under the EU taxonomy and at the same time, meet 
additional eligibility criteria established by the EBA. Using forward looking 
methodologies, it is suggested that the EBA investigates whether there are groups 
of assets/activities under the EU taxonomy that show a lower financial risk, and, 
specifically, a lower credit risk profile. The supporting factor would only apply to 
such eligible assets after the capital has been computed as usual and therefore be 
used as a “discount at checkout”, irrespective of the use of the standard or the 
IRB/IRBA approach, the type of financial product or its duration. 
The EBF would also recommend including a prefer ential treatment for short -term 
corporates exposures, in order to reflect their less risky nature. 
Moreover, in respect of minimising the impact of the SA on corporate financing 
through a more appropriate risk sensitive framework, we would urge legislators to 
adequately reflect the risk associated with short term exposures . Namely, RWs in 
the SA do not take into account short maturity as they do under the IRB maturity 
adjustment factor, which reflects short -term maturity as a risk mitigant. Only in 
case of Exposures to Banks with origin al maturity < 3 months a favourable RW is 
permitted. No such favourable RW is taken into account in the Corporate exposure 
class in Basel III. This will also significantly impact on capital markets transactions, 
such as SFTs, which are normally short -term exposures of < 1year. Legislators 
should therefore consider aligning the RWs for short-term exposures to corporates 
with short term transactions to institutions.

16) Views are sought on the costs and benefits of implementing the 
specific treatment of SL expos ures provided by the Basel III standards 
(paragraphs 44-48). In particular, how does this treatment compare with 
the current treatment in terms of risk -sensitivity, impact on RWAs and 
operational burden? Please provide relevant evidence to substantiate your 
views. 
We welcome the introduction of a Specialized Lending assets class under the 
standardized approach.  
As a preliminary remark, it is worth noting that very few SL exposures have an 
external credit rating. Therefore, it is of utmost important to asse ss the impact of 
the treatment proposed for unrated SL exposures. 
The Basel III standards may lead to a large reduction in the volume of funds 
allocated to the most penalised activities with a detrimental effect on the real 
economy. The most impacted asset/exposures classes are the ones which undergo 
a rather drastic increase of standardized risk weightings and / or the highest 
difference between IRB approach and the Standardized approach (indirect impact 
due to the output floor).  
Moreover, only risk sensi tive approaches are able to select the most suitable 
lending activities, contributing to the stability of the banking sector. The less risk 
sensitive the framework is, the more opportunities for regulatory arbitrage are 
created, incentivising firms to seek  higher risk assets as a means of boosting 
returns. 
We welcome the Basel proposals to increase the granularity of the standardized 
approach for both Project Finance (§47 page 14) and IPRE sub -asset-classes (§67 
and 73).  
However, the revised framework fails to properly reflect the low risk profile of the 
specialized lending asset class as a whole. 
The revised approach is not consistent across the four main SL categories. 
Object Finance transactions (inc luding Aircraft, Rail and  Shipping) will receive a 
fixed 100% RW. This approach is not risk sensitive and the same risk weight for 
Object Finance as for non-investment grade unsecured corporate  
Commodity Finance: the BCBS proposes a RW of 100% for all Commodity Finance 
transactions. This level does not take into account the structure  and the nature of 
the collateral. 
Project Finance (PF): the high-quality criteria enabling to have a 80% RW are very 
restrictive at this stage and would apply to very few projects. Therefore, most PF 
transactions will receive a 100% RW or even higher 130% in pre-operational phase, 
i.e. equal or higher than for a non-investment grade unsecured corporate exposure 
(100%) whereas project lenders would have a comprehensive security package. 
Statistics computed by independent companies (Moody’s, S&P or GCD) as well as 
non-public analyses conducted by major players in the SL financing highlight the 
low risk profile of SL portfolios with loss rate equivalent to investment grade 
corporate portfolios. However, SL  receive higher risk weights than corporate 
unsecured exposures under both and SCRA methodologies.  
Existing data collected at BCBS and EBA level are not sufficient to properly assess 
the appropriateness of the new Basel risk weights. While the European Commission

It may be worth considering the addition of a value-based parameter to the

large, complex and expensive installations such as power plants however it is not

Considering historical performance and benchmark with corporate exposures the 
standardized approach for SL should b e recalibrated and further refined by

An alternative could be to introduce a more granular standardized approach with

19) In your view, which  other aspects, if any,  should be considered in 
the context of revising the treatment of SL exposures? 
Even if other financial players such as insurers and pension funds are natural 
holders of long-term liabilities, banks are better placed to structure SL transactions.  
Indeed, structuring such a transaction does not fit well with standardization 
expected by institutional investors: it requires teams of expert engineers 
specialized per sub -asset class. Banks  meet these needs. They usually have 
dedicated SL teams in charge of advising and arranging such financings, which also 
requires a specific monitoring during the drawing period (several drawdowns, 
waivers, etc.). However, to be profitable there must be a critical mass of 
transactions diversified enough to cover the bank’s fixed operating costs. 
Moreover, banks offer comfort to other long-term investors. Banks are in a natural 
position of structurer and lender. This dual role brings safety for other long -term 
lenders. Indeed, it helps to assure the alignmen t of banks’ interests with those of 
other stakeholders. It can be seen as making banks putting ‘skin in the game’ 
(similar to sponsor/originator’s retention in a securitization), which brings 
considerable comfort to (and is in fact a usual requirement of) investors 
Given the str ucturing experience required to originate Specialized Lending, any 
shift of these activities from banks to insurers would take time, while the revised 
Basel III framework would probably quickly imply banks management arbitrages 
in terms of activities, thus implying an abrupt slowdown in Europe in the financing 
of infrastructure, objects, commodities and real estate. 
 
21) Views are sought on the costs and benefits of the revised standard 
treatment for equity exposures under Basel III (paragraph 49 -50). In 
particular, would you consider any further differentiation among equity 
exposures (apart from “speculative unlisted equity exposures” and 
“national legislated programmes” – see 1.1.4.2. and 1.1.4.3.) warranted, 
and if so, how should t his differentiation be made and what would be its 
prudential rationale? Please elaborate and provide relevant evidence.  
Yes, we would consider a further differentiation among equity exposures 
warranted, because the BCBS framework subsumes too many exposures under the 
same risk category, while their risk profiles are different. 
Under the current proposal unlisted equity investments would jump from 150% to 
400% without justification based on evidence. 
A high-risk weight associated with unlisted equity exposures may put at risk bank’s 
support to the financing of the real economy, through private equity and venture 
capital investments (in particular start -up’s investing in fintech) as banks own or 
have large stakes in these companies. An additional category should be considered, 
in line wi th Article 155 of the CRR, of 190% risk weight for private equity and 
venture capital exposures in sufficiently diversified portfolios. The current proposals 
do not take into consideration the diversification within a private eq uity portfolio. 
Footnote 30 also refers to "long-term business relationships" with corporate clients, 
which could also be a criterion to be applied to receive the 190% risk weight. 
Also, the 150% risk weight for banks’ subordinated debt seems to be penalising.

The new risk weights im plemented for equity exposures, severely penalizes 
intragroup equity holdings  and equity holdings in ancillary services undertakings 
for a group of banks, and therefore the banking activity itself. We think that given 
the nature of these holdings the previous standard treatment should be maintained. 
Regarding intragroup equity exposures, the new risk weights implemented for 
equity exposures, severely penalizes banking groups with significant bank 
subsidiaries. We think that the new regulations should be neu tral with regards to 
banking structures. In addition to this, penalizing intragroup equity treatment may 
act as a disincentive to the acquisition of banks within or outside the EU. For this 
reason, we think that given the nature of these holdings, a lower risk should be 
assigned to them maintaining t he current CRR standardized treatment. In this 
respect, we welcome the request made by the Commission to the EBA to carry out 
a further impact assessment of Basel III on intra-group equity exposures.

An asset class fo r which a dedicated treatment is deemed to be warranted are

23) Do you agree that speculative unlisted equi ty exposures such as 
investments in private equity or venture capital firms should be subject to 
a relatively higher RW than other equity exposures? If you disagree, 
please explain and provide relevant evidence to substantiate your view. 
No. 
The current proposal – with the exception of the exemption listed in footnote 30 on 
page 17 of BCBS document d424 – implies that all private equity investments are 
speculative in nature and subject to a 400% risk weight, with out providing 
justification based on evidence.  
The term “private equity”, which encompasses equity share capital not listed on 
public exchanges, can be used to refer to fundamentally different types of 
exposures. Different types of private equity investme nt strategies exist, mainly 
related to the maturity stage of the company and / or the type of investment tool

The calculation of the LTV  ratio should be updated in order to better reflect the 
accurate and current value of the property. The revised standardised framework 
considers the value of the property at origination as a cap for the calculation of the 
LTV ratio. Therefore, the pass age of time renders those valuations outdated, an 
important issue especially for residential real estate mortgage portfolios which are 
typically long -term loans. Retrieving value at origination would also pose non -
negligible concerns in terms of compliance costs. 
We are generally supportive of the approach of align ment to Basel to maintain 
global consistency, we consider introduction of a cap at the valuation of property 
at origination to be problematic for the following reasons: 
First, the cap results in a material reduction in risk sensitivity, at a time when the 
need for increased risk sensitivity in the standardised approach is of paramount 
importance as a result of introduction of the output floor. Capping the property 
value at loan origination dilutes the predictive power of LTV (the sole determinant 
of the RW under SA-CR) as a metric.  
Second, the methodology of capping the valuation in this way is likely to be an

origination reflects the property market at an arbitrary point in t ime. Loans which

mortgaged during its lifetime or not, despite all other risk characteristics remaining 
unchanged. To address this issue, legislators should consider using current MV 
unless a genuinely anti-cyclical valuation method can be devised. 
Third, the cap may have a material RWA impact which creates adverse incentives 
and consequences on the real economy. Adverse incentives include shorter term 
lending (increasing uncertainty of refinancing for cons umers) and a movement 
away the requirement for firms to monitor collateral values when assessing credit 
risk. This would encourage good market practice of constantly monitoring values. 
This could also increase the costs associated with this type of lending  activity 
(unjustified for sustainable increases i n property valuation) causing a reduction in 
lending and / or increased funding costs which would likely need to be passed on 
to the consumer.

2.  Specialized Lending (SL) 
The finalized package could lead to unintended consequences on SL transactions 
where the only asset (and capacity to generate cash flows) of the structure is the 
collateral. Thanks to a comprehensive security package and strong contractual 
structure, recoveries of SL usually come from a combination of sources from 
different counterparts. Assets are not often sold. Emergence of default is generally 
achieved through restructuring of the loans, on the residual asset lives.   
If the effect of the collateral improves the recovery rate of the transaction, its effect 
cannot be solely measured. As a consequence,  the proposed LGD floor formula is 
not adequate for SL. An input floor on the final LGD of the transaction would be 
more adequate. SL transactions are part of the Corporate asset class and Basel III 
states that the Corporate input floors will apply. These LGD minimum levels were 
not calibrated for SL. As a consequence, the minimum LGD will be included in the 
range [15%; 25 %] i.e. 15% for a fully secured transaction after a haircut of 40%; 
25% for an unsecured transaction. 
- Eligibility criteria were not defi ned for SL and should be adapted. For 
instance, a 25% -LGD floor would apply to project finance transactions as 
collateral might not be considered as eligible under revised Basel III criteria, 
also Project Finance are clearly secured transactions.  
- These mi nimum levels are set at the level of the average of observed 
historical LGD of specialized lending portfolios or are even much higher than

these averages. Setting a floor at the level of the average is not accurate. 
It seems that there is here a mis-calibration of these input floors. 
- A 40% haircut will be applied to the value of collateral, which aims to take 
into account the difficul ty to sell quickly and at a good price the collateral. 
But one of the strengths of SL is the capacity to reschedule the repay ment 
profile and postpone the maturity, in case of difficulties of the borrower. 
There is no emergency for selling the asset which can be operated and the

of fulfilling an ALM mandate.  
Neither the Internal Risk Transfer (IRT) treatment, nor the re -assignment 
treatment is fully suitable for supporting these ongoing non -discretionary ALM 
activities being carried out across the Trading Book and Banking Book boundary. 
Indeed, some cooperative groups have centralised in a dedicated entity, which is 
capitalised on a standalone basis, the management of the risks from the Banking 
Books of their regional banks. The centralized entity provides the group with the 
capability of hedging risk exposure s in the market on a consolidated basis that is 
often managed in a Trading Book. Such a centra lised organisation has proved 
efficient for the following reasons:  
• It is risk-reducing: the more concentrated the risk exposures in a single entity, 
the higher t he potential for risk offsetting, and the lower the global risk 
exposure;  
• It is control-strengthening: the centralisation of risks in a dedicated structure 
allows for optimised risk-monitoring, control and regulatory reporting. The bank 
is better positioned to monitor counterparty limits, thus contributing to a better 
risk management of the bank;  
• It is cost-efficient: the centralisation of risk management in a single structure 
enables to execute trades efficiently (volume effect), as well as to avoid 
replicating in each credit institutions of the Group the costly setup to access to 
clearing.  
For the same reason IRT is considered as a suboptimal solution to manage interest 
rate risk, it is of the utmost importance not to oblige institutions to re -designate 
each transaction when transferring for liquidity risk management purposes (i.e. 
LCR), securities from the trading book of the centralized institution operating in the 
market to other credit institutions of the Group.  
Proposal: The EBF proposes the following:  
• An option for banks to, at least, receive explicit recognition of those activities 
within the ALM mandate under a predefined policy that recognizes the need for 
ongoing buying and selling of securities across the Trading Book and Banking 
Book boundary for the sound and prudent management of liquidity. Therefore, 
banks should be exempted from re-classification requirements for instruments 
that are moved between regulatory books when those transactions are carried 
out with a “commercial” trading desk that exclusively conducts market making 
activities or provides investment services to clients wh ich are governed by 
specific requirements on an on-going basis.  
• To introduce the following oversight and controls for ALM related activities (i.e. 
oversight and controls fully aligned with the national Laws on the Separation 
and Regulation of Banking Activities (SRBA) – like the French law n° 2013-672 
of July 26, 2013):  
o Internal review by senior management and external approval by supervisory 
authorities for policies related to ALM activities;  
o Activities conducted in compliance with bank policy and fully  documented, 
in particular that transactions are executed at market price and meet criteria 
to demonstrate that the sale is real and not a “wash sale”;  
o Periodic review of arrangements (e.g. annual).

4. Internal risk transfer of from banking book to trading book (RBC25.21) 
The EBF thinks that the final FRTB standard (BCBS d457) is still very restrictive 
with respect to Internal Risk Transfers (IRT). In part icular, Equity and Credit IRT 
can only be recognized in case they are directly externalized using a per fectly 
matching trade with a third party. 
According to RBC25.21 an effective hedge of an equity exposure in the Banking 
Book is only the one where an ide ntical and opposite position is closed with the 
market from the Trading Book: 
25.21 (2) The equity exposure in the banking book is deemed to be hedged 
for capital requirement purposes if and only if: 
(a) the trading book enters into an external hedge from an eligible third -
party protection provider that exactly matches the internal risk transfer; and 
(b) the external hedge is recognised as a hedge of a banking book equity 
exposure. 
The requirement appears over-reaching in that it should suffice that the IRT to the 
Trading Book is exactly matching the Banking Book pay -off so that all risk is 
transferred from the Banking into the Trading Book. How such risk is then 
managed/externalised with the Street is a Trading Book management issue and 
any mismatches will attract Own Fund Requirements. It should suffice that a Bank 
has processes in place that demonstrate that IRT is exact and rigorously preserved. 
The point is particularly relevant in the case of issuance of structured notes from 
the Banking Book with embedded equity (Certificates) or Credit (CLN) derivatives. 
The request that an IRT is only valid if the der ivative is exactly externalised with 
the Street through an OTC transaction would severely impair the business model, 
generating an in -natural request for  OTC transactions to back -to-back risks that 
the Bank is typically managing via macro-hedges. 
In Europe, the Equity-linked notes business amounts to a several hundred billion 
notional. Having to hedge these issues with external counterparties in a perfect 
way (1:1) would severely impact the sales margin. A very large part of existing 
issues would need to be (perfectly) hedged retrospectively with an external party. 
This would lead to serious operational challenges and very high additional 
transaction costs. Consequently, the rules put in question the profitability of the 
whole business. Furthermore, issuing notes out of the Trading Book may be 
problematic for many banks, because this can result in a trading book VaR/ES 
dominated by un-hedgeable own credit spread exposure.  Moreover, capitalisation 
of the IRT Desk for internal risk transfer of general interest  rate risk on a stand -
alone basis, without diversification with other risk positions - once all risk transfers 
and their effectiveness are documented - seems to be unduly penalising. 
Proposal: The EBF recommends, at the very least, to remove or substantially relax 
the requirement to "perfectly" (i.e. 1:1) offset the internal hedge/IRT. Ideally, 
banks should be allowed to account for IRT where they can prov e that the latter 
are part of a sensible business model (as opposed to a capital arbitrage scheme).

4. Residual Risk Add-On (RRAO) on trades that hedge risks eligible to RRAO 
(BCBS MAR33) 
Banks often structure for their clients notes or derivatives that embed parameters 
subject to RRAO, such as correlation parameters. Since RRAO is driv en by gross 
notional exposures, any hedging strategy for those parameters (e.g. spread options 
hedging correlation risk) will increase the overall capital charge.  
In practice, several hedges could be necessary to hedge a given client transaction 
(e.g. cas h flow by cash flow hedging at trade inception, or periodic hedge 
rebalancing throughout the life of the transaction) and the resulting RRAO charge 
can be highly disproportionate compared to the initial risk. As an illustration, 
analysis performed on real/typical structured rates activities show that more than 
95% of total RRAO arise from spread option hedges, while less than 5% arise from 
client structured trades. 
Proposal: In order to restore a positive incentive for hedging correlation risk and 
avoid sid e-effects of transferring disproportionate capital costs to clients, w e 
propose that instruments traded for the sole purpose of hedging risks eligible to 
RRAO be exempt from the RRAO calculation. The identification of such hedges 
should abide with objective risk management criteria (e.g., traded in the interbank 
market, booked in a dedicated sub -portfolio, sensitivities are effectively reduced 
with the hedges). 
To illustrate: when a client, usually insurers, buys a structured rate note to protect 
itself against adverse movements of the yield curve, such as a 20Y Annual Coupon 
paying 4* 2Y20Y Constant Maturity Swap “CMS” Spread rate floored at 0, it implies 
20 coupons which must be hedged individually by the bank via options on CMS 
Spreads (2Y20Y, 2Y19Y, 2Y18 Y, etc.). Because the pay -off of the client trade is 
4*CMS Spread, the notional of options used as hedges must have a notional equal 
to 4*Notional of the client trade. Assuming CMS Spread falls into residual risk types 
i.e. a RRAO capital charge of 0.1%, t he total capital charge for a 100m notional 
client trade would be 100* (1+20*4)*0.1%=8.1m. This additional capital charge 
will be priced to the client – about 3m per annum. As a result, these interest rate 
risk hedges will typically become uneconomical for clients. 
To restore a positive incentive for clients to hedge, we propose in this example that 
options on CMS Spread used for the purpose of hedging the risks stemming from

www.ebf.eu 
 
the client trade are exempted from RRAO capital charge – the RRAO charge would 
be reduced to 100*0.1%.

The Industry is concerned about the complexities of the use of NMRF in back -

The back -testing aims at assessing “ the conservatism of r isk measurement

exceptions to all risk factors capitalised via NMRF stressed add -on (SES), since 
adequate capitalisation should get the same recognition. The industry proposes to

Daily Loss ≤  VaR+ Aggregation of all scaled SES-charges from current period

The aggregation of the scaled SES  add-ons would then follow exactly the same 
aggregation mechanism used  for capital purposes that has been justified by the 
bank.

the liquidity horizon scaling in Expected Shortfall, times the ratio of ES[current 
rolling one year]/ES[stressed  period] to rescale the SES charge to the current 
volatility regime. 
We also propose that the frequency of calculation of the scaling factor be monthly. 
For a 97.5% VaR -breach, a similar  formula holds, where one would use an 
appropriate version for SES; to be a 97.5% VaR equivalent.

9. Computation frequency of NMRF (MAR33.41) 
The Stress Scenario Risk Measure (SSRM) methodology developed by the EBA  for 
the capitalization of the non -modellable risk factors (NMRF) is computationally

number of equidistant steps spanning the S tress Scenario Risk Factor Range 
(CSSRFR) window and to which 2 additional revaluations are necessary to compute 
the kappa adjustment.  
Proposal: The daily frequency is adapted for risk profiles that may evolve 
significantly from one day to another. By def inition of non -modellability criteria, 
banks cannot take or unwind positions on NMRF in a way that risk profile would be

100 trades per annum. Therefore, we strongly recommend EBA to allow banks to 
refresh SES figures on a weekly basis.

We believe that capitalizing a hypothetical regulatory CVA distinct from the true 
accounting CVA distorts the essential link between  economic risk and capital 
requirement. We acknowledge that there is a need for more uniform and prudent 
CVA practices. However, we think that the Prudent Valuation is a more suitable

charge. In that respect, we note that CVA remains the only market risk in the Basel 
framework where the accounting measure is not used as an input of the capital 
requirement calculation. 
The main sources of divergence between accounting CVA and regu latory CVA can 
be classified in 2 categories: 
1. Parameters 
a. Loss Given Default (LGD) 
We acknowledge there is a broad-based adoption of market-implied parameters in 
accounting CVA including PDs, recovery rates and diffusion parameters. The Basel 
Committee logi cally imposes a regulatory CVA based on mar ket-implied 
parameters. We note that some flexibility is granted to use different LGDs in specific 
cases where “the bank can demonstrate that the seniority of the derivative 
exposure differs from the seniority of senior unsecured bonds”. 
We advocate that b anks should be able to use different LGDs for certain specific 
type of exposures, e.g. because they are secured (such as covered bonds, funds or 
project finance vehicles) or because their nature does not permit th e reliance on 
the credit market (see for instance the uncertainty around political intervention in 
the context of sovereign exposures), subject to being able to demonstrate that the 
use of such parameters are properly governed and validated within the cred it 
institution. 
b. Margin Period of Risks (MPoR) 
The final Basel standard imposes a regulatory CVA based on “9+N business days” 
margin period of risk (MPoR) where N is the remargining period. We advocate that 
such MPoR is overly conservative, not commensurate  with the risk truly incurred 
and not consistent with accounting CVA practices. 
2. Perimeter 
a. Securities Financing Transactions (SFTs) 
In the final Basel standard, fair-valued SFTs are included in the revised CVA capital 
charge on a mandatory basis. SFTs and o ther forms of collateralized borrowing 
should not be incorporated in the CVA regulatory framework. These instruments 
are not necessarily captured in accounting CVA. The credit valuation component of 
SFTs is embedded in the valuation base, much like a traded bond. On the one hand, 
the variations of fair value of an SFT are essentially driven by variations of repo 
rates. On the other hand, the IMM metric captures MTM variations due to variations 
in the SFT collateral value. Moreover, CVA is principally meaningful for longer term 
exposures whereas the vast majority of SFTs mature in 90 days or earlier.

clearly defined yet in the regulatory work.

increasing knowledge on such risk categories and with standards setting at

3) The current EU taxonomy is unlikely suitable for risk management 
purposes’ 
The risk management f ramework needs to cover the whole balance sheet in 
terms of risk analysis, management and control at bank level and needs to take 
into account all the elements in relation   to the counterparty. The current EU 
taxonomy was not developed for climate risk assessment at client/counterparty 
level. It has been designed so far for investment purposes - financing of specific 
projects and is not adapted to general purpose loans which represent circa 80% 
of total loans.  It also does not fully t ake into account the corporate strategy/ 
commitments in favour of carbon issuance reductions within a transition time 
path, which should be a key driver of climate risk mitigation. The suitability of 
the taxonomy for risk management purposes should therefo re be carefully 
assessed as part of the ongoing work of EBA.   
 
Supervisors’ and banks’ own climate risk related stress tests are still 
premature. Before proceeding to own stress-test, banks need firstly to assess 
their risks (which require the definition of common methodologies for transition 
and physical risks assessment) and also to build their base line scenario.  
Running a baseline scenario and measuring portfolios sensitivity with a limited 
number of scenarios would be a much more pragmatic approach b oth at the 
supervisory and banks level.   
 
In addition, climate risks related stress tests should not be included in the 
existing EBA stress test exercise as they raise many key concerns, such as: 
− Mismatch between the time horizon of traditional risk model s (3 years), on 
one hand, and the speed and time frame (that can be very long term around 
15-20 years) of climate risks materialization, on the other hand. 
− Difficulties, and sometimes impossibilities for obtaining data from clients 
(especially geolocation data for physical risks) 
− Translating climate scenarios into economic & financial scenarios 
− Impossibilities to include Sectoral details needed for climate stress -testing 
as they are too granular for existing stress-testing frameworks

risk of conflicts with the MS labour law.

211) Do you consider that corporate culture could and should be taken 
into consideration as part of the fit and proper assessment? If yes, please 
explain how this could be most effectively achieved. 
No. 
The integration of corporate culture into the fit and proper assessment is not 
relevant and is not clearly defined in the present document to be possible. It is not 
acceptable to have such a highly subjective, undefined and non-regulated criterion 
being assessed by a third party. Additional concepts like corporate culture do not 
provide any value for assessing members of the management body. 
We strongly oppose the insertion of such a highly subjective and institution-specific 
criterion to the fit and proper assessments. In any case, adherence to the corporate 
culture can only be assessed after the individual being part of the institution for a 
sufficient time period and should be considered as an obligation to  the institution. 
Moreover, management that has been assessed as fit and proper, can decide to 
change or adapt the corporate culture. 
Institutions may have different views as to what constitutes their corporate culture. 
The supervisors’ opinion on the cult ure of institutions might also differ. It is thus 
not measurable, even less so prior to a director becoming a board member. 
Therefore, it cannot become a criterion for institutions and competent au thorities 
to carry out their suitability assessments. The i nclusion of corporate culture 
presents a subjective burden that is excessive and arbitrary. 
 
Corporate culture is part of the strategy of institutions. Management body members 
are recruiting according to what people selected can bring to Board and the Group 
concerning strategy. 
Nonetheless, as already mentioned, an institution assumes its responsibilities in 
terms of selection of its own staff and more especially for its KFHs, which are 
selected by the General management in close relation with the Human Res ources 
which are experts in this domain. We do not see how this could be taken into 
account through the fit and proper assessment by an authority. It is the HR 
departments that are experts in these  areas, but it is independent of the fit and 
proper process. It is not up to European law to dictate and specify the culture of 
each institution.

Moreover, we would like to add that the hiring strategy and succession plans are 
specific to each institution and can constitute competitive advantage and a better 
adaptation to the specific situation of the institution. In our view, it is then not the 
role of the CRD EU legislation to interfere in this area. 
This assessment falls out of scope of the individual fit and proper assessment. The 
referral to art. 98 (7) in CRD  is not part of the fit and proper assessment but an 
assessment of the institution. Furthermore, this assessment would be hard to 
perform as corporate culture is collectively achieved and the corpo rate culture 
differs from institution to institution. Docum enting this for both institutions and 
NCAs will be impossible.   
 
212) What do you consider would be the benefits of, and/or difficulties 
encountered in, including the ability to create and promote the 
organisation’s desired culture as part of the “fit and prop er” assessment 
of members of the management body? 
The candidate is not part of the entity yet and it is a highly subjective criterion. It 
is easy to manipulate and to give the responses which are socially desired. 
We do not see how the competent authority will be in a position to assess such 
adequacy, as the company culture belongs to each institution, and cannot be easily 
appropriated by third parties. 
Besides it corresponds to important psychological aspects of the candidate and the 
common project of the institution. HR experts are specialist in this domain and 
these elements are not tick the box type with specific criteria.  
Including “culture” within directors’ suitability assessments would severely hinder 
the legal certainty of the whole fit and proper process, at least because of the 
following:  
(i) the difficulties in defining what “culture” really means and stipulating 
whether an agreement (or validation) is needed between the institution and the 
competent authority in terms of what the institution’s “culture” is;  
(ii) introducing purely subjective criteria to the assessment. 
Lastly, introducing corporate culture within suitability processes would amount to 
entrusting competent authorities (even if indirectly) with the decision over whether 
an institutions’ corporate culture is adequate or not (again, without pre-established 
or objective criteria).

• The clean portion follows the Revised AIRB 
(RIRB) approach whereas the insured portion 
follows the Foundation IRB (FIRB) approach.
• The haircut for ship finance is prescribed at 
40% under finalised Basel 3. This means that 
when a loan is 60% LTV or less, it is fully 
secured from an RWA perspective. 
• The RIRB approach is equivalent to the current 
AIRB approach except for LGD or PD input 
floors. 
• In this case, the LTV is high and the input floor 
does not bite but at 70% or 60%, the 15% LGD 
floor would kick in. 
• Finalised Basel 3 implies a substitution 
approach. Therefore, on the insured side, we 
apply the PD of the insurer. 
• Applying the logic suggested by the EBA in its
guidelines, we apply a mix of secured and 
unsecured prescribed LGDs, being 25% and 
45%. 
• In the present case, we end up with a blended 
LGD of 30%

• The Standardised Approach (SA) is a simple 
approach, which is already used by many banks 
now. 
• No PD or LGD concept here. Just risk weights 
(RW).
• IRB banks will get a discount under finalised 
Basel 3 framework (from 50% in 2022 to 27.5% 
in 2027).
• The Risk Weight of the unsecured insurer can 
either be 30% for (A-rated insurers) or 20% (for 
AA-rated insurers)
• No concept of double cover on the loan (the 
mortgage on the ship is completely ignored) but 
given the high risk weight on the clean portion, 
there is significant capital relief on the insured 
portion

Conclusions from the summary table
• Column A: Under AIRBA, an 80% LTV mortgage loan for a ship attracts a RW of around 40% without 
insurance, which is much better than 100% under Standardised Approach. 
• Columns B and C: when applying credit insurance on top of the mortgage, we obtain lower but still 
relatively conservative RWs of around 15%. That said, given that most of the margin goes to the insurer, 
a 25% gain in RWA is not necessarily RoE-enhancing.
• Column D: 30% is the result of the blend between 25% secured portion and 45% unsecured portion. 
Assuming this is correct, it ensues that a 60% LTV mortgage loan will be at 25% LGD and there will be no 
benefit attached to the insurance. By contrast, an unsecured loan will be treated entirely at 45% LGD. 
The next tables will show these examples.  
• Column D: the jump in RW is not extremely significant between current practice and FIRBA method but 
this is because there is a mortgage and the loan is high LTV. The gain in RWA becomes 17.5% down 
from 25%. So, at equivalent insurance cost, the RoE declines further
• Column E: interestingly, the starting SA floor is nearly equivalent to the FIRBA method. So, from 2023 
onwards, SA will be the binding constraint. By 2027, SA will have negated any benefit from credit 
insurance and the RW will be higher than the mortgage loan without insurance. This is because there is 
no double cover benefit whatsoever under SA. 
• One could take more insurance to counter this: at 75% insurance, the max RW is 34% under SA. It 
reduces the negative effect of FIRB and slows the effect of the SA floor but it is more costly.

Conclusions from 2 further examples
• Unsurprisingly, the example with a conservative LTV at 60% shows a much more favourable RW from 
the mortgage.
• Some AIRBA banks may as a result get no further benefit from credit insurance whereas others may 
obtain further capital relief.
• The application of finalised Basel III shows a significant uptick in RW from 11.5% to 19.8%. The insurance 
is still useful in getting RWAs down but taking into account its cost, it is doubtful whether banks will 
resort to it. 
• The jump in RW from Basel III to Finalised Basel III was only +14% for the 80% LTV loan. In case of the 
60% LTV loan, the jump amounts to a doubling or tripling of the RW! This shows the impact of Finalised 
Basel III on the less risky loans and the fact that insurance will not alleviate this impact. 
• The SA approach will anyway be the binding constraint and mean a multiplication by 4 of the RWA on 
this type of loans, unless the bank’s overall business model compensates for it. 
• The example of the unsecured loan shows on the other hand a significant capital relief (RWAs divided 
by 2).  So, one may hope that the insurance will serve a purpose here despite its cost. 
• The FIRB method will be the binding constraint in this case of an unsecured loan. 
• All in all, these examples show how all low LTV secured loans will be impacted by Finalised Basel III and 
how insurance will not help much in these situations. This will in turn trigger a greater adverse selection 
whereby more risky loans will be insured. It will potentially drive away a number of insurers and 
irreparably harm the market for credit insurance.

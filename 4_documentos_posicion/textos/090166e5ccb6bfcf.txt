Question 11: The short term preferential treatment of trade related bank exposure should be 
irrespective of whether the issuing bank is located in a country which applies a pru dential 
regime equivalent to the EU, as all these exposures are subject to ICC UCP 600.  
 
Exposures to banks with an original1 maturity of three months or less, as well as exposures to banks 
that arise from the movement of goods across national borders with an original maturity of six months2 
or less are assigned a lower risk weight. However, in the current EU CRR, a relevant part of trade-
related bank exposure does not qualify for the short term risk weights under the standardised 
approach, thus rendering these risk weights ineffective.  
 
The EU Standardised Approach as per the CRR 575 / 2013 does not allow to treat short te rm bank 
exposures on banks in ‘non-equivalent’ countries as exposures on institutions. Instead they have to 
be treated as corporate exposures, with the associated higher corporate risk weights. As a 
consequence, a relevant part of trade finance related bank exposure, especially exposures issued by 
banks in low-income countries, is currently excluded from the short term lower risk weight un der 
European legislation.  
 
According to CRR article 107.3 exposures to third-country investment firms / institutions / clearing 
houses and exchanges shall be treated as exposure to an institution only if the third country applies 
prudential and supervisory requirements to that entity that are at least equivalent to those applied in 
the Union. Article 119.5 repeats that financial institutions authorised and supervised by the competent 
authorities and subject to prudential requirements comparable to those applied to institutions in terms 
of robustness shall be treated as exposures to institutions. 3  
 
Due  to the strict Equivalent Countries definition in the CRR, any financial institution which is not 
based in one of the Equivalent Countries is not considered an institution and has to be treated as a 
corporate. Because less-developed countries generally do not qualify as Equivalent, the  short term 
risk weights for Trade Finance cannot be applied in cases where they are most relevant.  
 
The impact of the exclusion is material. The risk weights4 of corporate exposure are generally higher 
than for bank exposure, and moreover there are no lower risk weights for short term exposure, or 
short term trade related exposure. The difference between these treatments is mat erial, as can be

Trade related exposures on banks in all countries should be treated equally, irrespective of the 
country of the issuing bank: 
 
• Trade related exposures on banks in both equivalent and non- equivalent countries are all 
subject to ICC UCP 600. While an appropriate standard and level of supervision is a good 
requirement when assessing the risk of non-trade related bank exposures (e.g. loans), trade 
finance products such as Letters of Credit, SBLC’s and guarantees are subject to the ICC 
Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits - UCP 600 - irrespective in which 
country the issuing bank is based. The UCP 600 is a set of rules agreed by the ICC 6, which 
apply to financial institutions which issue Letters of Credit. Banks and lenders globally are 
subject to this regulation, which have proven crucial in standardising international trade, 
reducing the risks of trading goods and services, and governing trade. 
• The EU treatment of trade finance related bank exposure has to be aligned with the 
Treatment of trade finance under the Basel capital framework 7 The current EU definition of 
Equivalent Countries excludes various OECD countries like Korea, Israel and Chile. It also 
excludes banks in certain Middle Eastern countries, which are active in Trade Finance and 
generally have a strong bank sector (e.g. UAE, Qatar). Most importantly the current EU 
definition excludes banks in developing and Low Income Countries, who are active in Trade 
Finance covering strategic imports which are crucial to develop the economies in these 
countries. It has been already been recognized by the World Bank and the Basel Committee 
in 2011 that the impact of the regulatory framework on trade finance programs in support of 
developing countries, in particular their coverage and impact on LICs (ie Low Income 
Countries), should be mitigated, which was recognized by the waiver of the sovereign floor for 
unrated banks, with the aim to reduce capital requirements for banks engaged in trade 
finance and thus foster the import of goods for low income countries.

Question 57 / 58 / 82 - 84: Cash Conversion Factors  
The CCFs applied under the FIRB are the same as those in the standardised approach in the revised 
Basel III. The FIRB CCF’s have been aligned with the SA and AIRB CCF’s. The most relevant CCF’s 
for trade related bank exposure are: 
• A 100% CCF will be applied to guarantees and SBLC’s serving as financial guarantees for 
loans and securities.  
• A 50% CCF will be applied to certain transaction-related contingent items (eg performance 
bonds, bid bonds, warranties and standby letters of credit related to particular transactions). 
• A 20% CCF will be applied to both the issuing and confirming banks of short-term self-
liquidating trade letters of credit arising from the movement of goods (eg documentary credits 
collateralised by the underlying shipment).

• Guarantees and SBLC’s are amongst the most contingent of Trade Finance instruments as 
they are used for specific transactions. We would therefore consider a 20% conversion factor 
more appropriate, as is currently defined in the CRR for SBLC’s. We recommend  that a 20% 
conversion factor will also be adopted in the revised CRR for guarantees, which are used for 
specific transactions. For further evidence refer to the paper published by ICC and GCD.  
• LC’s confirmed: we consider the 20% conversion factor of the revised Basel III more 
appropriate than the 50% of the current CRR. A confirmed LC remains contingent, unless the 
issuing bank does not honour its acceptance at LC maturity. Considering the low PD’s of the 
ICC Trade register, the vast majority of confirmed LC’s remains contingent and a 20% 
conversion factor is therefore more than conservative already. 
• Bid and Performance bond: whereas it is positive that bid- and performance bonds have now 
specifically been labelled in the revised Basel III, the 50% risk weight is considered on the 
high side compared to the actual drawings under such instruments which we observe in 
practice. For further evidence refer to the paper published by ICC and GCD.  
 
Conclusion and recommendation: the revised Basel III CCF’s for SA and FIRB are not materially 
different from the current CRR. Particularly the 100% risk weight for transaction specific guarantees, 
performance bonds and SBLC’s should be lowered. The factors have to remain conservative, but not 
become overly conservative compared to actual conversion factors observed in practice for trade 
finance exposures.

Question 62: costs of reducing the scope of internal modelling: further background on the 
impact of the 2.5y FIRB Maturity Floor 
 
Under the FIRB all exposures11 are assigned a maturity value (M) of 2,5 years, including trade finance 
related bank exposures. Applying a maturity of 2,5 years  is mismatched with the actual short tenor of 
trade finance transactions and results in unnecessarily high regulatory capital charges for a trade 
finance transaction. 
 
The short term nature of trade finance is recognized throughout the CRR under both the standardised 
and the advanced IRB approach. The Standardised Approach assigns lower risk weights to all bank 
exposures with an original maturity of three months or less, as well as exposures to bank s that arise 
from the movement of goods across national borders with an original maturity of six months or less.  
 
Under the AIRB banks could assign a maturity of at least one day for qualifying short-term exposures 
which are not part of the institution's ongoing financing of the obligor. Qualifying short-term exposures 
include self-liquidating short-term trade financing transactions connected to the exchange of goods or 
services with a residual maturity of up to one year. Trade finance is defined in the CR R as financing, 
including guarantees, connected to the exchange of goods and services through financial products of 
fixed short-term maturity, generally of less than one year, without automatic rollover.

Particularly banks / institutions in developing and least developed countries (as defined by IMF and 
World Bank) tend to have a weaker credit rating. Such banks do however play a significant role in 
facilitating trade as confirmed  letters of credit provide exporters with addit ional protection against 
any losses incurred from importers’ and issuing banks’ failure to meet their obligations of payments. 
The FIRB capital requirement for banks on poorer ratings does not do any justice t o the low default 
rates displayed across the bo ard as demonstrated by ICC, which is especially true for the shorter 
tenors. On the contrary: compared to the lower PDs demonstrated by the ICC Trade Register, the 
FIRB will require the confirming banks to hol d disproportionately high regulatory capital fo r 
exposures from such transactions, making this business unfeasible for the confirming banks and 
more expensive for exporters and importers. This is not in line with the intentions of the World Bank 
Basel Committee to mitigate the impact of the regulatory framework on trade finance programs of 
developing countries14. 
 
Given that banks can only opt for SA or FIRB,  a comparison between SA and FIRB in terms of 
consequential RC/asset ratios leads to a highly un -level playing field. Depending on portfolio 
characteristics, banks may prefer one option above the other, which is an undesirable and highly 
impactful consequence of the fact that FIRB is not adapted to the specifics of Trade Finance. This is 
particularly worrisome from a competitive- as well as a risk-taking point of view.  
Furthermore we observe that the reduction of risk weight is minimal in poorer rating classes. 
Consequentially, there is little benefit from reducing tenor, and the impact of that is less t han for 
better-rated exposures. This is counter intuitive: especially for lower -rated counterparties, a shorter 
tenor should be ‘rewarded’. The current insensitivity in this curve thus yields an adverse incentive.  
Possible solutions for this dilemma include: 
1. Regulatory guidance to allow utilization of ICC Trade Register empirical PD’s for trade 
related bank exposures. This would require the exception that two PD’s are allowed for the 
same rating class, which is thus far not the case in the Basel IRB models. 
2. Regulatory guidance to allow util ization of both SA trade related bank exposures by a bank  
currently on IRB 15, without the requirement of a material change request 16 as it may take 
years before such request is addressed by the authority.

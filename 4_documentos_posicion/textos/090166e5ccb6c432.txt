I – Executive summary  
On December 2017, the Basel Committee published a document called “Basel III: Finalising post-crisis 
reforms”.  
French banks prepared this document in order to address it to European Commission officers that are 
in charge of designing the new European regulation. Its objectives are:  
1. Put forward the impacts of the regulation on trade finance business and as a consequence, 
on real economy  
(i) International Trade is a strong contributor of economic activity in Europe. 
European corporates need banks to finance their exports/imports through 
dedicated Trade Finance products. European banks became instrumental in 
developing and ensuring competitiveness of European corporates through 
efficient and innovative Trade Finance products. 
(ii) Energy/ metals mining / an d Agri industries are key to ensure a strong level of 
efficiency and autonomy in Europe. Oil & Gas companies, steel and base metal 
industries, Agri and food corporates have been able to grow in Europe, ensure 
autonomy and supply basic needs of all European s namely thanks to the global 
expertise developed by European banks in Trade Finance. 
(iii) European banks are among Global Leaders in Trade Finance activities playing a key 
role in supporting European economy. However, some expected regulatory 
changes will have  a direct impact on their competitiveness. Traditional Trade 
Finance products shall become costlier for the banks. These will have no other 
choice than to increase price of their services or reduce their Trade Finance 
services.  
(iv) European Corporates will be  impacted. They will be less competitive or need to 
find alternative offers from non -European financial institutions and / non -
regulated entities. Should new financing players emerge, they may not bring the 
same level of expertise and transparency. The bes t standards brought by the 
European banks in terms of compliance, money laundering controls; sustainability, 
environment & social policies, etc. might not be addressed by these new players 
in a proper manner.  
(v) Trade Finance exposures in banks can be classi fied in various portfolios: 
commodities finance (Specialized Lending) when the underlying goods and type of 
transaction correspond to the regulatory criteria, but also corporate and/or bank 
exposures for other types of underlying goods or transactions.

2. Suggest some upgrades/clarifications on the regulation  
This document will focus on five topics that strongly impact trade finance business:  
1. CCF on technical guarantees or transaction-related contingent items  
2. Definition of UCCs (Unconditionally Cancellable Commitments) 
3. Maturity used under IRB approach 
4. LGD input floors and haircut on the collateral value for commodities trade 
5. IRB foundation LGD value for bank exposure linked to trade Letter of Credit activity. 
The treatment of unrated corporates in SA at 100% RW is also a very penalizing for trade activities of  
A-IRB banks (through the output floor based on the SA), as trade activities have large exposures to 
unrated corporates. We urge the Commission to take into account FBF proposals regarding the 
important issue of unrated corporates in ECRA. There is no unique rule here for ECRA and SCRA and 
for an unrated corporate (for example a subsidiary of a group) which has an Investment Grade quality, 
a 100% RW would apply whereas 65% would apply under the SCRA rul es, thus implying an unlevel 
playing field between Europe and the US. It is key for European exports to support trade activities and 
thus  enable banks to use their ability to assess credit rating for unrated corporates.  
Those combined impacts may increase the RWA charge of a typical trade finance instrument by x 2,5 
(and the increase may reach even x 6 1). This increase will, for sure have a strong impact on Banks 
practices in this business either by increasing the pricing, or (if not possible or not to th e extent 
needed), by reducing the exposure to those products. In every case, this lack of clarification or excess 
of conservatism may have an important and unintended impact on European economy by increasing 
financing solution pricing or by pushing the mar ket to use less secured (being counterproductive) 
financing solutions and shadow banking or unsecured transactions.  
 
French banks urge the European Commission to take into consideration the impact of those five 
topics on trade activity in Europe and consider the suggestions developed in this document:  
1. Maintaining the CCF for technical guarantees at 20%  
2.Clarify the definition of UCCs, using the national discretion offered by the Basel Committee and 
transpose footnote 53 
3. Maintain the effective maturity as a risk driver in terms of differentiation, in F-IRB too, with a 1 day 
floor.  
4. Applying directly to A-IRB LGDs a 10% the LGD input floor for high -quality commodities 
transaction  
5. Define a lower IRB foundation LGD for bank exposure linked to trade Letter of Credit activity

introduced by CRR for transaction-related contingent items (20%) is already conservative enough.

2. Classification as commitment, Unconditionally Cancellable Commitment (UCC) or 
uncommitted  
BCBS position: “A 10% CCF will be applied to commitments that are unconditionally cancellable” 
French Banking Industry position: For trade finance we support the possibility of using the national 
discretion  regarding the defintion of a commitment and hence exclude from commitments the 
cases that fulfill the foot note 53, for  Corporates and Financial Institutions ( unconditionally 
cancellable  and for which banks receive no fees).  
In this section, we also refer to the publication issued by the Basel Committee concerning the proposed 
regulation “Basel III: Finalising post -crisis reforms”. In page 25 and 26 of the documents, especially 
concerning the credit conversion factor that is applied to UCC (cf. paragraphs 78 and 84, footnote #53) 
 
Unconditionally Cancellable Commitments (UCC) are essential for financing the economy. It enables 
banks to grant financing solutions to corporate clients with the possibility to monito r / restrict any 
drawings before any sign of weakness is identified. For example, undrawn Trade Finance lines are often 
provided on an uncommitted basis, requiring a prior agreement of the bank before any drawdown by 
the client. The same situation is encou ntered in case of undrawn lines for discounting receivables. 
These commitments should not be unduly penalized. 
The Basel Committee allows jurisdictions to exempt under national discretion certain arrangements 
from the definition of commitment, provided tha t 4 conditions are met. Therefore, we suggest the 
adoption of the following definition for commitments:  
 “Commitment means any contractual arrangement that has been offered by the bank and accepted 
by the client to extend credit, purchase assets or issue credit substitutes and that is recorded in the 
financial statements. It excludes arrangements that satisfy the following conditions:  
i. the bank receives no fees or commissions to establish or maintain the arrangement and, 
ii. the client is required to apply to the bank for the initial and each subsequent drawdown and, 
iii. the bank has full authority over the execution of each drawdown, regardless of the fulfilment by 
the client of the conditions set out in the facility documentation, including situations of  
overdrafts, where clients can draw but the bank monitor clients exposures and can interrupt 
drawing at any time, and  
iv. the bank’s decision on the execution of each drawdown is only made after assessing the 
creditworthiness of the client immediately prior to drawdown(*). ” 
(*) condition (iv) is deemed satisfied when banks have monitoring tools procedures to detect any 
significant deterioration of a client’s credit quality.” 
Basel III restricts (footnote #53 p25) the exempted arrangements to certain arrangeme nts for 
Corporate and SMEs. FBF suggests removing this limitation which is not justified economically if the 
four conditions are met.  
 
  
 
 
3. Maturity 
BCBS position: “National supervisors may allow the effective maturity to be fixed at 2.5 years (the 
‘fixed maturity treatment’).” 
French Banking Industry position:  We suggest maintaining the current IRB treatment of effective 
maturity  for advanced and foundation methods. 
 
The Basel Committee allows a jurisdiction (at national discretion) to keep applying the ef fective 
maturity instead of the fixed maturity treatment of 2,5 years.  
French banks consider that maturity is a key risk driver for Trade Finance exposures and believe it is 
relevant to use It in the future IRB approach. 
 
For IRB Foundation approach, the effective maturity can be chosen by the National Supervisor 
(paragraph 107). In paragraph 111, the effective maturity is mentioned for export, confirmation letters 
of credit ie bank risk (the exporter bank is in risk on the importer bank).  
French Banking Industry strongly recommends maintaining the current IRB treatment of effective 
maturity for foundation and advanced methods, including FI , taking into account the current 1 day 
floor on trade instruments such as Letter of Credits and 90 days for Discounts of receivables as 
mentioned in CRR art.162-33  and paragraph 111 (IRB) of the document d 424 of the Basel 
Committee ,  and the five years cap.

4. LGD input floors and haircut on the collateral value penalizing commodities trade 
We appreciate that the BCBS decided to maintain internal models for SL. Nevertheless, the revised 
BIII text is very penalizing, regarding both the Standardised Approach, which considers 
Commodities Finance like unsecured corporate loans and the IRB, which now includes high LGD 
input floors together with a unique fixed haircut on the asset value, thus removing any risk 
sensitivity. We underline that such LGD input floors and fixed haircut, regarding SL, were not 
discussed nor tested in the QIS before the publication of the BCBS Dec 2017 text. It seems SL 
activities were somehow omitted in the final text regarding input floors and by consequence a very 
penalizing treatment is applied, designed for general corporates, not for SL.  
French Banking Industry position: In order to re-introduce risk sensitivity, we propose more 
granularity for RW for the SA depending on the quality of the collateral and on the structuring 
features and a 10% LGD input floors applying directly to the A-IRB LGD  in order not to penalize low 
risk transactions, 
In this section we refer to the publication issued by the Basel Committee concerning the proposed 
future regulation “Basel III: Finalising post-crisis reforms”. In page 67 to 69 of the document, especially 
concerning the LGD under the advanced approach and the applicable haircut level: 
 
The revised Basel framework, in which specialized lending exposures continue to be unduly penalized 
compared to unsecured corporate exposures, is in our opinion, still not adequate in its current design 
particularly for Commodity Finance. It is also mostly a subject for European IRB banks (versus banks in 
the United States), which have dedicated and experienced teams, with good track records, whereas 
the revised Basel III framework will very negatively impact these activities. 
 
 
Commodity Finance covers the financing of the physical supply chains to three primary commodities 
sectors, namely, “Energy” (Oil & Gas and others), “Metals” (and Minerals), and “Agricultural Products” 
(Softs and others). Commodity Finance lending is at the forefront of the real economy where banks 
facilitate physical commodity supply chains that fuel industry, feed manufacturing and nourish 
populations. It does not include the financing of manufactured products trade. 
 
Commodity Finance relates to short term self -liquidating facilities, (i.e. that can well proceed 
independently of the borrower) and benefit from securities on the commodities financed and on 
receivables. 
 
Imposing measures which penalize this form of Speci alized Lending may result in a withdrawal of 
liquidity from many borrowers whose performance ability relies on this form of finance. 
 
It has to be highlighted that Commodity Finance loss rates (0.12% 4) are lower than corporate 
exposures. This can be explained by: 
- The self-liquidating nature of such financings 
- Their short term nature (i.e. 30-90 days) 
- The liquidity of the underlying (inventory or receivable) that are financed 
- The level of control that Banks can exercise over the collateral 
- The quality of the security package related to such financings

Advanced IRB approach 
The revised Basel framework applies unduly penalizing LGD input floors 5 in A-IRB which denies the 
observed good recovery data, the comprehensive security package from which SL lenders benefit, as 
well as the risk sensitivity proposed in the IRB -A approach where LGD formula takes into account 
variations of the asset value. The IRB LGD input floors were not designed for SL exposures, but for 
general corporate exposures and were actually not tested in the QIS done before the publication of 
the final text: 
- for unsecured corporate exposures, the LGD input floor (25%) is set roughly at 2/3 of the unsecured 
LGD (40%),  
- whereas for SL and more specifically in Commodities finance, the input floors (comprised between 
10%/15% and 25%) correspond to roughly 100% or more of the observed LGD (13.3%6);  
Setting a floor at the level of the observed average does not make sense and penalizes the best 
transactions and most of the good ones. The high-quality Commodities transactions should be able 
to benefit from a 10% floor  
 
 
In the calculation formula for this LGD floor, we also believe that the uniform 40% haircut level 
proposed by the Basel Committee, mirroring the level proposed in the IRB -F approach, is in fact a 
rejection of the IRB-A approach, as it does not allow IRB-A banks to reflect differences in the quality of 
collateral and transaction structures between various transactions. French banks use  risk sensitivite  
A-IRBmodels, which already take into account potential price risk when considering the nature of the 
collateral and the structuring features of the transaction (price risk hedges or fixed price also taken 
into account). Indeed, price risk regarding the value of the collateral is already closely framed by 
Tripartite Agreements (TPA) between the client, the  Bank and a Broker or through a margin call

5. Lower LGD value in IRB Foundation for bank exposure for Letter of Credit Business under 
UCP rules 
 
BCBS position: the A-IRB approach cannot be used for the exposures in the bank asset class 
(paragraph 34). Under foundation approach, senior claims on banks will be assigned 45% LGD 
(paragraph 70) 
French Banking Industry position:  We suggest a lower LGD value under foundation approach for 
Bank exposure linked to Letter of Credit activity under UCP 600. 
 
The Uniform customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, 2007 Revision, ICC Publication No 600 
(“UCP”) are rules that apply to any documentary credit (including standby letter of credit) when the 
text of the credit expressly indicates that is subject to these rules (Art 1 of UCP 600) 
In the trade business under UCP 600, the risk scheme of letter of credit is: the exporter corporate 
(beneficiary) in risk on his ban k, the negotiating or confirming exporter bank in risk on the issuing 
importer bank,  the issuing importer bank in risk on the importer corporate (applicant).  
The Letter of credit can be discounted by the exporter corporate without recourse: bank exposure , 
letter of credit transaction corresponds to a trade flow, not a bank financing. The discounting and/or 
confirming bank will receive the LC repayment from the issuing bank at maturity date. At the same 
time (technical delay), the issuing bank receives pay ment from the importer and repay the 
confirming/discounting exporter bank. 
The issuing bank default doesn’t mean a stop of trade activity between importers and exporters. To 
avoid to be excluded from this activity under international rules, banks have ince ntive to fulfill their 
commitments even in case of default period, with the repayment of a trade transaction from the 
importer at the same time. 
French Banking industry therefore also recommends to not mix this bank risk trade activity with other 
bank business in term of recovery. As risk driver, French banks request for this activity a lower LGD 
applied for the foundation method, this bank activity being less risky. 
 
   
 
 
Conclusion: 
This document highlights some important enhancements/clarifications that French banks would like 
to suggest to European Commission in order to have a consistent regulation in the Trade Finance 
market:  
 
1. Maintain the CCF at the conservative level of 20% (maintain CRR Annex I ) 
2. Transpose footnote 53 regarding UCCs and ensure its consistency for Corporate and FI 
counterparts, using the offered national discretion 
3. Maintain the effective maturity as a risk dri ver in the capital charge framework (including FI 
exposures), also in F-IRB and with a one day floor (as transposed in the current CRR).  
4. Aligning downwards to 10% the LGD input floor for high -quality commodities transactions and 
removing any reference to a unique 40% supervisory haircut as well as any limitation to the 
modeling of collateral . This 10% LGD input floor would apply directly to the A-IRB LGDs.  
5. Define a lower IRB foundation LGD for bank exposure linked to trade Letter of Credit activity 
under international rules 
6. Apply FBF proposed solutions for unrated corporates. (please see FBF answers to the 
consultation).  
 
French banks remain available in case the European Commission needed any precision on the above 
arguments.

Implementing the Final Basel III Reforms in the EU 
 
We are pleased to be able to provide comments to the European Commission on the final Basel 
III reforms in the EU. 
 
The International Underwriting Association of London (IUA) represents international and 
wholesale insurance and reinsurance companies operat ing in or through London. It exists to 
promote and enhance the business environment for its members.1 
 
As part of our membership services, IUA operates a Political Risk and Trade Credit underwriting 
committee, comprising a large proportion of our members p roviding insurance to financial 
institutions. This committee has assisted in preparing this response, while we have also worked 
closely with other relevant associations, such as the Lloyd’s Market Association (LMA). 
 
As we are an insurance trade association, we deemed three questions (Q64, Q102 and Q109) 
directly relevant to our members’ insurance operations and, consequently, we will only 
concentrate on these areas. 
 
Question 64: In your view, which other aspects, if any, might be considered in the context 
of the revision of the scope of internal modelling to address RWA variability? Please 
provide relevant evidence to substantiate your views. 
 
We respectfully request that the Eur opean Commission considers that, although insurance 
companies fall within the financial institution definition, the low number of defaults observed for 
insurance companies is the result of the unique regulated capital of insurance companies as 
well as the priority afforded to policyholders. Therefore, where financial institutions’ exposure to 
insurance companies is in the context of using non-payment insurance as Credit Risk Mitigation 
(CRM), the use of the AIRB approach is warranted given the protections a gainst default by 
insurers such as ringfenced assets and capital, with strict reserving requirements imposed by 
insurance regulators. This is further enforced by the privileged position that banks hold as 
policyholders (as stated, for example, in Directive  2009/13/EC: “ insurance claims take 
precedence over other claims against the insurance undertaking”).

Key risk differentiators that should be permitted in modelling the PD and LGD of banks’ claims 
as policyholders: 
 
1. The banks’ claims as policyholders are in a privileged position compared to other unsecured 
creditors’ claims in the unlikely event of the bankruptcy of an insurer (see, for example, The 
Insurers (Reorganisation and Winding Up Regulations) 2004, Part IV, r egulations 21-26); 
regulated insurance companies have minimal, if any, preferential debt. Furthermore, 
borrowings by insurance groups are done at the holdings level, outside the regulated entity 
which holds the capital and thus are structurally subordinated. Typically (for all but the largest 
and best-rated insurers) the debt ratings (Credit / Issuer Default Rating) of insurance groups 
are lower than the claims paying rating (Financial Strength Rating) of an insurer, as reflected 
in ratings of insurers published by credit rating agencies.  
 
2. Moody’s, Standard & Poor’s and Fitch rating agencies all provide Insurance Financial 
Strength Ratings which address an insurer’s ability to pay policyholder obligations and claims 
punctually and also reflect the expected financial loss suffered in the event of default.  They 
also assign ratings to specific instruments issued by the insurer or its holding company 
(Issuer Default Ratings), which are often notched, or rated, below the Financial Strength 
Ratings.2 By way of co mparison, it is worth noting that a number of EU insurers are rated 
higher than the long-term Issuer Default Rating of A+ 
 
3. Banks’ priority claims as policyholders is most readily compared to banking depositor 
preference versus unsecured creditors. In addit ion, Deposit Guarantee Schemes (DGSs) 
afford depositors further protection under regulatory supervision. Insurance policyholders 
benefit from a similar scheme. The European Insurance regulator, EIOPA, has implemented 
an Insurance Guarantee Scheme that func tions as a similar backstop resource to the 
policyholder protection regulations. 
 
4. The priority ranking of policyholders is explicitly protected in the Solvency II Directive3:  Article 
275 of DIRECTIVE 2009/138/EC OF THE EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE 
COUNCIL of 25 November 2009: “1. Member States shall ensure that insurance claims take 
precedence over other claims against the insurance undertaking in one or both of the 
following ways: (a) with regard to assets representing the technical provisions, insuranc e 
claims shall take absolute precedence over any other claim on the insurance undertaking; or 
(b) with regard to the whole of the assets of the insurance undertaking, insurance claims shall 
take precedence over any other claim on the insurance undertaking with the only possible 
exception of the following: (i) claims by employees arising from employment contracts and 
employment relationships; (ii) claims by public bodies on taxes; (iii) claims by social security 
systems; (iv) claims on assets subject to rights in rem.”

7. The performance of non -payment insurance as CRM for banks has been exceptional.  
According to a survey of leading brokers of transactional (single risk) insurance, in the period

operational failure by the insured institution (failure to comply with explicit policy 
requirements), but insurers still paid 44% of those claims in settlement agreements.

Question 102: In your view, which other aspects, if any, should be considered in the 
context of revising the CRM framework under the SA-CR [standardised approach]?  
 
In revising the CRM framework under the SA -CR, we respectfully request that the E uropean 
Commission considers the position of the bank as policyholder and the unique strength of 
insurers as CRM counterparties. We suggest that the EU Commission consider clarifications in 
the Guidelines that cater for the unique advantages of credit insurance as CRM, particularly with 
respect to superior recoveries.  Insurance is a significant supporter of European bank lending, 
both within the EU and in emerging markets, for transactions and in geographies where banks 
have fewer CRM options. 
 
Reason 1: Enhanced counterparty: Credit insurance, provided by regulated insurers, affords 
the banking industry an enhanced counterparty risk over and above that of a direct unsecured 
creditor. The fact that external credit agencies assign Issuer Default Ratings (IDR) for the benefit 
of d irect unsecured creditors that are generally lower than the Financial Strength Rating 
assigned for the benefit of policyholders is evidence of this. It therefore is not the same as a 
direct exposure to the Credit Risk Mitigation (CRM) provider, as with a guarantee from a banking 
institution. In light of this, we believe the application of Loss Given Default (LGD) floors should 
take this stronger creditor position into account. 
 
Reason 2: Insurers are low-correlated Credit Risk Mitigation providers: The insurance industry 
is less correlated to the policyholder than other banking and financial institutions. The core 
business of multiline insurers  operates in international property, casualty and specialty 
insurance and reinsurance markets and so is less exposed to financial risks than other CRM 
providers. This reduces systemic risk and we believe the application of LGD floors should 
consider this ma terial benefit. This provides institutions using insurance as CRM with well -
capitalised, stable counterparties.   
 
Reason 3: Support where other CRM tools not available: As insurance underwriters are also 
able to assess complex transactions and are prepare d to provide CRM for institutions’ 
exposures in emerging markets, insurance is able to support lending where other CRM tools 
are not available.   
 
Consideration should be given to: 
 
1. Claims of banks as policyholders benefit from regulatory ring -fencing of a ssets to secure 
outstanding liabilities to policyholders at the operating company level; bolstered in 
circumstances where the obligor is in distress by provisioning required by insurance 
regulators for exposures where the insurer has a potential claim liability.  This ring-fencing of 
assets for the benefit of banks as policyholders should be recognised, and therefore the LGD 
should be reduced to acknowledge this benefit to banks as policyholders, rather than 
creditors, of an insurance undertaking.

2. As noted in the Fitch Recovery Rating scale (Fitch Insurer Rating Criteria, 11 January 2019, 
p.105-106), recovery rates for policyholders could be expected to be well above the recovery 
rate implied by the 45% LGD floor currently prescribed for financial i nstitutions including 
insurance companies.  
 
3. As a reminder of how non -payment insurance operates, banks do not expect insurance to 
act as an immediate source of repayment, unlike parent company guarantees that are 
obtained as a credit enhancement in support of a subsidiary. Banks would generally expect 
to work with the debtor to remedy a default or discuss rescheduling in the event this is 
feasible, in the knowledge that the insurer will pay a valid claim where required.  Even if able 
to claim immediately, these banks generally prefer to attempt to address the problem 
themselves. Moreover, the problem is often resolved within 180 days of the borrower default, 
through (late) payment or restructuring. (Policies also adapt to work-outs; policies are usually 
amended to reflect a revised repayment schedule.)   
 
4. The claims process for a bank under a non -payment insurance policy operates in the 
following way: 
 
4.1 The policy is already tailored to the specific exposure that the bank is running and 
the bank has a direct relationship with the insurer, allowing communication and 
certainty during the claims process. 
 
4.2 A claim can be made if the work-out has not been agreed by the time the cure/claim 
settlement period has elapsed (although, as noted above, the preferred course is 
normally that the policy is restructured to follow the workout for the reasons detailed 
above).  
 
4.3 The claims payment process is highly prescribed and includes a detailed timeframe 
and specifies the steps and information the bank must take or provide to successfully 
conclude the process. 
 
4.4 The insured’s rights under the contract, including damages for late payment, are 
protected by law and precedent. 
 
4.5 The policy allows for active engagement by the insured bank to ensure its claim is 
processed in an acceptable manner.

Question 109: In your view, which other aspects, if any, should be considered in the 
context of revising the CRM framework under the IRBA? 
  
We recommend that the European Commission consider clarifications in the Guidelines that  
cater for the unique advantages of credit insurance as CRM, particularly with respect to superior 
recoveries.  Insurance is a significant support of European bank lending both within the EU and 
in emerging markets, for transactions and in geographies where banks have fewer CRM options.  
 
Insurance is a well -capitalised, well -regulated sector capable of managing the credit risk it 
underwrites without threat to the stability of the financial sector.  During the global financial crisis 
(2007-2009), non-payment insurance proved its worth as a credible credit risk mitigant by paying 
out over USD2.5 billion in claims to banks and commercial entities.  
 
Since then banks have increasingly turned to this product to support their lending, particularly 
as a risk distribution tool that enables banks to  increase their lending activity. Certain banks’ 
ability to obtain regulatory capital relief on the non-payment insurance product has also made it 
a more economically feasible, as well as effective, risk transfer that compares favourably to 
other CRM tools. 
 
The IUA would welcome future discussions with the EC or the EBA in relation to any of the 
issues that arise in this Consultation or any aspect of CRM relating to the CRR. 
 
1. The LGD for institutions’ exposures to insurers as policyholders should reflect this 
privileged position rather than being assigned the same LGD for the institution’s direct 
exposure as creditor. 
  
1.1 This improved recovery position of institutions using non-payment insurance as CRM is 
not the same as their recovery position as direct ex posures as an unsecured creditor: 
therefore, this should not be considered a comparable exposure.  The following support 
this position: 
 
1.1.1 As stated in Directive 2009/138/EC, claims against insurers by policyholders 
take precedence over other claims [see our answers to other questions, for 
example, Question 64]. 
1.1.2 The Insurer Financial Strength Ratings provided by external credit assessment 
institutions such as Moody’s Standard & Poor’s and Fitch are often notched, or 
rated, higher than the Issuer Default Ratings provided to unsecured creditors.  
This also reflects the lower expected financial loss suffered in the event of 
default.  
1.1.3 The actual outcome for policyholders in the (relatively rare) event of insolvency 
justifies a lower LGD.

2. Non-payment insurance as CRM is an important support for bank lending; change s 
contemplated in the finalisation of Basel III in Europe risk reducing the economic 
efficiency of the product as CRM, which could have a significant negative impact on 
lending and trade and put European banks at a competitive disadvantage. 
 
2.1 For example, a  broker of transactional (or “single situation”) insurance has confirmed 
that USD 2.4 billion in coverage placed for a banking client’s trade and export finance 
lending supported USD 13.5 billion in contract values.4 
 
2.2 Preliminary data from a LMA/IUA sponsored survey of leading insurers and brokers of 
“transactional” or single -situation non -payment insurance show that every USD1 of 
insurance supported on average USD17 in bank facilities financing economic activity, 
including project finance, corporate lending and trade finance.   This type of insurance 
supports European banks and the corporates that benefit from their financing. The 
survey results were affirmed by preliminary data from the whole -turnover, or portfolio 
credit insurance market, which showed a similar multiplier effect.  The preliminary data 
also substantiated that non -payment insurance is a valuable tool supporting bank 
lending in emerging markets and for transactions where CDS and other CRM tools are 
not available: roughly two-thirds of the non-payment insurance registered on the survey 
was for exposures in emerging markets, with 26% of support provided in Africa, for 
example, where banks have little or no recourse to other private sector risk transfer tools. 
 
2.3 Bank respondents to a survey conducted by the International Association of Credit 
Portfolio Managers use credit insurance to support a wide variety of lending: short-term 
trade finance, asset-backed lending, and project finance, all of which support investment 
and trade.  Also of note was the use of credit insurance for emerging market lending and 
for sub-investment grade corporate credits as well as investment grade credits, as other 
credit risk mitigation is not usually available for these exposures.  The majority of IACPM 
respondents were banks based in EMEA, specifically Europe: removing their ability to 
benefit from this unique CRM would be disadvantageous. 
 
2.4 As an example of the reliance of small and medium size business (SME) on bank 
financing supported by non -payment insurance, a n SME plumbers’ merchant with an 
EU country distributorship for some European products has a cash cycle that requires 
payment for product a full 2 months before cash is received from buyers.   Terms to 
suppliers are 30 days after end of month from despatch on the continent.  The product 
is brought into the EU country weekly, and orders are made up for construction industry 
customers where payment terms are typically 60 days end of month (and there are 
retentions).

The business depends on credit insurance being in place, with sales made up to the 
level of the credit insurer’s limit on each buyer, the bank financing the company’s 
invoices and sharing in credit insurance claims.   The company has variously arranged 
the credit i nsurance policy itself, attaching the bank as joint policyholder for financed 
debts, and has also used the bank’s invoice finance facility whereby the bank buys the 
invoices and is the policyholder.  Both solutions provide the valuable protection for the 
receivables asset against bad debt (non -payment within 6 months of due date) and 
insolvency, and enable the working capital finance to flow so that the business can 
thrive. 
 
3. Use of non-payment insurance is correlated neither with insurers’ other exposures or 
liabilities, nor with the banks’ exposure to the underlying obligor. 5  This substantially 
lowers systemic risk. 
 
3.1 The insurance industry’s ability to absorb large losses is well tested: The figure paid by 
those same insurers during the global financial crisis – the most severe test of the non-
payment product to date – was roughly USD 2.5 billion; at the same time the insurance 
industry handled roughly USD 100 billion in natural catastrophe losses 6. Capacity for 
this class of insurance has grown significantly since then, with some insurers still making 
recoveries 10 years later, reducing the loss to insurers. Evidence of the resilience of the 
insurance market is also reflected in the figures from 2017 and 2018 in respect of losses 
paid due to hurricanes and  other natural catastrophes; in 2017 insurers paid roughly 
USD 144 billion and in 2018 they paid USD 85 billion, with no recoveries expected from 
these losses; yet additional capital has already replaced the losses. 
 
3.2 The performance of non -payment insurance as CRM for banks has been exceptional.  
According to a survey of leading brokers of transactional (single risk) insurance, in the 
period 2007 to 2018, 486 claims (totalling USD 3,189,199,638) were made by financial 
institutions on their non -payment polic ies, of which 471, or 97% (totalling USD 
3,068,833,457), were paid in full and within the required timeframe. The remaining 
claims were rejected due to operational failure by the insured institution (failure to comply 
with explicit policy requirements), but insurers still paid 44% of those claims in settlement 
agreements. 
 
Depending on the outcome of this consultation and the February EBA Consultation, the  non-
affirmation of the efficacy of non -payment insurance as CRM could shrink the availability of 
trade, export, structured credit and corporate financing from banks who have incorporated 
partnership with insurers in their business and risk management models.

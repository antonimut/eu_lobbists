Response to question 191 by Triodos Bank 
 
Triodos Bank applauds the Commission’s courage to introduce longer term risk considerations in project 
finance that is actually longer term oriented, such as infrastructure that serves a public need, in the 
new Article 501a CRR, as brought home in co -decision with Parliament and Council in CRR2. We 
encourage the Commission to expand on this concept when thinking about integrating sustainability in 
the prudential framework. This could regard several options: 
a) requiring banks to measure, monitor and manage the carbon footprint of their total exposures; 
b) incentivize longer term considerations by adding a layer in assessing banks’ risk management 
(pillar 4; or a new chapter in pillar 2)  
c) steering banks away from fossil based exposures when they don’t measure them. 
 
a) Carbon accounting by banks 
Outside of looking at climate risk at a deal -by-deal level, we are in favour of increasingly steering 
complete portfolios towards a decarbonisation path, thereby reducing both transition -related and 
systemic risk created by climate change. 
 
In this area, banks have the possibility to show at a portfolio level how progress is being made 
towards decarbonisation, using customer data in combination with sector available statistics from 
reputable institutions. We note that a good approach has been developed by the Partnership for 
Carbon Accounting Financials (PCAF), enabling financial institutions to assess and disclose greenhouse 
gas emissions of loans and investments. Another initiative is undertaken by the 2 Degrees Initiative to 
extend their PACTA tool to bank lending, which provides such a metric at sector and borrower basis. 
Both organisations invest in relevant statistical data to be able to measure in a science-based way 
progress towards the Paris goals of limiting climate change, which seems the only valid way of 
evaluating actual contribution to preventing climate risk and related financial damage.  
 
An effective provision to this end could be achieved with adding for example the following elements 
to  
Article 449a CRR, regarding disclosure requirements: 
“a description of significant concentrations of credit exposures against carbon-related assets 
i. The parameters that the institution used to evaluate the impact of short -, medium- and long-
term climate-related risks on lending and financial intermediary services, if these are material;  
ii. a description of the role of the board with regard to the evaluation and management of 
climate-related risks. 
iii. for climate-related risks, as per the recommendations of the Task Force on Climat e-related 
Financial Disclosure and the Article 2c of the Paris Agreement, a forward -looking scenario 
analysis disclosing how the lending portfolio aligns with the Paris Agreement’ objective of 
limiting global warming well below 2°C.” 
The disclosure provisi ons can follow the EU’s taxonomy for environmental classification of financed 
activities, using existing sub-categories of the NACE classification. 
 
 
b) longer term considerations in risk management 
The current prudential framework has a time horizon of 1-3 years, and as such it cannot weigh against 
each other, or take into account both credit risk and long -term climate risk within one calibration. In 
our view, to mix the short-term credit risk considerations with longer term climate risk considerations, 
would lead to conceptually unsound discussions with potentially undesired side effects or incentives. 
 
A potential solution is to make a conscious distinction between credit risk and climate/ESG risk of which 
the horizon is much longer and more unpredictable. Basically this means that the prudential framework

should be supplemented by incorporating long-term risks in such a way that banks will logically choose 
to finance more sustainable activities. This could be done in a transparent, conceptually sound way by 
including a new Pillar to the framework. As such, Pillar 1 remains fully focused on a quantitative way 
to calculate (in this case) credit risk with a one year horizon, Pillar 2 includes stress testing from a 
viewpoint of guaranteeing sufficient capital with a 3-year horizon and in Pillar 3 financial institutions 
disclose relevant information which is auditable, not just from a short to medium-term risk perspective, 
but also from a long-term perspective by including information about sustainable & unsustainable parts 
of the business.  
  
In order to capture the new risk type’s horizon, we propose adding a fourth Pillar. Pillar 4 would  
incorporate at least to measures: transition risk, and decision making.  
 
“Transition risk” is the risk of materializing known stress events. It can easily be incorporated in stress 
testing and could lead to add-ons in pillar 2; Article 325bj(1) CRR could be adjusted as follows: 
 
“(g) the institution shall frequently conduct a rigorous programme of stress testing, including reverse 
stress tests, including climate scenarios, which shall encompass any internal risk- measurement model. 
The results of these str ess tests shall be reviewed by senior management on at least a monthly basis 
and comply with the policies and limits approved by the institution's management body. (…)” 
 
Additionally, it could be made more explicit through EBA guidelines (e.g. Internal Gov ernance or Loan 
Origination and Monitoring) that a view on sustainability is included credit decision making.  
  
Incentivizing credit institutions is possible via the setting of metrics. Practically, the methodologies 
mentioned under (a) above allow determ ining for an institution for each sector to what extent its 
lending contributes to reaching the goals of the Paris agreement. Where this can be proven for a 
material proportion of business, and without cherry picking, this should be recognized from a capit al 
requirements point of view, thereby influencing asset allocation, pricing, and ultimately the behaviour 
of the market. A bank using this kind of forward looking methodologies for one specific sector will have 
a lending portfolio in this sector aligned t o the Paris Agreement, and in consequence, will have lower 
transition risks. As a possible example, two elements could be measured: the proportion of sustainable 
business within a financial institution and secondly, the trend of this proportion since the last reporting 
date. The exact level of the resulting incentive on capital levels, should be topic of discussion and 
investigation, but we can imagine that a correction in either total capital level or the respective capital 
levels of asset classes would result from these new longer term risk management requirements. 
 
 
c) Steering banks away from fossil based exposures if they don’t manage their footprint 
The ultimate stick to force banks that have not integrated carbon accounting of climate considerations 
in their risk management yet, is an add -on on their credit risk exposures. This could look like the 
following suggestion for regulation:   
 
Article 110a  Adjustment for climate impact 
 
1. Institutions shall apply the provisions in this Title to exposures wi thout a negative greenhouse -gas 
impact or with a significantly decreasing greenhouse -gas impact on a credible path to zero by 2035. 
Where an exposure has a negative greenhouse-gas impact, or where the greenhouse-gas impact is not 
known to the institution, institutions shall multiply the applicable risk weight with a factor [1,25 in 
2025]. 
2. EBA shall develop draft regulatory technical standards to specify the calculation of the greenhouse-
gas impact for each of the exposure classes and types of exposures identified in this Title. EBA shall 
submit those draft regulatory standards to the Commission by [two years after entry into force]."

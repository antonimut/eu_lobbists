Waste Shipment – Revision of EU rules 
Tesla input to the European Commission’s inception impact 
assessment 
 
 
General remarks: 
Tesla welcomes the European Commission’s intention to review the EU’s Waste Shipment Regulation 
(WSR) in light of the new Circular Economy Action Plan and the European Green Deal. Moreover, we would 
support making sure that the outcome of the WSR revision process is fully aligned with: 
- The ongoing revision of the End of Life Vehicles Directive; 
- The ongoin g revision of the Batteries Directive and the new upcoming regulatory framework for 
batteries (the provisions regarding end-of-life procedures in particular).  
 
Overall, we support efforts to lay down a transparent, coherent legal framework that operators can follow 
for a safe and efficient transboundary shipment of waste across the EU. Securing a harmonised framework 
at EU level, with a consistent implementation across Member states, is also crucial for the EU’s consistency 
and credibility on waste import and export management vis-à-vis its international partners. A clear and 
well-enforced framework should  contribute to the well-functioning and integrity of the EU ’s single 
market.    
 
Below, w e highlight t wo current challenges pertaining to lithium-ion battery waste  specifically, with 
examples. We supplement this with recommendations for possible improvements.  
 
We remain available to answer questions or address comments on the below in the next steps of the 
revision. 
 
1. Uniform and unambiguous classification of undamaged Li-Ion batteries as non-hazardous 
 
The WSR implicitly classifies lithium-ion batteries under the European Waste Code “16 06 05”. This code 
implies that batteries are considered as ‘ non-hazardous’ – rather than as ‘hazardous’ – waste and can 
therefore be transported under Annex VII shipping documentation.  
 
In spite of this, it is our experience that some Member States are classifying Li-Ion batteries as hazardous 
waste; or are not able to provide unambiguous answers.  
Since most European countries do not have domestic recycling capability, t his means import and export 
notifications are required that typically only cover a limited time period, a limited amount of shipments, 
and have to be performed by a specific supplier. If any of these change, a new notification is needed which 
can take months.  
The tight supplier requirements mean that there is very little if any competition, leading to sometimes 
excessive prices for shipment.

All this increases the cost of battery recycling dramatically, which ultimately hampers the development of 
a viable European recycling industry and increases the cost of emobility. 
 
Recommendation 
It is urgently necessary to enable safe and seamless cross-border transport of undamaged Li-Ion batteries 
for recycling. To achieve this, s uch batteries should be explicitly and uni formly declared non-hazardous, 
with no room for national interpretations or different classifications, nor need for notification.  
 
2. Streamline notification for shipment of hazardous batteries  
 
For those batteries that can pose shipment dangers, e.g. dama ged batteries, WSR notification 
requirements should be streamlined.  
The process to request a notification under the WSR is currently different across Member states , with 
each using their own system, some more advanced than others. The lead time from the moment of filing 
for a notification to the outcome is unpredictable and can be very long, often months.  
 
Recommendation 
A clear and harmonised notification process for shipment of damaged batteries would create a safer 
environment for the management of battery waste . A central European system would be best, with 
uniform administrative requirements and reasonable lead times.

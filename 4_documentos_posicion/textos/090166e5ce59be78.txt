EEB input to Inception Impact Assessment for the revision of the Industrial Emissions Directive (IED)  
Summary: The EEB welcomes the findings of the European Commission that a review of the IED is necessary to 
improve delivery and effectiveness with respect to its goals. The current goal is to achieve a high general level of 
environmental protection from industrial activities, with priority to pollution prevention over control. Pursuant to 
the EU’s Green Deal, our economy will have to undergo a major transformation to become a circular, 
decarbonised (by 2040) and zero pollution economy  (by 2050) whilst al so aiming to achieve  a toxic free 
environment (by 2030).  
The current framework should be transformed to lead the transition to a truly sustainable  industrial production 
framework at EU level, via a reformed IED. The IED is unique in its ambition of an ‘integrated approach’ meaning 
to address all environmental media impacts, and to set the best possible technical regulatory standards to prevent 
negative impacts from a given industrial activity. It should be transformed to become the new  zero-pollution 
industrial production regulation.  The EEB fully supports the e mphasis made on preventing pollution , in full 
integration with circular economy and decarbonization policies.  
The EEB has already identified a number of bottlenecks that require a review of the IED, as indicated in 
the Roadmap submission 1, the targeted questionnaire submission and interventions made at the focus 
group stakeholder meetings 2 and in its industrial point source specific submissio n to the Industrial 
Strategy discussion3. 
Therefore, we welcome the conclusion of the European Commission that a review is necessary, the preliminary 
list of problems identified and -to a large exten t- the objectives and policy options  identified. The EEB would 
like to hereby confirm its positions made in earlier submissions and address specific items under section 
B “Objectives and policy options ”. The EEB further supports any more specific comments made by our 
members in this consultation. 
“Baseline Scenario” The EEB objects to maintain the status quo, based on the previous findings made.   
Some improvements could be achieved through review of secondary legislation or change of practice and not 
the IED provisions. Some examples of Commission Implementing Decisions needing improvements could be as 
following: BREF review rules (2012/119/EU OJEU L63/1), the reporting obligations under the IED (2018/1135/EU 
OJEU L205/40) and the E-PRTR (2019/1741 OJEU L267/3). Specific issues linked to enforcement deficits, such as 
how to address measurement uncertainty and to improve standardised, accurate and reliable measurement 
methods and mitigation techniques are needed for each pollutant through relevant standardisation work so to 
consistently uphold standards set by BAT Conclusions.

However, we consider that the most effective approach is achieved through a review of the parental framework. 
The IPPC-Directive was reviewed because too much flexibility was offered for its implementation, thereby 
undermining its effectiveness to deliver impact in terms of improved protection levels and stimulating clean 
production methods.   
The main weaknesses that remain relate to derogation from BAT possibilities, vague legal obligations with large 
degree of discretion to Member States in permitting and enforcement, meaning an un even playing field for 
industry and undermining progress to wards its full potential. Even if the  IED restricted this flexibility, we see 
similar issues occurring, in particular in relation to BAT -C implementation , access to information and public 
participation and enforcement of more ambitious environmental performance. Further, the EEB maintains its view 
that the Seville Process needs to improve, IED provisions need to be adapted so it becomes fit for purpose of 
delivering the circular, decarbonised and zero pollution economy, within the pre 2040 timescale set. This requires 
legislative interventions and ambitious standards.   
We believe there is a strong case for the need to change the regulatory instrument to a “ Regulation”, thereby 
restricting the undue flexibility (ab)used by stakeholders in defining BAT and for Member States in  the 
implementation stage.     
“Inclusion of additional sectors ” The EEB disagrees with this too restrictive approach on scope definition , as 
presented in the inception IA.  
The EEB takes the view that a review of the approach on how to define BAT is necessary, not to just add further 
additional sectors or lower thresholds which would at best deliver incremental improvements, rather than the 
required transition of industrial activities to  become a circular, decarbonised and zero pollution economy  in the 
required timescale of action. The following points are highlighted as to “scope” definition / approach: 
1) need for a change in the approach of how industrial activities are regulated, by setting BAT for l owest 
ratio ‘environmental impact of industrial activity’ versus ‘public good/service provided’ , in order to 
promote the industrial activity with the least  negative environmental impact for the provision of a given 
product/service. The Commission should pr ioritise the following items: energy production, water quality and 
supply, protein production, resource management, substitution of chemicals of concern. 
The current scope could be explained on the basis that, historically, the approach of the IPPC/IED is aimed to 
prevent and control negative environmental impacts from the “most damaging” industrial activities or large 
volume manufacturing at the given point in time of legal drafting.  A more forward -looking approach would 
instead define Best Available Tech niques (BAT) for providing the best ratio of health/environmental impact of a 
given product or service from a given industrial activity. To give a few examples: currently for energy industries, 
the scope is to prevent / control pollution from highly polluting (fossil based) energy industries, including thermal 
combustion plants above a certain thermal capacity threshold, instead of defining BAT on how to produce in the 
best way energy (electricity, heat or mechanical energy). Asbestos production is still listed, despite not having any 
production in EU due to its hazards, it does not make sense to define BAT on how to produce asbestos with less 
harmful impacts. On the other hand, the current IED scope also covers textiles production or food and drink 
production (although only for large scale), which enables a broader approach as to the wider life cycle impacts 
or options to consider on how to best deliver that product/service. The current scope formulation (Annex I of the 
IED) is one of the major shortcomings in terms of approach taken to define BAT for the industrial activities 
addressed and therefore the effectiveness of the IED to set the standard for “clean and sustainable production” 
within the new zero pollution ambition. Those limitations are however due  to political reasons (certain Member 
States and industry do not like to be restricted in how they may conduct an industrial activity) . Due to the scale 
of urgency of actions required and long investment cycles, it is no longer acceptable to promote increm ental 
improvements only when a faster and deeper transition is required. 
2) Other environmentally impacting activities should be covered as well (e.g. aquaculture, data centres, other 
forms of animal rearing e.g. cattle and other impacts from mining industries) without losing focus on the desired 
public good / service to be provided.  See more information on this aspect in the EEB targeted questionnaire 
submission (precited).  
Stationary asphalting plants generate air (including odour and noise) emissions and generate other negative 
environmental impacts also largely due to the fossil input fuels used , but are not included . Crushing plants for 
concrete and other mineral (constru ction) waste release a considerable amount of dust. Neither the processing 
of mineral waste classified as hazardous nor the processing of non -hazardous mineral waste is listed in Annex I 
of the IED. However, some of the crushed mineral waste has to be classified as hazardous waste due to its content 
of heavy metals and/or organic compounds  but would probably not reach the high thresholds set . Accordingly, 
toxic pollutants and/or CMR-pollutants are released with the dust especially during the processing of such waste. 
The shredding of metal waste and the preparation of waste for thermal recovery is included under the IED, but 
not the treatment of mineral waste, even if the environmental relevance of these sectors should be of the same 
order of magnitude. More information will be sent to the Commission services on this item. 
We fully support the Commission findings on the general need to extend the scope and revise thresholds set.  
3) The ‘prevention first ’principle should be rigorously implemented and prioritised over end -of pipe 
pollution reduction.  
4) The notion of BAT should be clarified to mean technical achievable performance levels that provide the 
best overall environmental protection outcomes.  
5) The EU ‘safety net’ requirements –i. e. the binding minimum requirements-are extended and revisited 
for preventing impacts from the most polluting industrial sectors (e.g. energy intensive industries and in 
particular coal/lignite combustion, correction factor of 0.5 is for any application of the refinery bubble) 
6) The EU should become the frontrunner in environmental performance standards that are fully coherent 
with the Sustainable Development Goals and a zero-pollution ambition. The current situation requires action 
that is both bold and swift. Considering the lengthy  investment cycles for industry regulated by the IED (10 -20 
years), the strengthened policy framework needs to be in place before first revision cycles of relevant industrial 
production standards (e.g. EU BREFs). The EU must promote frontrunner industry practices and to give a direction 
for the transition path to take.  This also implies that the ‘technology neutrality’  dogma is to be abandoned:  if 
breakthrough techniques are identified in being most effective to deliver on the above objectives, they  should 
be clearly favoured, and outdated techniques should be subject to ‘negative BAT’. 
Improvements should be achieved through the revision of the legal provisions and the BREF review rules. 
“Implementing issues”: The EEB supports this finding.  In this respect we would like to highlight the following 
aspects: 
- Un-even implementation of the BAT-C standards, often leading to a default 1:1 transposition of higher BAT -
AEL levels set; 
- Ignoring the interplay of relevant Environmental Quality Standards (Art 18) both at the level of defining the 
BREF benchmarks and in the implementation phase; 
- Various methods and cost benefit assessments used to provide for A rt 15.4 derogations, uneven way of 
ensuring early and effective public participation in the procedures; 
- Undermining of the energy efficiency BREF requirements, mainly due to EU-ETS (Article 26/ Art 9 of the IED); 
- Significant shortcomings in enforcement (sanctions and penalties) 
- Shortcomings in compliance promotion and benchmarking of industries (see point on Access to information) 
as well as findings by our member FrankBold on measurement uncertainties applied to air monitoring. 
See previous inputs made on those aspects in the targeted questionnaire submission.

“Coherence with other EU le gislation” The EEB supports this finding  but would caution against limiting the 
assessment to the E-PRTR only.  See previous inputs made on those aspects, e.g. Roadmap submission and targeted 
questionnaire submission and EEB submission to EU Green Deal/Industrial Strategy.   
The EEB welcomes the intention to improve external and internal policy coherence and provide added value and 
effectiveness in their implementation. So far, shortcomings or incoherence have either been driven due to attitude 
issues rather than legal provisions e.g. not to enable BAT -C to go beyond REACH substitution requirements, 
incoherence to achieve phase out of PHS from industrial activities despite Water Framework Directive / Minamata 
Convention obligations.  Coherence should be improved so that the revised IED framework provides added value 
on the following aspects to be addressed unde r the integrated approach: Air quality, Water quality, Circular 
economy, Waste management, Sustainable use of resources, Chemical risks and hazards, Energy and 
Climate change (decarbonisation).  Any legislation referred to under Article 18 (EQS) should be c arefully 
assessed and the revised IED framework adapted/improved so to deliver better on those objectives e.g. Water 
Quality Directive and daughter directives, Marpol Convention, Waste Framework Directive and Circular Economy 
Action Plans, Chemicals legisl ation (e.g. REACH, Biocides, Plant Protection Regulation Seveso III Directive, 
Stockholm Convention etc), Ambient Air Quality Directive and NEC -Directive, Habitats Directive, Minamata 
Convention and secondary legislation.. 
Inconsistencies / incoherence with EU legislation have been identified on the following:  E-PRTR, Aarhus 
Convention and EU ETS / Climate change agenda.  
The new IED should provide a mechanism to ensure its provisions are fully consistent so to ensure that industrial 
activities will be circular, decarbonised (by 2040) and aligned to the zero pollution economy (by 2050) with a sub-
goal of toxic free environment (by 2030).  
Improvements should be achieved through the revision of the legal provisions (change to “Regulation”) 
and possible review of listed EU legislation e.g. EU-ETS (deletion of Article 26) and revised E-PRTR.

  
 
 
Answer to the inception impact assessment (roadmap) 
on the Industrial Emissions Directive. 
 
 
The Industrial Minerals Association Europe  (IMA-Europe) welcomes the opportunity to provide its feedback 
to the European Commission on the inception impact assessment, Directive 2010/75/EU. It is only through 
these assessments that directives such as the Industrial Emissions Directive (IED) can be designed fit -for-
purpose. Therefore, it is a priority for the minerals sector to engage in a constructive dialogue with  
policymakers to ensure that the EU environmental objectives will be achieved together in line with the socio-
economic developments and sector competitiveness. 
 
A sustainable future starts by keeping our sector competitive and by addressing potential regulatory obstacles 
in EU legislation related to long permitting times . It is our firm conviction that when revising the Industrial 
Emissions Directive, a proportionate and forward-looking value chain approach should be considered. 
 
OUR CALLS 
 
Industrial minerals are an enabler of multiple values chains and as a sector does not "cause high 
pollution for which IED could be an appropriate policy instrument". Mineral extraction inevitably gives 
rise to a range of temporary environmental impacts. Still, during mineral exploration and the development of a 
deposit, the environmental impacts tend to be local, and the operators aim to prevent or mitigate them. The 
sector places a strong emphasis on monitoring all risks associated with extractive industries. In Europe, the 
industrial mineral industry has to comply with a set of complex and stringent regulatory instruments under EU 
law to ensure safe extractive operations, which comes in addition to numerous requirements enacted by the 
Member States, at both national and local levels. Compliance with the Mining Waste Directive (Directive 
2006/21/EC) and the BAT Reference Document for the Management of Waste from Extractive Industries 
(2018) under Directive 2006/21/EC requires operators of mine sites to develop detailed proposals for site 
closure which maintain a sustainable industry. At all sites, biodiversity, resource use or waste management, 
underpin the decision-making process 
 
The ex-ante robust impact assessment is necessary to evaluate the impact of inclusion under the 
IED. Since the industrial mineral sector is mainly composed of small and medium-sized enterprises, enlarging 
the scope of the IED to our sector would have a significant administrative and financial burden for our industry 
but also for the downstream depending on minerals . Any listing of potential BATs for planning, construction, 
operation and land-use settings is likely to be impracticably long. Mineral extraction is purely mechanical and 
not energy-intensive; for most type of products , no chemical substances are applied, which results in no 
significant impact on the levels of pollution for air, soil or water.  Any actions taken should avoid creating 
disproportionate obstacles for operators and regulators alike.

Minerals are traditio nally considered as national natural assets for which access is governed by 
subsidiarity principle and therefore ha ve been governed by national or regional, sometimes local 
provisions. The legal provisions concerning the management of raw materials in a broad sense are 
enshrined in distinct national and occasionally regional sectorial legislation of Member States.  Inclusion of 
industrial minerals  in the IED is unlikely to have any effect on the techniques and technolo gies used for 
extraction in the EU , triggering changes to national permitting regimes that would hinder development and 
implementation of new technologies in mines . Existing regulation , BAT Reference Document for the 
Management of Waste from Extractive Industries (2018) in accordance with Directive 2006/21/EC  already 
require that all operations use practices and techniques  that are BAT as agreed in guidelines of deriving 
BAT’s derived fol lowing the 2012/119/EU Co mmission decision and achieve a generally high  level of 
protection of the environment for the extractive industries.  
 
The IED is not the right policy instrument to address 'Circular economy' framework objectives . 
Industrial Minerals are used in a wide range of applications and products. Recovering these minerals from 
their manufacturing products would be technically challenging, energy-consuming and, ultimately 
environmentally unsound. However, although the mineral s may not be recyclable per se, many of them live 
second, third, fourth or even an infinite number of uses thanks to the recycling of the applications they are 
used in1, thus contributing to the circular economy 2. On February 2019, the consultant Ricardo published a 
study3 assessing the contribution of the IED to meeting circular economy objectives. It gathered and analysed 
information on five circular economy topic areas of ene rgy use, materials use, waste generation, use of 
hazardous chemicals and industrial symbiosis.  The contribution was concluded  to have, in general, a low 
contribution to achieving a circular economy. The study also recognised  the different aims of both types of 
legislation, since the IED permitting is based on the installation performance, and Circular economy is usually 
presented as a value -chain approach. The IED might not be the main tool to deliver on circular economy 
priorities. 
 
The Seville process must be limited to the control of the relevant industrial emissions.  Environmental 
authorisations through the IED cannot become the tool for controlling all types of legal requirements in the 
industry, nor should it become the appropriate tool for every upcoming environmental challenge by default.  
The mineral sector is especially complex due to geological, geochemical, climatological, and social conditions 
of the location of each deposit . As shown in the BAT Reference Document for the Management of Waste 
from Extractive Industries (2018) , it is difficult if not impossible to compare the different situation in different 
Member States as every deposit is geologically quite unique and BAT conclusions are guiding the authorities 
in the permitting process. However, a common denominator was a challenge identified during the preparation 
of these BAT’s.   
Directive 2006/21/EC already requires application of site-specific BAT to the management of waste resulting 
from the prospecting, extraction, treatment and storage of  mineral resources, and under that legislation , the 
resulting BREF (2nd edition, 2018) is risk-based and neither prescriptive nor exhaustive.

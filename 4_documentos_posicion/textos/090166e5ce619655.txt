Cefic views with regard to the Inception Impact Assessment (IIA) of the IED 
Brussels, 20 March 2020 
 
 
 
Introduction  
This paper intends to provide Cefic views on certain issues regarding the topics and issues raised in 
the Inception Impact Assessment (IIA), launched by DG ENV on March 24, 2020.  
 
The drafting of BAT reference documents describing Best Available Techniques (so -called Sevilla 
process, BREF process or BAT process) as well as their implementation into national law are of great 
importance for the chemical industry and its ongoing development in Europe. After all, more than 20 
BREFs more or less directly relate to or impact the chemical sector. 
 
The chemical industry as one of the major stakeholders in the ‘BREF process’ is therefore committed 
to support any further review of the IED.  
 
Cefic already supported the evaluation of the IED in 2018/19 and endorsed its members to provide 
broad response to the public consultation (PC) and the Targeted Stakeholder Survey (TS).  
 
Cefic Position 
Cefic believes that the IED is compatible with the ob jectives of the European Green Deal and could 
therefore be used to some extend as a n instrument in the context of the Circular Economy Action 
Plan. For this to happen, we would call on the Sevilla process to be used to its full potential:  
For instance, the integrated approach – cross-media effects on emissions and use of resources 
(potentially leading to reduced emissions of greenhouse gases) – could be used more systematically 
to address resources efficiency issues when selecting Best Available Techniques (BATs).  
However, the IED should be considered in perspective of the other legislative frameworks like and 
not limited to the Water Framework Directive or the Wastes Framework Directive and their 
associated sisters Directives and Regulatio ns1. Legal certainty should be ensured throughout the 
revision process. To this end, no new BREF review cycle should be initiated until a revised IED enters 
into force. 
Cefic is concerned with the notion in the IIA documents referring to the costs and benefits. The paper 
states: “Assessments carried out so far show that costs for compliance with the requirements of the 
IED and BAT conclusions are relatively small.” → This topic was also bro ught up by Cefic and other 
members of the industry during the IED evaluation workshop on Dec 17th, 2019. Further in its 
submission to the IED evaluation Cefic stressed that a report (Cumulative Cost Assessment for the EU 
Chemical Industry” (Ref. Ares (2016 )3304226 – 11/07/2016)) issued by COM clearly shows that the 
costs for compliance with the IED and comparable legislation are considerable, as it states on page 7:

Detailed considerations 
Coming back to the scope of the IIA and going more into detail, we would like to provide the 
following suggestions, following the topics as listed in the IIA: 
 
1. Baseline Scenario  
 
As outlined during the IED Evaluation, Cefic generally views the IED as functioning well with a limited 
call for an in-depth refit process.  
 
2. Inclusion of additional sectors  
 
Cefic supports the inclusion of additional sectors in order to achieve an even b roader reduction of 
emissions across Europe. Having a broader view will also help to put the contribution of e.g the 
chemical sector to overall emissions in a better and more just perspective. Here we could envision a 
better selection of the ‘right’ KEIs, and the relevance of processes, sectors and their actual emissions 
should be given greater consideration (focused approach).  
 
3. Implementation issues  
 
Options to enhance consistency of Member State implementation of IED requirements  should be 
explored. As Cefic has outlined in its response to the IED evaluation we believe that implementation 
across the member states is not always fully consistent.  
 
4. BREF process  
 
Concerning the BREF process Cefic would like to provide the following statements: 
 
A sound collection of data forms the basic structure of any B REF process and is central to the 
subsequent derivation of BAT conclusions and emission ranges. The data must be representative and 
correctly reflect the relevant issues in a meaningful way. System/battery limits for installations and 
permits as well as the d emarcation between different (vertical / horizontal) BREFs must be clearly 
defined so that comparability of data is a given. This, we believe is currently not the case as there is 
no clear definition of an ‘installation’ nor a ‘site’. Data collected withou t this distinction might 
therefore not be comparable.  
 
The right approach for system limits will also help to support new processes in light of circular 
economy. Today, a large integrated chemical site might be ‘divided’ as the units in this site might fall 
under different BREFs. Each BREF however requires adherence to specific BATs and AELs. A truly 
integrated approach (e.g. the use/re-use of ‘internal’ wastes and side-streams/by-products) could be 
easier if these virtual boundaries created by the BREFs a re overcome. For example: two units at the 
same industrial complex/site may fall under two different BREFs. Using of a side stream of Unit A in 
Unit B may be environmentally beneficial but prohibited by the limitations imposed on Unit B. A way 
out should be to foresee such uses by adding respective footnotes to the BAT conclusions. 
 
In recent years there has further been an increasing tendency to collect data on more and more 
substances (KEIs, key environmental issues). The aim should however be to identify only the 
environmentally relevant pollutants, considering also the impact on circular economy. Use and re -
use of e.g. secondary raw materials may be hampered if traces of e.g. substances of concern prohibit 
such use when they lead to higher emissions of a given unit, though the overall benefit for an entire 
integrated site might very well outweigh the impact of thes e emissions. Another example would be 
limitations on the use of wastewater: if water consumption is pushed down because of water saving 
efforts, the ‘bleed’ discharges may be too concentrated to comply with legal (AEL) limits.  
 
.  
5. Access to information, participation in decision making and access to justice  
 
The BREF process is a data driven process, and sound data are the essence of the BREF process. With 
increasing focus on economic factors (e.g. specific emissions of CO2, specific consumption of energy) 
we foresee an increasing need for the BREF process to collect sensitive business information. Cefic is 
clearly committed to support the process but rules of confidentiality and full compliance with the 
laws on competition are an essential prerequisite. Without a proper protection of sensitive data the 
future collection of such data might not be possible anymore.  
 
 
6. Contribution to the circular economy  
 
As written in the introduction section of the paper, Cefic believes that the IED could be used to some 
extend as an instrument to support a sound implementation of the Circular Economy Action Plan 
(CEAP) with regards to the efficient use of resources, energy efficiency.  
 
Cefic agrees with the notion expressed by OECD  that the IED may provide a suitable fr amework for 
the inclusion of e.g. aspects of Circular Economy, but other policy instruments (e.g. waste framework 
directive) might be a better fit for this purpose (see also topic 4 above).  
 
 
Regarding IED and the CEAP, we would like to quote from the current DRAFT of activity 5 of the OECD 
BAT project, which also addresses these topics and comes to the following conclusions: 
 
“Circular Economy: 
 
It should be kept in mind that adding new criteria to the assessment of candidate BAT might 
additionally extend the duration of this process, which already spans across several years. 
 
The IED may not be the best instrument to achieve circular economy objectives. Whilst the EU policies 
on circular economy aim to produce environmental benefits by avoiding mining and production 
activities, the IED (which was established prior to the adoption of the EU’s major policies on circular 
economy) sets out to reduce harmful industrial emissions across the EU, in particular through better 
application of BAT. That is, their objectives are divergent. Some of the key circular economy measures 
– such as the use of reused, repaired or upgraded products or recycled materials – only indirectly affect 
industrial emissions, and perhaps not even in a positive way, consi dering such products or materials 
can be less reliable or pure than new products and primary materials. 
 
 
 
7. Use of IED in the context of Carbon neutrality of the EU economy  
 
As a member of the Alliance of Energy Intensive Industries, Cefic rejects the use of IED art 9 to tackle 
the GHG emissions on top of the existing EU ETS. This is viewed as counterproductive and would 
simply duplicate legislative frameworks. 
 
We would like to quote from the current DRAFT of activity 5 of the OECD BAT project: 
“BAT is usually not used to directly address greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. This is partly due to the 
existence of other policy instruments addressing such emissions in many countries and regions, such 
as emission trading systems in the European Union.”  
 
 It is therefore crucial to balance both sides: energy (=GHG) efficiency and pollution reduction. Further 
lower emissions could need more abatement techniques which could  lead to increased consumption 
of water and energy and/or increase d production of wastes.  As such a life-cycle approach (which 
boundaries need still to be defined) to using abatement technologies in the context of IED would be 
advisable.   
 
Cefic supports the use of IED and BAT Conclusions in the field of energy efficiency.  
 
8. Coherence with other EU legislation  
 
As the Public Consultation showed, most of the stakeholders share Cefic’s view that the IED is 
coherent with the respective EU policies. As such we do not believe the (scope of the) IED should be 
extended further in order to cover these topics as well. They are well taken care of in other pieces of 
legislation. Technically the IED would provide a good basis for additional topics, e.g. like 
decarbonization or greenhouse gases. Notwithstanding the above however, our main concern is that 
the already time-consuming process deriving BREFs and BATs/AELs would be further extended and, 
potentially, overloaded.  If the IED would cover additional legislations this would also mean that 
additional experts (REACH, Tox, LCM, Waste, …)  would be required in the TWG. This would enlarge 
the TWG even further and could make the process unmanageable.

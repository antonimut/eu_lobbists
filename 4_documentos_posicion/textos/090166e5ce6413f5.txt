Feedback from FuelsEurope to the 
Revision of the Industrial Emissions Directive - Inception Impact Assessment 
FuelsEurope welcomes the possibility to provide its feedback on the Inception Impact Assessment (IIA) 
on the revision of the Industrial Emissions Directive. FuelsEurope considers the IED an essential 
regulatory policy instrument since it ensures a very high level o f protection of the environment as a 
whole through the setting of permit conditions based on the Best Available Technologies (BAT) and its 
Associated Environmental Performance Levels (AEPLs) established through a structured exchange of 
technical information with all stakeholders involved. 
A. Context, problem definition and subsidiarity check 
The Inception Impact Assessment refers explicitly to the evaluation of the IED and to its findings , 
although implementation of IED is still in progress (e.g. some BAT Conclusions have not been published 
yet and the 4 year compliance period have not expired for some others) and its preliminary evaluation 
has not been completed yet FuelsEurope note with surprise that at the time of the publication of this 
IIA, the report of the IED evaluation has not been made publicly available yet. We provided feedback 
on the preliminary findings during the 17 December 2019 workshop and through written comments 
on 17 January 2020. We would have been delighted to see how our feedback had been taken into 
account in the final report. 
Since this IIA is reinstating some of those preliminary findings that we have already commented, we 
take this opportunity to consistently provide feedback on them. 
 
B. Problem the initiative aims to tackle/objectives and policy options 
The recent evaluation of the IED has identified several broad areas  where the operation of the 
legislative framework might be improved to better contribute to those goals. As we commented during 
the evaluation, we do not contest the finding according which  the legislative framework might be 
improved to better contribute to the achievement of goals in some areas. However , it was unclear 
whether the IED required any changes or if, on the contrary, other regulatory measures s hould be 
reconsidered instead. We invite the European Commission to assess which policy measures should be 
amended where a better alignment is searched. 
Implementation issues 
In exploring options to enhance consistency of Member State implementation of IED requirements 
we suggest that industry experts are consulted. The Industrial Emissions Expert Group set up by the 
European Commission could benefit from the expertise gained b y industry through the 
implementation of the IED and the IPPCD, its predecessor. We suggest that the IEEG could organise 
working sessions in presence of the IED Article 13 Forum members with a view to discuss the issues at 
stake. 
Contribution to reducing industry emissions to water 
The link between the IED and the Water Framework Directive (WFD) is dealt with at local level) via the 
IED article 18 that addresses the compliance with Environmental Quality Standards. Local conditions 
define local effect and cannot be generalized.

At the kick-off of any BREF revision process, the pre-identification of Key Environmental Issues (KEIs) 
step may help select WFD Priority Substances that meet the relevant criteria and for which a robust 
data collection may be undertaken. 
 
Elaboration of BAT conclusions (options to improve the BREF elaboration process will be explored) 
Our sector is willing to work closely within the IED Article 13 Forum on how to develop a systematic 
methodology to derive BAT AELs.  We deem that such a methodology will facilitate the work of the 
TWGs in identifying BATs and help deriving BAT conclusions in a consensual way. 
In that respect, we believe that the integrated approach 1 - which sha ll remain one of the key 
underlying principles of the IED - needs to be systematically applied when identifying BATs and 
deriving BAT AELs. 
From an IED revision timing perspective, it is of utmost importance to guarantee a legal certainty  
during any BREF revision processes. To this end, no new BREF review cycle should be initiated until a 
revised directive enters into force. 
Contribution to the circular economy 
It is inadequate to attribute progress in circularity to one piece of legislation.  
The role of the IED and in particular of the REF BREF (e.g. REF BREF 2014, BAT 2&14 or REF BREF 2003, 
BAT 10) is impossible to assess with accuracy but it was relevant (developing a waste management 
plan is paving the way for reuse where possible for instance). 
Where circularity has increased or will increase (change in raw materials, feedstock), that may have 
positive impact on climate. Hence assessing the impact on the environment is key and the IED has its 
crucial role to play here (integrated approach). 
 The IED is not the main driver for Circular Economy  as, beyond  e.g. imposing a waste 
management plan, it does not establishes specific BATs/BREFs. There are other legislative acts 
that promote circularity (e.g. Renewable Energy Directive). 
Circularity strongly depends on the availability of materials, which by definition vary 
geographically, and in nature and quantity. FuelsEurope does not see how meaningful BAT -
AEPLs could be derived  nor how a “KEI” could even possibly be pre -identified such that a 
robust data collection could be organized with a view to allow such a derivation. 
 Many of the changes leading to Circular Economy are driven by climate change and energy 
efficiency considerations. In this context, the IED may play its “integrated approach” role 
preventing installations to emit more pollutants to air/water when e.g. using waste feedstock 
or reusing products (secondary raw materials). When setting best available techniques for 
these processes, cross media effects must be addressed. 
The IED scope covers the ind ustrial site. Therefore, any contribution to circular economy objectives 
should be explored in the wider context of a system dev elopment process. The IED alone  may not 
trigger/achieve the intended goal of reusing secondary raw materials, as the value chain would need 
to be created first and it would require joint efforts and cross-sectoral integration.

 We believe that the IED has no vocation a priori to promote circularity but that on the contrary 
when circularity occurs, then the IED should ensure that the  integrated approach aiming at 
regulating plants is maintained with a view to ensure a high level of protection of the 
environment as a whole.  In particular, when circularity leads to the development of new 
industrial processes  (e.g. innovative secondary r aw material production or waste recovery 
processes) those should be regulated through a BAT -based permitting process to ensure a 
high EU -wide environment protection. Only viable economic activity can start in a global 
competition (changes are mostly market  driven). Therefore, market creation mechanisms 
would be essential to trigger investment in circular economy.  
 Synergies among different industries/sectors would need to be incentivized (e.g. boosting the 
collection of waste bio-feedstock or municipal waste in a municipality close by, lightening the 
administrative burden of obtaining the end-of-waste status).  
 Mapping existing industrial clusters and the infrastructures that the elements of those clusters 
depend on would be useful. Such would serve as guida nce to industries belonging to such 
clusters to inspire themselves in case replication of existing successful symbiosis may happen.

Coherence with other EU legislation 
As stated above we support ensuring coherence across the EU regulatory requirements. It is not 
always straightforward to identify which changes should be brought to them with a view to maximise 
alignment. Regarding the example provided in the I nception Impact Assessment (“enhanced 
coherence with the E-PRTR Regulation”) we invite the European Commission to explain what it intends 
to do since the IED was adopted in 2010 and the December 2017 E-PRTR fitness check concluded it is 
fit for purpose. We therefore do consider that those two texts are coherent and do not need to be 
changed a priori. 
 
C. Preliminary assessment of expected impacts 
“Assessments carried out so far show that costs for compliance with the re quirements of the IED and 
BAT conclusions are relatively small.” 
FuelsEurope and others contested this backward looking “finding” during the evaluation since it is not 
substantiated with facts and figures. Moreover as we commented during the feedback period to the 
IED evaluation roadmap not even 10% of the total IED installations (more than 55.000) were covered 
by BAT conclusions older than 4 years. Hence the difficulty to already assess the costs impact of 
compliance. 
“Where it has been possible to assess, the societal benefits from the IED have substantially outweighed 
its economic costs” 
There was a very limited number of studies (one to our knowledge), the results of which had been 
challenged by the sector at stake. 
“By ensuring that the legal framework delivers cost-effective environmental improvements, any impact 
on the competitiveness of EU industry and consequently on its employees will be minimised.”

During the evaluation and again during the 17 December 2019workshop FuelsEurope commented that 
it is not correct to compare cost and benefits to conclude whether the measures applied are cost -
effective (see the March 2012 Guidance2, section 2.3.7.2.7). 
Moreover, deriving that such measures have no impact on the global competitiveness of the EU 
industry is premature and not documented. Concawe performed a forward-looking study in 2014 3, 
assessing the impacts (Capex/Opex) of the REF BREF BAT conclusions and drew conclusions that clearly 
demonstrate that they are unlikely to be insignificant. 
 
D. Evidence base, data collection and better regulation instruments 
FuelsEurope is looking forward to the upcoming consultation and stakeholder meetings to give its 
views on the revision of the IED  and is suggesting that the consultation will address the issues 
mentioned above . Despite the current unprecedented circumstances that we and our member 
companies are facing due to the COVID-19 outbreak, we will endeavour to provide any useful inputs 
and r obust data that are needed to carry out the upcoming impact assessment and inform the 
European Commission’s work.

Energy Intensive Industries’ statement on GHG abatement measures 
Interaction with the Industrial Emissions Directive 
 
The energy intensive industries remain committed to develop and implement GHG abatement 
measures that contribute to the achievement of a climate -neutral EU economy. In doing so, they 
want to ensure full coherency with any other policy measures that address other environmental 
issues, in particular the Industrial Emissions Directive 4 that is the backbone o f the environmental 
legislation applicable to large industrial installations (i.e. the integrated approach based on the BAT 
concept). 
In the spirit of better regulation principles and considering that ETS regulates the GHG emissions, 
the energy intensive industries reject the option to regulate GHG emissions under an IED permitting 
regime (without prejudice to the article 9.15). 
The purpose of this note is to restate the meaning of the Master Plan6 recommendation as well as to 
confirm our position regarding the way both directives should cohabit in the future, further to some 
misinterpretations of the Industry position. 
Background

includes the following recommendation (see page 34):

The Industrial Emissions Directive permitting process should be adapted to support GHG abatement 
measures in energy -intensive installations throughout the transition . The low carbon emission  
technologies under development should be assessed as potential emerging techniques during the BREF 
drawing and reviewing process.  
Studies have identified several technological solutions to reduce GHG emissions of energy -intensive 
industries and their products and allowing them to contribute to t he transition to climate neutrality. 
The main low-carbon pathways, applicable to most of our industries, are referred to on pages 25 -26 
of the master plan.  
GHG abatement measures may entail environmental impacts, for most activities under the scope of 
the IED, in particular for the energy intensive industries. 
In that respect, the IED permitting process may be adapted to support the deployment of those 
breakthrough technologies.  One option could be to adapt the article 15(5) 7 with a view to allow 
testing those technologies (a priori not referred to in the more recent BAT conclusions applicable to 
the sectors at stake) and assess more broadly their possible wider impacts on the environment and 
their compliance with the existing BAT conclusions where relevant. 
The concept of performance benchmark is already covered by the EU ETS Directive and the EIIs do not 
see the need for double regulation & overlapping policies (ETS as market -based instrument to tackle 
GHG emissions and IED as control and command/BAT driven to tackle other emissions). 
 
In conclusion, the energy intensive industries reaffirm that they: 
- remain committed to develop and implement GHG abatement measures that contribute to 
the achievement of a climate-neutral EU economy; 
- support a full coherency with the Industrial Emissions Directive and its integrated approach 
based on the BAT concept; 
- reject the option to regulate GHG emissions under an IED permitting regime (without 
prejudice to the article 9.1).

ReFuelEU Aviation - Sustainable Aviation Fuels (SAF)
 
Feedback of Neste Corporation
 
 
Neste Corporation (see 
​
www.neste.com
​
) supports the Green Deal’s ambition for climate neutrality in
 
2050 and welcomes the opportunity given by the Commission to provide input to the publication of the
 
Inception Impact Assessment (the Document) on the ReFuel EU aviation sustainable aviation fuels.
 
 
General Remarks
 
 
As mentioned in the Document, the aim has to be a stable policy framework over a sufficient time
 
horizon to provide investors with the necessary confidence to invest in the production of sustainable
 
aviation fuels and for airlines to pursue an efficient fuels policy.
 
 
The price gap between SAF and fossil jet fuel is the main challenge.  The aim of the selected
 
(combination of) policy options needs to be to tackle this challenge. Experience from on-road is
 
showing that a mandate is most effectively ensuring the desired uptake, while being market-based
 
and thus cost-efficient.
 
 
Feedstocks and Sustainability - regulation to be in place in all the options
 
●
A wide feedstock base has to be ensured for SAF.  The document is making reference -
 
maybe non-intentionally - only to Annex IX Part A and electro-fuels (Power-to-Liquids PtL).
 
However, all sustainable feedstocks under RED II need to be accepted; there are e.g. plenty
 
of waste and residue feedstocks which are not explicitly listed in Annex IX Parts A or B
 
●
The word Advanced biofuels should be used only in accordance with the definition created by
 
SGAB
 
 
1
●
Sustainability regulations of RED II are established in Europe and need to be the basis for
 
SAF specific regulations
 
●
It is important to decide on the upcoming Delegated Acts as soon as possible in order to bring
 
the necessary security for the developers and investors e.g. for PtL. Existing unavoidable
 
concentrated CO2 sources need to be eligible, and the rules for assessing the additionality of
 
renewable power supply need to be practicable
 
●
Later on, if and when sustainability regulations are developed, it is necessary to look into
 
further feedstocks without the risk of ILUC.  These can be e.g. grown on degraded or
 
contaminated areas, as shown in the sustainability regulations of CORSIA.

Ramp-up of global and European SAF production has already started and can continue rapidly,
 
provided that the necessary regulatory decisions are made:
 
●
International testing and standardization of SAF started already in 2006.  The first standard
 
was approved in 2009, and now, already 6 SAF pathways are approved, with more in the
 
process
 
●
Presently, there is already small scale production both in the USA and in Europe, and several
 
production facilities are under construction and many more planned
 
●
The ICAO SAF Stocktaking Seminar concluded on 1 May 2019 that altogether 26 producers
 
were planning to ramp up their production rapidly, with announced projects reaching a level of
 
about 5 million tonnes per year by 2025, and about 6.5 Mt/a by the end of this decade
 
●
Neste is one of the producers.  Our existing SAF production capacity (in Europe) is currently
 
100 kt/a.  The ongoing investment in Singapore will add 1 Mt/a to this in 2022.  Additionally,
 
the feasibility study of additional 450 kt/a SAF capacity in Rotterdam is ongoing
 
●
A rapid ramp-up of SAF production can benefit from the existing HVO capacity.  A significant
 
portion of the HVO production (presently about 6 Mt/a globally and growing) could on short
 
notice be redirected to SAF, ahead of the additional capacity build-up triggered by a secured
 
demand for SAF.  This can speed up and smoothen the transitions
 
●
It is important to ensure adequate support for new promising feedstocks that are not yet
 
commercially viable, like CO2 and green hydrogen - see also PtL.  This can be done in several
 
ways, and they are addressed in the text below.
 
 
In the comments below, we are following the potential policy measures in the order as they are listed
 
in the Document.
 
 
 
1 SAF Blending Mandate
 
 
Long term experiences from the on-road sector from the EU but also from other countries have shown
 
that a blending or GHG reduction mandate is the simplest and the most reliable policy instrument for
 
achieving reduction in traffic related emissions.  A mandate is also market-based and thus
 
cost-efficient, since it leaves all the details and the optimization to the market.
 
 
 
Lead times for new biofuel plants are long.  Thus, a mandate needs to be decided as soon as
 
possible, needs to start with low volumes with subsequent ramp-up, and to be long term in order to
 
provide the certainty needed to trigger the investments.  On the other hand, a mandate can easily
 
come into force immediately, as described in the general remarks above. A mandate needs to go
 
hand in hand with a wide feedstock base.
 
 
Ambition level needs to be high in order to achieve the path towards climate neutrality by 2050.  For
 
this, a minimum of 8% to 10% is needed by 2030; if decided rapidly, this can realistically be achieved.

Support for new feedstocks like CO2 and green hydrogen (PtL) can be realised within a mandate by
 
submandates, although optimal ramp-up - balance of technology push and supply potential etc. -  is
 
difficult to achieve.
 
 
 
2 Revision of the Multiplier defined in RED II
 
 
The opt-in mechanism for SAF for the fulfilment of the fuel supplier obligation in RED II is a quick and
 
simple regulatory means for the promotion of SAF, and it is used also in the US federal as well as the
 
Californian systems.  However, there are a few weaknesses, which mean that the opt-in can only be
 
used for the initial SAF deployment:
 
●
The opted-in SAF volumes are reducing volumes from the on-road sector, where
 
decarbonization is also urgently needed
 
●
The present multiplier of 1.2 is not high enough to adequately address the price gap between
 
SAF and fossil jet fuel.  On the other hand, the higher the multiplier, the lower the combined
 
SAF and on-road biofuel volume, and hence decarbonization effect.
 
 
 
3 A central auctioning mechanism
 
 
There are several ways to structure a central auctioning mechanism. Neste does not believe that they
 
could be financed for very large volumes of SAF, but could fit well as a support mechanism for a
 
limited period of time.
 
One possible objective could be the ramp-up of PtL production.  Central auctioning would bring to the
 
developers the needed financial certainty.  The society would ensure the ramp of the production,
 
while following the market-readiness closely.
 
 
 
4 Funding mechanism
 
 
Neste agrees with the statement in the Document.
 
 
 
5 Prioritisation
 
 
A prioritisation of feedstocks for one or the other transportation mode should not be done.  Aviation is
 
certainly the hardest sector to decarbonize without liquid fuels, but both the continuation of mandates
 
and other support for on-road as well as the rapid ramp-up of SAF uptake with SAF mandates are
 
urgently needed for achieving the climate neutrality goals.

What is needed instead is to ensure that all sustainable feedstocks defined in RED II are utilised as
 
described in the General Remarks above.
 
 
 
6 Voluntary agreements
 
 
Voluntary agreements between producers have been and continue to be an important driver on the
 
SAF market.  But they cannot bring the volumes that are needed and that can be ensured by policy
 
measures.
 
 
 
7 Technical facilitation and support initiatives
 
 
Until now, the USA has held the leadership and carried the main financial burden for the public
 
support for the development of new SAF pathways.  It is time for Europe to take actions in this
 
respect.
 
 
 
8 Monitoring
 
 
Neste agrees with the statement in the Document.

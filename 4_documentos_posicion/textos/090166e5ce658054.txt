Airlines for America
® (A4A), the principal trade and service organization of the U.S. airline 
industry,1 appreciates the opportunity to provide feedback on the above-referenced European

the global aviation sector, and as detailed below, our carriers have a strong interest in the 
initiative and SAF.  
 
By way of background, the aviation industry has a strong climate change record and a 
continuing commitment to further reduce its climate impact. Between 1978 and the end of 2018, 
the U.S. airlines improved their fuel efficiency by more than 130 percent, saving nearly 5 billion 
metric tons of carbon dioxide (CO2) – equivalent to taking more than 26 million cars off the road 
on average in each of those years. And we carried 42 percent more passengers and cargo in 
2018 than in 2000, while emitting only 3 percent more CO2.  
 
We are committed to limiting and further reducing these emissions. In 2009, A4A and our 
member airlines joined with aviation partners globally in establishing a stepwise set of emissions 
targets: a 1.5 percent annual average fuel efficiency improvement through 2020, carbon-neutral 
growth from previously-expected 2020 emission levels, and an aspirational goal of reducing net 
CO2 emissions by 50 percent in 2050 (relative to 2005 levels), subject to critical aviation 
infrastructure, technology, operations, and SAF advances achieved by governments and 
industry.2 The efforts our airlines are undertaking to address greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions 
are designed to limit their fuel consumption, GHG contribution, and potential climate change

The availability of SAF in significant quantities is a key pillar to the achievement of aviation’s 
GHG emissions reduction goals. As drop-in fuel that can reduce lifecycle GHG emissions by up 
to 80% while also helping to improve local air quality, SAF are particularly vital to aviation since, 
unlike road transport (cars, trucks, buses, etc.), the aviation sector will remain reliant on liquid 
fuels for years to come. For well over a decade, A4A and its carriers have been working 
diligently to lay the groundwork for the establishment of a commercially viable SAF industry. In 
2006, we were instrumental in creating the Commercial Aviation Alternative Fuels Initiative® 
(CAAFI), which seeks to facilitate the development and deployment of SAF.3 CAAFI has played 
an integral role in obtaining the certification of the 6 SAF “pathways” that are now recognized 
under the ASTM International specification for aviation turbine fuel from alternative, non-
petroleum sources (i.e., ASTM D7566). Various of our member carriers, moreover, have 
entered into offtake agreements over the years with SAF producers in a concerted effort to spur 
the SAF industry and utilize the fuel, and at least one A4A member has invested in a SAF-
producing company. In short, we have been and remain deeply committed to the development 
of a commercially viable SAF industry. 
 
With this backdrop in mind, we applaud the Commission for undertaking the ReFuelEU Aviation 
initiative. A4A agrees with the problem statement set forth in the Roadmap. We particularly 
agree that despite our concerted and continuing efforts: 
 
• Aircraft technologies “such as electric or hydrogen-powered aircraft are not expected to 
be mature enough to play a significant role in commercial aviation in the next decades”;  
 
• “[T]he present production and use of SAF in the EU [and, indeed, elsewhere] is still 
negligible”; 
 
• “The production cost of SAF is currently at least twice as high as that of conventional jet 
fuel and higher than that of sustainable alternative fuels used in other transport modes,” 
meaning that “SAF are, in absence of support, not an economically attractive substitute 
to conventional jet fuel”;4 
 
• “In the absence of a long-term, stable policy framework with sufficient incentives, the 
necessary confidence for major investments in SAF production is not provided,” which 
leads to the result that “production costs remain high and production levels are 
negligible”; and 
 
• “SAF approval can be expensive, lengthy and challenging” from a technical standpoint, 
and “[t]hese constraints can lead fuel producers to favour using their products in other 
lower-risk markets e.g. road transport, where fuel approval is less challenging.”

Ultimately, we fully concur that policy options for “boosting the supply and demand for [SAF] in 
the EU” need to be explored. As discussed in greater detail below, however, the focus should 
be on providing incentives for the energy sector to increase SAF supply rather than mandating 
any SAF quotas on fuel suppliers or SAF usage requirements on airlines.  
 
Part B of the Roadmap lays out the different measures that will be considered by the 
Commission. The first one listed is a SAF blending mandate. While A4A supports the creation of 
“a stable policy framework over a sufficient time horizon to provide investors with the necessary 
confidence to invest in the production of [SAF] and for airlines to pursue efficient fuels policy,” 
we believe it is premature for the Commission (or any individual Member State) to consider the 
imposition of a blending mandate. As the Roadmap acknowledges, SAF are still a long way 
from being commercially viable at scale. Until they are, the Commission should focus instead on 
positive measures and incentives that are geared towards improving cost-competitiveness, 
expanding supply, and establishing the long-term commercial viability of SAF.  
 
The approach taken by Norway is instructive. On January 1, 2020, a SAF blending mandate of 
0.5% by volume of total aviation fuel sold per year, excluding the military, went into effect in the 
country.
5 We understand from a European airline that the mandate has led to a significant 
increase in the price of SAF. Due to the lack of SAF availability, the mandate has had the 
predictable effect of shifting pricing leverage to SAF producers. A4A is concerned that the same 
outcome and market distortion would result if the EU or one or more Member States were to 
impose a blending mandate.  
 
Virtually all of the other options described in Part B amount to positive policy measures and 
incentives and, hence, are appropriate for consideration in the next phase of the Commission’s 
work, the impact assessment. Of particular interest to A4A are a potential increase of the 1.2 
multiplier for aviation in the recast Renewable Energy Directive (RED), a mechanism under 
which funding assistance would be provided to SAF producers and/or airlines, feedstock 
prioritization for SAF (over green (i.e., renewable) diesel and other road transport alternative 
fuels), a central auctioning scheme for SAF, a collaborative platform to facilitate SAF purchase 
agreements, and technical facilitation and support for SAF producers seeking qualification and 
approval of their fuel.6 
 
With respect to the RED, while a 1.2 multiplier should help, a 2.0 multiplier would do more to 
overcome the strong economic incentive that leads biorefineries to choose to produce lower-
cost green diesel, which can be used in road and maritime transport applications, over more 
complex and costlier-to-produce SAF. 
 
Funding assistance and other financial measures (e.g., tax incentives) would undoubtedly aid in 
getting the SAF industry off the ground. For example, both Red Rock Biofuels and Fulcrum 
BioEnergy, with which A4A members have SAF supply agreements, received supportive grants 
through a program under the U.S. Defense Production Act to assist in start-up activities, and

The following principles should guide EU action to support commercially viable SAF production 
and deployment through financial mechanisms: 
 
• Implement a policy framework that prioritizes aviation and SAF production and use over 
road and maritime transport alternative fuels. The road and maritime transport sectors 
do indeed have other “alternatives to decarbonize,” such as electric vehicles, whereas 
aviation does not have similar viable alternatives.  
• Select policy timeframes that match return-on-investment timeframes, usually ten to 
twenty years, to reduce investment risk.  
• Seek opportunities for public-private partnerships in SAF production.  
• Provide continued support for SAF research and development, including development 
of feedstock supply chains and new and innovative SAF production technologies. 
 
Regarding feedstocks, we encourage the Commission to analyze all potential SAF feedstocks 
(e.g., municipal solid waste, animal fats, waste oils and greases, waste gases) and production 
pathways as part of the impact assessment.  
 
In closing, A4A wishes to emphasize that we would be pleased to participate as a stakeholder 
during the impact assessment phase. This includes participating in the external study and in 
information-gathering events, and being contacted for an interview(s) by Commission staff. In 
addition, while strongly supporting the Commission’s focus on increasing the production and 
deployment of SAF as a part of the basket of measures “[t]o tackle the [GHG] emissions from 
aviation,” A4A notes as well our support for the continued implementation of the Single 
European Sky (SES) initiative, which on its own should bring about a significant emissions 
reduction. We are pleased that further work on SES is also a priority for the Commission and 
look forward to engaging on that work as well.  
 
Thank you for consideration of our feedback on the SAF Roadmap. Please do not hesitate to 
contact me if you have any questions.

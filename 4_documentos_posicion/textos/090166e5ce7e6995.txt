 
Environmental Investigation Agency (EIA) Response to Ozone Regulation Inception 
Impact Assessment 
 
EIA welcomes the Inception Impact Assessment and the intent expressed to improve 
the Ozone Regulation in several ways.  
 
EIA agrees with the areas for potential improvement, namely: 
• Enhancing its efficiency, coherence and clarity 
• Attain additional emissions to the extent possible 
• Extending the Regulation to cover new ODS. 
We agree that a high level of ambition and strict implementation rules is essential to 
avoid any backsliding and agree that the four policy options cover the main areas to be 
improved, namely: 1. Higher level of emission reductions; 2. Improving efficiency while 
preserving effective prevention of illegal activities; 3. Ensuring good monitoring; and 4. 
Improving coherence and updating.  
The Inception Impact Assessment states that the “The objective of the review is 
predominantly to achieve the overall policy objectives in a more efficient, coherent and 
clear manner”. However, we urge the Commission to consider that maximising 
emission reductions is equally a priority, in line with meeting EU climate targets and 
the Green Deal. The ODS that continue to be produced in large quantities are potent 
short-lived greenhouse gases. For example, HCFC-22, a commonly produced refrigerant 
and feedstock, has a GWP100 of 1760 and a GWP20 of 5280 (AR5). We urge that GWPs as 
well as ODPs are considered throughout the impact assessment that will be prepared.  
EIA is concerned at the number of new ODS that are not covered by the Montreal 
Protocol, which increased by 41% between 2017 and 2018. These need to be carefully 
monitored and controlled going forward.   
We note the need for continued vigilance given continued illegal imports of ODS into 
Europe and unexpected emissions of CFCs and CTC. In the last decade, there have been 
ODS seizures reported to the Montreal Protocol by Spain, Bulgaria, Netherlands, Croatia, 
Czech Republic, Ireland and Italy.1 The ODS licensing system has been a success, 
however we fully support the need to exploit synergies with the Single Window for 
Customs and to align with the F-Gas Regulation to bring about a fully effective 
licensing system.  
With respect to emissions, we note that the significant remaining ODS use is for 
feedstock. We expect a full analysis of reported emissions from feedstock use and

In 2018, 4967 tonnes of virgin HCFCs were imported, mostly from China.2 We expect 
that HCFC-22 represents a large proportion of these imports, although this data is not 
reported by the EEA. The production of HCFC-22 results in the by-production of HFC-23, 
a highly potent greenhouse gas. The EU F-Gas Regulation, through Article 7, intends to 
control emissions of HFC-23, not just direct emissions in the EU, but also emissions 
related to the manufacturing of HFCs in other countries.  
Article 7(2) states: Without prejudice to Article 11(1), the placing on the market of 
fluorinated greenhouse gases and gases listed in Annex II shall be prohibited unless, 
where relevant, producers or importers provide evidence, at the time of such placing, 
that trifluoromethane, produced as a by-product during the manufacturing process, 
including during the manufacturing of feedstocks for their production, has been 
destroyed or recovered for subsequent use, in line with best available techniques.  
 
Although the F-Gas Regulation has been in force for five years, and EIA is not aware of 
any reports of non-compliance with Article 7, it is clear that there is limited destruction 
or recovery of HFC-23 outside Europe. A new paper3 published in Nature in January 
2020 reported higher than ever emissions of HFC-23. In fact, atmospheric 
measurements of HFC-23 indicate emissions in 2017 were at an all-time high of 15.9 
Gg/yr (15,900 metric tonnes). Based on the pledged reductions from China and India, 
researchers expected emissions to be in the region of 2.4 Gg/yr by 2017, with China’s 
reductions accounting for the majority of the expected reduction of 17.1 Gg/yr in 2017 
(15.2 Gg from China, 1.9 Gg from India).  
The Ozone Regulation should therefore be revised in order to require the destruction 
and recovery of HFC-23 in line with best available techniques, with consideration of 
ways to improve implementation also applicable to the F-Gas Regulation.  
The Commission Staff Working Document makes clear that significant improvements 
can be made to promote recovery, recycling, reclamation and destruction of ODS. Only 
a few Member States have extended responsibility to the producer. Therefore the 
legislation should Require Extended Producer Responsibility Schemes Meeting 
Minimum Requirements. In addition to requiring Member States to ensure the 
establishment of EPR schemes, the EU should also include certain minimum 
requirements on collection, reclamation, recycling, disposal facilities, equipment 
provision to certified technicians, reporting and awareness raising – similar to the 
approach to EPR schemes in the Directive (EU) 2019/904 on the Reduction of the Impact 
of Certain Plastic Products on the Environment (also referred to as the Single-Use 
Plastics Directive”).

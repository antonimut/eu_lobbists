World Shipping Council Comments 
FuelEU Maritime – Green European Maritime Space Initiative 
23 April 2020 
 
 
The World Shipping Council (WSC or the Council) is a non-profit trade association that represents 
the liner shipping industry, primarily operators of containerships, vehicle carriers, and roll -on/roll-off 
vessels.  The Council’s members carry over 90% of the world’s containerized trade and include several of 
the world’s largest lines headquartered in the European Union.  WSC Member companies transport over 
40 million TEUs of European export and import cargo each year or roughly two-thirds of the EU’s seaborne 
trade by value; around 10 million TEUs of feeder cargo to and from the EU’s trans -shipment hubs and 4 
million TEU of cargo moved in pure intra EU trade. WSC member companies play a defining role in 
European transport and logistics. Their operations and investments extend beyond ships to port terminals, 
warehouses, truck companies and the information technology systems that are critical for EU logistics and 
supply chains.  The World Shipping Council is inscribed on the EU Transparency Register under number 
32416571968-71.  
 
 The World Shipping Council welcomes the opportunity to offer comments on the problem 
definition, objectives, and policy options outlined in the Inception Impact Report prepared for the FuelEU 
Maritime – Green European Maritime Space.     Following the approach outlined in the Inception Impact 
Assessment document, we have organized our comments into tw o general discussion areas: (1) problem 
definition and (2) objectives and policy options.   
 
1. Problem Definition 
 
 WSC agrees with many of the key issues identified in the Inception Impact Assessment that 
contribute to the challenge to move the maritime transport sector to low carbon and zero carbon fuels 
and technologies.  Lack of predic tability and high risk of investment choices are central to the current 
challenge.  Today, we know certain fuels such as e -ammonia and e -hydrogen represent promising 
candidates for the needed energy transition, but too little work has been done to address onboard and 
shore-side systems engineering to arrive at any conclusions as to whether a given fuel will prove 
practicable for a given ship  – especially larger ships that  must transit thousands of miles while carrying 
substantial quantities of fuel.  For this reason, energy density and materials science relevant to both 
onboard and onshore storage are issues requiring focused attention.  WSC fully supports action to address 
these important questions.  WSC also recommends that the initiative be expanded to support specialized 
research and development as well as the development of specialized shipboard engineering systems that 
will prove critical to developing the next generation of zero-carbon ships.  
 
 Fuels  The Inception Impact Assessment observes that the number of sustainable fuels and 
technologies currently available in the maritime sector is limited.   WSC agrees with that observation.  The 
Impact Assessment also seeks to boost “the uptake of technologically mature sustainable fuels and 
technologies.”  Page 2 of the assessment goes on to note the following:  “ Presently, liquid biofuels and 
electrically synthesized hydrocarbons have the highest energy density followed by natural gas and bio-gas,

as well as other synthetic fuels (e.g. electricity-based e-gas).  These fuels are compatible with the existing 
assets and infrastructure (liquid or gaseous ) and can therefore be deployed immediately  in existing oil or 
LNG-fueled vessels.”   From the above statement from paragraph 2, page 2 of the Inception Impact 
Assessment we understand that DG-Move considers liquid biofuels, electrically synthesized hydrocarbons, 
natural gas , bio-gas, and other syn thetic fuels (e.g. electricity -based e -gas) as technologically mature 
sustainable fuels capable of immediate use.  While there are a variety of technical questions that may 
arise with the availability of synthetic sustainable fuels  and the compatibility of  some biofuels, a second 
question arises – namely, does the Commission seek to also explore the development and use of green 
ammonia and/or green hydrogen as a maritime fuel?   If yes, WSC believes that the initiative should 
include provisions that support  the technical development work that is necessary to make use of other 
promising sustainable fuels possible in the maritime sector.  This is especially true for those fuels that hold 
the potential to deliver zero or near-zero emission ships. 
 
Considering the  uncertainties outlined in the Inception Impact Assessment  we believe that it is 
important that we do not work on the assumption that a particular fuel or family of fuels will constitute 
the principal fuel that will drive the next maritime energy transi tion.  With adequate research and 
technical development, we may find that a single fuel type – combined with a storage and combustion 
system, will prove optimum for most ship types.  However, we may also see a more complicated energy 
transition where multiple fuels are being used in different trades and applications. If we accept this 
present uncertainty, we also need to be careful that we do not catalyze the development of fuel 
infrastructure that is premised on a given assumption (namely, that one given fuel is the fuel of the future). 
 
While shore -side fuel infrastructure and production of green fuel stocks will be critical to a 
successful and efficient energy transition, we may  undermine the s peed and efficiency of that transition 
if we inadvertently encourage or catalyze the development of infrastructure that (in hindsight) may be 
considered a mistake leading to stranded assets.  Alternatively, substantial investments in the wrong fuel 
infrastructure may lead to a secondary, unintended consequence.  In short, we should avoid catalyzing 
the use of a particular fuel type that is later recognized to be an inferior choice, but whose use is extended 
significantly because the level of investment in the shore-side and onboard infrastructure is so substantial 
that both private and government actors are reluctant to move to alternatives that are more efficient and 
more environmentally beneficial. 
 
Recognizing the above points, we believe the initiative w ill be clearer in its direction if DG Move 
further clarifies which alternative sustainable fuels the initiative seeks to boost uptake of, and whether 
the initiative also aims to support development of promising zero carbon fuels and technologies that are 
not technically mature, or put another way, are not yet ready for immediate use in most maritime 
applications. 
 
Price and Technology   Considerable attention in the write -up is devoted to the issue of price.  
WSC agrees that price is always a fundamental and  substantial factor in determining the use of a given 
fuel or technology in the marketplace.  That said, we also think there is a risk in relying too heavily on a 
pricing strategy to achieve the outlined goal of limiting the use of existing marine fuel types in the absence 
of identified zero carbon technologies and fuel(s) that all vessels, including trans-oceanic ships can make 
use of.   
 
3 
 
 
Because of the uncertainty about which zero carbon fuels and technology systems will ultimately 
prove workable, it is difficult to predict total pricing for various potential alternatives.  In other words, in 
order to increase investment certainty, the first issue that must be addressed is whether a given fuel 
should be a significant part of the long -term energy mix for shipping (and perhaps other sectors).  Once 
that investment certainty has been  better established, it is possible to have a more informed evaluation 
of the relative prices (and total costs) of such fuels.  This means that it is critical to undertake and support 
the technical work necessary to narrow the field of what fuels and technologies offer the most promise.  
Addressing long-term viability of various fuels from the beginning as part of the policy process will increase 
the chances that we  avoid an accelerated energy transition that i s later derailed and delayed due to a 
variety of well-intended but mistaken investments.  
 
There has been considerable discussion in some circles about subsidizing or “buying down” the 
cost of higher cost alternative fuels to encourage greater uptake.  Subsidies may make sense in scenarios 
where the fuel offers very significant emission advantages, but we should also ask ourselves if buying 
down the cost of an alternative fuel that offers only modest emission im provements will set in motion a 
series of market responses that effectively delay the development and deployment of zero -carbon fuels 
and technologies?  This is especially important because zero carbon fuels and technologies are critical to 
an energy transition that will deliver dramatic changes both within the maritime sector and in the broader 
economy. 
 
Interdependency   The Council very much agrees with the Commission’s recognition of how 
important interdependent energy demand will be if we are to see su fficient demand in the marketplace 
to enable the scale of investment required.   The World Bank has estimated that investments ranging from 
1.4 – 2 trillion US dollars will be necessary in the next 30 to 50 years to facilitate a transition to zero carbon 
fuels.  Most of  that investment will need to occur on land as production of green fuel stocks and the 
development of the necessary distribution and storage infrastructure dominates the field of needed 
investments.   Most experts recognize that maritime fuel demand alone is often inadequate to justify the 
needed capital investments.  This not only applies to the substantial capital required for production  
investments, but also to the investment necessary for local suppliers and terminals.  Consequently, we 
need to look at the development of zero -carbon fuels for use across a wide spectrum of industries and 
other users to provide the aggregate demand that is needed to motivate the necessary investments. 
 
While demand across multiple sectors will be important to  stimulate the necessary investment 
confidence, we also believe that is very important to actively support complementary efforts at the global 
level to identify and develop the most promising zero carbon fuels and technologies.  In this context, we 
would s pecifically encourage the Commission to support the proposal to the IMO to establish an 
International Maritime Research and Development Board (MEPC 75/7/4 ) and to explore what EU 
mechanisms developed through the Green Deal may be established to facilitate cooperation between the 
EU and the IMO to accelerate the development of zero carbon fuels and technologies, including zero 
carbon fuels and technologies that can be used on large transoceanic ships.   
 
Carbon leakage potential and Split Incentives   Relatively little discussion is devoted to carbon 
leakage and split incentives.  WSC agrees that the  proposed policy initiative needs to consider if the

2. Objectives and Policy Options 
 
 The Inception Impact Assessment states that the initiative aims to reduc e emissions from 
maritime transport by accelerating the uptake of sustainable alternative fuels and power in operation and 
at berth.  In addition , the assessment also notes that “While some of these fuels are technologically 
mature, their deployment in the maritime sector and their use remains extremely limited and the existing 
uncertainty disincentives economic actors to invest in new technologies.”    
 
 Some of the alternative fuels that are available today offer modest reductions in GHG emissions 
when compared to HFO , MGO, or LNG, while still presenting issues of compatibility when blended with 
other fuels.  Promoting the uptake of alternative fuels that are immediately available may make sense in 
some circumstances, but the principal  objective should remain focused on the developm ent and 
introduction of zero carbon fuels and technologies. This is imperative if we are to see the introduction of 
zero-emission ships in 2030.  
 
 The Council would like to stress that many of the fuels that will be critical to accelerate a transition 
to zero carbon ships require the development of onboard engineering systems and the design of on-shore 
storage and distribution systems that are not yet developed.   This requires intensive technical initiatives 
that include the development of prototype appli cations and the resolution of a variety of technical 
challenges that address energy -density issues and the design of systems that can be deployed across a 
wide range of ship sizes and types.  In some cases, small vessels offer a platform to test specific 
engineering systems, but we also need to recognize that the solutions needed for larger ships may require 
fundamentally different systems designed to handle the significant demands associated w ith onboard 
storage of the fuel and the need to sail long distances without refueling.    
 
 Shore-Side Electricity   The assessment makes multiple references to shore -side electrification 
while at berth.  Concerning at-berth controls, the Council would like  to stress that the primary benefits 
associated with electrification through at-berth requirements are the reduction of NOx, sulphur, and PM.  
At-berth controls also reduce carbon emissions, but the highest cost -effectiveness is associated with 
reduction of criteria pollutants.  Years of experience in California also demonstrates that it is extremely 
challenging to outline a regulatory structure and to build out the physical infrastructure that enables ships 
to connect when the obligations apply to numerous ships.   
 
 If as it appears the EU is considering a major investment initiative in shore side power for ships 
while at berth  certain elements need careful consideration.   While these investments will be extremely 
costly and complicated, the air quality benefits derived from this investment for larger ships is effectively 
 
5 
 
limited to auxiliary engine emissions while at berth.  We w ould naturally hope that this massive 
investment across ports throughout Europe would provide significant benefits for decades into the future.  
Moreover, broader policy goals should be considered.  For example, if we seek an accelerated transition 
to zero-carbon fuels such as green hydrogen or green ammonia  over the next 10 – 20 years we should 
consider what impact that goal will h ave on port electrification efforts that may become stranded 
investments.  If we successfully introduce zero-carbon fuels over that period (and we need to be successful 
in meeting this goal) , the value and demand for electrical power at -berth will be greatly diminished for 
the majority of larger ships using EU ports  that will be using zero-carbon fuels .  Shore-side power for 
smaller harbour craft and ferries may remain strong, but a continent wide investment programme aimed 
at electrification of larger ships while at berth may prove to be a poor investment decision with limited 
emission benefits as larger ships migrate to non-battery zero-emission fuels. 
 
 Considering the above risk, we would suggest that a goal -based standard for at -berth emissions 
that does not mandate the use of electrical power is more likely to result in investments that are likely to 
enjoy long -term use  and are also cost -effective when evaluated on the cost per tonne of e missions 
reduced.  Equally important we would reduce the significant risk, complications, and huge investment 
burdens that would follow a plan to mandate electrical power while at berth in EU ports.  WSC is also 
happy to share with interested EC staff the lessons learned and experience gained with the at -berth 
regulations in California. 
 
  Should the EU proceed with a mandatory at -berth electrification programme we would stress 
that the regulations must set forth clear obligations on shore side facilities ( e.g., ports and terminals) as 
the success of the programme is highly dependent upon the design and construction of adequate 
infrastructure.  How effective the programme will be is highly dependent on the layout and design of the 
various electrical sub -stations, and with the legal consequences that will apply to shore -side facilities if 
power-ready ships are unable to connect due to inadequate infrastructure.  This may appear obvious, but 
the regulatory structure, legal obligations, and physical infrastructure necessary to design and implement 
at-berth requirements across multiple member states will require an acute level of attention to detail that 
is unusual for most regulatory initiatives. 
 
 Policy Options   Section B of the Inception Impact Assessment out lines three possible policy 
options beyond the baseline no action scenario.   The first option calls for “support measures aiming at 
boosting market uptake of sustainable alternative fuels (e.g. facilitating access to funding, differentiation 
of port fees, etc.).”   While this option offers little detail at present and will need further development 
to offer specific comments, WSC supports this option in concept. 
 
 The second option calls for “Prescriptive requirements on blending / definition of the share of 
sustainable alternative fuels and/or shore-side electricity to be used by ships in operation and at berth.”  
While prescriptive blending mandates can add certai nty to demand and even lower price, we also know 
from experience that special interest groups can succeed in mandating blends that are profitable for a 
group of suppliers but offer limited air quality benefits.  Careful consideration of any prescriptive mandate, 
including cost-efficiency would be critical to consider in this area. We would also note that those biofuels 
that offer the most significant GHG benefits have very limited production potential.  A policy approach 
that prescribes specific blending ma ndates involves considerable risk that the resulting market 
 
6 
 
response may not deliver the expected GHG benefits.  This approach would require much greater 
definition to offer more definite views. 
 
 The third option calls for “Goal -based performance requirem ents on the carbon -intensity of 
energy used in marine operations and at berth, no  [sic] prescribing the type of fuels to be used .”  While 
goal-based approaches are often supported in concept, it is not clear that a goal based on the carbon-
intensity of ene rgy used in marine operations and at berth  is likely to be an effective and efficient 
mechanism to accelerate the introduction of zero-carbon sustainable fuels.  Such an approach may result 
in a series of incremental improvements that avoid the more fundamental changes in fuels and technology 
that the challenge requires.  See earlier remarks calling for expansion of the initiative to support the  
research and technical demonstrations that are critical to accelerating the introduction of zero carbon 
ships. In addition, a carbon -intensity metric would be poorly suited to outlining reductions of NOx, 
sulphur, and PM generated through at-berth operations.  This is important from a policy context as the 
most significant air quality benefits and cost -effective redu ctions to be achieved through at -berth 
obligations would require that those goals be defined through pollutant -specific reduction goals (e.g., 
NOx, sulphur, PM, VOCs) and not carbon-intensity. 
 
 As a general principle WSC believes that policy options shoul d consider the sustainability of the 
fuel(s) to be used and should reward those owners and operators that choose to invest in the necessary 
trials and use of sustainable fuels.   We also believe that it is appropriate to offer greater incentives and 
weight to the development and use of zero-carbon fuels and technologies. 
 
 Reflecting on earlier comments, including observations made in the definition of the problem, the 
Council believes that acceleration of the introduction of zero-carbon fuels into the maritime sector will 
require focused initiatives devoted to developing shi p-specific engineering systems to utilize zero 
carbon fuels, testing, and prototype development of both onboard and shore-side fuel delivery systems.  
WSC further believes that developme nt of regional initiatives in this area can complement the proposal 
currently before the International Maritime Organization to create an International Maritime Research 
and Development Board (IMRB).  A global effort in this area, combined with a complemen tary EU 
Programme with similar objectives should provide a practical mechanism to accelerate the introduction 
of these fuels by recognizing and addressing the technical barriers that need to be resolved to accelerate 
introduction of the most promising fuel candidates. 
 
 The World Shipping Council welcomes the opportunity to offer comments on this initiative and 
we hope that the Commission will continue to consult with WSC as it further considers the important 
objectives outlined in this effort.

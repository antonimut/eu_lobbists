Feedback from Danish Shipping on the inception impact assess-
ment on FuelEU Maritime 
Danish Shipping welcomes that the European Commission has pub-
lished its Inception Impact Assessment for the FuelEU Maritime initiative. 
Danish Shipping supports initiatives aiming at reducing emissions from 
maritime transport and clearly an increased uptake of sustainable alter-
native fuels for propulsion and auxiliary purposes is key to meet the 2050 
targets. In this sense it is important to ensure both the availability and a 
high penetration of sustainable low and zero carbon alternative fuels at 
the market.  
 
Danish Shipping has the following remarks to the Commission’s Incep-
tion Impact Assessment. 
 
• We fully support action in this area. Today one of the main issues 
seems to be deployment, which impedes both the market supply 
and demand developing. Also, promoting alternative power for 
shipping cannot be considered a long-term effort. In order to se-
cure carbon neutral shipping in a few decades, action must be 
taken in the short term, as there is a time lag with design, ordering 
and building of vessels. At the same time, action must be carefully 
analysed to ensure actual effect and avoid unintended implica-
tions.  
 
• The EU should really aim at broadening the supply basis of alter-
native fuels and at the same time, the EU should also push for a 
strong enforcement on the quality, i.e. both in relation to the sus-
tainability and the functional/chemical aspects of these fuels.  
 
• All initiatives must be complementing/aligned with the global ap-
proach from the IMO given the global nature of shipping.  
 
• The Inception Impact Assessment points to interdependencies 
with other EU initiatives. It is essential that new initiatives do not 
only seek to ‘fill the regulatory gap’ but that such initiatives will al-
so be carefully aligned with existing EU regulation on renewable 
and alternative fuels.

• The initiative is intended to be flag-neutral which is an essential 
point in order to avoid competitive advantages for non-EU flagged 
ships.  
 
• As with all other policies it is essential not to punish first movers. 
 
• Following is the initial assessment of the policy options:   
 
o Re 2) Possible support measures should be focused on 
deployment. For many years the EU has offered support 
funding for e.g. R&D on fuels, but this has not solved the 
‘Chicken and Egg’-issue. The initiative mentions targeted 
tax incentives to promote the use of sustainable alternative 
fuels, which could be a way forward. However, it is essen-
tial that such measures cannot be proceeded with before 
alternative fuels can be supplied and a price gap between 
fossil and non-fossil is identified.  
 
o Re 3) Prescriptive requirements could give opportunities for 
a push for certain fuels. However, the fuel mix mandated 
must be considered thoroughly and based on several crite-
ria including cost-efficiency. Moreover, such initiative will be 
highly dependent on strong enforcement. Prescriptive re-
quirements should also be focused on mandating real ac-
tion from the ports, e.g. roll out of infrastructure for supply 
of the new fuels and shore power.   
 
o Re 4) Goal-based performance is also the focus for discus-
sions in the IMO. As such, there is good basis for alignment 
between IMO and EU measures. However, a goal-based 
approach may be a short-term solution to improve carbon 
intensity but does not seem to be enough to push deploy-
ment and ensure a market for alternative fuels.  
 
• The definition of sustainable fuel should be clearly defined also in 
terms of upstream sustainability issues. An increase in demand 
for biofuel and fuel blends originating from palm oil or other feed 
stock oils often linked to unsustainable production methods is un-
desirable. Shipowners may not be able to tell unsustainable from 
sustainable biofuels as the physical properties are the same. The

basis for an informed decision must be provided not only to ship 
owner but to other stakeholders as well. The sustainability of fuels 
could be ensured by requiring certification and documentation via 
the Bunker Delivery Note.   
 
• Sustainability of fuels should clearly be accounted for and ship 
owners should be rewarded for taking decisions in this direction. 
  
• The initiative also covers emissions at berth and shore-side elec-
tricity which is emphasized several times as a possible solution. 
Running on electricity supplied from shore or zero-emission pro-
duction on board rather than on electricity generated by fossil 
fuels can significantly reduce the volume of CO2 emitted by a ship 
while in port. In this regard, focus should be on the goal, i.e. re-
ducing emissions at berth, rather than prescribing the means. 
 
• In relation to shore-side electricity it is mentioned that it could be 
used for providing electricity while at berth and for charging ships 
batteries and that this should have a positive impact on uptake of 
electricity-powered ships. It should be noted that the systems re-
quired for shore power is different form systems used for charging 
batteries on ships. Furthermore, a requirement for non-emission in 
ports is technology neutral and also increases competition, 
whereas port power requirement could create monopoly supply at 
the point of the port. 
 
• A strategy for smart sector integration is rightly identified. Action 
for alternative fuels should not be solely viewed from the perspec-
tive of the maritime sector in isolation. The sector integration and 
the production of new fuels for other sectors (land or air transport, 
heavy industry) is likely to be a key element in the fuel switch of 
the marine sector. Thus, the real market for alternative fuels must 
be created in the energy sector. Initiatives to promote alternative 
fuels in the maritime sector should therefore also aim to send a 
strong signal to the energy sector of significant future demand. 
There is then a technical and practical role for EU regulators to 
ensure that once such solutions are broadly developed, the Euro-
pean energy network and not least port infrastructures and pro-
cesses are ready to support the uptake from maritime transport.

• The current procedures for favourable tax treatment for shore-side 
electricity under Directive 2003/96/EC are cumbersome and a 
lengthy procedure. A permanent arrangement is needed to ensure 
legal clarity and certainty for port and ship operators about the tax 
measures applied for the purpose of promoting the use of shore-
side electricity. Further, the taxation of alternative fuels and elec-
tricity supplied to a battery-powered or hybrid-electric ship for pro-
pulsion should be further evaluated in order to close the cost gap 
between fossil fuels and alternative fuels and electricity.

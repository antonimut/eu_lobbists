Following the recent political developments on national and EU level , announcements from car 
manufacturers that they will be increasing the electrification of their fleets, and the growing level of 
action at a city level: the transport transformation has truly begun. As such we feel it is the appropriate 
time to revisit the Alternative Fuels Infrastructure Directive.   
 
The transport sector is particularly affected by decarbonisation and will need to step up its emission 
reduction efforts as in order to achieve climate neutrality by 2050, a 90% emission cut in the sector is  
needed. We continue to believe that electricity, at least for road transport, is the best solution. Only in 
2019, the EU’s electricity sector decreased its greenhouse gas emissions by 12% and there is a clear 
ambition from the European power sector to ach ieve carbon neutrality well before 2050. As the 
electricity mix decarbonises, increasing the use of electricity in transport can ensure a more efficient 
decarbonisation of this sector.   
 
From car manufactures and site owners, through to public authorities  and energy companies meeting 
the growing demand for e -mobility services and delivering the transport transformation is a joint 
endeavour. We are already helping to deliver a pan-European charging infrastructure that ensures that 
e-mobility is an attractiv e option for customers.  We are completely focused on delivering intelligent 
networks and innovative customer solutions and have already made extensive investments in electric 
mobility and the networks of the future. On a group level, we  are preparing for the ramp -up of electric 
mobility both in the networks and in the area of customer solutions. As a leading charging 
infrastructure manufacturer, we have already installed 36,000 charging points worldwide for private 
and business customers as well as 180 High Power Chargers in Europe.  
 
The construction and operation of charging points is of great importance for the success of the 
transport transformation. In pursuing this goal, we welcome a review of the AFID to ensure it is fit for 
the future of mobility. We feel that the review shall consider:  
  
1.The pivotal importance of funding for e -mobility charging:  To facilitate the wider uptake and 
establishment of a true EU wide e -mobility market, public funding  or financing  of charging 
infrastructure for every use -case will remain a vital necessity in the short and mid -term. This applies, 
however, especially for high-power charging of different magnitudes . Ultra-Fast Charging (UFC) plays 
a key role in alleviating “range anxi ety” and making e -mobility a viable replacement for long distance 
journeys. However, the business case currently does not stack up. UFC requires a large upfront 
investment with little certainty on the payback period. Even under very optimistic start -up scenarios, 
the expansion of the UFC is not feasible in the next 5 -6 years. At the same time, the development of a 
public UFC infrastructure is vital for a successful ramp -up of electromobility. While this is the case, 
public funding  or financing  plays an impo rtant role in leveraging private investment. Thus, further 
public funding opportunities for ultra-fast charging points are needed and the funding itself needs to be 
increased to a real rate of  70% per charger (Capax and Opex should be applicable ). Currently, public 
funding rates per charger are at approximately 10 -30%. However, this is not sufficient to leverage the 
required level of private investment. To further support this approach, steps should be taken to ensure 
that each member states allows the blending of funding from different levels of Government (i.e. EU, 
Federal, Regional) , thus, creating synergy effe cts whilst avoiding unnecessary  (additional) 
administrative burden. This would share the cost of realising a real funding rate of up to 70 % across 
different budgets. As for where the funding should be focused, greater attention should also be given 
to the TEN-T Comprehensive Network, as the Core Network has, to date, been the sole focus.   
 
2. Faster approval procedures and a single point of contact for all administrative concerns:  
Protracted approval procedures can unnecessarily delay the construction of public charging 
infrastructure by up to 12 months. It is important to set up the charging points quickly lowering costs 
arising from delay a s well as enabling the charging point to pay for itself quickly. The EU should push 
for the creation of a single point of contact for each member state/region that can deal with planning 
concerns for both construction as well as grid connection, helping to  accelerate the approval

processes. Further, the central point of contact could clarify questions from companies in advance of 
an approval procedure and, thus, prevent unnecessary complications and misunderstandings.   
  
3. A European -wide scalable chargin g infrastructure requires greater standardi sation of 
legislation, and the removal of local specificities: A standard specification - of what should and 
what should not be regulated - should be put in place across European member states to allow easily 
scalable pan -European business models. The EU should act to avoid local specificities which add 
unnecessary co sts and are limiting the customer -friendly experience . These are not only limited to 
laws made by governments, but also to regulations laid out by concessionaires of public spaces. An 
example for this would be the requirement by French law to provide an ad ditional and very specific 
type of charger on public sites (i.e. a triple -charger on ultra -fast charging sites) and the additional 
requirements placed by highway concessionaires. Looking only at the first matter, this already causes 
extra costs of €30,000 upwards, requires additional space on already limited locations, and provides a 
service that is of very limited interest to the majority of customers.    
  
4. There should be no mandatory obligation for charge points to connect to a specific roaming 
platform: The electric vehicle charging market is still nascent and heavy regulatory burdens should 
not be applied at this point. Instead of mandating the use of a single provider the Commission should 
explore the use of open standards (e.g. Open Charge Point In terface) that allow for bilateral 
relationships, platforms and platform to platform connections. A fixed obligation to join a roaming 
platform merely adds an extra layer between the customer and the service provider/charge point 
operator, and thus contributes to increased costs and inconveniences for the customer.   
  
5. Data sharing between vehicles and energy providers is essential to unleashing the full 
potential of e-mobility: Unlocking the full potential of mobility depends, in large part, on the abili ty to 
integrate increasing numbers of EVs into the energy system. While this is a challenge to manage 
increases in peak energy demand and guarantee energy security, it is also an opportunity to empower 
the customer and give them the ability to monetize the  flexibility their vehicle provides. Access to 
vehicle data is essential to making this work. Especially the charge level and the charging capacity of 
the battery. This goes beyond data from intelligent metering systems (as noted in the current AFID in 
Recital 28). Fair access to data will reduce grid -expansion costs, increase flexibility by enabling car 
batteries to become part of the energy system and provide insight into customer’s behaviour allowing 
us to offer more innovative solutions. Thereupon, the implementation of the ISO 15118 standard as an 
internationally valid standard for vehicles and infrastructure in a timely manner is vital for the wider 
integration of electric vehicles into the electricity grid (sector integration), secure data transmissio n as 
well as additional value added services. Please find a more comprehensive analysis on the topic in the 
attached documents.   
  
6. Grandfathering of existing assets:  If the Commission proposes new regulatory or technical 
requirements for charging point s installed in the EU, the grandfathering of existing assets should also 
be provided for. New rules and regulations should only apply to charge points installed after they come 
into force. The investment case for charging points is already challenging, the  requirement to 
upgrade/refit infrastructure when new regulation is passed means that investment is not focused 
where it should be - on expanding the network.   
  
7. Charging infrastructure for heavy -duty transport: Due to EU´s CO2 emission targets for tracks 
and heavy-duty transport in general, new technologies are also expected within this specific transport 
mode. We as well as  truck manufacture rs itself, therefore, see a huge potential for the direct 
electrification of trucks and the intelligent combination of public charging infrastructure, which provides 
green energy for cars and trucks simultaneously. Given this background , the planed revision should 
also cover this new area of necessary and intelligent infrastructure. This would help to coordinate 
necessary private investment at an early stage, and thus, avoid sunk investments.

8. Member States should commit to ambitious and realisti c national action plans to deploy 
public charging infrastructure in a timely manner:  Overall, Member States have taken up steps to 
increase their charging infrastructure supply (i.a. “Masterplan Ladeinfrastruktur” in Germany) and 
introduced policy intervention (i.a. “Wohnungseigentumsmodernisierungsgesetz” in Germany) to better

Members of the European Parliament  as well as other stakeholders are already expressing caution , 
however, the current crisis must not be overcome at the expense of the set climate objectives.  
Significant measures and stimulus packages will be needed after the crisis is over and we believe 
these necessary measures can, and should, contribute significantly to the clean mobility transition.

Position zur Implementierung der ISO 15118  
 
 
Hintergrund  
Die bestmögliche Integration der Elektromobilität in die Energieinfrastrukturen setzt eine intensive Zusammenar-
beit aller beteiligter Akteure (Hersteller, Betreiber von Ladesäulen und Verteilnetzbetreiber) und einen entspre-
chenden Austausch von Daten, vor allem an der Schnittstelle Fahrzeug und Ladesäule voraus. Genau diese Kom-
munikationsschnittstelle beschreibt die Norm ISO 15118.  
Möglich werden hierdurch neben einem intelligenten Lastmanagement auch Funktionen wie Plug  and Charge 
(PnC), also eine Authentifizierung/Autorisierung und Start des Ladevorgangs durch einfaches Verbinden des Kabels 
mit dem Fahrzeug.  Bei perspektivisch immer höheren Elektrifizierungsgraden w ird zudem ein intelligentes Lade-
management essentiell . Damit wird dies zur wichtigsten Schn ittstelle der Sektorkopplung . Elektroautos können 
hier als unterstützendes und stabilisierendes Element in dreifacher Hinsicht gesehen werden :  
▪ als steuerbarer Verbraucher, der bestenfalls vorwiegend  dann Strom bezieht, wenn aus regenerativen 
erneuerbaren Energiequellen (EE) verfügbar 
▪ als Speicher für potentiell überschüssigen Strom aus EE,  der möglicherweise sonst abgeregelt werden 
müsste, und 
▪ im Rückspeisefall für die Behandlung von Spannungsschwankungen  durch lokale Einspeisung von Blind-
leistung (perspektivisch). 
Um den hierfür notwendigen reibungslosen Datenaustausch zu gewährleisten, bedarf es eines solchen gemeinsa-
mer Kommunikationsstandards, der die Interoperabilität zwischen Ladesäule, Fahrzeug und Mobilitätsanbieter si-
cherstellt, einen fairen Wettbewerb zwischen allen Marktteilnehmern gewährleistet und dem Kunden ermöglicht, 
einfach und problemfrei zu laden. Die Herausforderung ist, die Kommunikation diskriminierungsfrei, offen in-
teroperabel und sicher zwischen allen Marktrollen zu gestalten.  
Authentifizierungszertifikate direkt in die Fahrzeugelektronik integriert  
Die technische Umsetzung verlangt eine direkte Integration von Vertragszertifikate im Fahrzeug. RFID-Karten oder 
das Smartphone sind zum Laden dann nicht mehr nötig  (aber möglich), es muss nur noch das Kabel eingesteckt 
werden. Die sichere Authentifizierung und Autorisierung des Fahrzeugs für den Ladevorgang, die intelligente La-
desteuerung sowie die Abrechnung erfolgt dann automatisch. Die dafür notwendige abgesicherte Kommunikation 
basiert auf digitalen Zertifikaten und Signaturen im Rahmen einer Public -Key-Infrastruktur (PKI).  Diese PKI-
Infrastruktur muss von einer unabhängigen Stelle erarbeitet und aufgebaut werden.  
Wahlfreiheit bei Verträgen  
Für Fahrzeugkäufer und -halter muss Wahlfreiheit bei der Entscheidung für Vertragszertifikate bestehen. Um die 
für sie bestmögliche Entscheidung treffen zu können, müssen ihnen alle notwendigen Informationen zur Verfü-
gung stehen. Außerdem sollten sie die Möglichkeit haben, ihre Verträge jederzeit zu wechseln, zu löschen . Oder 
zwischen mehreren Zertifikaten auszuwählen oder auch PnC zu deaktivieren.

Vermeidung unfairer Wettbewerbsvorteile  
Unfaire Wettbewerbsvorteile für einige wenige Marktteilnehmer müssen ausgeschlossen werden. Grundsätzlich 
gilt es zu vermeiden, dass einige Marktteilnehmer durch die Implementierung der ISO 15118 einen Wettbewerbs-
vorteil bekommen oder andere faktisch vom Wettbewerb ausgeschlossen werden.  
Position - Empfehlung 
Wir empfehlen hiermit ausdrücklich, dass die Weiterentwicklung und Implementierung der ISO 15118 , auf Basis 
des hier dargelegtem,  beschleunigt wird. Bei richtiger und konsequenter Nutzung durch alle relevanten Akteure 
wird die Kundenfreundlichkeit des Ladens erhöht und damit die Attraktivität der Elektromobilität als Ganzes deut-
lich gesteigert. Allerdings sehen wir im Hinblick auf die Umsetzung der Norm die Notwendigkeit, bestimmte Vor-
bedingungen zu definieren, um eine diskriminierungsfreie und faire Zusammenarbeit aller Akteure bei der Umset-
zung der Elektromobilität zu gewährleisten. Folgende Punkte müssen bei der Implementierung der ISO 15118 da-
her im Hinblick auf Kundenfreundlichkeit und Wettbewerbsfähigkeit berücksichtigt werden: 
1. Eine verbindliche Norm wird von allen Automobilherstellern genutzt und umgesetzt   
2. Prüfung des Rollouts einer entsprechenden Funktion bei Elektrofahrzeugen und Transparenz über Hoch-
lauf von Elektroautos mit ISO 15118  
3. Prüfung des Rollouts einer entsprechenden Funktion mit ISO 15118 bei AC - und DC - Ladesäulen unter 
Berücksichtigung der Mehraufwände dieser Funktionalität bei der weiteren Modifizierung der öffentli-
chen Förderrichtlinie für Ladeinfrastruktur. 
4. Freie EMP-Wahl des Kunden beim Erwerb eines Elektroautos beim Automobilhersteller muss gewährleis-
tet sein   
5. Einfache und kostenlose EMP -Wechselmöglichkeiten sowie die Hinterlegung mehrerer EMP -Zertifikate 
für den Kunden. Dies muss diskriminierungsfrei, jederzeit und nutz erfreundlich möglich sein

Common goals for a future-orientated European recharging infrastructure 
Enabling every European driver of electric vehicles to use electric charging infrastructures across the 
EU has been a central aim of the Directive 2014/94/EU on the deployment of alternative fuels 
infrastructure (AFID). To achieve this goal and to facilitate the charging  process for the customer , 
interoperability of the charging infrastructure is a fundamental need. 
Since its entry into force in 2014, the market for electric vehicles and associated charging infrastructure 
has evolved rapidly. Yet, c hallenges with regard to  the availability  and ensuring Europe -wide 
interoperability of charging infrastructures abound. Consequently, a revision of the directive is needed, 
which the European Commission announced for 2021 as part of the European Green Deal1. 
The European Commission has already launched its evaluation of the directive in the Sustainable 
Transport Forum (STF)2 and announced a public consultation in the beginning of 2020. These initiatives 
are welcomed and will help achieve an ambitious revision of the directive. 
1. Customer-friendly charging and interoperability 
▪ Customer-friendly charging means the charging operation is simple and comfortable, i.e. any 
customer should be able to recharge at any public recharging point in Europe knowing it will 
work. It also has to be ensured that customers have the freedom to select the electromobility 
service provider (EMSP) of their choice regardless of any preceding limitations. 
▪ A revised AFID will need to s upport measures for securing the quality of charging . Vital e-
mobility aspects that need to be addressed include: customer-friendly charging, integration into 
the electricity grids , elementary quality and service levels 3 as well as  security of data 
transmission. 
▪ E-roaming solutions and B2B contracts demonstrated how to successfully enable 
interoperability of infrastructures and increase customer -friendliness. Especially for large 
markets with many market actors,  platform-based e-roaming has proven to be the most cost-
effective way of enabling interoperability. To further increase interoperability, a stronger focus 
should be laid on  the interconnection of European roaming solutions in a non-discriminatory 
way. Therefore, the reviewed AFID should contain a legal definition of roaming services. 
▪ The choice of protocol s to enable the communication between EMSP and CPO  (charge point 
operator) must be left to the market parties, however, harmonized standards should be aspired 
to. This is also fundament al for offering additional value -added services in an efficient and 
simple way in the future. Fair4 access for all EMSPs to all CPOs within a competitive framework 
is another important element of customer-friendly charging as well as ad hoc charging, both of 
which have to be addressed in more detail in a revision of the AFID as well. 
▪ Apart from the enhanced customer-friendliness via its Plug&Charge function, the  
implementation of the ISO 15118 standard  as an internationally valid standard for vehicles and 
infrastructure in a timely manner addresses vital e-mobility aspects as it enables the integration

▪ Considering the strong involvement of European industry players  from the energy and

▪ Customer-friendly ad hoc charging also concerns pricing. There is a need for uniform provisions,5 
for clear and transparent pri cing towards consumers across Europe  (e.g. bill ing per kWh). 
Additional pricing mechanisms (e.g. blocking fees) for some use-cases are reasonable. 
2. Scaling up and financing charging infrastructure 
▪ In order to enable electric mobility that is truly European and ensures borderless travel 
throughout the Common Market, targets for high-power charging of different magnitudes  
need to be set by every Member State  correlating with the number of available high -power 
charging capable cars as well as to the number of non-public charging points. 
▪ Future European policy on charging infrastructure needs to ensure that locations for normal and 
high-power charging stations will become available in a public and transparent manner . 
Therefore, setting uniform and reliable conditions across the Union is of outmost importance to 
be able to face competition across regions. 
▪ In the short- and mid-term, public funding of charging infrastructure for every use-case (private, 
workplace, public, highway) will remain a vital necessity, in particular when the market is still 
in agile development with lots of uncertainty  as regards  the availability of electric vehicles. 
Funding for such infrastructures delivers the best results if it is made contingent on technical 
criteria. Best practices can be found across Europe, e.g. with regards to technical criteria 
requiring digital connectivity, e-roaming and the use of open protocols and standards.

▪ Business models that are long-term sustainable are the ultimate goal. In the mid-term, focussing 
on the economic operation of public recharging infrastructure will further increase consumer-
friendliness and the comfort of charging.  Utilization rates are the main levers for economic 
operation. 
▪ Consequently, the market uptake of vehicles  and corresponding infrastructure  need to be 
rolled out in parallel. However, any number of charging infrastructure units will not only need 
to correspond to the number of veh icles but also  to their individual traits . Intelligent target-
setting will need to take account of the actual charging capacities of vehicles to be introduced 
to the market in the upcoming years. 
▪ Similarly, high -power charging infrastructure should support vehicles with up to 800-volt 
architecture and its rollout shall be downward compatible for all new chargers with respect to 
car architecture development. In this case , it has to be ensured that c orresponding standards 
are made mandatory for the vehicles as well. 
▪ Notwithstanding the final mechanism of target-setting in the European Union, metrics need to 
better distinguish between types of charging, in particular the definition of fast and high-power 
charging (< 50 kW, ≥ 50 kW, ≥150 kW and ≥ 250 kW) needs to be clarified. 
▪ As soon as they are available, upcoming standards for recharging technologies in the freight 
sector, such as High Power Charging for Commercial Vehicle Charging (HPCCV) need to be set 
by a future regulatory framework similarly to the provisions on the Combined Charging System 
(CCS) at the moment. Standardization efforts need to be supported by the Commission, which 
should make use of its power to issue mandates to CEN/CENELEC as soon as a standard has been 
established. Furthermore, the legal framework should already address the integration of 
autonomous charging systems of the future. A trans-European charging network for trucks has 
to be established. 
3. Promoting and implementing innovation as well as ensuring freedom and 
privacy of data 
▪ Tariffing in contract-based charging and value-added services are key areas of innovation; in 
order to secure customer-oriented development and flexibility, detailed prescriptions should be 
avoided and the current focus of the AFID on ad hoc charging should be maintained. 
▪ Apart from enhanced customer-friendliness, future-proof recharging infrastructure will need to 
address the integration into the wider electricity system to enable sector coupling. In order to 
ensure that this is the case, appropriate rules to enable flexible energy markets , which are 
needed to support vehicle-to-grid technology, need to be implemented. The further uptake will 
be featured by the CCS communication protocol ISO 15118. 
▪ The future regulatory framework should support flexibility, enable and incentivize load 
management and smart charging via the use of vehicle batteries as well as intelligent 
bidirectional charging for use cases at home and at the workplace. Therefore, targets should be 
set by every Member State addressing scalable technical framework conditions with regards to 
communication, data, security and billing standards.

have access to extensive and comprehensive data respecting consumer privacy aspects in the 
right quality in order to enable consumers to make use of value-added services. 
▪ Moreover, consumers  shall have the right to freely control their data related to the charging 
process (e.g. ISO 15118) and legitimate market players to offer additional services.

Combined evaluation roadmap/inception impact assessment 
SolarPower Europe’s feeback 
 
 
Electrification of transport can be a game changer for the transport  sector, still responsible for a 
quarter of greenhouse gases emissions in the EU. With falling technology costs, e lectric mobility can 
allow for the fast and efficient decarbonisation of road transport, while reduc ing air and noise pollution 
and improving health conditions  in cities. Furthermore, with sector integration, a smart 
electrification of transport can also be a game changer for the energy system  as a whole. The 
rapid connection of new charging points to the grid will require a modern grid planning process and a  
further development and digitalisation of the grid . In addition, EV batteries will represent up to 28 % of 
the total battery capacity in 2030 according to IRENA1. The ability to activate the flexibility of this battery 
capacity will be critical for the integration of renewables and the new electric loads of a renewable-
energy based system. On the other hand, thanks to their increasingly competitive prices, renewable 
energies and solar will be key to supply the cost-competitive electricity supporting the business case of 
electric mobility and to produce cost-competitive renewable hydrogen for transport at low costs, using 
excess renewable electricity production. 
 
Yet, several obstacles remain:  
• Public support and guidance are still needed to enable the economical operation of  charging 
infrastructure. In addit ion, the current charging network remains  unevenly distributed  on the 
continent.  
• Lacking grid capacity in certain areas contributes to the delayed connection of charging points.  
• Smart charging deployment is limited by immature energy flexibility markets and obstacles to 
the access to vehicle data, particularly the charge level and the charging capacity of the battery.  
 
 
An ambitious and smart transport infrastructure policy is key to unlock the smart and 
renewable-based electrification of transport. 
 
The Alternative Fuels Infrastructure Directive should promote the direct and indirect use of  
renewable electricity across all transport modes, as the most cost-efficient way to decarbonise 
the transport sector  in the next decade. 2 The current Directive covers  alternative fuels which are 
derived from fossil fuels , and therefore emit CO2 . However, to support the achievement of climate -
neutrality by 2050, the reviewed AFID must re -direct all future investments towards the  deployment of 
zero emission mobility  and infrastructures . Establishing mandatory targets for the roll -out of 
infrastructures supporting the uptake of “zero emission”3 transport modes is a pre-requisite in 
this regard. The Directive must also empathise the need for a  Comprehensive Network coverage, 
as the current focus on the Core Network contributes to worsening social and economic disparity, and 
constitutes a major disincentive to the widespread deployment of electric mobility.  
 
The contribution of a revised Alternative Fuels Infrastructure Directive to the decarbonisation 
of both the transport and the energy s ector should also be carefully examined.  SolarPower 
Europe particularly recommends the following: 
 
• The inception impact assessment should evaluate the impact of the deployment of a  smart 
alternative fuels infrastructure on the energy system and the grid in tegration of renewables, 
using evidence produced by energy institutions such as IRENA or European grid operators.  
 
• Investigating the cost of such an infrastructure should take into account the reality of low, and 
declining, costs of utility-scale PV electricity. The economic potential of co-locating large scale 
PV generation and charging infrastructure (using motorways, cities and other hotspots of

• In doing so, the inception impact assessment should consider obstacles and overlaps existing 
in current EU and national transport regulation (such as the design of public procurement) , 
energy regulation (such as the design of electricity markets , energy taxation rules  and 
administrative barriers to the deployment of power purchase agreements), and digital regulation 
(access to vehicle data, data protection) . It shoul d explore the need for a comprehensive 
framework for a “Smart Alternative Fuels Infrastructure” linking the reviewed Alternative Fuels 
Infrastructure Directive with relevant energy and digital regulations promoting smart charging.  
 
• The revision should also consider the possible overlaps and synergies between AFID and the 
much welcome Renovation Wave initiative. An effective combination of both initiatives could be 
used to roll-out smart charging infrastructures procuring precious flexibility resources, promote 
the uptake of rooftop -solar installations supplying charging points with direct renewable 
electricity and bridging the “infrastructure gap” by facilitating the access to new alternative fuels 
infrastructures for residential consumers in particular. It could help support a private charging 
network (when setting public infrastructure targets), bolstering the provisions of the existing 
Energy Performance of Buildings Directive.

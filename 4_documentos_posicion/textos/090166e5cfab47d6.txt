COMBINED	EVALUTION	ROADMAP	/	INCEPTION	IMPACT	ASSESSMENT	“DEVELOPMENT	OF	POST	EURO	6/VI	EMISSION	STANDARDS	FOR	CARS,	VANS,	LORRIES	AND	BUSES”	Executive	summary	• The	latest	Euro	6	and	Euro	VI	regulations	have	yet	to	be	fully	implemented	in	the	EU	fleet.	New	CO2	targets	will	push	the	introduction	of	more	low-	and	zero-emission	vehicles.	These	vehicles	will	make	a	difference	regarding	the	primary	objective	of	a	new	pollutant	emission	regulation,	which	is	to	help	improve	air	quality.	• The	need	for	a	new	EU	regulation	must	be	first	assessed	against	what	the	existing	EU	measures	will	deliver	for	improved	air	quality	to	determine	if	a	new	EU	regulation	is	needed	to	close	any	gap,	or	other	measures	might	be	more	proportional.	• Any	 new	 regulatory	 actions	 taken	 at	 EU	 level	 must	be	 justified	with	 the	balance	 between	additional	 cost	 v’s	 air	 quality	 benefits	taken	 into	 account.	Any	 action	 taken	 at	 EU	 level	 must	adhere	to	the	principles	of	proportionality	and	subsidiarity.	• After	the	COVID-19	pandemic	is	over,	we	will	still	face	a	major	global	economic	crisis.	To	save	employment	and	the	independence	of	European	mobility,	costs	must	drive	any	decisions	if	the	improvement	in	air	quality	is	not	justified	or	is	not	significant.	• The	auto	industry	is	a	global	business	and	emission	control	technology	applied	on	new	vehicles	is	 becoming	 similar,	 especially	 in	 the	 key	 global	 markets.	 Therefore,	 any	 action	 taken	 at	 EU	level	must	take	into	account	the	benefits	of	global	harmonisation	of	proportional	regulations	–	this	would	be	highly	beneficial	to	the	EU	industry	operating	in	many	global	markets.	• Any	 justified	 action	 taken	 at	 EU	 level	 should	 address	 regulatory	 simplification	 and	clarity,	to	help	avoid	any	misunderstanding	of	what	the	regulation	means,	and	also	tests	that	may	have	no	additional	value	on	the	key	aim	-	to	help	improve	air	quality	or	help	reduce	CO2	emissions.	• Any	justified	action	taken	at	EU	level	should	lay	out	a	clear	roadmap	with	clear	industry	lead-time	 from	 when	 all	 parts	 of	 EU	 regulations	 are	 complete.	 In	 this	 period	 of	 uncertainty	 for	 all,	the	Commission	should	now	make	it	clear	that	the	technical	content	of	the	current	Euro	6	and	Euro	 VI	 regulations	 are	 fixed	–	so	 industry	 can	confidently	get	 on	 with	 recovering	 from	 the	current	crisis.

Introduction	The	following	views	to	the	inception	impact	assessment	referred	to	above	are	provided	by	ACEA,	on	behalf	of	the	European	car,	van,	truck	and	bus	manufacturers.	The	European	 auto-manufacturers	 (represented	 by	ACEA)	would	 be	 severely	 affected	 by	 any	radical	 change	 to	 the	current	 legislative	 framework	and	we	 would	therefore	request	a	 thorough	consideration	of	our	comments	that	represent	the	legitimate	interests	of	our	entire	sector.	The	impact	of	the	COVID-19	crisis	and	the	short-	and	zero-time	working	practices	applied	by	all	of	ACEA’s	 members	for	 several	 weeks	 means	 the	comments	presented	 here	may	 not	 reflect	 all	comments	 that	ACEA	members	may	 wish	 to	 make.	That	 being	 said,	 and	 for	 this	 particular	consultation,	 ACEA	contributes	as	 best	 as	 we	 can	at	 this	 time	 but	 ACEA	 reserves	 the	right	 to	update	 these	 comments	 at	 a	 later	 date	when	our	business	returns	towards	more	normal	conditions.	With	that	in	mind,	this	document	aims	to	comment	on	all	sections	of	the	Commission’s	inception	impact	assessment	roadmap.	A.	Context,	evaluation,	problem	definition	and	subsidiary	check	Context	It	 is	 recognised	that	this	 initiative	is	part	 of	the	Green	 Deal	package,	 which	ACEA	has	already	indicated	its	support	for	and	we	have	communicated	our	10-point	plan	(1)	to	support	the	EU	toward	effectively	delivering	such	an	ambitious	Green	Deal	and	the	aims	of	carbon-neutral	road	transport	by	2050.	Despite	having	no	impact	assessment	yet,	the	Green	Deal	focuses	primarily	on	climate	change,	it	also	announces	 that	“more	 stringent	 air	 pollutant	 emission	 standards	will	 be	 proposed”	and	ACEA	supports	the	need	to	improve	air	quality,	especially	in	the	major	urban	areas	of	the	EU.	However,	only	if	it	has	been	first	demonstrated	that	the	current	legislative	framework	(i.e.	the	latest	Euro	6	regulation,	the	latest	Euro	VI	regulation	and	the	CO2	legislation	driving	more	and	more	zero-	and	low-emission	 vehicles	onto	 EU	 roads)	will	 not	deliver	enough	to	 achieve	 the	 EU’s	 target.	In	 this	respect,	ACEA	remains	committed	 to	 engage	 with	 the	 Commission	 to	 contribute	 on	 the	development	of	new	regulations.	The	 European	 automotive	 industry	 needs	 a	 strong	 and	 consistent	 internal	market	so	individual	member	 state	or	even	local	 initiatives,	 such	 as	diesel	 and/or	 gasoline	bans,	are	a	 threat	 to	 the	integrity	of	the	internal	market. to	justify	the	 need	to,	 as	 the	 Commission	 document	 says,	 “do	 more	 to	“clean”	the	 combustion	engine”	as	a	means	of	avoiding	fragmentation	of	the	internal	market.	In	 fact,	Member	States	or	 cities	 planning	a	 ban	 of	 the	 internal	 combustion	 engine	often	do	 this	regardless	of	the	actual	level	of	emissions	of	vehicles	but	as	a	result	of	a	political	commitment. Evaluation		Taking	 into	 account	 the	 comments	 above,	ACEA	takes	 note	that	 the	evaluation	mentions	the	impact	that	Euro	 6	and	 Euro	VI	 vehicles	 will	 have	 on	 air	 quality.	ACEA	 and	several	other	stakeholders	have	been	 pushing	 from	 the	very	beginning	 of	 the	 process,	 even	 before	 AGVES	officially	started,	for	 a	 top-down	 approach	that	would	assess	 the	 contribution	 of	 Euro	 6	vehicles	and	Euro	VI	vehicles	and	the	impact	the	CO2	legislation	will	have	on	improved	air	quality	and	then	determine	what	gap	remains	in	order	to	determine	what	potential	new	emission	regulations	would	need	 to	do	 and	 from	 when.	Furthermore,	since	this	initiative	is	part	 of	the	Green	 Deal,	 ACEA	believes	that	such	an	evaluation	must	thoroughly	consider	the	other	related	aspects	of	the	Green	Deal,	such	as:	- The	 ambitious	 CO2	 legislation	 that	 will	result	 in	an	increasing	 number	 of	 zero-	and	 low-emission	vehicles	onto	EU	roads,	and,	- The	presented	plan	 toward	 automated	 and	 connected	 multimodal	 mobility	 that	will	reduce	congestion	and	pollution,	especially	in	urban	areas.	ACEA	 supports	 a	 thorough	 assessment	 of	 the	evaluation	 baseline.	 Only	by	identifying	 the	strengths	and	weaknesses	of	the	Euro	6	and	the	Euro	VI	regulations	will	it	be	possible	to	come	up	with	any	beneficial	 adjustments.	 However,	 ACEA	 does	not	understand	why	the	 baseline	 only	covers	the	periods	up	to	2019.	Both	Euro	6	and	Euro	VI	have	more	stringent	regulatory	steps	(i.e.	Euro	6d	and	Euro	VI	Step	E	and	which	should	be	fixed)	that	only	enter	into	force	after	this	date.	Such	a	limited	baseline	assessment	will	not	provide	the	full	picture	of	what	the	latest	Euro	6	and	Euro	VI	regulations	will	deliver	to	improved	air	quality	over	time	as	the	fleet	renews	(the	speed	of	which	can	be	enhanced	by	many	supportive	non-technical	measures).	Therefore,	ACEA	 requests	the	period	 of	evaluation	 of	 the	 baseline	is	based	 on	a	 timeframe	 that	accounts	 for	 the	 wider	 introduction	 of	vehicles	 approved	 to	 the	 latest	emission	standards.	However,	 this	does	 not	 mean	 waiting	 years	until	full	 market	 penetration	is	 achieved.	The	 latest	Euro	6	and	Euro	 VI	 vehicles	 are	 becoming	more	 widely	 available	 now	 and	 the	 Commission’s	consultants	 have	the	expertise	to	 analyse	 and	 model	 the	impact	the	introduction	 of	 these	 latest	vehicles	will	have.

Problem	the	initiative	aims	to	tackle		The	first	sentence	in	this	section	is	quite	revealing.	It	admits	the	objective	of	developing	post-Euro	6	 and	 post-Euro	 VI	 standards	 is	 not	 clear	 but	 the	 Commission	 perceives	 there	 is	 a	 problem	 and	once	the	results	of	the	evaluation	are	available,	the	objective	will	be	‘refined’.	As	noted	above,	ACEA	requests	a	top-down	rather	than	a	bottom-up	approach.	This	would	mean	that	the	development	of	post-Euro	6	and	post-Euro	VI	regulations	should	be	considered	only	once	we	have	clearly	identified	the	problem	this	initiative	aims	to	tackle.	ACEA	 finds	 this	 section	 particularly	incomplete	 and	 misleading.	Statements	such	 as	“the	 current	standards	do	not	sufficiently	contribute	to	the	decrease	in	air	pollution”	is	simply	incorrect.	It	belittles	the	massive	investments	that	manufacturers	have	made	to	the	latest	and	cleanest	Euro	6	and	Euro	VI	 vehicles,	 it	 neglects	 the	 impact	 those	 vehicles	 will	 have	 on	 improving	 air	 quality	 as	 the	 fleet	renews,	it	is	not	based	on	reality	or	backed-up	by	scientific	data	and	it	could	give	the	wrong	signal	to	the	rest	of	the	world	that	Euro	regulations	do	not	work.	While	the	Euro	 6	and	 Euro	 VI	regulations	have	 been	 introduced	 in	 a	 stepwise	 approach,	 it	 is	 of	common	knowledge	that	was	never	appreciated	by	industry	and	the	number	of	different	steps	has	led	to	increased	levels	of	stringency	and	increased	levels	of	complexity,	especially	for	the	Euro	6	regulation.	What	 is	 known	today	 as	“Euro	 6d”	is	already,	in	all	 practical	 terms,	 “Euro	 7”	 since	 it	bears	practically	no	resemblance	to	the	earlier	steps	of	Euro	6.	A	clear	analysis	of	the	problem	the	initiative	aims	to	tackle	would	also	help	to	avoid	some	of	the	considerations	in	 the	impact	document,	such	 as	 an	 emission	regulation	should	“...	represent	the	state	of	the	art	in	emission	reduction	technology”.	Emission	limits	and	potential	new	pollutants	have	to	be	 defined	 on	 the	basis	 of	environmental	 need,	not	 based	 solely	 on	 the	 availability	 of	 better	emission	reduction	 technology.	 That	 also	 begs	 the	 question	 when	 is	 a	 “better	 technology”	considered	 to	 be	 ready	–	certainly	not	 something	 that	 is	 far	 from	 proven	 market-readiness	 or	something	that	is	the	intellectual	property	of	a	single	commercial	entity.	Taken	literally,	‘state	of	the	 art	for	 emissions’	could	 even	 mean	 replacing	 internal	 combustion	 engines	 with	 electric	powertrain.	A	clear	analysis	of	the	problem	is	the	only	way	to	respect	the	principle	of	proportionality	which	the	Treaty	on	the	Functioning	of	the	EU	(TFEU)	requires	the	Commission	to	respect	when	formulating	proposals	for	new	regulations.	Lastly	in	this	section,	it	says	that	“...	on-board	diagnostics	(OBD)	has	not	proven	to	be	an	efficient	tool	 for	 limiting	 tampering	 of	 vehicles	...”.	 OBD	 was	 never	 intended	 to	 be	 a	 tool	 for	 limiting	tampering.	 It	 has	 the	 capability	 to	 detect	 when	 certain	 emission	 critical	 components	 fail	 in	performance	or	components	would	be	removed	but	OBD	does	not	do	more	than	record	data	(that	can	be	read-out	by	anyone)	and	inform	the	driver	via	a	dashboard	malfunction	indicator.	In	that	respect,	it	is	incorrect	to	blame	OBD	for	not	limiting	deliberate	tampering	while	OBD	performs	a	
	
	 		 		26/05/2020	 5			 	
very	good	function	detecting	emission-related	failures	and	pointing	towards	the	likely	cause	of	the	failure.	 There	 are	 many	 other	 ways	 to	 help	 prevent	 tampering	–	many	 of	 which	 ACEA	 already	raised	with	the	Commission	and	member	states	several	times	already.	Basis	for	EU	intervention	(legal	basis	and	subsidiarity	check)	Managing	 and	 recovering	 from	 the	 COVID-19	 crisis	 is	 the	 priority	 for	 all,	 to	 get	 the	 European	economy	moving	and	the	wheels	of	industry	turning	again.	The	automotive	industry	is	a	key	EU	industrial	sector.	It	is	a	key	employer,	key	contributor	to	EU	taxes,	key	contributor	to	EU	growth	and	 economic	 output	 and	 a	 key	 investor	 in	 research	 and	 development.	 Industry	 supports	regulatory	action	where	it	is	justified	and	needed,	but	the	first	questions	regulators	must	now	ask	themselves	are:	“What	will	the	full	implementation	of	the	Euro	6	and	the	Euro	VI	regulations	via	fleet	renewal,	plus	 the	 gradual	 uptake	 of	 zero-	and	 low-emission	 vehicles	 into	 the	 EU	 fleet,	 do	 to	 address	European	air	quality	where	problems	still	persist?”	and,	“How	 can	 we	 speed-up	 the	 renewal	 of	 the	 fleet	 with	 the	 latest	 very	 clean	 Euro	 6	 and	 Euro	 VI	vehicles?”	Only	 when	 those	 questions	 are	 answered	 should	 regulators	 be	considering	 asking	 industry	 to	make	another	substantial	effort	costing	millions	of	Euros,	if	not	more.	When	 it	 comes	 to	 emission	regulations	for	 the	 type-approval	 of	 motor	 vehicles	 to	 permit	 their	unrestricted	use	for	transport	within	the	EU,	ACEA	is	supportive	of	an	approach	being	taken	at	EU	rather	than	national	level.	Such	an	initiative	prevents	the	fragmentation	of	the	single	market	and	industry	and	customers	benefit	from	this.	While	 the	 principle	 of	 subsidiarity	is	mentioned	in	 this	roadmap,	 thus	fulfilling	 the	 rules	 of	 the	TFEU,	 ACEA	 believes	that	 it	 is	already	necessary	 at	 this	 stage	 to	account	 for	the	 principle	 of	proportionality,	 which	 is	also	set	 by	the	 TFEU.	 An	 early	 consideration	 of	 this	 principle	 would	ensure	 that	 the	 outcome	 of	 the	 on-going	 work	 would	 result	in	 any	proposals	or	 alternative	measures	being	proportionate	to	the	objective	and	would	be	to	the	overall	benefit	of	the	EU	and	its	citizens.	In	 that	 respect	 and	 in	 order	 to	 determine	 a	 proportional	 approach,	 ACEA	asks	again	 to	immediately	prioritise	the	assessment	 of	what	 the	 existing	 Euro	 6	 and	 Euro	 VI	 regulations	 will	deliver	to	improved	air	quality	through	natural	fleet	renewal	plus	the	impact	of	the	CO2	legislation	that	will	result	in	more	low-	and	zero-emission	vehicles	(see	section	‘Evaluation’	above).	Then	we	would	know	the	 objective.	Only	 then	can	it	be	 determined	 if	 the	 objective	 (i.e.	 improved	compliance	 with	 air	 quality	 targets)	 can	 best	 be	 achieved	 through	 action	 at	 EU	 level,	i.e.	 new	emission	regulations,	or	 the	 objective	 can	 best	 be	 achieved	 by	targeted	national	measures,	 for	example,	national	/	local	schemes	to	accelerate	fleet	renewal,	improved	traffic	management,	city	access	restrictions,	local	authority	fleet	/	bus	fleet	retro-fit	or	replacement	schemes.

B.	Objectives	and	policy	options	While	the	objectives	raised	in	this	section	are	clear,	we	believe	that	today,	it	is	too	premature	and	inadvisable	to	start	 restricting	 the	 initiative	to	 the	 few	presented	options	 which	dramatically	narrow	the	range	of	possibilities	that	any	justified	new	emission	regulations	could	cover.	For	instance,	we	simply	do	not	understand	why	Option	2	has	to	rely	on	Option	1	and	Option	3	has	to	 rely	 on	Option	 2.	In	 practice,	 this	will	restrict	the	scope	 of	 the	different	 scenarios	under	consideration.	For	example,	all	options	rely	on	the	fact	that	the	next	emission	regulation	should	be	common	 to	 the	 light-	and	 heavy-duty	 worlds.	We	 would	 agree	that	 some	 common	 concepts	should	 be	 mutualised	 between	 both	 legislations	but	 we	strongly	believe	that	 these	 legislations	must	 remain	 differentiated.	Today,	every	 country	 in	 the	 world	 addresses	light-duty	and	 heavy-duty	 vehicles	separately,	 also	 because	 of	 their	 different	 vehicle	 utilities	 and	 businesses.	ACEA	cannot	stress	this	 high	 enough	-	trucks	 are	 not	 simply	 bigger	 cars.	A	regulation	 with	 a	common	approach	to	pollutant	emission	limits	for	all	vehicles	would	have	serious	implications	for	industry	since	the	philosophy	behind	the	light-duty	and	heavy-duty	legislations	is	not	the	same	at	all.	For	trucks	carrying	goods,	the	emission	limits	must	take	into	account	the	utility	of	the	vehicle.	In	other	words,	a	truck	carrying	30	tons	of	goods	should	not	be	treated	in	the	same	way	as	a	truck	carrying	only	15	 tons	–	the	utility	of	 the	 first	 truck	is	 higher	and	 it	benefits	the	 environment	by	having	one	 truck	 on	 the	 road	 instead	 of	 two.	 Such	 reasoning	 is	 simply	 not	applicable	to	 the	passenger	cars	world;	an	A-segment	vehicle	must	meet	the	same	emission	limits	as	a	C-segment	vehicle	since	they	both	merely	fulfil	the	same	in	terms	of	passenger	mobility.	One	new	regulation	for	all	would	also	likely	destroy,	in	one	go,	all	opportunities	for	international	[technical]	 harmonisation	–	at	 least	 for	 heavy-duty	 vehicles	 where	 EU	 manufacturers	 sell	 similar	products	worldwide.	ACEA	made	this	particular	point	to	the	Commission	well	before	the	current	AGVES	 process	 started,	 so	 it	 is	 disappointing	 not	 to	 see	 any	 mention	 in	 this	 inception	 impact	assessment	of	the	benefits	that	a	new	EU	heavy-duty	emissions	regulation	could	bring	for	industry	through	harmonisation.	Lastly,	for	this	section,	it	would	appear	just	two	scenarios	for	pollutant	emission	limits	would	be	evaluated	–	one	 in	 Option	 1	 and	 another	 more	 stringent	 one	 in	 Option	 2.	That	 would	 appear	 to	quite	severely	restrict	the	options	and	it	begs	the	question	how	would	the	Commission	determine	the	mix	of	individual	pollutants	and	the	respective	scenario	limits	within	each	of	those	two	Options	and	 where	 is	 the	 environmental	 analysis	 (mentioned	 several	 times	 above)	 to	determine	 the	objective	in	order	to	justify	the	need	for	new	emission	limits?	Until	 the	 environmental	 analysis	 is	 first	 done	 to	 justify	 the	 need	 for	 new	 emission	 limits,	ACEA	demands	that	all	of	scenarios	would	at	least	be	duplicated	to	consider	options	so	that	new	light-	and	heavy-duty	emission	legislations	remain	distinct	and	separate	from	each	other	C. Likely	economic	impact	Today,	it	is	too	soon	to	pre-empt	the	economic	impact	of	the	scenarios	-	this	can	only	be	through	the	result	of	a	thorough	economic	and	environmental	impact	assessment.		For	instance,	it	is	not	obvious	that	a	simplified	procedure	for	a	given	test	has	any	beneficial	cost	impact.	The	cost	relies	mainly	on	the	nature	and	the	number	of	tests	that	must	be	performed	for	type-approval	and	compliance	monitoring	purposes.	Another	point	is	this	section	(also	mentioned	in	the	section	before)	is,	“...	monitoring	of	real-world	emissions”.	Up	to	now	it	remains	unclear	what	is	behind	the	term	‘monitoring’.	ACEA	understands	‘monitoring’	 being	a	‘measurement’	(not	 more)	of	 real-world	 emissions,	like	on-board	 fuel	consumption	 monitoring	 (OBFCM).	However,	 the	 word	 ‘monitoring’	suggests	that	 a	 measured	value	 has	 to	 be	 analysed	 in	 a	 certain	 way	 with	 a	 so	 far	 undefined	 output.	 As	 long	 as	 it	remains	unclear	what	 is	 meant	by	 ‘OBM’	it	 is	 precipitate	 to	say,	 “[…]	 the	 relative	 costs	 of	 continuously	monitoring	real-world	emissions	are	not	expected	to	be	great“	or	“[…]	synergies	from	simplifying	or	replacing	the	existing	on-board	diagnostics	(OBD)“	.	Furthermore,	 ACEA	has	a	 very	strong	 reservation	regarding	the	 statement	 that	 trade	 is	 not	expected	 to	 be	 affected	 by	 a	 revision	 of	 the	regulations.	Emission	regulations	set	in	 the	 EU	are	harmonised	in	 the	 UNECE	and,	today,	for	both	the	light-	and	heavy-duty	sectors,	the	applicable	emission	legislations	are	common	with	those	used	in	other	global	regions,	especially	Contracting	Parties	under	the	1958	Agreement.	Changes	 to	 the	 technical	 content	 of	 new	 EU	regulations	would	 also	 impact	 opportunity	 for	harmonisation.	For	example,	in	the	case	of	heavy-duty	vehicles,	both	the	US	EPA	and	CARB	are	looking	to	include	in	their	regulatory	updates	many	aspects	of	the	Euro	VI	regulation	that	address	on-road	 emissions	 testing	 using	 PEMS.	 If	 a	 new	 EU	regulation	would	 move	 away	 from	existing	concepts,	for	example	PEMS-ISC,	another	harmonisation	opportunity	would	be	lost.	Therefore,	 a	 drastic	 change	 in	EU	emission	regulations	and	in	the	 philosophy	 of	 the	 regulations	would	have	an	adverse	effect	on	regulatory	harmonisation	which	will	certainly	result	in	an	impact	on	trade	with	other	global	regions	for	the	European	industry.	The	EU	automotive	industry	is	one	of	the	key	industrial	sectors	in	the	EU	players	and	a	major	contributor	to	the	EU	trade	balance.	We	would	therefore	request	this	consideration	is	taken	on-board	so	that	international	harmonisation	is	specifically	included	in	this	impact	assessment	roadmap.	Simplification	 is	 mentioned	 several	 times	 in	 this	 section,	 as	 a	 way	 of	 enhancing	 the	trustworthiness	of	EU	emissions.	It	is	not	the	role	of	legislators	to	make	regulations	the	public	can	‘understand’	 or	 to	 make	regulations	simpler	 because	 it	 might	 help	 get	the	regulations	agreed,	make	it	easier	for	authorities	to	implement	and	maybe	somewhere	have	an	economic	benefit	for	regulators	 and	 authorities.	Regulations	must	 do	 the	 job	 they	 are	 designed	 to	 do	 in	 the	 most	proportionate	way;	but	it	is	right	to	seek	to	improve	the	application	of	the	Commission’s	principles	
	
	 		 		26/05/2020	 8			 	
of	‘Better	Regulation’.	While	 this	 section	 states	 that	the	 effect	 on	 SME	would	 be	 minor,	 ACEA	takes	 the	 view	 that	a	‘simplification’	could	have	a	significant	impact	on	SMEs.	Industry	accepts	that	a	significant	part	of	the	complexity	of	today’s	legislation	was	via	request	from	the	industry	in	order	to	accommodate	some	special	processes,	 for	 example	multi-stage	operations	that	 often	 rely	 on	 SMEs	modifying	base	vehicles.	An	oversimplification	that	does	not	fully	consider	this	aspect	could	actually	lead	to	an	 increased	 burden	for	 SMEs.	Here,	ACEA	 would	 therefore	 welcome	 a	 statement	 that	 the	Commission	will	at	least	seek	for	no	detrimental	impacts	on	SMEs.	Finally,	due	to	their	characteristics,	the	many	manufacturers	who	produce	vehicles	in	low	volumes	are	likely	to	face	economical,	technical	and	logistic	burden	if	challenged	by	EU	policy	that	does	not	take	into	account	their	specific	case.	Likely	social	impacts	ACEA	does	not	agree	with	the	assumption	that	employment	would	be	positively	affected	by	this	initiative. For	example,	it	 has	 never	 been	 demonstrated	 that	 going	 from	 Euro	 5	and	 Euro	V	 to	Euro	 6	and	 Euro	VI	has	had	 any	positive	 impact	 on	 employment. Consultation	of	citizen	and	stakeholders	No	comment	although	we	expect	the	timetable	will	need	to	be	revised	due	to	the	COVID-19	crisis.	Will	an	implementation	plan	be	established?	If	justified	to	be	a	proportional	measure,	a	new	set	of	emission	regulations	would	certainly	result	in	considerable	 cost	 for	 the	EU	automotive	industry,	 not	 only	 for	 ACEA	 members	 but	 also	 for	 all	actors	across	the	entire	value	chain.	In	order	to	thoroughly	plan	for	these	investments,	and	all	other	cost	related	to	an	increasingly	wide	legislative	 framework,	the	 industry	 needs	 to	 know	 as	 soon	 as	 possible	 how	 and	 when	 such	 new	legislations	 could	 enter	 into	 force.	ACEA	 would	 have	 therefore	 welcomed,	 as	 part	 of	 this	consultation,	 to	 see	 a	 clear	 first	 draft	time	 schedule	 to	allow	 our	 members	 to	plan	 for	any	upcoming	investments.	A	first	helpful	step	would	be	for	the	Commission	to	confirm	there	will	be	no	further	tightening	or	changes	to	the	Euro	6	and	the	Euro	VI	regulations.	This	will	at	least	give	industry	some	confidence	in	a	period	of	regulatory	stability.	A	 second	 step	 would	 be	 for	the	 Commission	 to	 come	 up	 with	 a	 regulatory	 roadmap	 as	 soon	 as	possible.	One	critical	milestone	in	that	roadmap	must	be	a	decision	if	a	further	emission	regulation	is	 needed	 at	 all	 (based	 on	 the	 evaluation	 of	 what	 the	 Euro	 6	 and	 Euro	 VI	 regulations	 and	 CO2	regulations	will	deliver	in	terms	of	improved	air	quality).	Also,	 the	 roadmap	 should	 at	 least	 provide	 as	 an	 indication	 when	 a	 fully	 justified	 new	 regulation	would	 apply	 for	 compliance	 purposes.	 Of	 course,	 a	 clearly	 defined	 period	 of	 industry	 lead-time	must	 start	 from	 when	 all	 of	 the	 political	 and	 technical	 regulations	 necessary	 to	 apply	 new	regulations	have	been	completed.	It	is	not	a	matter	of	knowing	what	the	politically	agreed	target	is;	industry	must	also	know	how	to	measure	and	plan	compliance	with	the	target.	ACEA	refers	to	other	regional	rule-making	processes	that	provide	satisfactory	clarity	for	industry	lead-time.	 For	 example,	 the	 United	 States	 Clean	 Air	 Act	 provides	 4	 years	 lead-time	 from	 when	regulations	 are	 completed	 	-	industry	 expects	 the	 EU	 to	 follow	 at	 least	 a	 similar	approach	 to	guaranteed	lead-time.	Global	policy	makers	usually	take	into	account	the	specific	cases	of	low	volume	manufacturers	as	they	make	only	a	marginal	contribution	to	the	total	emissions	of	the	vehicle	fleet.	Therefore,	it	is	essential	 that	any	future	justified	 EU	regulations	take	 into	 account	 the	 specific	 nature	 of	 the	manufacturers	of	these	vehicle	categories	in	terms	of	lead-time	or	other	special	provisions.	However,	industry	cannot	accept	that	regulators	would	set	in	stone	a	date	of	application	for	any	future	 regulations	 that	 would	 not	 thoroughly	 provide	 the	 necessary	 industry	 lead-time	 once	 all	regulatory	 steps	 have	 been	 delivered.	 This	 would	 simply	 jeopardize	 the	 competitiveness	 of	 the	sector.

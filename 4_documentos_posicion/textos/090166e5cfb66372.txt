Modern methods for protecting the environment and climate. 
Recommendations for post Euro 6-/VI regulations in Europe
Summary 
Part of the „Green Deal“ published in December 2019 by the European Union was its “Zero 
Pollution Ambition” that serves as a roadmap starting in 2025 towards a mobility free of 
emissions. This roadmap is necessary to both reduce the ongoing air pollution within the EU and 
slow down climate change.  
The European Commission has the opportunity to become leading in the world  regarding 
reductions of pollutant and greenhouse gas emissions originating from road traffic by applying 
post Euro-6-/VI-regulations (informally called ‘Euro 7-/VII’). 
The future post Euro-6-/VI-norm has to be expanded to cover emissions throughout the whole 
life cycle of operations . To achieve this a further improved periodic exhaust emissions testing
is required to take place as part of the periodic technical inspection.  
Up to the present day, the EU-Commission’s announcements lack the clear commitment  to 
treat emission deficiencies of vehicles with internal combustion engines the same way as 
a deficiency in the roadworthiness of a vehicle  are dealt with today, as their damage potential, 
although not outright visible, surpasses that of safety issues.1
 Independent of age or mileage, for air quality it is utmost important that motor vehicles 
meet legal emission thresholds throughout their entire life cycle  and not just during 
the first years of operation. To ensure individual testing and valid evaluation of future 
motor vehicles it is obligatory to implement vehicle specific emission limits already 
within the scope of type approval. These limits then have to be complied with throughout 
the whole life cycle. To achieve this the respective procedure of the EU exhaust type 
approval have to be updated. 
 Periodic exhaust emissions testing  makes a significant contribution to environmental 
and climate protection, since it ensures that the emission behavior of motor vehicles 
does not deteriorate significantly over the entire period of use. 
 Exhaust aftertreatment systems and other emission-reducing devices have led to 
unchanged air pollution despite the steady growth in overall traffic. In order to 
sustainably improve air quality, it must be ensured that all emission-reducing 
devices in motor vehicles function over their entire service life. This presupposes 
that malfunctions are recognized and eliminated as good as possible. 
 Improving the functionality of on-board diagnostics (OBD) through more 
extensive access to standardized data, including relevant software information, 
would make a periodic exhaust emission testing much more efficient and robust.

Recommendations for post Euro-6-/VI regulations 
The European Commission has the opportunity to be the world3 leader with the post Euro 6-/ VI 
regulations and to strengthen the credibility of its legislation after the diesel scandal. The 
European Commission's “Green Deal” provides for the approval of emission-free new types in the 
EU from 2035 4. Since the introduction of the Euro 1 emissions standard, up to 36 months have 
passed on average before a new emissions standard was introduced. A new emissions standard 
must therefore already be very comprehensive and take the entire life cycle of the motor vehicle 
into account in order to make the transition period until 2035 as emission-free as possible.  
Previous announcements by the EU Commission as part of the planned impact assessment so 
far showed little implication of these ambitions. 5 Up to the present day, the EU-Commission’s 
announcements lack the clear commitment to treat emission deficiencies of vehicles with 
internal combustion engines the same way as a deficiency in the roadworthiness of a vehicle are 
dealt with today, as their damage potential, although not outright visible to the naked eye, 
surpasses that of safety issues. This is also the view of the United Nations with its 17 goals of 
sustainable development. At the Third Global Ministerial Conference on Road Safety in February 
2020, it was stated that road safety and environmental compatibility are inextricably linked 
together . Specifically, this means closer integration of Goal 3 " Ensure healthy lives and promote 
well-being for all at all ages" and Goal 13 " Take urgent action to combat climate change and its 
impacts by regulating emissions and promoting developments in renewable energy ".6
Improvements in type approval regulations 
The environmental and climate policy goals of the EU Commission will not be achieved while 
there is an increasing simplification of the homologation and test procedures for emission 
inspection of motor vehicles in the EU taking place. The emission behavior in real traffic of 
modern vehicles with complex exhaust aftertreatment systems as well as sophisticated engine 
controls cannot be validly homologated and assessed with simple means in the emission type 
approval test. As a result, the risk of incorrect assessments would increase significantly. Rather, 
future test procedures must be designed in such a way that they are able to record emissions 
and consumption as realistically as possible . In this context, harmonization of regulations 
between cars and trucks would be conceivable and desirable. 
Regulations relating to vehicle design that are susceptible to interpretation and the lack of 
measures to protect against tampering have led to the widespread use of, for example, devices 
to deactivate the emission control system in commercial vehicles.

Type approval regulations prone to interpretation and the lack of measures to protect against 
tampering have led to the widespread use of, for example, devices for deactivating the emission 
control system in commercial vehicles by customers and fleet operators. These devices so-called 
adblue emulators lead to significantly increased NO x emissions in real operation of commercial 
vehicles. In addition, damaged or removed diesel particle filters continue to be a problem. Today's 
exhaust aftertreatment technologies cannot withstand manipulation attempts and potential 
cyberattacks. A future regulatory framework for type approval and Euro 7-/VII legislation must 
define significantly stricter requirements for the protection against tampering of motor vehicle 
systems. At the same time, this regulatory framework has to create the prerequisites for a valid 
periodic emission testing in order to achieve a higher level of confidence. It should not be possible 
to manipulate highly complex and costly exhaust aftertreatment systems with a 15 Euro device 
that can be legally purchased on internet platforms. 7 Because the more complex and expensive 
exhaust aftertreatment systems and their operation in new motor vehicles become, the higher 
the motivation of the owner to avoid the necessary expensive repairs. 
With the provision of vehicle-specific information in standardized form on software, data, 
sensors and actuators of the exhaust aftertreatment systems , the function and effect of the 
emission-restriction systems can be validly assessed over the life cycle of the motor vehicle in 
addition to measuring real emissions as part of the periodic exhaust emission testing. 
Manipulations on the system can be detected at the same time. The sale and distribution of 
manipulation hardware and software should generally be prohibited in the EU.  
Application of the advantages and efficiency of periodic exhaust emissions testing 
In addition, the tendency of the European Commission to take a critical look at the measurement 
of real emissions from vehicles in traffic as part of the periodic exhaust emissions testing in favor 
of type approval procedures, which only take effect up to a certain mileage or age of the motor 
vehicle. Euro 7-/VII motor vehicles will be in operation on our roads for a long period. Accordingly, 
the type approval framework must make sure, in addition to requirements for the exhaust gas 
emissions of new motor vehicles, that the motor vehicles can be inspected efficiently over their 
entire life cycle. 
The comprehensive periodic exhaust emission testing  continues to make an important 
contribution to improving air quality, not only today but also in the future, in addition to the 
complex emission measurement as part of the type approval. It is aimed at the vehicle owner 
and thus ensures that all motor vehicles comply with the prescribed exhaust emission limits over 
the entire period of use. It therefore makes a significant contribution to ensuring that the high 
requirements for emissions behavior resulting from the requirements for type testing are 
implemented sustainably and permanently in Europe. 
Scientific studies prove that regular maintenance of motor vehicles leads to a 10 to 25 percent 
reduction in emissions compared to poorly maintained motor vehicles. It was found that the 
repair of only 20 percent of the motor vehicles resulted in an overall 80 percent lower pollutant

Adjustments to the legal regulations for periodic exhaust emissions testing have a positive effect

We call on the EU Commission to make an adjustment to Directive 2014/45/EU, applicable 
since May 2018, on periodic roadworthiness tests for motor vehicles to the current state of 
the art while considering to extent it with other pollutants and measurement methods to 
be checked as part of the post Euro-6-/VI norm. For example, the necessary check of the NO x
emissions of vehicles in traffic has so far not been taken into account, as has the particle 
emissions, which make a significant contribution to the fine particulate air pollution in cities.
Preliminary findings after the introduction of optimizations of the periodic exhaust emission 
testing in Germany 10 have shown that approximately 5 percent of the motor vehicles in traffic 
have not passed the emissions test. In the statutory inspection period of two years, the failure 
rate even increased by 20 percent (about 300,000 motor vehicles) due to the reintroduction of 
the tailpipe measurement. With regard to Euro 6 standard diesel vehicles, the failure rate was 15 
percent higher than before the measures were introduced. 
In order for the periodic exhaust emission testing to continue to be executed efficiently and 
effectively, it is necessary that its requirements are already taken into account in the design 
specifications and type-approval requirements. This includes more extensive access to 
standardized information from on-board diagnostics (OBD), free access to defined areas of the 
motor vehicle and data exchange. Detailed suggestions are made in the attached appendix.

Annex 
1.) Guiding principles for future Euro 7 / VII regulation 
1.1) Compliance with the obligations from the “Green Deal” of the EU Commission 
With the “Green Deal”, the European Union announces a “zero pollutant action plan”, in which 
it sets out a “clear path towards emission-free mobility from 2025”. 
To protect independence and to avoid conflicts of interest, vehicle manufacturers, technical 
services, and approval and supervisory authorities should only perform their original roles within 
the scope of type approval. In this way, associated impairments in the quality and safety level 
can also be avoided. A clear division of tasks strengthens the necessary trust in the overall system 
and ensures fair and transparent competitive conditions in Europe. The periodic exhaust 
emissions testing consequently also requires the independence of the testing persons and 
organizations. This provision guarantees a compliant vehicle inspection and the necessary trust 
of the vehicle owner. 
1.2 Reduction of carbon dioxide (CO 2) emissions 
The sharp rise in CO2 caused by transport and industry in the past few decades is held to account 
for increasing the greenhouse effect. This in turn is the cause of the current global warming. 
Road traffic still accounts for a large proportion of CO 2 emissions worldwide and in Europe. It 
must therefore be a primary goal to further reduce internal combustion engine consumption and 
thus CO2 emissions. This is an important aspect in order to achieve the goal of European climate 
neutrality by 2050 specified in the “Green Deal”. 
1.3 Monitoring of previously unrestricted air pollutants from motor vehicles 
Many air pollutants that are emitted by motor vehicles are currently not regulated - although 
they have negative effects on health and the environment. 
The following air pollutants should also be taken into account in future emissions legislation: 
Particulate matter  is the air pollutant that has the greatest impact on European health, 
according to relevant WHO studies 11. This also includes corresponding particles that arise from 
engine combustion. These essentially include carbon (C), which is also referred to as soot, higher-
boiling hydrocarbons (HC)  and sulfates. They also arise as a corrosion product of the engine and 
combustion product of additives from the engine oil. Regulation (EU) 2017/1154 defines in 
attachment IIIA particle number emissions as the total number of solid particles in the exhaust 
gas of a motor vehicle. The determination of these air pollutants within the scope of the type 
approval must be reproducible in the future in the periodic exhaust emissions testing.

Appropriate limit values, suitable measuring methods (e.g. particle number measurement) and 
a reference must be defined in the legislation.  
Ammonia ( NH3) contributes to air pollution from particles. The emissions of this pollutant are 
only regulated for heavy commercial vehicles, although cars with an SCR catalytic converter also 
emit high levels of ammonia. Ammonia is created when AdBlue is overdosed with existing SCR 
technology. Appropriate test methods for the emission behavior must be developed. 
Nitrous oxide ( N₂O) and methane (CH 4) contribute significantly to the greenhouse gas 
emissions of a motor vehicle. While the methane emission limit values for heavy commercial 
vehicles have been tightened, methane as part of a total hydrocarbon limit value for light 
commercial vehicles is insufficiently recorded. There are no emission limits for nitrous oxide. In 
order to effectively limit the emission of these pollutants, direct emission limit values must be 
set that apply under all driving conditions. Taking into account their climate-warming effects, 
these emissions should be considered in the future as CO2 equivalents in the legislation.12
Formaldehyde and acetaldehyde are carcinogenic chemicals. They are emitted from motor 
vehicles whose fuel is mixed with ethanol. The admixture of biofuels (such as ethanol) is required 
by EU law for normal gasoline with up to 10 percent ethanol. Particularly high amounts of these 
dangerous pollutants are emitted by vehicles that use a high proportion of ethanol. For flex-fuel 
vehicles that run on up to 85 percent ethanol (E85) and are sold in the EU, emission limits for 
both formaldehyde and acetaldehyde  must be set 13. Such a limit already exists for 
formaldehyde in the United States. 
The CLOVE consortium noted in 2019 that the above pollutants must be taken into account in a 
future Euro 7-/VII regulation.  The measurement under real driving conditions is technically 
feasible.14
In order to achieve the EU's “zero pollutant target”, all of the above-mentioned pollutants must 
be limited by limit values for a tailpipe measurement in Euro 7- / VII vehicles, as they are 
dangerous to health and the environment. 
A technology-neutral approach for all pollutants should be found . All motor vehicles must 
comply with the emission limit values, including those using compressed natural gas (CNG, LNG), 
liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) or hydrogen (H), as well as gasoline engines with manifold 
injection (PFI), plug-in hybrids (PHEV) and motor vehicles who will use synthetic fuels in the 
future.

1.4 Tamper protection 
The requirements for security against manipulation - particularly in the case of software 
manipulation - must be specified and made more stringent. Certified software versions including 
security measures (e.g. checksums incl. checksum procedure, hash codes etc.) must be specified 
in the approval documents and must be available for periodic emissions testing via the 
standardized OBD interface.  
We also advocate an adaptation of UN regulations No. 24, 83 and 96  with more extensive 
requirements for the construction of motor vehicles in order to make manipulation of emission 
systems more difficult and to facilitate detection of manipulations.  In addition, 
requirements for the design, effect and function of the systems are required. To ensure that 
engine tampering can be detected, exact requirements for access to information about the 
relevant systems and their components are required. 
These measures can at least make software manipulation significantly more difficult. 
1.5 Lower limit values for NO x (NO 2) 
Nitrogen oxide emissions  are generally a problem in the European Union. In fact, the transport 
sector causes approximately 40 percent of European NO x emissions. 80 percent of these are 
emitted by diesel vehicles. Measures taken within the engine (optimization of the injection 
system, etc.) mean that current exhaust gas limit values can no longer be met. Exhaust 
aftertreatment is therefore crucial. For these reasons, it appears necessary to integrate the NO x
emissions check into the periodic exhaust emissions testing in the future. The European Directive 
2014/45/EU for periodic technical vehicle inspection had already considered test methods for 
measuring NO x values and limit values for NO x emissions. A corresponding procedure was 
examined and specified on behalf of the German Federal Environment Agency.15
Nitrogen dioxide (NO 2) in particular damages human health. However, it is currently only 
regulated within the framework of an overall NOx limit value. The problem with this approach is 
that the amount of NO 2 varies significantly between different vehicles. In order to effectively 
regulate the emissions of NO2, a separate, independent emission limit value must be specified in 
the type approval requirements - in addition to the total value for NOx.16
Currently applicable limit values for nitrogen oxide emissions have remained unchanged for 
more than ten years, but after the introduction of WLTP and RDE they have to be complied with 
even under more demanding framework conditions. However, data from the RDE databases (real 
driving emissions) of the European and Japanese automobile manufacturers' associations show 
that lower limit values are possible with technologies that some manufacturers are already using 
today.

3. Regulation adaptation and compliance over the entire life of the vehicle
For air quality, it is important that the motor vehicles comply with the legal emission limits 
throughout their entire lifespan  - and not only during the first few years. 
3.1 Improvements of the on-board diagnostics (OBD) and tamper protection 
Many entries (data) are provided or specified in the current OBD standard SAE J 1979, but are 
defined as 'optional' (European adaptation). These options are rarely used consistently by vehicle 
manufacturers. With sufficient and comprehensive data, the periodic exhaust emissions testing 
could be carried out much more efficiently. For this purpose, information from the exhaust 
aftertreatment sensors must be readable in a standardized form as part of the exhaust gas 
analysis via the OBD interface,  just like temperature sensors, pressure sensors, NOx and lambda 
sensors. In addition, data on exhaust aftertreatment systems must be accessible at the periodic 
technical inspection. This includes e.g. information on sensors, the latest particle filter 
regeneration, the NH3 loading level of an SCR catalytic converter or urea injection. 
Since dating of the OBD is currently inadequate and allows different interpretations, the 
minimum scope should be defined as a standard in the type test regulations . This is also 
necessary to ensure that the emission control systems comply with the limit values throughout 
the life of the vehicle - and that emission-critical systems are not damaged, deactivated or 
removed. The vehicle identification, the identification of the engine, all exhaust gas-relevant 
sensor data, current operating states of the engine and the exhaust aftertreatment systems as 
well as secured fault memory of exhaust gas-related systems and components should be 
specified as a minimum requirement in the information sheet of the type approval. The World 
Wide Harmonized OBD (WWH-OBD) according to ISO 27145, ECE49 and UN GTR5, which already 
in 2012 replaced the conventional OBD in the commercial vehicle sector with Regulation (EG) 
595/2009 and Regulation (EU) 582/2011, would also provide a significant improvement in OBD 
functionality in the passenger car sector. This also contributes to a standardization and 
simplification of the regulations. 
In order to improve the identification of vehicle manipulations , a list of emissions-critical 
errors should be drawn up and parameters for evaluating the software should be clearly defined 
in the future Euro-7/VII regulation. These errors should also be saved permanently by the motor 
vehicle. Vehicle manufacturers should provide the authorities and inspection organizations with 
appropriate parameters, as is already happening in California 20. To prevent manipulation from 
the outset, the protection against manipulation of the emission control system must be 
significantly improved within the framework of the Euro-7/VII regulation . In addition, as 
mentioned above, stricter penalties for manipulation at national level should be introduced. 
A significant improvement in protection against and the detection of tampering can be achieved 
if the software versions (2.4) certified in the course of the type approval could be read out and 
checked during the periodic exhaust emission analysis via the standardized OBD interface.

3.2 Introduction of an on-board monitoring (OBM)
New technologies offer promising opportunities to facilitate lifelong compliance in addition to 
OBD assessment. On-board monitoring (OBM), for example, has the potential to be taken into 
account in five to ten years as a useful addition to the periodic emissions test. The on-board 
monitoring (OBM) in combination with a periodic emission test basically leads to an 
improvement in the emission behavior of the motor vehicles . However, a prerequisite is the 
standardized and, above all, reliable provision of relevant data. For legal reasons, emission 
measurement values in particular may only be used in the EU for periodic technical vehicle 
inspection if the reliability of these measurement values is regularly checked (calibrated) 
according to a specified procedure. There are currently no standards or inexpensive technologies 
to establish this extensively. 
In contrast, the acquisition of CO 2-relevant data from engine management is faster . Like any 
other technology, the OBM must be regularly checked (calibrated and adjusted if necessary) to 
ensure proper functioning over the entire life of the motor vehicle (wear, manipulation, etc.). The 
accuracy requirements for such measuring systems are high. In addition, the requirements of the 
General Data Protection Regulation must also be taken into account in the case of corresponding 
regulations, since personal data of the vehicle user may also be considered at the OBM. 
3.3 Independent testing and inspection of motor vehicles throughout their lifetime
In order to ensure compliance with the emissions regulations, the EU Commission and the 
Member States were provided with considerable competencies to carry out market surveillance 
and conformity tests (In-Service Conformity, ISC) within the framework of Package 4 of the 
legislation on real driving emissions (RDE)  of light commercial vehicles. As soon as the new 
competencies come into force in September 2020, the EU Commission should make full use of 
them and regularly test the motor vehicles. 
In the case of heavy commercial vehicles, the responsibility for correct tests during operation 
(ISC) must also be taken by bodies that are independent of the manufacturer. 21 Only 
independent market surveillance and conformity testing during operation can ensure that 
the emission limit values are complied with at all times.
The test requirements for all motor vehicles should be extended to CO2, fuel consumption and 
electrical range . The electrical range for zero-emission vehicles must be recorded immediately 
in the VECTO (Vehicle Energy Consumption calculation Tool).

motor vehicles. These must be adjusted at the same time as the Euro 7 / VII proposal; 
especially in chapter 8 "Environmental pollution" necessary modifications are required. 
In order to take additional pollutants into account in the periodic exhaust emissions testing, new 
limit values and measuring methods have to be developed and tested in some cases. This is time-
consuming - and a corresponding preliminary run is required before implementation so that 
everyone involved can ensure the necessary technologies and preparations. In addition to 
Germany, other European countries such as Belgium, Switzerland, Spain, UK and France, actively 
deal with the specification and introduction of a particle number measurement for diesel 
vehicles as part of the periodic emissions exhaust testing. The Netherlands are the first country 
in the world to have introduced a procedure for particle number testing in diesel vehicles. Such 
a measurement to assess particle emissions only takes 10 to 30 seconds. The Netherlands had 
previously determined that around 9 percent of diesel vehicles (cars and vans) have no or a 
defective particle filter, partly because the filter was removed illegally.23
In the future, the periodic technical vehicle inspection should be able to validly assess all relevant 
pollutant emissions with just one test procedure - regardless of the drive type.

4. Consideration of further emissions in the new regulation (brakes, tires, etc.) 
Future EU regulations should also include particle emissions from tire and brake wear. This is the 
only way to reduce the impairment of air quality to the best possible level through really all 
particle emissions from the operation of the motor vehicle. 
Physically, particle emissions from tire and brake wear differ from motor vehicle exhaust, 
particularly with regard to their size (> 1,000 nm). However, tire wear can never be completely 
eliminated. In the future, for example, it would be conceivable to have more precise labeling on 
the label of the tire model, which the consumer can use to assess tire wear. 
Emission limit values for brakes should also be included in the future emissions regulation . 
The CLOVE consortium is studying the future regulation of vehicle emissions on behalf of the 
European Commission. In addition, several solutions for reducing brake wear particle emissions 
have already been developed. For example, through the EU-funded LOWBRASYS project, which is 
testing the use of new lining and disc materials, alternative braking strategies and a particle 
collection system. It was able to reduce the emission of micro and ultrafine particles by as much 
as 50 percent. The introduction of test methods and limit values for brake wear emissions within 
the framework of the Euro 7-/VII legislation will drive forward the development of such new 
technologies in the future and reduce these particle emissions.

The Federation of Austrian Industries’ c ontribution to the European Commission’s 
Inception Impact Assessment 
The Federation of Austrian Industries  (Industriellenvereinigung, IV) is the voluntary and 
independent organisation to represent interests of more than 4.500 members in the 
manufacturing sector, credit and insurance sector, infrastructure and in dustry-oriented 
services in Austria. On European level, the Federation of Austrian Industries is a member of 
BusinessEurope. 
IV supports measures to respond to the urgency of environmental challenges. However, it also 
needs to be emphasised that, amid the COVID-19 outbreak and crisis, a ‘business as usual 
scenario’ is not possible. It is imperative to include the COVID-19 consequences on business 
sectors into assessments and to base the consultations’ results on reliable data  and reality 
checks. As a consequence, any new initiatives, which are highly important for Europe’s future, 
should reassess the current economic situation on macro and micro level and potential 
developments to prioritise vital projects and initiatives for recovery or where repercussions of 
COVID-19 are still in place.  
Therefore, initiatives that aim at establishing stricter CO2 standards for the automotive industry 
(and therefore raising the costs for manufacturers) in the near future have to be assessed with 
greatest caution. It should be avoided that  the automotive industry is put in an even more 
precarious situation, as the sales slump and the loss of sales of e -models (planned for this 
year) already cause difficulties in achieving current environmental goals. 
The Federation of Austrian Industries (IV) welcomes the ambition to transfer to a 
sustainable economy supported by low-carbon modes of transport and supports regulation that 
benefits companies due to greater legal certainty and less a dministrative burden. In order to 
make the European transport sector more sustainable,  it is important to emphasise that the 
mobility and energy infrastructure  as a whole need to be adapted, modernised, and  
expanded. 
IV asks the Commission to consider the following points when developing the proposal for a 
regulation for new European emission standards: 
1. Focus on implementing the 2030 targets: Implementing the well-designed and recently 
established climate-framework for 2030 must be a key priority. Any traje ctory does not 
necessarily need to follow a linear path, as many uncertainties remain on how to reach the 
EU’s long-term climate ambition. Planned reductions can suddenly in - or decrease in a 
different way than envisioned today, largely depending on the development and successful 
deployment of break-through technologies. Calling for even more stringent and time-bound 
targets c ould overburden the European automotive i ndustries and endanger EU jobs.  
Hence, emission limits for regulated air pollutants should be practical and not  exceed 
current climate ambitions. 
2. Avoid administrative  and economic  burdens: Simplification of the Euro vehicle 
emission standards which is likely to result in an increased consumer trust in the Euro 
standards is very positive. All three presented options include such a simplification. Option 
1 offers this benefit with a positive economic impact, which benefits Europe’s strong car 
manufacturing industries. This supports and aids the industry’s ne cessary recovery after 
the COVID-19 induced economic crisis. On the other hand, the proposed Option 3 requires 
technologies for continuously monitoring real-world emissions over the lifetime of vehicles 
and might result in an increase d administrative burden. Moreover, unlike Option 1 and 2, 
Option 3 is expected to entail higher costs for everyone – in the short term for 
manufacturers – which results in higher prices for consumers and decreased international 
competitiveness of European car manufacturers. More stringent emission standards also

affect the affordability of new post -Euro 6/VI vehicles , which can easily  lead to social 
exclusion and can be counterproductive to the green transition, if new, cleaner vehicles are 
unaffordable. Measures taken for new emission standards should be simple and 
proportionate, and balance economic , ecological and social interests  in order to reach 
inclusive and sustainable goals. 
3. Support technology neutrality  and multimodality:  It will be key to adopt  a 
technologically neutral approach to allow for sufficient flexibility in reaching the proposed 
targets. Thus, IV welcomes the technology -neutral approach chosen by the EU 
Commission and the inclusion of the transport and mobility sectors to achieve the energy 
and climate goals by 2030 and 2050. Multimodal transport needs a strong boost as this will 
increase the efficiency of the transport system. New technologies offer enormous potential 
for Europe’s economy and enable completely new mobility concepts that can ultimately 
lead to an improvement in quality of life,  and a reduction in emissions. In addition to 
technology neutrality,  it is important to follow an integrated approach (with mandatory 
impact assessments and cost analys es), the promotion of the principle of innovation and 
the sufficient involvement of relevant (business) stakeholders. 
4. Promote clean energy infrastructure: An important condition for a sustainable 
transformation is security of supply and affordable energy at competitive prices. 
Large-scale projects – such as power lines, hydropower plants, rail connections – are 
necessary for a successful energy and mobility transition. I t is up to politicians to provide 
the right framework conditions for more (renewable) energy generation, a system that 
avoids unnecessary costs and to facilitate the completion of the internal energy market. 
However, approval processes for infrastructure projects that sometimes take years or even 
decades are not uncommon in these areas. This is not only a problem for national supply 
security, but also for projects of particular public interest with regard to the Trans-
European Transport Network  (TEN-T). Thus, licensing procedures need to be 
accelerated and public opinion towards large infrastructure projects has to become more 
favourable. Transforming and decarbonising the mobility sector, can only happen with the 
necessary infrastructure in place. This does not only include fuelling/charging infrastructure 
for vehicles, but also energy infrastructure to transport the fuel/electricity to 
fuelling/charging stations. 
5. Increased investment needs and ideal investment conditions: Transforming European 
mobility and transport systems will only be possible through consistent, evolutionary 
research, development and innovation both in technical and socio-economic terms, as well 
as through disruptive technological breakthroughs. In orde r to support this,  appropriate 
national and European resources and instruments are required , especially for 
industrial key issues, key technologies, research, development and innovation (e.g. 
Horizon Europe, structural and innovation funds). Additionally, Important Projects of 
Common European Interest (IPCEIs) and an increased collaboration between EU 
institutions, Member States and industry are fundamental to strengthening strategic value 
chains in Europe. It is also essential that European financing instruments (e.g. funding 
via the EIB) include energy innovations for transition technologies and enabling 
technologies. Thus, an  incorporation of  ‘brown’ criteria into the currently agreed EU 
Taxonomy could negatively a ffect investment opportunities , also in th e mobility and 
automotive sectors. Since bank loans play an important role in corporate finance, this could 
result in an economic disadvantage for many companies  in the mobility sector . It must 
therefore be ensured that high-emitting companies that reduce their CO2 output through 
active measures are not excluded from the Taxonomy or categorised as ‘brown’. 
6. Avoid carbon and investment leakage:  Around 13.8 million people work in the EU 
automotive sector, and the turnover generated by the automotive industry represents over 
7 % of EU GDP. The automotive industry has an important multiplier effect in the economy. 
The i ndustry contributes significantly to finding solutions to mitigate greenhouse gas 
emissions and to reach the climate targets as describe d in the Paris Agreement. Thus,

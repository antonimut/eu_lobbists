The European Green Deal: an opportunity to define standards that meet requirements  
The European Green Deal promises consistent targets for the reduction of polluting emissions. While 
the automotive manufacturers have been at the forefront of reducing emissions of air pollutants from 
passenger cars over the past two decades, there remains a need for further emission reductions as the 
vehicle population and distance traveled continue to increase. Within the transport sector, and 
particularly when it comes to pollution in urban areas, implementing well-proven and cost -effective 
control techn ologies capable of better reducing pollutant emissions  is required to meet  air quality 
targets.  
 
Although alternative and low carbon fuels represent an innovative solution in the achievement of climate 
objectives, there will still be a large need for co nventional vehicles and fossil fuels . These internal 
combustion engine vehicles can and should be brought closer to zero emissions, including the design of 
vehicles with improved onboard emission reduction systems and introduction of more efficient 
monitoring.   
 
Although Euro 6/VI have brought considerable improvement, Member States seem to lag behind their 
neighbours in terms of vehicle emission control. To achieve its pollution ambitions and significantly 
improve air quality for European citizens as promised in the Green Deal, the European Commission must 
seize the opportunity of this review to update its standards and develop a regulatory framework in line 
with its own ambitions. This can be accomplished by strengthening and expanding the standards in the 
Euro 6/VI legislation to achieve more comprehensive real driving control of primary pollutants and their 
impact on secondary pollutants.  A key factor lies in improving  evaporative emission reductions from 
petrol-fueled vehicles in order to lessen air quality impacts on EU citizens.  
The necessity to bolster evaporative emissions’ reduction 
Post Euro 6/VI Type Approval should be able to guarantee that a vehicle is as clean as possible under 
all driving conditions and over its entire useful life. The recently updated Euro6d evaporative 
requirements - including canister pre-aging, increased number of diurnals to two (48 -hr diurnal), and 
the reduced  effective SHED limit - are all positive measures that improve the control technology 
response, reduce emissions, and enhance air quality.  
 
However, international data reviews show there are still large segments of the European evaporative 
program that continue to lag other major markets, including the U.S., Canada, China, and Brazil. 
Examples include zero -evaporative technology, control for extended parking, best achievable control 
technology for refueling, and consideration for hot driving conditions. Indeed, Euro 6 standards are not 
as comprehensive as U.S. 1996 standards and do not provide the control needed to improve air quality 
on days where ozone and secondary organic aerosols (SOA) formation can be most problematic. 
European evaporative standards result in control technology packages that lack canister capacity, purge 
rates, permeation control, and On-Board Diagnostic (OBD) leak detection that were required in the U.S., 
Canada, China, and Brazil to achieve the desired air quality benefit.  
 
In contrast to the U.S. and China where Stage II was phased -out due to inefficiency and lack of cost -
effectiveness, the EU continues to rely upon and promote Stage II as its sole means for control of 
refueling emissions. With the EU’s ambitious pollution reduction targets, it is an appropriate time to 
consider Onboard Refueling Vapor Recovery (ORVR) which has been shown to provide the best 
achievable control. In combination with multi -day diurnal control, cost -effectiveness studies by major 
markets show that the cost impacts would be fully covered by the fuel savings. Additionally, it should 
be noted that European v ehicles and vehicle technology, while meeting the current Euro standards,

Euro 7 legislation: ensuring a comprehensive approach to vehicle emission control 
We would recommend considering a comprehensive revision of Euro 6/VI legislation as proposed in 
option 3 of the impact assessment. For better environmental impacts, this restructuring of the regulatory 
framework should include evaporative emissions control technologies at the forefront of the European 
strategy. One can observe clear differences between the same vehicles/model sold in the U.S., Canada 
and China versus EU Member States. To comply with EU objectives and ambitions, the near-zero control 
of evaporative emissions is needed to improve air quality on the days during which citizens are most 
impacted. This would include zero-evaporative technology, control for extended parking, best achievable 
control technology for refueling, and consideration for hot driving conditions. 
 
In order to focus control on days when evaporative emissions contribute the most to problematic levels 
of air pollution, the European Commission might consider an alternative approach for conducting a cost-
benefit/impact analysis for evaporative emissions.  Previous impact analyses for evaporative emissions 
have focused on modeled emissions during average conditions, with impacts averaged over the EU-28. 
Evaporative emissions are greatest, and the impacts to ozone and PM2.5/SOA most s ignificant, during 
hot and sunny conditions, and especially during heat waves. Data show that conventional emission 
models significantly underestimate evaporative emissions during heat waves and that there is value in 
using a more comprehensive approach.  Member States will continue to face air quality challenges in 
the future if an alternative approach is not taken.   
 
The emergency traffic measures and vehicle bans seen in several Member States in response to air 
quality problems create inconvenience to t he public and are a drain on the European economy. These 
effects could be included in future cost -benefit/impact analyses. As an example, in the U.S., the 
Environmental Protection Agency has been aggressive in minimizing emissions through stringent 
regulations and thus avoiding these draconian emergency measures experienced by EU citizens. The 
EU should consider the same strategy of focusing their regulations to control emissions on days in which 
air pollution has the potential to be most problematic and emergency measures are needed. If European 
standards aim to help address the problem for all relevant circumstances (including the more 
demanding), vehicle exclusion zones or other emergency vehicle restrictions would not be needed.  
 
In order to maximize the benefit of new standards, a change in approach on developing and justifying 
the new regulation is needed. The European Green Deal and the post -Euro6/VI framework have the 
great potential for improving European air quality and health. The ability to make these recommended 
changes is supported by internationally developed data as well as specific data linked to the above -
mentioned argumentation. Considering EU ambitions towards the environment and the options laid out 
in the “development of post -Euro 6/VI emission standards for cars, vans, lorries and buses” roadmap, 
as well as the scientific data and examples across global markets, Option 3 has the most impact on 
meaningfully improving air quality.

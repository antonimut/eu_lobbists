Human Rights Watch’s Recommendations to the European Commission for the 
reform of the EU’s Generalised Scheme of Preferences  
May 2020 
Human Rights Watch welcomes the launch of public consultations by the European Commission for the 
reform of “Regulation (EU) No 978/2012 applying a scheme of generalised tariff preferences” (or “GSP 
regulation”). In this document, Human Rights Watch formulates recommendations for reform in the 
following areas: 
A. Human and labor rights conditionality; 
B. The list of conventions included in the regulation; 
C. The GSP monitoring process; and 
D. The GSP withdrawal procedure. 
 
A. Human and labor rights conditionality 
The GSP regulation grants developing countries preferential access to the EU market, if countries meet 
obligations in respect of certain human rights, including labor rights, environmental protection and good 
governance standards. Specifically, the current regulation foresees three different schemes: standard 
GSP, GSP+ and Everything But Arms (EBA).  
GSP+ beneficiaries have the obligation to ratify and effectively implement the 27 human rights, labor 
rights and environmental treaties listed in the regulation. Their implementation is subject to close 
scrutiny by the Commission (positive conditionality) – none of which is the case for standard GSP and 
EBA beneficiaries.  
All beneficiaries are instead subject to negative conditionality: the EU can temporarily withdraw tariff 
preferences in case of “serious and systematic violation of principles laid down in the conventions” 
listed in part A of annex VIII of the regulation (art. 19). Benefits can also be temporarily withdrawn from 
GSP+ beneficiaries if they fail to maintain ratification of the conventions or to implement them, 
formulate reservations incompatible with those conventions, or fail to fulfil their reporting duties (art. 
15). 
Following a mid-term assessment of the GSP regulation published in 2018, the European Commission 
has mandated an external stakeholder to conduct an impact assessment of various options for the 
reform of the regulation. One of the options under consideration is that of harmonizing some of the 
provisions of the three schemes. 
The positive conditionality framework is the only one that actively promotes ratification of the 
conventions and gives the European Commission leverage to promote their implementation, together

with the possibility to engage in a constructive dialogue concerning any possible constraint that might 
prevent the adoption of the necessary reforms. In many cases, the ratification of UN human rights 
treaties also triggers engagement by the related treaty bodies, mandated with monitoring and assisting 
state parties in the implementation of the treaty provisions.  
The ratification and proper implementation of new treaties may require at times complex legislative and 
institutional adjustments. It would also create new, arguably burdensome, reporting duties vis-à-vis the 
concerned treaty bodies and, possibly, the EU. However, these considerations should not discourage the 
option of mainstreaming the GSP+ positive conditionality framework to standard GSP and EBA 
beneficiaries in the new regulation. Rather, the Commission should consider enhancing its support to 
beneficiary countries as necessary to strengthen their preparedness to respect, protect and fulfil the 
right enshrined in the concerned treaties and the related reporting obligations. The Commission may 
also consider separate lists of conventions to be ratified by GSP+, standard GSP and EBA beneficiaries, 
and/or introduce timelines for progressive ratification of the conventions. 
Recommendations: 
1. Extend the requirement to ratify and effectively implement fundamental human rights, labour 
rights and environmental treaties, and the related EU monitoring (positive conditionality), to 
all standard GSP and EBA beneficiaries; 
2. Conduct needs-assessments in GSP beneficiary countries to identify possible institutional 
constraints that may prevent the proper implementation of the rights enshrined in the 
conventions; provide capacity-building and budgetary support to local institutions and civil 
society as necessary to progressively overcome the constraints identified; 
3. Based on the needs assessment, consider separate lists of treaties and separate timelines for 
compulsory ratification and implementation by GSP+, standard GSP and EBA beneficiaries. 
 
B. List of conventions included in the regulation 
The current regulation requires GSP+ beneficiaries to ratify and implement 27 conventions as a 
condition to benefit from the scheme’s trade preferences. Options for reform include the possibility to 
expand that list, though only treaties that have been ratified by all EU member states can be considered. 
4. Besides extending the positive conditionality to all GSP beneficiaries, if necessary with caveats 
as described in the previous section, Human Rights Watch recommends adding the following 
treaties or instruments to those listed in annex VIII of the regulation: 
 UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities; 
 First Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights; 
 Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court; 
 Paris Agreement. 
Regrettably, several other important treaties, including inter alia the UN Convention for the Protection 
of All Persons from Enforced Disappearance, the Optional Protocol to the UN Convention Against 
Torture, ILO C.155 on Occupational Health and Safety, ILO C. 169 on Indigenous and Tribal Peoples, and 
ILO C. 190 on Violence and Harassment in the World of Work, have not yet been ratified by all EU 
member states at the time of writing. The Commission should consider including them should they be 
ratified by all EU member states before the new regulation comes into force.

C. Monitoring process 
Every year, each GSP+ beneficiary receives from the Commission a “scoreboard”, or list of issues, in 
which its performance in the implementation of the 27 conventions listed in the GSP regulation is 
assessed. The assessment is carried out by the Commission largely on the basis of reports by UN treaty 
bodies. EU institutions and the concerned state’s authorities then engage in a dialogue on the issues 
identified in the scoreboard. Neither the scoreboard nor the content of the dialogue are public. 
Every two years, the European Commission publishes country reports highlighting each GSP+ 
beneficiary’ performance in the implementation of the Conventions. While the country reports do by 
and large identify the right issues of concern, transparency around the monitoring process and 
interactions with the country authorities is extremely limited. The reports fail to identify any 
recommendation formulated by the EU to the country authorities, the related timeline for compliance, 
and the consequences foreseen in case of failure. They also fail to refer to decisions of human rights 
treaty bodies under the individual complaint procedures as indicators of the GSP+ beneficiary’s 
compliance with the treaties. Furthermore, there is little if any indication about the methodology 
followed to compile the report, on possible external actors involved, and on consultations with and 
possible input by civil society. 
While the Commission argues that confidentiality is necessary “in order to build trust between the 
parties that subsequently discuss it”, non-governmental organizations (NGOs) have pointed out that this 
excludes them from meaningfully participating in the process. Confidentiality also limits the possibility 
for the European Parliament, local and international media and civil society to scrutinize the work of the 
EU institutions involved. 
Finally, doubts have been expressed concerning the length of the monitoring cycle, which is currently of 
two years. In particular, it has been noted that a longer monitoring cycle would give beneficiary 
countries more time to focus on and produce at times complex legislative reforms to adjust their 
normative framework to the requirements of the conventions. 
While some reforms may be complex in nature and possibly require lengthy assessment studies and 
political and economic considerations, numerous human rights violations, including in particular the 
targeting of political opponents, perceived critics, human rights defenders, trade unionists, 
environmental activists, religious leaders, journalists, lawyers, and NGOs, occur out of a clear political 
will to repress dissent. Pending the necessary reforms to improve the country’s legislative framework if 
and as needed, authorities often refuse to resort to the available tools to quickly address and remedy 
their wrongdoings, such as ordering the release of unjustly jailed individuals, allowing prison visits by 
families, lawyers and international observers, implement supranational decisions, and issuing 
interpretative decrees to limit the negative effects of overbroad criminal legislation. 
An emblematic case is that of Azimjon Askarov, a human rights defender unjustly jailed in GSP+ 
beneficiary Kyrgyzstan since 2010: in 2016, the UN Human Rights Committee found that his detention 
was arbitrary, that he was held in inhumane conditions, tortured, and mistreated, and urged Kyrgyzstan 
to immediately release Askarov and quash his conviction. At the time of writing, Kyrgyzstan is still 
refusing to implement that decision. On 13 May 2020, the country’s Supreme Court upheld the life 
sentence against him, despite international pressure to implement the Committee’s decision.

Remarkably, the Commission’s GSP+ report on Kyrgyzstan failed to even mention his case and the 
relevant Human Rights Committee’s decision. 
Recommendations:  
5. Clarify the meaning and implication of ‘effective implementation’ of human rights treaties as 
defined in Article 2-l of the GSP regulation, and identify criteria to ensure that there is no 
obstacle to the protection of key human rights, including the rights to freedom from torture 
and freedom from slavery, effective protection of the right to life, against arbitrary detention 
and enforced disappearances, in GSP candidates and beneficiaries;  
6. Hold at least yearly meetings with local and international civil society to thoroughly consult on 
each beneficiary country’s implementation of the conventions. Allow adequate time for 
meaningful exchanges, and ensure participation of all EU actors involved in the monitoring 
process, including EEAS and Commission officials, the Head of the EU delegation, and the 
human rights focal point; 
7. Conduct similar consultations with civil society to identify obstacles that may prevent the full 
implementation of the obligations enshrined in the treaties by GSP candidate countries, and 
develop a public plan of action accordingly; 
8. Create a complaint procedure so that interested parties can request the Commission to 
conduct a review of laws, policies and practices that may create obstacles to the effective 
implementation of provisions of the treaties listed in the regulation; 
9. Develop specific indicators to measure a country’s progress or lack thereof in the 
implementation of the conventions, including, among others, the implementation of treaty 
bodies’ decisions on individual complaints; 
10. Besides findings by UN, ILO and other bodies, take into account civil society’s findings and 
recommendation, as well as possible European Parliament resolutions, in the compilation of 
the scoreboards; 
11. Include in the scoreboards and in the bi-yearly reports specific sections on the situation of civil 
society in the country, including reference to jailed or harassed human rights defenders, 
political activists, journalists, lawyers and NGOs. Make reference to European Parliament 
resolutions, statements, communications and opinions UN Special Rapporteurs and working 
groups, and decisions by UN treaty bodies. 
12. Publish, at least in part, the scoreboards, including a list of the specific recommendations 
formulated by the Commission with regards to the effective implementation of the 
conventions and in relation to individual cases of concern; 
13. Extend the strengthened GSP+ monitoring process to all standard GSP and EBA beneficiaries. 
14. Keep the monitoring cycle to 2 years. 
 
D. Withdrawal procedure 
Currently, GSP trade preferences can be temporarily withdrawn by the Commission for all or certain 
products in case of “serious and systematic violation of principles laid down in the conventions listed in 
Part A of Annex VIII” of the regulation (art. 19). GSP+ benefits can also be withdrawn if a beneficiary 
country does not maintain ratification of one of the conventions, fails to respect their binding 
undertakings (such as reporting to treaty bodies) or formulates reservations incompatible with the 
conventions (art. 15).

The decision on whether to trigger the withdrawal procedure is at the Commission’s full discretion. The 
conditions to be met by a beneficiary country in order to prevent the temporary withdrawal are 
communicated to the country authorities only and are not made public.  
During the monitoring phase and the enhanced dialogue preceding the withdrawal, the Commission 
consults with local and international NGOs and other stakeholders to assess the situation in the 
beneficiary country; however, consultations only concern progress or lack thereof in the country’s 
human rights record, and do not touch upon the benchmarks identified and communicated by the 
Commission to the country authorities, nor the scope and width of the measures under consideration. 
Once the decision is announced, the European Parliament or a majority of EU member states can 
oppose it. 
Besides the limited transparency of the monitoring process, which was addressed in the previous 
section, the main weaknesses of the current process lie mainly with its perceived arbitrariness and 
limited flexibility of the options available. 
On the perceived arbitrariness: Human Rights Watch recognizes that GSP is one of the many tools 
available to the EU to promote respect for human rights in its foreign policy. EU institutions inevitably 
make complex considerations concerning whether and what tool to use on a case by case basis. 
Therefore, the assessment of the EU action to address human rights violations in GSP beneficiary 
countries should not only look at the triggering of the measures foreseen in the GSP regulation, and 
should rather be conducted holistically, taking into account possible other action undertaken either 
bilaterally (such as the adoption of targeted sanctions, the implementation of the various EU human 
rights guidelines by delegations on the ground, the issuing of statements and demarches, and attention 
to human rights in other areas of bilateral cooperation) or in international fora (such as statements at 
the UN and support for initiatives to promote accountability).  
However, the EU’s demonstrated reluctance to consider triggering the mechanism even in the face of 
serious human rights violations in GSP beneficiary countries, especially when there is no meaningful 
resort to any of the other tools available outside the GSP regulation, contributes to the perception of 
arbitrariness and can eventually lead to macroscopic double standards. Emblematically, the Commission 
rightly decided to partly withdraw Cambodia’s EBA trade preferences in reaction to President Hun Sen’s 
reluctance to reinstate basic civil and political rights in the country; at the same time, neighboring GSP 
beneficiary Vietnam suffered no consequence despite comparable, chronic violations over the past few 
years, including a considerable spike in the arrests and convictions of peaceful dissidents and the 
adoption and constant implementation of draconian legislation used to repress free expression. 
Furthermore, the deterioration in Vietnam’s human rights record had no consequence on the 
negotiations of a lucrative free trade agreement with the EU, which was signed in June 2019 and 
eventually ratified in March 2020. 
Furthermore, the enhanced monitoring has shown how the Commission may at times 
disproportionately focus on certain human rights violations and not or not enough on others. In a 
February 2020 report on the enhanced monitoring of Bangladesh, the Commission rightly dedicates 
ample space to labor rights, with little consideration for other pressing issues such as enforced 
disappearances and extrajudicial killings.

Limited flexibility: the current regulation allows the EU to reinstate tariffs “in respect of all or of certain 
products originating in a beneficiary country”. It does not, however, allow for a gradual reinstatement of 
those tariffs, be that in relation to all or some products. The process is also strictly time-bound: once the 
procedure is triggered, it kicks off a 12-month period of intensive monitoring and engagement, at the 
end of which the decision is announced, and it comes into force after a further 6-monht grace period. 
Introducing the possibility to gradually increase tariffs against specific, publicly announced benchmarks 
and deadlines, extending the timeline if and as necessary, could help build pressure on governments to 
comply on specific issues, and trigger public debates around their reasons to resist compliance. 
Recommendations:  
15. Publish, at least in part, the scoreboards, including specific reference to the recommendations 
formulated and the indicators identified for progress, as highlighted in the previous section; 
16. Ensure that thorough consideration is given to all the most serious rights violations in the 
countries concerned, and that benchmarks and indicators are formulated accordingly; 
17. Consider introducing the possibility to progressively reinstate tariffs against specific, publicly 
indicated benchmarks and clear deadlines for action.

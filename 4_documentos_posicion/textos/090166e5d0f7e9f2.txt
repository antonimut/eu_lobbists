CEEP INPUT TO THE CONSULTATION ON THE EVALUATION OF THE 
ALTERNATIVE FUELS INFRASTRUCTURE — (AFI DIRECTIVE/2014) 
  
CEEP, the European Centre of Employers and Enterprises providing Public Services and Services of 
General Interest (SGIs), welcomes from the European Commission to evaluate the Alternative Fuels 
Infrastructure Directive (AFID). Since its publication in 2014, this legislation focused primarily on 
private vehicles and lorries, public transport infrastructure was left as a voluntary consideration to the 
Member States; hence, little has been done to set up alternative fuels infrastructure for buses. 
Although some Member States included public transport in their National Policy Frameworks (NPFs), 
quite little was done in most of these Member states, as demonstrated in the Commission Staff 
Working Document on the assessment of the NPFs (2019). For this reason, CEEP, as a representative 
of public transport operators, we call on the European Commission to include public transportation 
and ensure an adequate investment support when evaluating the AFID. 
 
Main challenges for many public electric and alternatively-fuelled buses with regard to the 
infrastructure:  
 
Financing the transition and ensure a diverse finance scheme:  
Both alternatively fuelled buses – especially electric and hydrogen buses – and the new infrastructure 
can be very costly and is still a major barrier to the adoption of these technologies. So far certain 
technologies can only be purchased and managed on a project basis and with additional public funding.  
 
Lack of infrastructure:  
There are very few public charging or refuelling points available to public transport companies. 
Especially for on-road charging, it would be good if cities set up such infrastructure. The same applies 
to large hydrogen plants and bio methane plants, which could be operated by the city.  
E- or H2-Charging stations that have been erected for public transport (i.e. e-busses and H2-busses) 
and by public transport operators must not be opened to third parties without their agreement. The 
stations must be available for public transport operators at any time to guarantee a reliable and 
consistent transportation of passengers.  
 
 
Availability:  
The industry has to offer vehicles and infrastructure in sufficient numbers and at acceptable prices.  
Measures such as mandatory targets or quotas at an EU level will help to ensure manufacturers place 
more alternative fuelled vehicles on the European market. Current wait times for these vehicles are 
long, partly because manufacturers sometimes prioritise markets outside Europe.   
 
Grid capacity and inclusion of Distribution System Operators (DSOs):  
All charging processes of Electrical Vehicles (EVs) have to be facilitated by the distribution grid. With 
an increasing number of charging locations for EVs on the road, it will be necessary to reinforce 
electricity distribution grids in a well-directed and timely way. The impact of the development of the 
recharging infrastructure resp. the increase of the charging processes on the electricity distribution

grid should be taken into account in the impact assessment. It is, therefore, pivotal that distribution 
system operators (DSOs) are included in the early stages in the process of the roll out of alternative

No one-size-fits-all solution for hydrogen refuelling infrastructure:  
As the regulation for refuelling points and storage of hydrogen varies cross Member States, there is no 
clear or harmonised solution and operators must tailor the specification to their own needs based on 
their local situation. 
Local and city authorities, through their Sustainable Urban Mobility Plans (SUMPs), should have a 
crucial role in the planning, coordinating and facilitating the provision of public charging points.  
The creation of one European charging market / EU-roaming:  
Currently, several unfavourable European provisions prevent the establishment of a well-functioning 
EU-wide roaming system for e-charging. All provisions hampering the development towards a 
European roaming system should be removed. 
 
For this reason, CEEP would like to highlight the following recommendations: 
 
1. A new focus on bus charging and refuelling infrastructure  
The AFID should require Member States to set up the necessary infrastructure for buses to enable the 
sector to meet the targets set in the Clean Vehicles Directive. To this end, Member States should 
cooperate closely with the local level for the planning and scaling up of infrastructure for low/zero 
emissions buses. Taking into account the variety of organisational set-ups of who builds and operates 
the refuelling or recharging stations, smart financing schemes have to be established at EU, Member 
State and regional level, if not done yet. These should be accessible to city authorities and transport 
companies, and support not only the set-up but also the operation of such infrastructure during this 
current market uptake phase. As the choice of fuels and charging concepts differ from one network to 
another, targets for such infrastructure points shall not be rigidly set up.  
However, Member States must take ownership of the problem of missing infrastructure and tackle this 
problem. Mandatory targets should be set up based on smart criteria to support a thin adequation of 
charging infrastructures with the diversity of actual and future recharging needs. In doing so, they 
would take into account that infrastructure for buses can be needed both in the public sphere (on-
road charging) and on private ground (depot charging). 
 
2. No changes to the list of fuels  
 
The current AFI Directive establishes a list of alternative fuels: electricity, hydrogen, biofuels, synthetic 
and paraffinic fuels, natural gas (including biomethane, CNG, LNG) and LPG. This list is still relevant and 
includes some of the fuels most commonly used in the public transport sector. As the new Clean 
Vehicles Directive (just passed in 2019) refers to this list when defining what a “clean vehicle” is (Article 
4 (4) (b)), it has to remain unchanged in order to ensure legal stability. Otherwise, the sector risks 
making investments based on the current CVD rules that will be useless tomorrow if the AFID 
paragraph changes. UITP emphasises that the new AFID should leave flexibility to local authorities and 
operators to choose the technology best fit to their needs; it should be as technology-neutral as 
possible and allow for innovation which can support the deployment of clean vehicles in the sector. 
 
3.  Standards for e-bus charging interfaces (vehicles/infrastructure)

A European standard for e-bus charging interfaces is much needed and expected in the bus sector to 
gauge investments and build up fully interoperable electric bus systems where buses (multiple brands) 
and chargers (different manufacturers) are compatible and can work with each other. Under the 
patronage of UITP, the relevant stakeholders worked on an exhaustive bundle of standards - electrical, 
safety, communication and mechanical. Many of the mapped standards are already finalised and can 
be used. Some still need to be created, others needed to be revised or updated. 
 
4. Reporting on public transport infrastructure  
 
With a new focus on public transport infrastructure, and taking into account the procurement targets 
of the Clean Vehicles Directive, Member States should in the future report in more detail on what they 
did to set up the charging and refuelling stations for public transport vehicles. 
 
5. Managing National Charging Points for private vehicles 
In addition, data should also be made available on charging point location and utilisation for private 
vehicles. Both sets of data are crucial for planning a network of alternative fuel infrastructure. Member 
states should maintain accurate and up to date national charging point registries of public and private 
alternative fuel infrastructure and make this data freely available in accessible formats. However it 
must be clear, charging stations that have been erected for public transport (i.e. e-busses and H2-
busses) and by public transport operators must not be opened to third parties without their 
agreement. The stations should ordinarily be available for public transport operators at any time to 
guarantee a reliable and consistent transportation of passengers.   
 
6. Central coordination bodies 
Given the highest density of alternative fuelled vehicles will be in cities, Member States should require 
cities to establish central coordination bodies for their geographic areas to facilitate charge and 
refuelling point installation. These coordination bodies should provide advice, guidance and manage 
data on charge and refuelling point availability. It should gather all the private and public stakeholders 
involved into the development and operation of those infrastructures (including involved DSOs), to 
identify most cost-efficient targets and tools to be implemented. 
 
City and local authorities can have a strategic role, not least through their Sustainable Urban Mobility 
Plans (SUMPs), in the planning, coordinating and facilitation of public charging points. This role is not 
sufficiently considered in the directive, leading to underachievement in view of targets set. The 
Directive should encourage Member States to cooperate with local authorities on introducing charging 
infrastructure planning in these plans. 
 
7. Inclusion of DSOs in the planning process at an early stage to ensure grid capacity:  
The role of DSOs in e-mobility is key as it provides a secure and reliable connection to the network, at 
the request of the customer, thereby enabling to establish a well-functioning EVs recharging 
infrastructure. Furthermore, DSOs are providing not only the connection point, but also ensure the 
quality of supply and managing of metering consumption.  
All charging processes of EVs have to be facilitated by the distribution grid. With an increasing 
number of charging locations for EVs on the road, it will be necessary to reinforce electricity 
distribution grids in a well-directed and timely way. The impact of the development of the recharging 
infrastructure resp. the increase of the charging processes on the electricity distribution grid should 
be taken into account in the impact assessment. 
 
8. Creation of one European charging market / EU-roaming:

Currently, two unfavourable European provisions prevent the establishment of a well-functioning EU-
wide roaming system for e-charging. 
 
- Consumption-based charging: Annex 1 point 10.5 of directive 2014/32/EU stipulates the 
installation of a measurement device with a display showing the charged kWh at every 
charging station. Currently, many charging stations do not have such a display. Therefore, it is 
not legal to charge kWh prices at most European charging stations but only time-based tariffs. 
This unfavourable situation should be addressed as soon as possible since consumption-based 
charging is an explicit wish of the customers. This problem could be solved by stipulating in the 
revised AFID directive that a display on a mobile device is enough to meet the requirement 
(and no display has to be installed at every charging station). 
- European Tax Law. According to AFID, charging electricity for electric cars is defined as a 
service. But according to tax law, the charging of electric cars is seen as electricity supply (a 
good). This has several negative effects. The most important repercussion is that as e-mobility 
provider you would need to have a registered business in every European country. Otherwise 
your customers are not allowed to charge at charging stations outside the country, where they 
have the contract. In the revision of AFID this issues needs to be addressed..

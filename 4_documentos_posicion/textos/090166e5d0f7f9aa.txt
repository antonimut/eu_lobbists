Introduction 
 
  The World Shipping Council (WSC or the Council) is a non-profit trade association that 
represents the liner shipping industry, primarily operators of containerships, vehicle carriers, 
and roll -on/roll-off vessels. The Council’s members carr y over 90% of the world’s 
containerized trade and include several of the world’s largest lines headquartered in the 
European Union. WSC Member companies transport over 40 million TEUs of European export 
and import cargo each year or roughly two-thirds of the EU’s seaborne trade by value; around 
10 million TEUs of feeder cargo to and from the EU’s trans -shipment hubs and 4 million TEU 
of cargo moved in pure intra EU trade. WSC member companies play a pivotal role in 
European transport and logistics. Their op erations and investments extend beyond ships to 
port terminals, warehouses, truck companies and the information technology systems that 
are critical for EU logistics and supply chains. The World Shipping Council is inscribed on the 
EU Transparency Register under number 32416571968-71.  
 
 The World Shipping Council welcomes the o pportunity to offer comments to this 
Public Consultation. In response to the earlier Evaluation Roadmap and Inception Impact 
Assessment WSC provided a formal submission that is attached as an Appendix to these 
comments.  Our earlier response covered many of the questions raised in the current 
consultation regarding deployment of low and zero -carbon fuels and technologies for the 
maritime sector and supply of at berth electric power to vessels in EU ports.  Recognizing the 
relevance of our earlier comments we have appended those comments  for the purpose of 
this consultation as supplemented by the additional comments below in reply to specific 
questions in the consultation.  
 
WSC Replies to Individual Questions in the Public Consultation   
 
General assessment of the Directive’s relevance and scope (questions related  to the 
evaluation) 
 
Question 2:  Currently, the Directive covers electricity, hydrogen, biofuels, synthetic and 
paraffinic fuels, compressed natural gas (CNG), liquefied natural gas (LNG) and liquefied

petroleum gas (LPG) as main alternative transport fuels. In your view, is this scope still 
appropriate in the context of the long -term objective of the European Green D eal to reduce 
transport emissions by 90% by 2050? 
 
 As argued in WSC’s response to the Evaluation Roadmap and Inception Impact 
Assessment, the emergence of near zero and zero-carbon fuels for use onboard a broad range 
of vessels including large transoceanic ships will be critical to the maritime sector’s ability to 
transition to non-fossil-based fuels.  Among the most promising candidates for possible use 
in the maritime sector are green  and blue  ammonia, green  and blue  hydrogen, and e -
methanol.   Blue ammonia and blue hydrogen are not zero-carbon fuels, but they may prove 
to be a necessary and useful transition to green ammonia and green hydrogen.  E-methanol 
may be produced as a synthetic fuel , but it may also prove a useful transitional fuel as  it can 
be used at ambient temperature and does not require special cooling or specialized metals 
that are necessary for the use of ammonia or hydrogen.  The revised Directive’s scope should 
be extended to cover these fuels. While  the system designs necess ary to use these fuels on 
the large transoceanic ships that WSC members operate  are not yet known, it is important 
that they remain part of the planning process to provide for the production and distribution 
of the fuels that will complete the maritime industry’s green energy transition.  
 
 With respect to the vital infrastructure choices that need to be made , it is important 
not to undermine the speed and efficiency of that transition by inadvertently encouraging or 
catalysing the development of infrastructure that (in hindsight) may be considered a mistake 
leading to stranded assets.  Similarly, substantial investments in the wrong fuel infrastructure 
may lead to a secondary, unintended consequence.  Namely, it is important  to avoid 
catalysing the use of a particular fuel type that is later recognized to be an inferior choice, but 
whose use is then prolonged significantly because of the level of investment in the shore-side 
and onboard infrastructure.  These investments can be so substantial  that both private and 
government actors are then reluctant to move to alternatives that are more efficient and 
more environmentally beneficial. 
 
Question 5.  Currently the Alternative Fuel Infrastructure Directive addresses publicly 
accessible fuels infrastructure only. Should it also address infrastructure not accessible to the 
public? 
 
 The infrastructure that is required by the shipping industry is typically found in areas 
that are not accessible to the public. Therefore, the Directive should cover all infrastructure, 
both publicly accessible and not publicly accessible. 
 
Policies 
 
Question 2. Right now, Member States are obliged to establish targets for the roll out of 
alternative fuels infrastructure through their national policy frameworks. However, those 
national targets are being set without using a common methodology. In your view, how useful 
are mandatory deployment targets for Member States that are derived by using a common 
methodology to ensure a coherent minimum alternative fuels infrastructure roll out in the 
following areas: 
- Electricity for short-sea shipping 
- Hydrogen for short-sea shipping 
- LNG for maritime vessels 
- On shore power supply at maritime ports 
- Electricity for port service provisions (pilotage, towage, cargo handling equipment) 
 
 It is importan t to obtain better clarity in what shipping activity is understood to be  
“short sea shipping.”  Are we to define short sea shipping by vessel size, the company, or the 
geographical coverage of the service? Some WSC Member companies operate small container 
ships, but these also operate in some trans -oceanic services so the distances th ey cover are 
significant.  Many WSC Member companies also operate large  trans-oceanic vessels, but 
these are fully capable of carrying out short sea cargo services between EU Member States , 
as part of a longer intercontinental voyage.  However, because of the large power and 
propulsion requirements of these vessels, these are the very vessels for which there are an 
extremely limited set of currently available – or soon to be available - low or zero emission 
fuel options.  Consequently, it would be useful to have greater clarity on what is meant by 
short-sea shipping to provide more considered comments.   
 
 As noted in our earlier comments (attached), emissions generated at berth by many 
ship types constitute a very small percentage of overall carbon emissions produced by a given 
ship.  The latest MRV Report indicates these emissions constituted 6% of total carbon 
emissions.  Of course, ships also generate other emissions through auxiliary engine emissions 
while at berth including NOx, SOx, PM, BC, and VOCs.   If one sets forth to require all or most 
ships to connect to shore -power while at berth, this requires substantial capital investment 
for any ship covered, but it also requires very substantial and expensive infrastructure 
investments on shore.   The utility and cost-effectiveness of these investments run the risk of 
having a limited period of use if one considers that we expect many large ships to move to 
zero-carbon or near-zero emission fuels over the next 10 to 30 years.  If one further considers 
how long it will take to build out the necessary shore -side electrical infrastructure and the 
limited ceiling of what emissions are captured by this investment, it is not at all clear that an 
EU wide programme requiring large ships to connect to shore-power is a cost-effective public 
investment.    
 
 WSC believes the more cost-effective investment would be to intensely research and 
develop those fuels and technologies that offer the greatest potential to transition the fleet 
to zero or near -zero em ission fuels and technologies.  As noted in our earlier comments 
submitted in response to the Evaluation Roadmap we also believe there are significant issues 
and draws backs with prescribing that all ships must reduce at -berth emissions via one 
mechanism – namely connecting to shore -side power.  At a minimum, port electrification 
efforts should be focused on smaller specialized vessels that may be more likely to use battery 
based propulsion systems well into the future because the power demands are relative ly 
small and the potential to recharge on a frequent basis is high. 
 
Question 13. Do you believe that further mandatory technical requirements/standards are 
required to ensure full interoperability of infrastructure and services across Europe? 
 
 The current Directive appropriately identifies the need for technical specifications  
ensuring full interoperability of refuelling infrastructure to be l aid down in European or 
international standards.  In addition, the Directive states that the technical specifications for 
maritime transport adopted pursuant to the Directive are consistent with international rules 
adopted by the IMO.   That will be equally important going forward to ensure a suitable 
alternative fuels framework and refuelling network is established at the EU level.

 The World Shipping Council welcomes the opportunity to offer comments on the Evaluation 
Roadmap and Inception Impact Assessment.  Our comments focus on two areas relevant to the 
commercial maritime sector:  1) infrastructure development  for zero-carbon fuels, and 2) At -berth 
requirements and shore-side electricity.  
 
1. Infrastructure Development for Low/Zero Emission and Low/Zero-Carbon Fuels 
 
 The consultation is intended to identify how revision of the EU Alternative Fuels Infrastructure 
Directive can assist “the market uptake of new vehicles, vessels and aircrafts using low and zero -
emission fuels. ” We understand that goal to include alternative fuels that reduce or eliminate 
emissions of NOx, sulphur, and PM , but also carbon.   An approp riate strategy for the supply of 
alternative fuels needs to balance these goals and support progress to reduce emissions across all 
these pollutants. This requires careful short, medium and long -term consideration, to plan for the 
efficient uptake of fuels  that will satisfy these goals, not least in view of the very significant 
investments that will required to develop a European wide network for alternative fuels.  Addressing 
long-term viability of various fuels from the beginning as part of the policy pro cess will increase the 
chances that we avoid an accelerated energy transition that is later derailed and delayed due to a 
variety of well-intended but short-lived investments. 
 
 The emergence of near zero and zero -carbon fuels for use onboard a broad range  of vessels, 
including large transoceanic ships, will be critical to the maritime sector’s ability to transition to non -
fossil-based fuels.  Among the most promising candidates for possible use in the maritime sector are 
green ammonia, green hydrogen, and e-methanol.  However, there remain several important 
technical questions to be addressed before there can be greater clarity on which fuel or fuels will 
prove to be most practical and cost-effective.   
 
While a number of these fuels have potential for use,  it is important to recognize  that the system 
designs necessary to use these fuels on large transoceanic ships are not yet developed.  Moreover, 
the form of the fuel (liquid or gaseous) remains a significant consideration due to issues surrounding 
energy density (particularly important to large ships that must travel thousands of miles before 
refuelling) and the material science requirements for both onboard and onshore storage and handling.  
These challenges, and whether they will be successfully resolved , contribute to uncertainty in the 
planning of infrastructure of zero -carbon fuels.  As noted in Section C of the Preliminary Assessment 
of Expected Impacts, there are significant financial risks with installing alternative fuel infrastructure 
because of the ongoing uncertainty that it will not serve those fuels that emerge as the most important 
energy transformation pathways. 
 
 Consequently, any policies in the revised Alternative Fuels Directive need to be fully cognizant 
of these uncertainties and should avoid catalysing development of alternative fuel infrastructure that 
may have a very brief lifespan, or limited air quality benefits.   In addition, the fuel infrastructure plan 
policies should be developed with a measure of flexibility that would facilit ate a rapid reorientation  
in the event that an alternative  fuel is overtaken by other  fuels that offer more dramatic  reductions 
in carbon and criteria air pollutants like NOx and PM.    
 
While shore-side fuel infrastructure and production of green fuel  stocks will be critical to a 
successful and efficient energy transition, we may  undermine the speed and efficiency of that 
transition if one inadvertently encourages or catalyses the development of infrastructure that (in 
hindsight) may be considered a m istake leading to stranded assets.  Alternatively, substantial 
investments in the wrong fuel infrastructure may lead to a secondary, unintended consequence.  In 
short, we should avoid catalysing the use of a particular fuel type that is later recognized to  be an 
inferior choice, but whose use is extended significantly because the level of investment in the shore -
side and onboard infrastructure is so substantial that both private and government actors are 
reluctant to move to alternatives that are more efficient and more environmentally beneficial. 
 
Because of the uncertainty about which zero carbon fuels and technology systems will 
ultimately prove workable, it is critical to undertake and support the technical work necessary to 
narrow the field of what fuels and technologies offer the most promise.  Addressing long-term viability 
of various fuels from the beginning as part of the policy process will increase the chances that we 
avoid an accelerated energy transition that is later derailed and delayed due to a variety of well -
intended but mistaken investments.  
 
Interdependency   WSC very much agrees with the Commission’s recognition of how 
important interdependent energy demand is to provide the financial justification for the scale of 
investment required.   The World Bank has estimated that investments ranging from 1.3 – 1.8 trillion 
Euros will be necessary in the next 30 to 50 years to facilitate a transition to zero carbon fuels.  Most 
of that investment will need to occur on land as production of green fuel stocks and the development 
of associated distribution and storage infrastructure dominates the field of needed investments.   
Most experts recognize that maritime fuel demand alone may be inadequate to justify the needed 
capital investment.  This not onl y applies to the capital investment required for production, but also 
to that needed to establish local and regional supply.  Consequently, we need to look at the 
development of low-emission and zero-carbon fuels for use across a wide spectrum of industries and 
other users to demonstrate the aggregate demand needed to motivate investments. 
 
Building on the preceding point, it is important to recognize that alternative fuels that have 
the greatest potential to succeed in the maritime sector are those fuels t hat  have, or will have the 
potential to be available on a global basis and at sufficient volume.  For this reason, we encourage that 
the evaluation to be undertaken needs to consider the availability of alternative fuels that are 
available today as well as the potential availability of future candidate fuels (such as green ammonia 
or green hydrogen, e-methanol, etc.) on a global scale.  
 
2. At-Berth Requirements /Shore-Side Electricity for Ships at Berth 
 
 Section A of the assessment makes note of “the need to oblige ships to use shore -side 
electricity to effectively reduce the impact of ships on local air pollution in port cities.”   WSC agrees 
that at-berth emissions contribute to local air pollution inclu ding NOx, sulphur, and PM.  We would 
recommend that improvements to reduce emissions generated by ships while at berth be addressed 
through an emission goal as opposed to prescribing that all or most ships shall achieve this goal by 
connecting to shore-side electrical power.   
 
 The reasons for this are multiple.  Based on extensive experience in California we know that 
the design and construction of electrical power vaults across a port complex is an extremely 
complicated and massively expensive undertaking.  Secondly, the demands to coordinate these capital 
improvement projects across both large and small ports in the EU would require years of planning and 
construction, accompanied by detailed rules outlining the respective obligations of shore -side 
terminals, the port authority, and the ships using the shore-side electrical vaults.  Moreover, the local 
electrical utility must examine distribution trunks, sub -stations, and other grid infrastructure to 
accommodate the very substantial electrical loads required by large ships.  During this time, it is very 
likely that a large percentage of ships visiting EU ports will have transitioned to one or more zero -
emission fuel systems such as green ammonia, hydrogen, e -methanol, or other attractive fuel / 
technology s ystems.  In this scenario, a massive shore -side electrification investment programme 
would result in a situation where the electrical infrastructure is vastly under-used or simply irrelevant 
for many ships. 
 
 For the above reasons, the Commission may wish to consider an approach that limits shore -
side electrical infrastructure to use by smaller harbour craft, ferries, and other vessels that may be 
likely to use battery power in the future.  At -berth emissions from large ships could be better 
addressed through goal-based standards that are more likely to be satisfied using a variety of low and 
zero-emission fuels and technologies.  In short, demand for shore-side power by smaller harbour craft 
and ferries with high visit frequency may remain strong, but a continent-wide investment programme 
aimed at electrification of larger ships while at berth may prove to be a poor investment decision with 
limited emission benefits as larger ships migrate to non -battery zero-emission fuels.   A goal -based 
approach would als o reduce the significant risk, complications, and huge investment burdens that 
would follow a plan to mandate electrical power while at berth in EU ports.  WSC is happy to share 
with interested EC staff the lessons learned and experience gained with the at -berth regulations in 
California. 
 
  Should the EU decide to proceed with a mandatory at -berth electrification programme we 
would stress that the regulations must set forth clear obligations on shore side facilities (e.g., ports 
and terminals) as the success of the programme is highly dependent upon the design and construction 
of adequate infrastructure.  How effective the programme may be is not only dependent on the layout 
and design of the various electrical sub-stations, it also requires legal consequences that will apply to 
shore-side facilities if power -ready ships are unable to connect due to inadequate infrastructure or 
the lack of availability of qualified shore-side connection crews.   
 
 The World Shipping Council welcomes the opportunity to offer comments on this initiative 
and we hope that the Commission will continue to consult with WSC as it further considers revision of 
the Alternative Fuel Infrastructure Directive.

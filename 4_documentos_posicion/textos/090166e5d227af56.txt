WASTE SHIPMENT REGULATION IMPACT ASSESSMENT – PUBLIC CONSULTATION 
POSITION PAPER 
 
 
From a regulatory perspective, the revision of the Waste Shipment Regulation by the European Commission is 
essential to alleviate the obstacles hampering free and fair movem ent of secondary raw materials in Europe and 
beyond.  
 
The issues at stake are instrumental for the recycling industry, such as the reduction of administrative burdens, 
improving the internal market for recycling and guaranteeing an unhampered access to in ternational markets, 
since alike other commodities secondary raw materials are priced and supplied globally. 
 
Derichebourg Environnement is one of the major players of the French and European recycling industry. 
Specialized in the recycling of metals from household and industrial waste streams as well as of end -of-life 
vehicles and electronics and electrical appliances, Derichebourg Environnement contributes for decades to the 
transition towards a more circular economy.  
 
We chose to develop 3 major issues in this position paper, in addition to our contribution to the public 
consultation:  
1. An unavoidable free and fair trade for secondary raw materials 
2. Supporting the expansion of the recovered solid fuels (RSF) sector 
3. The need to simplify administrative procedures  
 
*** 
 
1) An unavoidable free and fair trade for secondary raw materials 
 
Circular economy can only be achieved if there is free and fair trade for secondary raw materials, as they compete 
alongside primary raw materials. In that respect, regarding restriction of exports outside the EU, it is instrumental 
to distinguish between exports of unprocessed waste and those of secondary raw material, which are recycled 
materials meeting industry specifications or acknowledged standards. 
 
We are surprised by the will to reduce or even prevent exports of waste outside the EU, be them exports of waste 
before treatment or exports of processed secondary raw materials. 
 
While we cannot oppose the need to restrict exports of unprocessed waste that have harmful environmental and 
health impacts in third countries or can be treated domestically within the EU, restricting the export of waste 
understood as processed secondary /recycled raw material to countries outside the EU , is a real threat for the 
recycling industry. 
  
The policy presented in the Impact Assessment and the public consultation doesn’t distinguish at all: 
 Waste which have not yet undergone a treatment and is exported outside the EU to treatment or 
elimination plants, and for which we support the restriction for ecologic and public health reasons. For 
those unprocessed waste, a targeted control policy should be implemented and restrictions are 
understandable.

 Secondary raw material which have unde rgone a treatment/recycling process and is exported to supply 
the steel industry, for instance . For the export of secondary/recycled raw material, we would like to

The measures applied in this case would represent a sign ificant risk for all European recyclers. Market saturation, 
a sudden drop in prices and non -existent short -term outlets would plunge many companies into certain 
difficulties. It is a fact that some companies currently export 50% of their materials without an equivalent solution 
in the EU.  It is essential that the economic aspect of recycling is considered. The profitability of recycling, which 
until now has been based on a demand that exists partly thanks to export, must be safeguarded in the interest of 
all.  
 
 If the Commission wishes to keep the recycled materials within the EU, strong financial incentives shall be set to 
support the balance of the recycling industry, and minimum targets of post -consumer recycled content of 
materials in all new products s hall be fixed.  However, such incentives could only be efficient if the European 
market is sufficient to absorb all the recycled materials produced , which is currently not the case, as Europe has 
desindustrialised itself and relies on third cou ntries for so me streams (Turkey  for ferrous recycled metals, Asian 
countries for specific non-ferrous recycled metals). 
 
For instance, the European steel makers do not communicate on capacities within Europe to absorb our European

nor precise recycled scrap volumes targeted.

To avoid further un -levelling the playing field, c onditions related to free and fair trade of SRM shall not become 
more complex than the one framing imports of primary materials within Europe. 
 
Moreover, any unplanned additional supply of waste on the EU market, without the sufficient matching demand, 
will result in decreasing prices for waste materials and could lead to negative side-effects like a strong diminution 
of the collected waste and the increase of illegal traffics.  
 
 
2) Supporting the expansion of the recovered solid fuels (RSF) sector 
 
Regarding options to limit shipments of waste to energy recovery : energy recovery facilities are not equally 
distributed within the EU and MS regulations on energy recovery of final waste are not entirely harmonised.  
 
Energy production based on high calorific waste residues (which have already been through sorting and 
treatment processes) is still under development and recyclers need to have  access to facilities located in 
neighbouring countries to make up for the lack of local plants.  
 
Reducing landfilling and incineration of waste in Europe should rather be achieved through better eco -design of 
products to increase their recyclability and recycled content, and supporting the expansion of the recovered solid 
fuels sector, by encouraging industrials to shift towards a different consumption energy model, relying less on 
fossil fuels. 
 
RSF production capacity in France greatly exceeds national production, and due to the lack of national outlets, a 
large part of the volumes is redirected to facilities in Germany and Spain. RSF must be recognized as an energy 
product an d not as waste. RSF must be recognized as low carbon energy in comparison with other energy 
products (renewable or not) circulating within the EU, taking into account the savings in GHG emissions it allows. 
 
RSF combustion installations meet precise specif ications, and are directly purchased by industrial plants such as 
cement manufacturers, which have even higher specifications. Banning the export of these products could result 
in increased demand for less "clean", or even fossil, energies, and therefore an increase in pollution (fine particles 
and GHG emissions). 
 
3) The need to simplify administrative procedures  
 
Simplifying procedures and reducing administrative burdens is a clear objective of the current revision, in order to 
boost a circular economy. However, some  further measures should be studied to really support the 
secondary/recycled raw materials market, such as the timeframe to obtain the prior consent in case of a 
notification procedure, or certain administrative requirements. Technical aspects are detailed below. 
 
Incredibly long timeframe to obtain the prior consent. The most important obstacle today is the extremely long 
period between the moment a recycling company  initiates the notification procedure and the moment the 
agreement of the authorities is obtained. Most of the time, we have to wait between 6 months to 1 year, and the 
agreement only lasts for 1 year.  We have already had the case of the agreement delivered 11 months after the 
demand, which left only 1 month o f validity to the notification. The reasonable period between the  demand and 
the authorization should not exceed 1 month, or the validity of the notification should be longer.

Stora Enso reply to consultation on the review of the requirements for packaging and other measures to 
prevent packaging waste   
Stora Enso supports the revision of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive to harmonise packaging rules 
across the EU to ensure the free movement of packaging and packaged goods, as well as developing the single 
market for secondary materials. We are supportive of the revision of enforceable Essential Requirements that 
drive design for recycling and reuse. To that end, we would like to provide views on the Commission's 
understanding of the problem and possible solutions.   
 
A. Context, Problem definition and Subsidiarity Check 
The Essential Requirements and other packaging regulations should support the European Green Deal ambition 
of reaching climate neutrality by 2050. Packaging is meant to protect the packaged material, thus reducing 
waste and emissions. For example, in the case of food packaging, packaging extends the shelf life or the 
freshness of the content and reduces food waste. It has been estimated that in the EU every year some 88 
million tonnes of food are wasted, equivalent to 173 kg per person. Not only is this waste but it also contributes 
to climate change. The central goal of EU food safety policy is to protect both human and animal health. We 
cannot compromise on these standards, but we can do everything to prevent food waste and strengthen 
sustainability of the food system. Thus, we recommend taking into account the important role of packaging in 
preventing and reducing food waste and related emissions across resilient European food chain. 
Paper and cardboard packaging is food-safe and hygienic, sustainably produced with renewable and recyclable 
materials that have low carbon footprint. Paper and paperboard packaging are made of renewable fibres that 
can have several lives by recycling. In Europe, 85% of paper and paperboard packaging is collected for recycling. 
Recycling used packaging can reduce its low life cycle carbon footprint even further. Furthermore, renewable 
materials offer a climate friendly option, because after being recycled many times, renewable materials do not 
emit any carbon dioxide when recovered through energy recovery. 
Since the role of packaging is crucial in protecting products and preventing food waste, both reusable and 
recyclable packaging should be considered as part of the solution. In a circular economy, increased generation of 
packaging waste or decline in reusable packaging are not problems as such as long as packaging is recyclable and 
recycled. Thus, we propose that the problem definition should focus on reducing generation of packaging waste 
that can only be recovered through energy recovery or needs to be disposed trough landfilling. This would need 
to be supported by a robust definition of recyclability in the Essential Requirements, industry wide voluntary 
agreements to improve efficient use of material in the design of packaging and measures to strengthen separate 
collection, such as collection targets for specific packaging formats.

B. Objectives and Policy options 
When setting targets and measures, we suggest recognizing varying conditions for different materials and 
packaging formats instead of considering, for example, single-use packaging as one without distinguishing 
between different materials, such as plastic and paper and paperboard, and different packaging formats, such as 
packaging with long shelf life and on-the-go packaging. For example, when considering introducing recycled 
content targets for specific packaging formats; we recommend thinking of using recycled content only when it is 
safe and hygienic, especially in terms of food packaging and food contact materials.  
 
C. Preliminary Assessment of Expected Impacts 
When assessing optimal packaging formats it is important to consider the overall environmental impact over the 
product’s life cycle. Directive 2008/98/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council sets out the waste 
hierarchy while highlighting that when applying the waste hierarchy, Member States shall take measures to 
encourage the options that deliver the best overall environmental outcome. This may require specific waste 
streams departing from the hierarchy where this is justified by life-cycle thinking on the overall impacts. Thus, 
we recommend promoting and protecting optimal packaging solutions and systems with low life-cycle carbon 
footprint considering all the loops when making climate neutral and circular products.  
While refining the measures, quantitative and qualitative assessment of economic, environmental and social 
impacts should be considered for both reusable and recyclable packaging. Thus, we suggest considering that 
impact assessment also recognises varying conditions for different materials, end-uses and markets instead of 
considering single-use packaging as one without distinguishing between different materials. As an example, 
paperboard has continuously demonstrated resource decoupling over the decades by reducing the rate of 
resource use per unit of economic activity. An impact assessment on the differences between single use and 
reuse systems also needs to consider the costs of not making full use of existing systems across the value chain 
from packaging manufacturing to recycling.  It should also take into account the cost of implementing and 
operating reuse systems where they do not exist widely today.

 
 
 
Brussels, 26 August 2020 
 
ECOS response to the inception impact assessment on  
empowering the consumer for the green transition 
 
ECOS welcomes the Commission’s initiative to empower consumers to play an active role in the green 
transition. Despite the clear preference of  European consumers for more sustainable products, the 
absence of reliable information deprives from them of the opportunity to make informed purchasing 
decisions and fails to translate that preference into market demand  and potentially market 
transformation. Therefore, a new dedicated regulatory instrument is of vital importance in order to 
meet the objectives stipulated in the European Green Deal. Such an instrument should establish new 
rules and ensure a minimum harmonisation for the mandatory provision of information to consumers 
on sustainability aspects , while protecting them  against environmentally detrimental commercial 
practices. Having said this, for sustainable products to become the norm in the EU, the primary focus 
of the regulator must be on removing inefficient, toxic, wasteful and polluting products from the EU 
market altogether, complemented with, and not driven by, better consumer information (as is the case 
with the Ecodesign and Energy Labelling frameworks for energy-related products). 
 
Option 0: Business as usual 
• The “business as usual ” scenario is evidently not aligned with the goal of achieving climate 
neutrality by 2050 and of making sustainable products, services and business models in the EU 
the norm.  Consumers today are faced with shortening product lifetimes, lack essential 
information about environmental characteristics of their purchases, and are often exposed to 
misleading or unfounded information at the point of sale.  
• In the absence of specific and uniform EU rules, the introduction of national initiatives creates 
variation in the level of consumer protection as well as the availability of sustainable products 
in the EU,  while also giving rise to  trade barriers . Such national rules cannot be enforced 
uniformly across Member States and are insufficient to stimulate timely change.  
• The EU’s experience with voluntary agreements and self -regulatory initiatives (e.g. in the 
framework of the Ecodesign Directive) clearly demonstrate s that such instruments are 
inappropriate tools for a timely and uniform achievement of environmental and climate 
objectives. Moreover, m ultiple studies h ave shown that the current proliferation of 
environmental claims, marks, labels and logos, even when information is not factually wrong, 
leads to confus ion and biased purchasing behavio ur among consumers, and is therefore  
counterproductive. Similarly, guidance on environmental claims developed under the Unfair 
Commercial Practices Directive does not appear to be effective in tackling the proliferation of 
environmental product signage practices or greenwashing.  
 
Option 1: Amend existing consumer protection legislation 
• Although targeted amendments of existing EU consumer legislation (e.g. extension of the 
reversed burden of proof rules based on the lifespan of products or legislative measures that 
ban practices resulting in premature obsolescence) can have some positive impact, they are

• The existing legislative tools are not tailored or specific enough to set the much-needed

• They should be complemented with a stand-alone consumer protection instrument as well as

Option 2: A new stand-alone consumer protection instrument 
• The new regulatory instrument should establish mandatory horizontal information 
requirements on the key environmental characteristics of products , ensuring that such 
information is relevant, accurate, easily understandable and comparable. It should, moreover, 
comprehensively tackle both greenwashing and premature obsolescence. 
• The newly established  horizontal information requirements should be combined wi th 
mandatory product-specific provisions (e.g. under  Ecodesign and Energy Labelling 
Regulations) that ensure the effective promotion of durability, interoperability, reparability, 
upgradability, reusability and recyclability of products. This should include the introduction of 
ease of disassembly requirements for key parts, unrestricted and free access to repair and 
maintenance information  as well as  to spare parts  and diagnostic tools  to all market 
participants, and a cap on their price in relation to the overall price of the product. In addition, 
new horizontal obligations should be created on the mandatory minimum period of time for 
the availability of spare parts and/or updates as well as the maximum time-limit for spare part 
delivery. Use of software locks preventing repair should be disallowed.  
• Effective methodologies and rules should also be introduced for substantiating environmental 
claims in order to reduce false, misleading or unsubstantiated advertising, as well as to reduce 
confusion arising from the proliferation of environmental labels  (be they first -, second - or 
third-party verifi ed). To enhance the readability and trustworthiness of environmental 
information provided to consumers, tight policing is necessary of environmental claims that 
reflect EU environmental policy ambition such as the waste hierarchy, by establishing: 
o a “pre-approval” system for environmental claims at European level,  
o setting up a public register listing banned claims (which are too general, deceptive or 
irrelevant), and  
o introducing tougher penalties for such claims.  
• Taken together, the legislative instruments should effectively address the whole range of 
relevant aspects which allow consumers to weigh the environmental impact of their purchases 
at the time of purchase: durability  (including information on  the guaranteed lifespan of a 
product expressed in years and/or use cycles as well as software support when relevant ), 
reparability, upgradeability and reusability (including through the communication of an easily 
understandable aggregate A -G reparability score) , content of recycled material, as well as 
broader environmental and climate impacts. Moreover, in order to ensure policy coherence, 
it is important for ongoing policy initiatives (such as the planned regulation on green claims) 
to not result in overlapping or conflicting policy proposals.   
• The legislative instruments should also carefully tackle interactions between, on the one hand, 
environmental information arising from LCA type of methodologies, suited to mandatory 
environmental information on all product s of a similar category and , on the other hand,

Regulating sector specific claims 
The inception impact assessment refers to the objective of carbon neutrality several times. While ECOS 
supports this overall objective, we call for the EU to explicitly ban product claims such as “carbon-” or 
“climate-neutral” if neutrality is achieved through attributing an organisation’s emission offsets to 
products. This would not be in line with international carbon footprint standards (such as ISO 14067) 
and would provide the possibility for companies to advertise fossil-based goods as “carbon-neutral”, 
which is factually wrong.  
Moreover, the proliferation of misleading statements, marks and labels on short -lived products such 
as plastic packaging should be urgently tackled. While aspects relating to some single use items will be 
clarified as part of the Single Use Plastics Directive and its implementing documents, the EU should 
ensure policy coherence and ban claims that contradict such applicable EU legislation. As a result, it 
should not be possible to put an “I am not plastic” mark on biodegradable or bio -based cutlery, 
beverage containers, or any other such items covered by the Directive.  
Furthermore, the relevance of certain claims should be critically assessed against the available 
technology and infrastruc ture where products are marketed . Some claims observed on packaging 
products assume ideal, unrealistic scenarios to support them , as well as catch -all terms for 
technologies that are widely misunderstood by the public (e.g. chemical recycling).  An industri al 
compostable yoghurt pot  should only be claimed  to be  industrially compostable if proximity and 
likelihood of treatment in such a plant at the local level can be demonstrated. A re -sealable plastic 
container should not be branded “re-usable” if the reusable nature of the container relies only on the 
consumer and if the container design does not enable it to perform the function of a reusable product 
(i.e. to be washed, refilled, sealed and reused until one of the parts is permanently damaged as a result 
of multiple uses).     
 
Getting the metrics right 
It is important that the information provided to consumers is both reliable and accurate. In order to 
achieve this, we recommend the following:  
• Challenge the use of PEF/LCA approaches as the only determinin g factor for establishing 
requirements. Information to consumers needs to cover the main hotspots, including 
circularity aspects and those overlooked by LCA methodologies. 
• For single scores and aggregated environmental information, ensure that aggregation does 
not hide important trade -offs, for instance by only allowing disaggregated scores above a 
certain performance threshold to be aggregated.  
• Account for product family specificities especially when extending the scope of existing policy 
initiatives, such as the Ecodesign Directive. 
• Ensure standards and test methods are representative of consumer and product behavio ur, 
and effectively prevent any possible acts of circumvention which could lead to misinformation.

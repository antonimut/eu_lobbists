Policy Hub: Contribution “Consumer policy – strengthening the role of consumers in the 
green transition” 
31 August 2020 
 
The Policy Hub welcomes the opportunity to comment on the European Commission’s 
Inception Impact Assessment "Empowering the consumer for the green transition" . 
Addressing the issues of greenwashing (problem 2b) and the multiplication of labels (problem 
2c) has always been among the priorities of the apparel and footwear industry.   
 
In a circular economy consumers play a crucial role not only through making purchasing 
decisions but also because of their role in repairing, recycling or reusing a product. Therefore, 
the Policy Hub supports consumer empowerment and protecting consumers b y providing a 
harmonised communication framework. Such harmonised European approach to 
communicate trustworthy, comparable and reliable information is missing to date. We agree 
with the Commission's finding that the proliferation of standards and certifica tion schemes 
may make it difficult for the consumer to differentiate the sustainability performance of a 
product and can create challenges for companies that would like to communicate. Also, the 
multiplication of national labels (e.g. the French environmen tal impact labelling  (‘Triman’), 
German Green Button), the EU Ecolabel, along with the proliferation of similar (voluntary) , 
often commercial, labels creates a confusing landscape.  Harmonisation of this landscape  
would permit the reduction of potential burdens and barriers, reinforce the single market and 
help consumers in making informed sustainable purchasing choices.  
 
We share with the European Commission the belief that all parts of the value chain have a 
key role to play in promoting more circular consumption models. This means better informing 
consumers on the relevance of a product's footprint, as well as developing with the value 
chain innovative engaging ways to present this information to them . The latter is a crucial 
factor to influence consumption patterns. Over the past decade the industry has continuously 
worked on and invested in defining the tools and methodology to effectively communicate to 
consumers a s well as  tested communication vehicles as part of the Sustainable Apparel 
Coalition’s Higg Index transparency  work. The Higg Index was notably recognised by the 
European Parliament in 2016 as part of the Report on the EU Flagship initiative on the 
garment sector (2016/2140(INI)). It def ines effective public communication as being easily 
accessible, clear, complete, comparable, and credible. 
 
We believe that four principles need to be met in order to enable effective consumer -facing 
communications:  
• Trustworthy and reliable data needs to be collected throughout the value chain to 
allow for comparison; 
• A common methodology should be available and easily accessible for all companies in 
order to substantiate green claims they want to make; 
• A harmonised regulatory framework is needed that allows for the effective 
communication of sustainability information to consumers; 
• An economic impact assessment is needed to ensure that the future consumer 
protection instrument drives change and that it can be applied at scale.

Our Recommendations:  
 
• In light of this  and on the basis of problems identified in the Inception Impact 
Assessment, we recommend the Commission to further explore option 2 “A new 
stand-alone consumer protection instrument” to address problem 2b “vague, 
misleading or unfounded information” and 2c “proliferation of labels” . At the same 
time, further clarification and elaboration on what the Commission is envisioning are 
needed before recommending a course of action to address the other problems 
raised.  
 
• To solve the problem of unfounded claims in the interim and contribute to  creating 
more legal certainty related to problem 3  (“effective enforcement of existing 
consumer protection rules in these areas is difficult“ ), we recommend the European 
Commission to develop a guidance document for the Unfair Commercial Practices 
Directive (UCPD). This would provide clarity on how to interpret the current directive 
applying to claims of 'sustainability in the apparel and footwear sector'. In 
collaboration with the industry, first criteria for communication about product should 
be set, that are more stringent than todays. Existing tools and labelling schemes that 
meet these criteria (e.g. the Higg Product Module - Higg PM and Higg Brand and Retail 
Module - Higg BRM) should be listed in the Annex to the guidance document . Using 
these criteria in consumer communication have the potential to drive informed  
decision-making by the consumer and reward companies and products that are truly 
more sustainable . Impact -oriented transparency can accelerate industry 
improvement. 
 
• Finally, we recommend an overall harmoni sation of the European Commission ’s 
efforts across the various services and initiatives targeting transparency launched in 
2020: 
o At a product level with DG ENV’s initiatives on Green Claims and the Sustainable 
Product Policy Framework and DG JUST’s initiative on Empowering Consumers for 
the Green Transition.  
o At brand level with DG ENV’s initiatives on Green Claims, DG JUST’s initiative on 
Sustainable Corporate Governance and DG FISMA’s r eview of the Non-Financial 
Reporting Directive (NFRD).

In the following section we provide additional explanations of the Policy Hub’s asks. 
 
1. A system that enables transparency through traceability across a product’s value 
chain and allows for comparison between actors   
 
The Policy Hub is convinced  that the keys  to empowering consumers are traceability and 
transparency. The transition towards circularity requires the development and scaling up of 
an infrastructure that ensures traceability throughout the value chain. The industry has been 
 
 
 
 3 
working on developing methodologies and tools to manage its global supply chains - taking 
into account both environmental and social impact  (e.g. the Higg Index)  - and remains 
committed to continue advancing this process. However, traceability is only a mean s to an 
end and not a goal in itself : it provides visibility on the way products are made , enabling 
businesses to make more sustainable decisions, along the production of a product. Consumer 
facing transparency should drive change within the apparel and footwear industry. Enabling 
better informed consumer choices and accelerating a circular change within the industry 
should be at the core of the EU’s initiative at the same time. 
 
The Policy Hub calls for public -private partnerships to develop the underlying infrastructure 
for digital solutions that will  enable product traceability . In this context, exploring the 
digitalisation of evidence and standards to ensure verification and assurance would be 
appreciated. The Policy Hub recommends building on existing projects to advance 
traceability, such as the  “Enhancing Traceability and Transparency for Sustainable Value 
Chains in Garment and Footwear” project of the UNECE. Additionally, the EU should mobilise 
the potential of digitalisation of product information, adding to available information and 
evidence-based approaches to define, jointly with the industry, the content and methodology 
behind different solutions, such as digital passports, watermarks and tagging. A joint effort of 
the industry and government bodies should serve the purpose of setting-up an enabling 
framework in this area through new funds, partnerships, and pilot projects.  
 
2. A common methodology to substantiate environmental claims 
 
Claims about sustainability must be substantiated . This is a matter of legitimacy and 
competitiveness for the apparel and footwear sector. We believe the Commission should 
pursue different approaches  for product and brand claims.  The Policy Hub additionally 
contributes to to EU Commission’s consultation on “Substantiating Green Claims”.  
 
For product claims (e.g. carbon saving or self-claiming best-in-class), a common methodology 
needs to be developed to measure the sustainability impact of a product and throughout its 
value chain to ensure completeness, comparability and reliability of the collected information 
in the long term . Such a methodology needs to be scalable for companies of all sizes - from 
large to small and medium-sized companies. The Policy Hub recommends building upon the 
current effort of the EU Commission, civil society and the industry (including SMEs) to develop 
a common Product Environmental Footprint (PEF) methodology for apparel and footwear to 
substantiate environmental claims. Starting in 2023 when this common methodology should 
be available, collected information will be comparable, empowering businesses and 
consumers. This should be the basis for environmental claims. In the absence of this 
methodology we ask the European Commission to acknowledge the Higg Product Module 
(Higg PM) as a recognised reporting tool in the annex to the guidance document to the UCPD, 
as meeting certain criteria.  
 
For brand claims (e.g. company -wide improvement of carbon footprint by 20%) the Policy 
Hub recommends building on the industry’s learning s and efforts.  We recommend to the 
European Commission to harmonise their efforts with other initiatives such as the sustainable 
corporate governance initiative and the Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD). In the 
absence of a scalable and widely accepted Organisational Environmental Footprint (OEF) for

the apparel and footwear sector, we recommend to the European Commission to fast track 
the recognition of existing industry efforts (e.g. Higg Brand and Retail Module (Higg BRM), 
SBTI). 
 
3. A harmonised and standardised communication framework across Europe 
Global consumer research has also proven that on-product communications of sustainability 
information are successful when they are easily accessible, complete, comparable and 
trustworthy. We strongly urge the EU Commission to consider the  digitalisation (e.g. QR 
codes, Data Matrix) of these communication vehicles rather than traditional product labelling. 
It would provide better ways to share easily accessible, complete, comparable, trustworthy 
and, easy-to-correct information with consumers. It would also give brands the flexibility to 
provide more detailed information to consumers when relevant and useful to help them make 
more informed purchasing decisions. Finally, f rom an environmental perspective, an 
electronic labelling would avoid creating extra wa ste in the form of more or larger hangtags  
to accommodate the required information and its translations  in a given minimum 
font/format (since the information should remain legible).  
 
The Policy Hub recommends that the EU develops guidelines for a common approach on how 
to communicate on specific impacts of a product (e.g. CO2 emissions)  - but leave s full 
flexibility for companies to decide on the look and feel of the communication vehicle  (e.g. 
digital). These guidelines need to be developed joi ntly with the industry, building upon its 
experience and lessons learned on communicating with consumers (e.g. Communication test 
for the PEF footwear pilot). 
 
Finally, such a common communication framework needs to be scalable, and work for both 
small and large companies. 
 
4. Carefully assess economic impacts for operators 
The Policy Hub believes that it will be crucial for the economic impact assessment of this 
initiative to focus and carefully consider the additional compliance costs for operators  and 
actors along the value chain. It is very important that the cost of any additional requirement 
is carefully assessed, especially for SMEs, to avoid any disproportion ate burden or red tape. 
This is especially relevant in the context of the economic crisis that companies are currently 
facing due to COVID-19.    
 
ABOUT THE POLICY HUB 
Launched in May 2019 by the Sustainable Apparel Coalition, Federation of the European Sp orting Goods Industry, Global 
Fashion Agenda and their members, aims to unite the textiles industry and its stakeholders in developing and promoting a 
unified European policy framework that accelerates the transition to a circular system for the apparel and footwear industry. 
Between them the three organisations represent more than 300 brands, retailers, manufacturers and other stakeholders. 
 
SAC’s efforts for Meaningful Consumer Communication: 
The Higg Index is a suite of tools to measure sustain ability performance of brands, facilities and products in a standardized 
way. By measuring environmental and social practices, companies can benchmark their performance, identify opportunities 
for improvement, and strengthen partnerships throughout the val ue chain. Over the years, Higg Index adoption has scaled 
throughout the industry. Simultaneously, we have witnessed an increasing consumer demand for sustainability information. 
COVID-19 has only amplified this demand in 2020. 
 
Over the past few years, SAC has worked with its members to understand what type of information they want to share, what 
information and form of communication are most engaging to consumers. It is clear that SAC's approach to transparency 
 
 
 
 5 
must be scalable, engaging, credible, and support equal partnership in the supply chain. Companies have a variety of ways 
they would like to communicate sustainability performance, and fortunately the Higg Index provides a large dataset to work 
with.  
 
In 2021, SAC will launch Higg Index Co mmunication Guidelines explaining what information Higg users are and are not 
allowed to share about their Higg Index results. There will be a variety of claims that may be communicated; however, they 
must all be supported by a transparent Open Data Platform which publicly shares all Higg data that supports the claim being 
made. As part of this project, SAC will also provide a design, methodology, and supporting data requirements for 
communicating sustainability alongside a specific product. Solutions for this "on-product transparency" will be tested in 2020 
and launched with the Open Data Platform in 2021.

Reviving the European economy by targeting specific 
sectors and consumer spending 
 
 
The Covid-19 lockdown measures have caused the first global recession since 2009. Europe is 
particularly hit by the crisis at sanitary and economic levels.  The Spring 2020 Economic 
Forecast projects that the euro area economy will contract by a record 7¾% in 2020 and grow 
by 6¼% in 2021. Growth projections for the EU and euro area have been revis ed down by 
around nine percentage points compared to the Autumn 2019 Economic Forecast. 
 
While the current crisis could resemble previous ones – like the Asian economies during the 
SARS epidemic in 2002 or the H3N2 (Hong Kong flu) pandemic in 1968, with their V -shaped 
recoveries, or the 2008 financial crisis – the exceptionally long lockdown and absence of any 
systemic failure (so far) make today’s situation unique.  
 
As a matter of fact,  “The coronavirus pandemic has severely affected consumer spending, 
industrial output, investment, trade, capital flows and supply chains. The expected progressive 
easing of containment measures should set the stage for a recovery. However, the EU 
economy is not expected to have fully made up for this year's losses by the end of 2021 . 
Investment will remain subdued and the labour market will not have completely recovered.”
1 
 
The challenge now is to revive the economy, and more specifically, local demand, especially 
in the most seriously impacted industries, such as travel and tourism
2, food and hospitality 3, 
and leisure and transportation. 
 
It is, more than ever,  needed to find new tools to boost household spending and focus it on 
the local economic stakeholders who have been most impacted by the stay -at-home 
measures. This role could be played by solutions that target spending, such as social vouchers, 
that can direct time-limited funds on a given segment of the economy. 
 
1. Targeted spending: readily available, economically efficient solutions  
  
1A – Social vouchers: a highly socially responsible success story 
Social Vouchers, whether paper or digital, are social benefits attributed to workers collectively 
by their employers, public or private, carrying a right to access specific goods or services that 
improve their working conditions and facilitate their work-life balance (such as access to food, 
culture, transportation, childcare, holidays…). 
 
Such goods or services are accessible within a dedicated network of merchants and providers 
which is built through a contract-based relation with each merchant/provider by companies 
issuing social vouchers and ensuring thei r proper efficiency, notably by avoiding their 
conversion into cash.

Social vouchers, usually supported by a public policy and sustained by a national tax and/or 
social framework, enhance a virtuous social and economic model while stimulating local and 
national economy to meet a dedicated social purpose. 
 
Public authorities can also adapt social vouchers to distribute welfare to their citizens as an 
alternative to allowances in cash or in kind
4. 
 
Social vouchers have expanded around the world and steadily diversified over the past 70 
years to address an increasingly wide range of needs. More than 80 million people in over 41 
countries now use them worldwide to access goods and services in the food (meal vouchers), 
travel and tourism (holiday vouchers), and farming industries (agriculture vouchers), as well as 
for personal and household services, educational services, environmental products and sports 
and cultural activities. 
 
The system’s effectiveness stems from the fact that it is adaptable to any specific  situation, 
contexts and needs. Indeed, each time, social vouchers programmes are designed according 
to social policies ’ objectives and targets and ensure their correct deployment  by 
guaranteeing citizens local or national access to essential needs or determined types of goods 
and services.  
 
This specificity has been noticed by the European Commission over the years. Meal vouchers 
have notably been considered as means to promote efficiently national macro -economic 
objectives as the former European Comm issioner for Employment, Social Affairs, Skills and 
Labour mobility, Marianne Thyssen, declares in June 2017 while answering a Member of the 
European Parliament question: “The possibility to include meal vouchers in the remuneration 
package of employees is  common practice in many Member States. In general, meal 
vouchers are considered as an alternative to the direct provision of a canteen by the 
employer. In each country, the system is implemented through a dedicated legal framework 
which may provide for more favourable tax and social security rules. This is considered a 
legitimate tool for the promotion of specific economic and social objectives at national 
level”
5. 
 
Social vouchers have common characteristics featuring their implementation, differentiating  
them from money and making them efficient in reaching specific public goals. Consequently 
they can be  implemented in various social policies (to name a few, policies to strengthen 
physical health of citizens with nutrition tools, as well as access to sport and culture, policies to 
support mental health with work- life balance instruments as childcare or domestic services 
vouchers), but share the same principles and specificities.  
 
Main characteristics of Social Vouchers 
1. Instruments valid only in a single State (national use) 
2. Regulated by a specific social or tax framework intended to implement a social policy  
3. Provided on request of a company of a public entity 
4. Carrying a right for an individual to receive goods or services from suppliers having a 
commercial agreement with the issuer 
5. Which cannot be exchanged for money

Social vouchers constitute efficient alternatives to cash grants and in-kind allocations, and are 
featured to avoid two possible pitfalls: 
• Overly broad conditions of use that prevent the measures from being effective ; 
• Overly stringent restrictions that limit a user's freedom of choice, for example by focusing 
allocations on a single product. This can seem paternalistic to some people and the 
specified product may not correspond to their actual needs.  
 
Because social vouchers are never convertible into currency, they guarantee that the funds 
allocated to a given policy are used as stipulated, avoiding any misuses, as can happen with 
cash grants.  
 
The proven success of social  vouchers also stems from the close collaboration among 
stakeholders during their implementation , which is decentralized and incentive -based, 
completely unlike conventional government -backed income redistribution instruments. 
Instead, social vouchers are based on social dialogue, voluntary engagement and public -
private co-financing.  
 
 
1B – Targeted spending: a particularly effective economic stimulus, especially in a recession  
context 
 
The most widespread social voucher in the world is the meal voucher. Initially created as an 
alternative to the canteen, meal vouchers were designed to offer all employees an 
opportunity to have a meal during the working day. They are currently used for instance by 
70% of Belgian and Slovak employees, 56% of Romani an employees, or 25% of French 
employees. Meal vouchers are recognized as essential tool, both for employees, who enjoy 
better quality of life during the working day and increased purchasing power, and for public 
authorities, who are using them to improve the effectiveness of social policies and support 
sustainable recovery in the local food services industry.   
 
As a matter of fact, t argeted spending, notably through the use of social vouchers,  quickly 
increases consumption, with direct and indirect knock -on effects on the local economy that 
have already been measured:  
 
• The main direct impacts depend on how the system is tailored to the defined 
geographical region, the intended population and the network of service providers. 
Benefits for participating merchants and service providers include more regular 
revenue, sustained growth, repeat business and guaranteed reimbursement.  
 
Local merchants have observed  the knock -on effect of targeted spending, which 
stimulates demand and leads to additional spending by the beneficiary. In the case of 
meal vouchers, this knock-on effect is estimated at more than twice the initial voucher 
value. 
 
This assertion is valid for all social vouchers regardless the sector they are targeted to.

2. Reviving the economy through innovative targeted spending: expanding existing 
mechanisms and innovating to support the most impacted sectors 
 
2A – Channeling demand as a prompt solution for reviving the economy 
 
Social vouchers have now transitioned from paper to plastic cards to electronic solutions and 
mobile phone apps, which enable the fast, mainstream deployment of new, highly adaptable 
earmark mechanisms offering guaranteed transparency and traceability. 
 
New forms of targeted transaction s have seen phenomenal growth. In France , d igital 
platforms made it possible to roll out solutions for Action Logement in less than six months. As 
lockdowns and social isolation sp read, the travel and tourism industry urgently issued 
compensation vouchers to stranded travelers. In the United Kingdom , social vouchers to 
replace lunches for disadvantaged schoolchildren were distributed in just a few days. New 
prepayment services, which provide financing for restaurants before they are allowed to 
reopen, appeared within a few weeks. 
 
Particularly in the current situation, the adaptability and speed of digital earmarking solutions 
are proving to be powerful sources of stability. In this way, they are helping to cushion some of 
the impact of the crisis by underpinning the sustainability of industries that are essential to the 
local economy and community life. 
 
Furthermore, c hanneling demand to a specified economic sector could support stimulus 
policies by encouraging consumption in specific sectors , like food services and tourism, that 
have been hardly hit by the lockdown and are now suffering the economic consequences.

Other examples of relevant social vouchers practices to support frailed population :  
 
• Since 1992, the French Red Cross has been distributing food vouchers as an

• In Romania, a solution is supporting the social inclusion of Roma children  by

• Since 2013, a solution has been providing 500,000 farmers with access to fertilizers, 
with the goal of increasing local food security by improving farm yields (by around 
18% one year after the farmer joins the program) in several countries in Africa.

Boosting the path to a more sustainable framework while relaxing lockdown: the example of 
the Ecovoucher 
 
The Ecovoucher is a social voucher that has been launched in Belgium in 2009, in a double 
crisis situation: in the aftermath of the economic crisis, citizens where in economical difficulty 
with low purchasing power, and the country was facing a political crisis with no government 
to act. In this context, the National Labour Council (gathering social partners) creates the 
Ecovoucher with the threefold objective to i ncrease purchasing power of  workers, to 
encourage sustainable consumption and to boost local economy. 
• Today, the Ecovoucher is distributed to 1.6 million Belgian employee beneficiaries 
(35% of the working population) working for 80,000 different employers. They drive 
consumption in 10,600 affiliated merchants and support 1,300 jobs. 
• They represent €242 million in business volume in 2017. 
• 49% of purchases made with Ecovoucher generate additional carbon savings, which 
would not have occurred otherwise. 
• Since the European Commission's 2012 consultation on sustainable consumption and 
production, the Ecovoucher has been regularly stated as exemplary by the European 
Institutions, and in November 2019, it has been selected as a good practice by the 
European Stakeholder Platform on Circular Economy (a joint initiative of the European 
Commission and the European Economic and Social Committee).

2B – New targeted allocations for emergency lockdown situations 
 
As recently demonstrated by the various ways that social vouchers have been used in 
response to crisis situations, it is possible to quickly and easily broaden the types of goods and 
services that can be purchased with an increasingly innovative array of targeted expenditure 
media. 
 
Today, perhaps the most striking example concerns the food vouchers that were recently  
introduced for disadvantaged children in the United Kingdom . It took only a few days to 
respond to the UK government's request to create the vouchers in the form of scannable QR 
codes that can be used in a network of participating food stores. The solution provides more 
than one million schoolchildren with a weekly allowance of £15 to purchase a balanced lunch 
to replace the free school lunch they would normally eat, all without diminishing their family's 
purchasing power. A similar system was deployed in Chile in 2008 at the request of the Ministry 
of Education.  
 
Similarly, in Italy, childcare social vouchers financed by local provincial governments were 
quickly introduced for people working at home and for people and caregivers providing 
essential services, to keep the country functioning during lockdown.  
 
Now t here is talk of using similar social vouchers and traceability tools to support the 
distribution of certain essential products, such as protective masks or Covid-19 tests, as social 
isolation measures are gradually lifted. 
 
 
Conclusion 
  
The depth of the looming recession and the challenges posed by the recovery underscore the 
urgent need to provide new solutions capable of supporting faster, more efficient local 
spending and improving visibility for all economic stakeholders. 
 
If certain sectors of the local economy, such as tourism or food services, are going to survive, 
they will have to reinvent themselves. This transformation can be effectively supported by tools 
that could drive a virtuous consumer spending cycle by channeling revenue s. By giving the 
national government and local public authorities a way to direct spending to the segments 
most impacted by the sanitary crisis, these solutions can act as a lever to support an ambitious 
public stimulus policy, focused on rebuilding local economies in a commitment to social, 
economic and environmental sustainability. 
 
New systems inspired by social vouchers, which have proven their effectiveness for more than 
half a century around the world, should be added to the body of solutions availabl e to give 
governments and companies the resources they need to initiate and sustain the recovery. 
 
This unprecedented crisis represents an  historic opportunity for governments to revive the 
economy quickly, impactfully, temporarily and precisely by targeting outlays, in a powerful 
electroshock of confidence and consumer spending.

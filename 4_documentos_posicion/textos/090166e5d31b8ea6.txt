FoodDrinkEurope proposal for a harmonised framework for 
the voluntary use of the PEF in environmental product claims 
 
The PEF has been adopted as reference methodology for product environmental footprinting by the 
EU Commission, members states and relevant stakeholders in 2018. It has been co -driven by the 
food an drink industries, and so far, encompasses category rules for 8 relevant sub-sectors.  
This new methodology and its policy framework will ensure credible and reliable information to the 
consumer and thus prevent greenwashing. 
Building on previous FoodDrinkEurope reflections around the PEF methodology and its testing1, the 
current paper clarifies the current demands of FoodDrinkEurope for the policy framework in which it 
should be placed. In parallel an additional reflection paper on the use of environmental information 
has also been developed. 
Building on FoodDrinkEurope’s input to the European Commission public consultation on 
Environmental product policy in the circular economy framework , you will find below 
FoodDrinkEurope potential suggestions for the use of the PEF on environmental claims. 
1. Rules for use of the PEF 
• The policy framework should ensure that use of PEF will be voluntary with mandatory conditions.    
• The use of data on the Environmental performance of a product on quantitative a basis must 
remain as a voluntary exercise. 
• Whenever quantitative information on environmental performance of a product is used  (ie., 
communicated, required…)  by an actor of a product  supply chain or a related stakeholder  
(including or required for public purchase)  in the EU market , the PEF should be the reference 
methodology to calculate such data/quantitative information2. 
• The use of the PEF in the context described above must be standardised in an EU policy 
framework. Such framework should provide the tools on how to translate PEF results to 
communicable data along the chain and stakeholders. (ie. percentages, aggregation, indexes, 
indicators per impact category, hierarchisation of data per impact categories…)

• The policy framework based on the PEF should be developed with the support of interested 
industry stakeholders. 
• The policy framework must ensure that the use of data/quantitative information following the PEF 
methodology is verifiable, credible, scientifically reliable, comprehensive, clear, not misleading, 
and transparent,  be it linked to a PEFCR or not (Product Environmental Footprint Category 
Rules). 
• The policy fra mework should acknowledge and support additional category rules for other 
sectors.  
• The use of primary data, whenever possible, should be favoured by the future policy framework. 
Primary data has a major impact on detail of results and improvement visible over time for the 
companies using the PEF methodology.  
• The policy framework should be flexible and represent all possible ways of communicating 
quantitative environmental information existing in the value chains  today. The variety and 
complexity of food and drink products imply both significant initial cost and ongoing expenditure 
to perform environmental footprinting, which is  particularly burdensome when products are 
reformulated and new products are developed  or for SME’s. A flexible framework which would 
allow companies to decide what is most appropriate for their individual circumstances would be 
advisable.  
2. Verification of data provision 
• Whenever quantitative information on environmental performance of a product is communicated 
or required for public purchase, the correct use of the PEF methodology according to the policy 
framework and provision of data according to the PEF methodology sha ll be controlled by the 
relevant market surveillance authority at national level in a full and transparent way .  
3. Uses of the PEF methodology 
• The policy framework should support the use of PEF as a diagnostic tool for identifying hotspots, 
promoting continuous improvement or provide product information to consumers 
• The policy framework should always take into account and promote innovation to improve the 
performance of products, including their environmental performance along the life-cycle. 
• The policy framework should not provide indications on the comparison of one product against 
another. Existing data gaps and benchmark approach concerns in PEF do not allow for fair 
product comparison and risk misleading and confusing consumers, particularly if it is done in an 
overly simplistic way.   
• The communication of any results to consumers or other stakeholders should remain voluntary 
with mandatory conditions of use . In terms of communication vehicles , given the high 
diversity of products and actors along the production chains, operators should be able to use the 
means and format of communication that is the most suitable and effective to support informed 
choices by the recipient of the information, in cluding the use of digital technologies.  The policy 
framework should therefore not prescribe any favoured communication tool.

FoodDrinkEurope views on environmental 
information 
Over the last decades, the European food and drink industry has been a responsible and 
constructive leader in addressing the economic, social, and environmental pillars of sustainability in 
the food chain. In view of today’s growing environmental and societal challenges, and as a major 
contributor to the EU economy and the global food system, the industry is strongly committed to 
further engage in a swift transition towards more sustainable food systems3 in line with the UN SDGs. 
The food and drink industry will increase its engagement in anticipating changes in consumer and 
societal expectations alongside changing regulatory frameworks. It will remain a trusted partner of 
EU decision-makers in the definition of the policy framework for future -proof integrated sustainable 
food systems. 
Bearing this in mind,  FoodDrinkEurope supports a common European approach to what 
sustainability information about food products should be communicated to consumers and how. 
About 80% of the EU citizens are buying “environmentally friendly” products at least occasionally 4. 
However, defining an “environmentally friendly” product is extremely complex. Today there are over 
450 environmental labels spanning 25 industry sectors in the marketplace internationally, with about 
100 in Europe5. These are all based on different datasets and calculation methods.  
This proliferation of different labels  certainly encompasses certain risk of unclarity and confusion. 
Environmental information is already used at all stages of the supply chain and towards the 
consumers in a variety of ways (applications, digital platforms, websites). So far, no scientific study 
about the effective ness of environmental information to achieve further improvement of the 
environmental performance of the product or chain (at different stages or with regards to specific 
types of products) have been carried out.  
Over the years, the food and drink sector has been committed to provide reliable information and 
push for the development of a harmonised methodology of measurement and information adapted 
to the food chain. For example, the food and drink sector has been an active part of establishing the 
Commission initiative against ‘greenwashing’ in the context of environmental footprint initiative.   
 
In this, FoodDrinkEurope has actively supported the development of a European approach to the 
calculation of the environmental footprint of products.

The EU Product Environmental Footprint (PEF) initiative , in which the food industry has collaborated 
strongly via 11 food and beverage product categories , puts Europe at the forefront of international 
thinking and action in the environmental sustainability are(n)a. Based on this method, the EU now 
needs to establish a harmonised policy framework on how to make  standardised environmental 
information available. 
Building on previous FoodDrinkEurope reflections around Environmental 
Information6,FoodDrinkEurope would like to propose a set of fundamental principles which should 
underpin a n EU approach to the provision of environmental information about  the chain and  to 
consumers.  
Key principles 
1. A harmonised approach: 
­ FoodDrinkEurope is in favour of a harmonised approach in the context of a voluntary 
framework with mandatory conditions. This would secure the integrity of the Single Market 
and be in line with an open EU international trade approach.  
­ A voluntary framework with mandatory conditions leaves  Food Business Operators (FBOs) 
free movement, while assuring the credibility of the system. 
­ The harmonised rules need to be scientifically sound and adaptable to all types of food 
products and companies (i.e SME’s). 
­ Future developments of the information framework need to be sound and enforceable by 
industry.  
­ Such a scientifically based approach would show the global leading role of the EU on 
environmental action. 
2. Scientifically grounded, comprehensive standardised footprinting methodology:  
­ The environmental information used in footprinting needs to be based on verifiable, credible, 
scientifically reliable, comprehensive, clear, not misleading, and transparent data.   
­ Any approach to the provision of environmental information to consumers needs to be based 
on a robust, standardised, common methodology to calculate the environmental footprint of 
a product. 
­ The assessment of the environmental footprint needs to be based on a wide range of 
indicators (e.g not only focusing on carbon/ GHGe to prevent negative trade -offs in other 
environmental areas). 
­ The PEF  and its foundation in Life Cycle Assessment (LCA),  is the only scientific basis 
existing at the moment, in addition, that has been approved by Commission , MS, and third 
parties as the EU methodology to assess environmental impacts. 
3. A responsible and wide consumer choice:  
­ The EU food industry provides a wide range of different products to allow consumer choice. 
Environmental information triggers consumer interest but it is not the main driver for 
purchasing decisions.

­ To safeguard credibility a harmonised voluntary system needs to have clear rules and should 
be only applied where feasible and relevant. 
­ To enable meaningful consumer choice, any type of information provided needs to reflect the 
complexity of the food product and its production chain/process. The use of environmental 
information sh ould also not be oversimplified. The PEF methodology allows meaningful 
information about improvement of performance of a product in time, and environmental hot 
spots in the chain, but cannot be used for comparison between product categories. 
­ Benchmarking a category rule for a product is not yet methodologically solid enough to avoid 
misleading consumers. In addition it does not provide significant information to the consumer. 
­ In the PEF methodology, product categories and subcategories are defined to help f raming 
the rules for comparability and improvement over time inside a specific category.  Thus, the 
comparability between product categories is not possible in this methodology. 
4. Valuable consumer information: 
­ Valuable consumer information needs to enable consumers to make responsible and 
confident decisions. This means that information needs to be  understandable to informed 
consumers and unequivocal from a scientific perspective. 
­ That’s why simplicity should no t mean simplistic. Data underpinning the information that is 
conveyed to the consumer is very complex as is the case for environmental footprinting and 
environmental information.  
­ The provision of environmental information to consumers should not come at the expense of 
other relevant information thus the provision of environmental information should be left to 
the business operator. The best way of informing consumers will most probably be on 
alternative channels such as websites and other digital applications  
­ In the Food and drink sector,  the organic label provides the consumer with a certain degree 
of  environmental information. Product environmental information for food and drink products 
should complement and not compete  with the organic label.  
­ It is also necessary to avoid overburdening the consumer with an excessive amount of 
information, which risks creating confusion rather than enabling more informed choices. 
­ In this regard, space limitations on pack also need to be recognised and alternative 
information channels to product labelling, particularly digital platforms, need to be explored 
fully. 
 
FoodDrinkEurope is already reflecting on a policy framework for the use of PEF and would be happy 
to provide further details on this topic.

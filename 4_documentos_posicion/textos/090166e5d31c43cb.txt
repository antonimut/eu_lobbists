step, but the EU’s approach could be more ambitious
 
 
Zalando’s

From

would

and
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
comparable

what

​

The
capacity
to
engage
consumers
is
key
for
a
shift
to
purchases
of
more
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
sustainable products, it implies varying levels of information.
 
 
Zalando’s
research
highlights
three
challenges
to
shift
fashion
purchasing
habits
towards
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
more sustainable products.
 
 
 
First,
sustainability
is
an
important
criteria
for
customers,
but
it
comes
after
style,
price
and
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
size/fit.
 
 
 
Second,
lists
of
characteristics
are
not
adequate
to
drive
meaningful
change
in
fashion
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
purchasing
habits.
Our
customers
want
simple
relevant
information
and,
on
the
contrary,
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
providing
them
with
the
maximum
amount
of
information
can
lead
to
lesser
trust:
customers
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
feel overwhelmed and hence are wary this could be “greenwashing”.
 
 
 
Third,
engagement
is
crucial
to
close
the
“consumer
gap”
between
customers’
interest
in
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
sustainable
fashion
products
and
what
they
actually
buy.
Our
customers
engage
differently:
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
some
are
ready
to
invest
the
time
and
want
details
about
a
product's
characteristics,
others
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
simply want to engage and “buy sustainably”.
 
 
 
These
challenges
aren’t
new
or
specific
to
the
fashion
sector.
However,
fashion
products
are
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
specific
in
representing
discretionary
spending.
In
an
overly
simplistic
comparison,
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
consumers
looking
for
the
latest
fashionable
t-shirt
are
not
going
to
spend
as
much
time
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
comparing
options
as
when
buying
a
fridge.
However,
if
presented
with
alternatives
and
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
comparable information they may actually choose a more sustainable item.
 
 
These
varying
degrees
of
engagement
imply
different
layers
of
information.
​
Zalando,
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
the
SAC
and
several
of
its
members
tested
this
hypothesis
as
part
of
the
project
on
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
“On-Product
Transparency”
in
2020
for
the
Higg
Index.
It
will
ultimately
give
the
possibility
for
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
customers
to
make
fashion
and
sustainability
choices
at
the
same
time.
First
quantitative
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
testing
results
with
close
to
10,000
customers
show
this
is
the
right
approach
to
reconcile
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
our customers’ sustainability aspiration and their actual purchasing attitude.
 
 
Based
on
this
research,
two
policy
implications
are
worth
considering,
in
our
view.
First,
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
greater
sustainability
in
fashion
needs
to
consider
a
large
number
of
relevant
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
attributes
​
(compared
to
electricity
use
or
water
use
by
household
appliances
for
example).
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
These
are
a
full
range
of
social
and
environmental
impacts
throughout
each
stage
of
the
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
supply
chain,
from
raw
material
production
to
manufacturing,
sale,
use
and
end-of-use.
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Second,
​
this
information
would
be
best
shared
in
a
flexible
manner
​
,
rather
than
be
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
shared
all
at
once
(ie.
in
a
“layer
1”
of
information,
such

a
label).
EU
rules
should
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
therefore not prescribe how this information should be shared but rather set parameters.
 
 
6.
An EU label for fashion would not bring about the desired impact of continuous
 
progressive improvement
 
 
 
EU
labels
can
certainly
be
helpful
for
certain
categories
of
products,
where
a
simple
binary
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
differentiation
is
the
objective
(“label
or
no
label”).
In
this
case,
a
label
helps
to
differentiate
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
4
 

 
certain
products
from
others.
However,
we
are
convinced
that
for
more
complex
products
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
and
supply
chains
-
such
as
fashion
products
-
a
binary
approach
isn’t
the
most
effective
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
option to encourage continuous progress towards greater sustainability.
 
 
First,
the
complexity
of
simplifying
diverse
measurements
into
a
single
label
will
not
reflect
all
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
the
causes
consumers
care
for.
These
range
from
animal
welfare
practices
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
(responsibly-sourced
down
or
wool),
different
environmental
parameters
(water
usage
or
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
carbon
footprint),
to
preferences
for
natural
vs
synthetic
materials
(as
the
impacts
of
cotton
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
or viscose are usually ranked higher than polyester), etc.
 
 
Second,
criteria
for
labels
are
generally
set
for
a
given
period
of
time.
They
are
“fixed”
rather
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
than
“progressive”.
A
label
therefore
creates
an
incentive
for
companies
to
validate
its
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
criteria
but
not
for
continuously
improving
on
sustainability
and
be
compared
against
peers
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
through
the
use
of
an
industry
performance
benchmark.
On
the
contrary,
​
we
strongly
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
believe
an
improvement
approach
is
the
appropriate
mechanism
to
drive
continuous
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
positive and measurable change in the fashion industry.
 
 
Last,
the
process
of
developing
a
label
would
take
time
and
resources,
so
as
to
be
extensive
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
and
to
avoid
favouring
one
technology
over
another.
These
resources
could
perhaps
be
best
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
used to develop the EU PEF or similar approaches (such as the Higg Index).
 
 
 
7.
Conclusion
 
 
To

Sustainability

Such

-

-

or retailers like Zalando.
 
 
 
Against

“Empowering

green

It

Consultation: Empowering the consumer 
for the green transition  
EFIC feedback  
  1 September 2020  
 
The European furniture industries embrace EU circularity objectives and are already transitioning to 
and promoting circular business models based on reuse, repair, refurbishment and remanufacturing 
of products. A true circular economy can only be achieved through collaboration and requires the 
involvement of many actors, including policy makers, industry, experts, academia and consumers.  
Without the involvement of consumers, who are becoming more and more demanding, closing the 
loop will not be possible. The active involvement of consumers will strengthen demand in 
developing green markets and the green transition will be accelerated.  
 
The Eur opean furniture industries welcome EU initiatives to empower consumers for the green 
transition and our industry is interested in a continued dialogue with the EU institutions on the 
matter. There is a need for a harmonised system at EU level to provide in formation to consumers 
on the environmental performance of products. In this context, we believe that option 2 of the 
inception impact assessment would be the policy option yielding the best results, as it would lead 
to a more uniform framework, more consistent enforcement by the Member States and Union wide-
coordination and harmonisation.   
 
1. Harmonised consumer information concerning sustainability 
 
In the absence of Union rules on key sustainability information for consumers, Member States have 
(or are planning) to adopt specific legislation (see for example French law on Circular Economy of 10 
February 2020). This could lead to the fragmentation of the single market, to blocking economies of 
scale and to diminishing the availability and affordabilit y of sustainable products. Harmonised 
consumer information at EU level is therefore a must, as well as ensuring  coherence between 
initiatives under consumer and circular economy legislation.   
 
Adequate and harmonised information tools at EU level 
An EU -level information scheme on the main characteristics  of furniture products would allow 
consumers to compare different products on the market, make environment -oriented decisions by 
choosing the most durable and resource -efficient ones, and reward manufacturers who invest in the 
quality and sustainability of their products. The information provided must be useful to consumers , 
clear and concise, and contain details such as materials used, health and safety aspects, as well as 
circular criteria (e.g. repairabil ity, recyclability, separability of materials). However, information to 
consumers must not be provided on every furniture component and should be provided in various 
ways to reflect consumers’ needs.  A combination between online information, information in stores 
and labelling is optimal, depending on the circumstance and specific needs. Information to consumers 
could be provided in their own language online and in stores, while symbols should preferably be used 
in packaging, to minimize trade barriers and administrative burdens. It is important to  harmonise 
marking at least in the EU, with the use, when possible, of symbols on products/packaging, as opposed

Need for a common language  
It is imperative that definitions of parameters measuring circularity are harmonised  and set at 
EU/international level, to avoid a proliferation of different definitions at country level , to allow all 
stakeholders to operate under the same understanding and to provide harmonised and measurable 
information to consumers. Information must be truly r eliable and based on comparable parameters. 
As such, EU legislation should provide clear definitions for parameters, such as ‘long lifetime’, 
‘durability’, ‘reparability’, ‘reuse’, ‘recyclability’ and others, and definitions and measurements should 
be based on international standards to the largest extent possible.  
Product passport 
The announced introduction of electronic product passports, tagging and watermarks under the 
sustainable product policy could serve the purpose of providing relevant information  to consumers 
and the digital component will certainly bring benefits. Product passports have the potential to channel 
information between stakeholders in the value chain and if they are designed properly they can lead 
to the acceleration of the green transition and facilitation of circular business models. 
• The introduction of product passports must take place at EU level and their purpose must be 
defined by the EU policymakers, to ensure that they fulfil the desired benefits.  Product 
passports should promote international trade and competitiveness of the EU industry. It is also 
important for the product passports to acquire international acceptance and therefore t he 
system should be simple and adapted to global value chains.  
• These tools need to be easy to develop and not pose additional administrative burdens to 
businesses. 
• These tools should have a pragmatic approach and only provide information that is important 
for the consumer to make an informed choice or for the waste operator. Information provided 
via the passports may need to differ depending on specific needs and may have more than one 
purpose. The information should be limited to what is relevant  and necessary for a specific 
purpose (for example what is requested by the customer).  
• Standardisation bodies and global bodies should be involved in their design, to ensure that the 
information is reliable , to allow correct comparison of products and to enable mutual 
identification by different systems. Involving standardisation bodies will also ensure that no 
barriers are brought to global trade and introduce a harmonised view on how product 
passports should be produced, reported and read.  
• Industry must  participate actively in the creation of such product passports. Generally, 
consultation with all stakeholders must take place, given that the business sector will actively 
use the product passports, enter the information in the system and keep it updated. Business 
knowledge concerning information in the value chain exists and should be taken into account 
to ensure that the tools work in practice and that they are synchronised with other existing 
systems.  
• The design of the product passports must be based o n an impact assessment based on 
scientific grounds. The passports must be based on existing regulations and product legislation, 
such as REACH, RoHs and applicable standards. Criteria that are not based on regulations 
should be based on recognised scientific evidence.  
• Clear responsibilities and obligations must be set for accessing and sharing data and companies 
placing products on the market must own the information and be responsible for its accuracy. 
Confidentiality and competition issues must be addressed, too.

• The t ype of information that product passports could convey ranges from materials used, 
components, health and safety aspects and circular criteria (how to use it properly, 
repairability, separation of components, recyclability). However, the type of information to be 
provided will be defined by the purpose.  
• It is important to find the right level of detail to be provided, given the long and complex value 
chains, to ensure that product passports do not bring additional administrative burdens.  
• The information provided must be adapted to the specific produc t and depending on the 
regulations applicable to the product. Product passports must also be synchronized along the 
value chain and with existing information systems (based on EU legislation and voluntary ones) 
and the connection to these should be clarified (for example SCIP database). Double reporting 
must be avoided.  
• It is important to put in place effective monitoring and control  systems, to ensure that those 
who provide information are not disadvantaged compared to players who may not do so. 
Controls should also be placed over products imported into the EU.  
 
Proliferation of voluntary tools  
 
Many voluntary tools to promote product quality exist today in the EU and a lack of harmonisation in 
this respect leads to the use of local labels which are not the best option for exporting industries, such 
as the furniture sector. The number of environmental labels must be lowered to avoid confusing 
consumers. Applicable labels must have the widest application possible.  
 
Ecolabel and Ecolabel Regulation  
Regulation (EU) 2016/1332 should be subjec t to a  simplification in which non -product-related 
requirements (such as production conditions, occupational health and safety issues) are removed. 
These are either regulated by relevant EU and/or national regulations or can be covered by the EMAS 
requirements. On the other hand, there are no criteria (even though this is a challenge for our 
companies and products) regarding the separability of materials or components. Other topics from 
the area of extended producer  responsibility such as durability, repairability, interchangeability are 
also missing. However, all these topics are important in the Circular Economy context. Data-intensive 
requirements that have no value (bill of materials) should also be removed from the Regulation.  
 
The Ecolabel for furniture has proven to have a rather low uptake, which may be connected to many 
challenges, including that it is state-run, it involves high costs and it is not promoted by certifiers. To 
ensure that the Ecolabel works in practice, industry should have a more prominent role in the future 
review of the criteria and standardisation bodies such as CEN should be given the chance to contribute 
and provide technical expertise as well. Unfortunately , the industry’s considerations were not 
sufficiently taken on board by the competent authorities in the last revision of the Ecolabel criteria for 
furniture. This has led to the significantly low uptake of the label.  
In theory, a label that introduces demands on circularity could be seen as beneficial. However, 
contradictions between the aim to drive circularity and how the circular economy works in practice 
should be avoided. The prohibition of certain products can work against the easiness to repair or the 
design for disassembly, compromising reuse of furniture.

The Ecolabel should not be limited to PEF, as this is not a perfect tool to substantiate environmental

2. Public procurement  
Public authorities must promote products that have been manufactured according to c ircular 
principles and Green Public Procurement has an enormous potential to drive circularity, to contribute 
to the decarbonisation of the EU economy and to the EU’s overall climate and environmental 
objectives. When furnishing public spaces, the final choice should not be primarily based on economic-
driven criteria as this would not encourage investments in green and circular products. In this context, 
initiatives under the new Circular Economy Action Plan proposing minimum mandatory green criteria 
for Gr een Public Procurement, targets in sectoral legislation and compulsory reporting are most 
welcome. When it comes to supporting a real start of circular tendering, there are already some good 
practices to consider which include the reuse of materials, the r ecycling of waste and the use of new 
business models, such as leasing of interiors and furniture. 
3. Digitalisation  
Increased digitalisation has the potential to enable the transition to a circular economy and to reinforce 
the EU Single Market and the har monisation of rules and practices. The announced creation of a 
European Circular Dataspace could lead towards this objective. However, the proliferation of multiple 
databases with the same purpose should be avoided. Digitalisation must not place additional burdens 
on companies and initiatives must be proportionate to the goals to be achieved.

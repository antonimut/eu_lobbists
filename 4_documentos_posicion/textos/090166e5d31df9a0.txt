BAR COUNCIL FEEDBACK ON THE GREEN TRANSITION 
1. This is a response by the General Council of the Bar of England and Wales (the Bar 
Council) to the European Commission’s Inception Impact Assessment ‘Empowering the 
consumer for the green transition’ (the Impact Assessment)1.   
 
2. The Bar Council represents approximately 17,000 barristers in England and Wales.  It 
promotes the Bar’s high quality specialist advocacy and advisory services; fair access to justice 
for all; the highest standards of ethics, equality and diversity across the profession; and the 
development of business opportunities for barristers at home and abroad.   The Bar Council 
is also the Approved Regulator for the Bar of England and Wales. It discharges its regulatory 
functions through the independent Bar Standards Board (BSB).  A strong and inde pendent 
Bar exists to serve the public and is crucial to the administration of justice. 
 
3. The Bar Council supports the overall objectives of the Green Transition and welcomes 
the opportunity to provide its input. Our response follows the structure of the Im pact 
Assessment. 
 
A.  Context, problem definition and subsidiarity check 
Information at the point of sale 
4. It is correct that the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (the UCPD) and the 
Consumer Rights Directive (the CRD) do not expressly require traders to provide information 
on products’ environmental characteristics, lifespan or reparability. In our opinion, it would 
be difficult to argue, in relation to the vast majority of products, that the failure to provide 
such information constitutes a misleading omission under the UCPD (defined, broadly, as the 
failure to provide information that  “the average consumer needs …to make an informed 
transactional decision”). Indeed, the Impact Assessment notes that there is “restricted consumer 
demand” for sustainable products and services , making it difficult to argue that such 
information is required to make an informed transactional decision.   
 
5. It follows that the extent to which the UCPD can be used to affect the green transition 
is somewhat limited. Similarly, information on a product’s environmental characteristics does 
not form part of the pre-contractual information requirements under the CRD.

Commercial practices that cause confusion and misinformation, etc. 
 
6. Insofar as premature obsolescence is a commercial practice (we note that i t remains 
disputed by manufacturers), 2 it is likely to contravene the requirements of professional 
diligence under the UCPD.  However, we consider that this issue could and should be 
clarified, as set out below. 
 
7. Similarly, environmental claims that are not true or cannot be verified (greenwashing) 
are likely to constitute misleading actions under the UCPD. Indeed, the Commission 
Guidance on the UCPD expressly addresses greenwashing.3 
 
Enforcement  
 
8. As noted above, the failure to provide environmental pre -contractual information is 
unlikely to fall foul of existing consumer measures (namely the UCPD and the CRD). As for 
greenwashing, which we consider to be covered by the UCPD, we do not see any specific 
difficulties associated with taking enforcement action against traders making misleading 
environmental claims.  
 
9. Moreover, we query whether it is correct to state that the UCPD can only be used “on 
the basis of  a case -by-case assessment of individual cases ”. We note that the UCPD has been 
deployed by enforcement authorities in industry-wide enforcement action aimed at protecting 
the collective interest of consumers (e.g. the UK’s Competition and Markets Authority’s 
enforcement action against secondary ticketing platforms, care homes, online hotel bookings;4 
and European Commission coordinated action in relation to e.g. car rental companies and in-
app purchases5).  
 
B. Objectives and policy Options 
 
10. Whilst we consider that the UCPD is able to fill some of the gaps identified in the 
Impact Assessment (as detailed above), we recognise that its potential in achieving the Green 
Transition is limited. Accordingly, we support a hybrid Option 0 (Baseline) and Option 1 
(Amend existing consumer protection legislation) approach and wish to make the f ollowing 
observations: 
 
• Whilst we agree with the notion of empowering consumers through information, we 
query the desirability of further textual informational requirements. We note that e.g. 
under the CRD consumers are already expected to receive in excess of 20 items of pre-
contractual information. Instead, we would suggest that consideration should be given

• Although it is arguabl e that premature obsolescence would be covered by the

rarely form the basis of domestic enforcement action. Consideration should therefore 
be given to adding premature obs olescence to Annex 1 of the UCPD (i.e. the list of

• We do not see a basis for introducing other more specific rules in relation to 
greenwashing. Such practices fall squarely within the policy of the UCPD, which is an 
established legal instrument that is well-equipped to deal with them provided that it

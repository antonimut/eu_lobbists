Danfoss input to the European Commission’s roadmap on 
The Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive 
September 2020 
 
Danfoss welcomes the evaluation launched by the European Commission on the Urban Waste Water 
Treatment Directive (UWWTD). As we acknowledge the fact that the directive has played an important 
role to reduce pollution levels in water, we also believe that the directive should be updated to take 
into account other challenges, like climate change, resource scarcity and energy efficiency.  The EU 
Strategy for Energy System Integration offers also the opportunity to integrate the wastewater sector 
as part of urban energy networks. 
 
Wastewater treatment is usually one of the biggest costs for local authorities, as it consumes a lot of 
energy. According to the IEA Outlook from 2016, water supply, distribution and treatment account for 
50% of the electricity demand of the urban water sector. Furthermore, the IEA foresees that by 2040, 
the amount of energy used in the water sector is projected to more than double1.  
 
Denmark has set the objective  to make the  water sector energy and climate neutral  in 2030 . By 
combining energy efficiency and production, it is possible to cut net energy consumption of the whole 
water segment by at least 50%. A few larger water facilities in Europe are now energy neutral, as it is 
already the case with the waste water treatment plant in Marselisborg, in Aarhus. Not only the plant 
was able to cut its energy consumption thanks to installed drives  and digitalisation, it has become an 
actual power station and a biorefinery, producing more than 1 00% more energy than it uses  for 
treating the wastewater. It is the world’s first energy-neutral water service to “decouple” water from 
energy and the first to create an energy-neutral catchment area for 200.000 people. The case was even 
cited by the European Commission for best practice in its 2019 Reflection Paper “Towards a Sustainable 
Europe by 2030”2. 
 
As the roadmap rightfully says, the UWWTD does not sufficiently address the issue of the energy 
consumed by wastewater treatment. Thus, there is an opportunity to tackle it through the proposals 
considered, like energy audit requirements for facilities, recommendations and measures to improve 
energy efficiency and foster renewable energy production for collection and treatment.  
 
We believe that a solution would be to optimise the energy efficiency of the wastewater facilities with 
measures including the wider deployment of variable speed drives, better process controls with online 
sensors, and more efficient compressors and pumps. Additionally, the energy embedded in the sludge 
that remains from the wastewater can be recovered to generate electricity and heat in highly efficient 
CHP plants, as it is the case with the Marselisborg plant. The energy produced can be fed into electricity 
and district heating grids, used as a biogas for trucks or buses or be upgraded and injected into natural 
gas networks. Beside this it has been calculated that, more than 20% of Denmark’s households can be 
heated by installing heat pumps on outlet from wastewater facilities3. 
 
Furthermore,  by recovering t his source of energy, it  prevents that the greenhouse gases from the 
sludge, mostly methane, are released in the air to instead serve a better purpose.  As such, the water 
sector will become part of both the electricity and heating networks  in cities, while cutting our 
emissions. This is how we can implement the principles of circular economy into action. 
 
Therefore, we would like to recommend the following actions, upon the revision of the Directive: 
• Promote that wastewater treatment should become energy & climate neutral, in alignment 
with the objectives of the  European Green Deal . There is a significant potential to reduce

• Coherence with the principles of the Circular Economy Action Plan . For example, s ludge 
components can be reused as nitrogen or phosphor, while treated wastewater can be reused 
for irrigation or industrial processes.  
 
• Acknowledge the opportunities of digitalisation to obt ain greater energy efficiency gains . 
Higher digitalisation will also make facilities more resilient to pandemics, such as the 
coronavirus pandemic.  
 
• Evaluate the state of the market by collecting data on energy efficiency  through audit 
requirements for facilities above 100.000 PE before 2024.  
 
• Consider the treatment of sludge for facilities below 100.000 PE for energy efficiency use and 
sludge handling.  
 
• Address the issue of stormwater overflow , both because of the bathing water quality, 
biodiversity as well as basic pollution. Accordingly, the Directive should demand an overview 
over all stormwater overflows before 2024, including yearly pollution load per overflow, as 
well as to require a 95% reduction of overflow for the 80% largest overflows before 2030.  
 
• Consider micro pollutants removal e.g. pharmaceuticals and micro plastics as an important 
area to address and to set outlet limitations. We believe there is a need for more knowhow, 
concretely on  both the pollution load from wastewater and rainwater as well as  on the 
methods to eliminate pollution.

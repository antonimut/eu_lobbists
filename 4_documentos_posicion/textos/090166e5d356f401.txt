POSITION 
Response to the consultation on the  
Roadmap – Inception Impact Assessment 
Revision of the Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive  
 
September 2020 
 
Aqua Publica Europea, the European Association of Public Water Operators, welcomes the European 
Commission’s Inception Impact Assessment on the Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive 
91/271/EEC (UWWTD). Since its adoption, the Directive has proved to be a useful instrument to 
protect the environment and health, relying on the efforts of European waste water operators to 
remove pollution  from water, hence contributing to the objectives of the overarching Water Framework 
Directive 2000/60/EEC (WFD) and the Bathing Water Directive 2006/7/EC.  
 
As it as been announced that the WFD will remain unchanged, a revision of the UWWTD will be an 
even more important tool to address new (and old) challenges , also in consideration  of EU ambitions 
for a green – and blue – transition, and in particular the Green Deal.  
 
The COVID-19 pandemic has further shown the important role that the waste water sector plays within 
society both for efficient treatment to protect health - and thus benefitting well -being, as is also 
recognised in the United Nations’ Sustainab le Development Goals – and also having the potential to  
support public health efforts through analysis of waste water for virus residue  and early warning 
systems.  
 
The Impact Assessment and potential revision of the UWWTD provide an opportunity to ensure 
collective safety, address new challenges and contribute to EU ambitions. Based on the on-the-ground 
experience of 66 members, public utilities providing services to over 70 million people in Europe, Aqua 
Publica Europea would like to share the following contribution.  
 
*** 
 
1. Only a life-cycle approach to pollution can protect health and the environment in an effective 
way. We appreciate that the European Commission outlines possibilities to tackle pollution along the 
chain, starting with  preventive measures , in line with the precautionary principle, and possibly 
integrating extended producer responsibility  schemes. The benefits of a holistic approach, especially 
for contaminants of emerging concerns, have been repeatedly emphasised, including in the latest 
OECD report on financing water 1, noting in the case of pharmaceuticals : “the most long -term, large-
scale and cost -effective solutions to reducing pharmaceuticals in the environment is through 
preventative source -directed and use -orientated policy measures, early in the pharmaceutical life 
cycle”.

Because waste water treatment is and should be considered as a last resort solution to tackle pollution 
problems, additional treatment requirements should be conceived as part of a much wider approach 
towards long-term zero-pollution, in order to ensure they focus on where th ey are most needed  and 
most cost -effective, when  prevention is not  enough. We also note that so me pollutants, which are 
particularly difficult to remove once they reach the water system, for example, intentionally added 
microplastics, can be avoided altogether  through coordinated trade, industrial, consumer and 
environmental policy.  
 
We therefore encourage the European Commission to evaluate, in its Impact Assessment, how to 
address pollution at all stages  - production, consumption, disposal and finally treatment, and review 
the costs and benefits of the different alternatives  altogether, from financial, environmental and social 
perspectives. Reducing pollution will require collaboration of waste water services but also industries, 
as well as full transparency and public participation and awareness.  
 
2. A coherent  but flexible framework to tackle emerging global challenges . Waste water 
operators are instrumental to the achievement of several of the EU’s key ambitions in a changing 
world. The evaluation of the UWWTD has highlighted untapped potential for  increased energy 
efficiency and circular economy. Throughout Europe, waste water operators members of Aqua Publica 
Europea are alread y voluntarily committing effort s to develop their own projects for nutrient or energy 
recovery from sludge, energy recovery from waste water (see: HAMBURG WASSER ; VIVAQUA). 
Furthermore, conscious of their energy consumption, many waste water treatment plants  are moving 
to renewable energy  and energy production (see:  Scottish Water  generates and facilitates  more 
energy than its electricity consumption , Eau de Paris  produces renewable energy with about 12 000 
m2 of solar panels  on its installations;  EMASESA assesses the viability of using  oranges from public 
roads destined to be thrown away to produce biogas and power a WWTP) .  
 
The wide ranging solutions are also conditioned by the local and geographical contexts . Whilst energy 
efficiency and circular economy objectives are both important , the Impact Assessment  should also 
take into account that, in order for a future Directive to be fit for many years, it is important to consider 
potential trade-offs between different objectives : for example, higher treatment requirements can lead 
to more energy consumption in the plant as well as more polluted sludge that is then harder to 
integrate in a circular economy. Therefore, when political choice needs to be made, decision -making 
should be based on science -based information and on citizen consultation , who must be able to 
access transparent information on different alternatives.  
 
A flexible framework  that allows decision -makers and operators  to assess – also through mandatory 
auditing – the potential of individual WWTP to contribute to energy and circularity objectives appears 
as the most appropriate approach to address the shortcomings highlighted in the Evaluation report 
whilst taking into account and valorising the diversity of situations and challenges on the ground. 
 
3. Financing a waste water sector  that is  fit for decades and fair for all. Investments in waste 
water treatment are high and meant for the long term – 20-30 years, and as the Commission is well 
aware, the estimate d financing gap in the EU reaches €253 billion  by 2030, according to the OECD 2.

At the same time, costs are most often recovered through users’ bills  or general tax . It is thus critical 
that the possible new investments required are not borne disproportionately by households but that, in 
line with the Polluter -Pays principle and fairness imperatives, polluting industries contribut e equitably 
to protect health and the environment.  
 
In this sense, w e welcome the  European Commission’s mention of affordability issues as well as the  
proposal to take into account  Extended Producer Responsibility , which would prevent the indirect 
subsidisation of polluting actors by citizens . A clear and effective scheme applied homog enously 
across Europe would also be important to ensure a well -functioning internal market, thus avoiding the 
risk of ‘environmental dumping’ and also relieving local decision -makers from being obliged to choose 
between economy and environment.  
 
A combination of complementary financing streams should also be considered from different sources: 
environmental taxation opportunities,  green bonds,  contribution of sectors that directly benefit from 
waste water treatment upgrade and increased property value (prop erty owners, tourism, for example), 
EU funding and better prioritisation of waste water financing at national level , which can be supported 
by further EU guidance and reporting requirements. In addition, decisions on new (grey) infrastructure 
need to be proportionate to minimise the creation of  future liabilities. This is particularly important for 
small agglomerations,  where building oversized infrastructure may not be the most appropriate 
solution to solve the remaining issues. Of course, it is paramount that each policy option is supported 
by a full cost analysis that considers the range of alternatives.  
 
4. Addressing t he potential for nature -based solutions. In the face of climate change, and with it 
more intense rainfalls , as well as growing urbanisation, both elements affecting storm water overflow 
and urban runoff, w e encourage the European Commission to evaluate how g reen infrastructure can 
provide efficient, sustainable and cost-effective solutions. Relying on nature to create more permeab le 
“sponge cities”, or promoting vegetalised buildings for example, can be more efficient than costly grey 
infrastructure and fully  aligns with the EU Biodiversity Strategy. Concrete localised examples are 
proving the benefits of solutions that are more respectful of nature, but also emphasise the importance 
of a multi -disciplinary approach that integrates rainwater management in urban planning (see: 
Hamburg’s Risa strategy; Paris’ Plan ParisPluie) and calls for the participation of  all sectors, including 
building or transport, as well as citizens’ engagement.  
 
5. A legislation at the crossroads of many policies. As highlighted, it will be essential to review how 
waste water treatment objectives are affected and affect other policy objectives, in order to prevent 
uncertainties on the ground  and instead ensure complementarity . Some examples of such  
uncertainties have been highlighted by water operators and reported in Aqua Publica Europea’s report 
on the combined implementation of  EU water legislation. Furthermore, considering the parallel review 
of the Sewage Sludge Directive 86/278/EEC, it is important that the Commission ensure s coordination 
of objectives. As previously mentioned, waste water treatment is at a crossroads of many  sectoral 
policies, including energy or industry, which  also calls for working across silos to be able to build a 
robust and sustainable framework, encouraging cooperation and discussions both at EU and at local 
level. It is essential that WWTP are not con sidered the bottom end of our societal production 
consumption processes, but just one of the chain of a circular society.

Aqua Publica Europea welcomes the European Commission’s work for more sustainable and more 
resilient waste water treatment for the next decades. In these efforts, we encourage the adoption of a 
few principles to guide the Impact Assessment:  
 
 Fairness to ensure proportionate contribution of all relevant actors to treatment upgrade  and 
participation of the public in decision-making that directly impacts it;  
 Balance to seek the most cost-effective solutions to protect human health and the 
environment to high levels from pollution by fully evaluating different alternatives;   
 Flexibility to promote the solutions adapted to different contexts and that can work in the 
long-term; 
 Coherence with the range of objectives for a sustainable transition and in consideration of 
interlinked policies.

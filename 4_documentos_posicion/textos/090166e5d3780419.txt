Executive Summary  
 
  The World Shipping Council (WSC or the Council) is a non-profit trade association that 
represents the liner shipping industry, primarily operators of containerships, vehicle carriers, 
and roll -on/roll-off vessels. The Council’s members carry over 90% of th e world’s 
containerized trade and a substantial share of the world’s roll -on/roll-off cargoes.  WSC 
Member companies transport over 40 million TEUs of European export and import cargo each 
year or roughly two -thirds of the EU’s seaborne trade by value; arou nd 10 million TEUs of 
feeder cargo to and from the EU’s trans -shipment hubs and 4 million TEU of cargo moved in 
pure intra EU trade. Roll on roll off cargo accounts for the import and export of over 6 million 
of light vehicles to and from the EU and over EUR10 billion  worth of heavy machinery each 
year. WSC member companies - include several of the world’s largest lines that 
headquartered in the European Union - and play a pivotal role in European transport and 
logistics. Their operations and investments extend beyond ships to po rt terminals, 
warehouses, truck companies and the information technology systems that are critical for EU 
logistics and supply chains. The World Shipping Council is inscribed on the EU Transparency 
Register under number 32416571968-71.  
 
 The World Shipping Council welcomes the opportunity to contribute to this Public 
Consultation. To supplement its response to the FuelEU Inception Impact Assessment 
Consultation WSC would like to respond to a number of questions raised by the public 
consultation. In particular WSC would like to raise the following key points:  
 
 Efficiency improveme nts in maritime transport will not alone enable the sector to 
achieve the long-term carbon reductions target set at IMO and EU level. To do so the 
successful uptake of sustainable alternative fuels is critical.  
 The shipping industry needs the development of and improved access to new low 
carbon fuels compatible with the existing fleet that will still be in operation in 20 years. 
 To reach the more ambitious carbon reduction targets set for 2050, research and 
deployment pathways toward zero and near zero fuels and vessels must be  
established now in coordination with the IMO.

 WSC strongly counsels against mandating certain fuel types or fuel blends for vessels

Q. In your view, how relevant is the uptake of sustainable alternative fuels and diversifying 
the fuel mix of maritime transport in order to accelerate the decarbonisation of shipping (in 
comparison to efficiency improvements)? 
 
 The WSC considers that the successful uptake of sustainable alternative fuels – low 
carbon in the short term and zero or near zero by 2050 – is highly relevant in comparison to 
efficiency improvements.  Arguably, the most significant finding of the IMO’s 4th GHG Study 
published in August 2020 is that while the carbon intensity or efficiency of the fleet improved 
by 31.8% between 2008 and 2018, total GHG emissions from shipping increased by 9.6% from 
2014 to 2018.  This demonstrates that improving vessel efficiency has limits when faced with 
growing trade volumes.  It is clear that meeting the climate objectives of the IMO and the 
Paris Agreement will require a shift to new fuels and technolog ies with broad uptake across 
all ship types.  
 
 In order to reach the more ambitious carbon reduction targets that have been set for 
2050, appropriate research and deployment pathways toward the use of zero and near ze ro 
fuels available and vessels must also be developed now.  However, i t is also important that 
the sector has  access to the most environmentally sustainable fuels that are available and 
suitable for existing vessels, many of which will also be in operation 20 years from now.   
 
Q. What are your expectations concerning the uptake of sustainable alternative fuels in 
maritime transport in the nearest future, i.e. by 2025 (under the existing regulatory 
framework)? 
 
 As far as trans-oceanic shipping is concerned, there are no expectations that in 2025 
the fuel and vessel technologies required for the industry’s decarbonisation will have become 
available where required and on a significant scale.  There is a daunting amount of work that 
will be required to:  
(1) identify candidate zero or near zero GHG fuels;  
(2) assess the technical challenges for the  safe commercial deployment of those fuels , 
including the necessary engineering work to prove technical viability; and 
(3) prioritize scaling up the production and global supply infrastructure of the most promising 
candidates.  
 Promoting the uptake of alternative fuels that are immediately available is important 
to boost the short-term development of low carbon fuels for shipping.  However, focusing on 
the development and introduction of zero  and near -zero-carbon fuels and technologies  is 
imperative if we are to see the introduction of zero-emission ships in 2030.  
 
3 
 
 For context, transoceanic shipping faces a particularly challenging set of engineering 
issues due to the immense energy demands of these ships and their need to carry large 
volumes of fuel over long distances. Today, we know certain fuels such as e -ammonia 
represent promising candidates for the needed energy transition, but too little work has been 
done to address onboard and shore-side systems engineering to arrive at any conclusions as 
to whether a given fuel will prove practicable for a given ship.  We will most likely see different 
solutions for smaller vessels engaged in short-sea shipping, or the use of the same fuels, but 
with their use requiring highly differentiated engine and fuel storage systems.  In some cases, 
small vessels may offer a platform to test specific ship engineering systems, but we also need 
to recognize that the solutions needed for larger ships may require fundamentally different 
systems due to the on -board storage demands associated with the need to sail very long 
distances without refuelling. For this reason, energy density and materials science relevant to 
both onboard and onshore storage are issues requiring focused attention.  
 
Q. What policy measures are needed to accelerate the uptake of sustainable fuels 
 
 The single most important policy measure to accelerate the uptake of sustainable fuels 
for use in large transoceanic ships is to sponsor and undertake the technical research and 
development work necessary to identify which fuels and technology systems are most suited 
to transitioning to zero carbon . Research and technical development aimed at the 
development of zero-carbon technologies and fuels is vital. If we succeed in accelerating the 
development of the necessary engineering systems to use zero carbon f uels on large, 
medium, and smaller ships, we can expect the energy transition to proceed in a manner that 
is economically efficient as the technical pathways to achieve the transition will be much 
clearer.  Greater clarity in what technical pathways are most promising alongside operational 
and financial feasibility will enable the development of products and systems that can be 
produced with a much smaller risk of becoming stranded assets.  Fuels that meet these 
requirements will  still face significant chal lenges, but if these challenges are successfully 
addressed the GHG reduction benefits will be superior to any currently available alternatives. 
This research and development work constitutes a “critical pathway” to accelerating the 
energy transition in the maritime sector.   
 
  WSC encourages the Commission to make research and development focused 
on zero carbon fuels and the development of engine and fuel storage systems necessary to 
use these fuels onboard ships a key element of its approach. The EU’s work can and must be 
linked to efforts under consideration globally , such as the proposal in the International 
Maritime Organization to establish a n International Maritime Research and Development 
Board (IMRB).   The efforts can be mutually supportive , and if both the European Union and 
the IMO exhibit the political will to focus resources on this technical work we can proceed 
with the confidence that the energy transition in the maritime sector will occur much sooner 
and with greater efficiency than m ight otherwise be the case.  A global effort in this area, 
combined with a complementary EU Programme with similar objectives , should provide a 
practical mechanism to accelerate the introduction of these fuels by recognizing and

addressing the technical barriers that need to be resolved to accelerate introduction of the 
most promising fuel candidates.  By focusing on the practical, scientific and technica l means 
to decarbonise shipping the European Commission will truly be demonstrating its climate 
change leadership. 
 
 At the same time , the shipping industry needs the development of and improved 
access to existing low carbon fuels , and the Commission should also prioritise this work . 
Existing vessels are unlikely to be good candidates for retrofitting to use zero -carbon fuels.  
Therefore more low carbon sustainable fuels that are compatible with the existing fleet are 
needed if we a re to avoid a less satisfactory performance from these ships or  alternatively 
their premature recycling with harmful economic consequences.  
   
 Separately, t he EU needs to exercise care not to catalyse the development of fuel 
infrastructure that is premised on an unproven assumption that one given fuel is the fue l of 
the future . Doing so may undermine the speed and efficiency of energy transition by 
encouraging or catalysing the development of infrastructure that (in hindsight) may be 
considered a mistake leading to stranded assets. Put differently, encouraging heavy 
investment before a given fuel/technology has been thoroughly evaluated may lead to the 
extended use of an inferior fuel system because the level of investment in the shore-side and 
on-board in frastructure would discourage a move to more efficient and more 
environmentally beneficial alternatives. It is clear that a range of solutions will be needed to 
support the sector’s energy transition and that appropriate flexibility will be required.  
 
Q. The development and deployment of sustainable alternative fuels requires coordination 
among different economic actors (e.g. operators, fuel suppliers, ports, technology providers, 
etc.). It also requires a consistent approach to ensure availability of the fuel and guarantee 
the well-functioning of the internal market. The Green Deal foresees that EU action needs 
to be coordinated with action at global level, in particular the International Maritime 
Organization (IMO). However, what would be, in your opini on, the governance level best 
suited to address these objectives in the European Union? 
 
It is not clear that the coordination of economic actors is an appropriate action for 
government whether at national or European level.   Government actors can best assist by 
creating clear targets and appropriate regulatory frameworks that are consistent with the  
proper order and realistic timing of the research, development, and commercialization work 
that must be done before  low, near-zero and zero carbon fuels can be widely deployed on 
commercial ships of varying sizes and types.  This means that government policies must 
recognize that:  
 
(1) the first step in the regulatory/policy process is to support efforts that will answer the 
question of which zero carbon fuels have the highest probability of being technically 
and commercially feasible; 
 
5 
 
(2) once the most promising candidate fuels are identified, resources must be focused on 
the engine and fuel storage development necessary to move those fuels from concept 
stage to safe and practical deployment; and  
(3) regulatory measures designed to require timely transition to the new fuels must  be 
at global level and must align with realistic expectation s of market response by fuel 
suppliers, technology suppliers, etc. 
 
Once the structure above is in place, with the pieces arranged in the correct 
chronological order, then  industry – operators, fuel suppliers, ports, technology providers, 
etc. – will be in the best position to coordinate amongst each other in a free market to deliver 
and utilise the required fuels and technologies.   Thus, the primary focus of government 
intervention/assistance must be where there is arguably either a market failure or a market 
that is too slow: research and development of next-generation fuels to the point of safe  
commercial implementation on ships.  If the government can support efforts to reach that 
goal, and appropriate global regulatory requirements are at that stage set to compel uptake 
of those fuels, then the market will respond to finish the job. 
 
Q12. How should requirements for the use of sustainable alternative fuels and power be 
set in your view? 
For ships in navigation:  
a. Requirements on the share of specific sustainable alternative fuels to be used in ships 
fuel mix 
b. Performance requirements based on the carbon -intensity of energy used in marine 
operations.  
 
 WSC would strongly counsel against the Commission moving to mandate certain fuel 
types or fuel blends for vessels calling EU ports. Doing so would require significant on-board 
and onshore, fuel storage and fuel distribution investments by both industry and government. 
However, absent clarity about which fuel types represent even medium-term alternative fuel 
solution(s), a prescriptive approach is un likely to lead to the  desired outcome. Such an 
environment is instead likely to artificially constrain the development of other newer fuels , 
including the low / zero emission fuels that  are needed to reach climate change targets.  
Mandating fuel types or fuel blends may lock in the extended use of an inferior fuel system 
because the level of investment is so substantial that both private and government actors are 
reluctant to move to more efficient and more environmentally beneficial  alternatives. 
Performance-based carbon intensity targets for fuels are a much better choice, provided that 
such targets are set in accordance with proper consideration of the factors described in our 
answer to Question 4, above. Simply setting targets wit hout consideration of the actions 
necessary to meet those targets would be an arbitrary and ineffective strategy. It is also vital 
that any actions at EU level are aligned with agreements due to be reached at the IMO where 
work is already far advanced on t hese points, under the leadership of several EU Member 
States. 
 
For ships at berth:

a. Requirements on the share of specific sustainable alternative fuels to be used in ships 
fuel mix while at berth (incl. use of on-shore power)  
b. Performance requirement s based on the carbon -intensity of energy used by ships at 
berth 
 
 The European Commission has made known its intention to consider the introduction 
of a mandatory use of on -shore electric power for vessels at berth. From experience in 
California WSC knows that the design and construction of electrical power vaults across a port 
complex is an extremely complicated and massively expensive undertaking. The demands to 
coordinate these capital improvement projects across both large and small ports in the EU 
would require years of planning and construction, accompanied by detailed rules outlining 
the respective obligations of shore-side terminals, the port authority, and the ships using the 
shore-side electrical vaults. Moreover, the local electrical utility must  examine distribution 
trunks, sub -stations, and other grid infrastructure to accommodate the very substantial 
electrical loads required by large ships.. 
 During the time required for the steps described above , it is very likely that a large 
percentage of ships visiting EU ports will have transitioned to one or more zero-emission fuel 
systems such as green ammonia, hydrogen, e-methanol, or other attractive fuel / technology 
systems. In this scenario, a massive shore -side electrification investment programme would 
result in a situation where the electrical infrastructure is vastly under -used in the future or 
simply irrelevant for many ships. For the above reasons, the Commission may wish to consider 
an approach that limits shore -side electrical infrastructure to use by smaller harbour craft, 
ferries, and other vessels that may be likely to use battery power in the future.  
   At-berth emissions from large ships could be better addressed through goal -
based standards that are more likely to be satisfied using a v ariety of low and zero-emission 
fuels and technologies.  In short, demand for shore -side power by smaller harbour craft and 
ferries with high visit frequency may remain strong, but a continent -wide investment 
programme aimed at electrification of larger sh ips while at berth may prove to be a poor 
investment decision with limited emission benefits as larger ships migrate to non -battery 
zero-emission fuels.  A goal -based approach may also reduce the significant risk, 
complications, and huge  infrastructure investment burdens that would follow a plan to 
mandate electrical power while at berth in EU ports.  WSC is happy to share with interested 
EC staff the lessons learned and experience gained with the at-berth regulations in California.

ECSA Feedback FuelEU Maritime April 2020 
The European shipping industry welcomes the ‘FuelEU Maritime’ initiative to increase the 
use and availability of sustainable al ternative marine fuels and energy  sources. The 
development of new technologies and fuels is a necessary enabler towards 
decarbonisation of the shipp ing industry. European shipping companies already lead in 
trialling and deploying new fuels and technologies but support to build on this momentum 
is encouraged, while ensuring that European competitiveness is maintained.  
Member States to the  International Maritime Organisation (IMO) have committed to 
reduce the total annual GHG emissions from  international shipping by at least 50% by 
20501 whilst pursuing efforts towards phasing them out completely during this century. 
This objective will not be achieved without significant R&D towards the rapid deployment 
of zero-carbon technologies and propulsion systems, includin g sustainable alternative 
marine fuels. This should be complemented by efforts to ensure the highest levels of 
safety, quality, and a clear definition of sustainability. 
International effort 
The Inception Impact Assessment towards a forthcoming FuelEU Maritime proposal 
recognises that the number and volumes of sustainable alternative fuels and technologies 
currently available to the maritime sector is extremely limited, especially for deep sea 
vessels. Accordingly, the global shipping industry has submitted a proposal to the IMO to 
form the world’s first collaborative shipping R&D programme to support the development 
of low-carbon and zero-carbon fuels, energy sources, propulsion systems and other new 
technologies necessary to reduce the sector’s GHG emissions. This proposal2, based on a 
financial contribution per tonne of marine fuel purchased for consumption by shipping 
companies worldwide, is expected to generate about USD 5 billion in funding over a 10-
year period. It is a practical measure from the global shipping industry towards ensuring 
that new fuels and technology are worldwide available in time to reduce shipping 
emissions within the ambitious  time-frame agreed via the IMO. It must be stressed, 
however, that wider efforts from economic sectors well beyond the maritime industry are 
crucial for the development, production and proliferation of alternative fuels from 
renewable sources. 
Dependency on land-based infrastructure for low-carbon fuels 
While shipowners would  invest in new or retrofitted vessels and on -board equipment, 
these efforts are reliant on external investments in land -based infrastructure and 
production facilities for low  and zero -carbon fuels 3. We therefore welcome efforts to 
stimulate the production of new fuels on  a larger scale and  reduce the price gap with 
current technologies. Measures should ensure that such new fuels be available globally,

European Competitiveness 
We are already seeing promising results of alternative energy applications in short sea-
shipping in Europe. The European Commission is strongly supporting these efforts – for 
instance via one of world’s first fully electric ferries operating in the Baltic Sea7, the use 
of methanol on a large RoPax ferry8, the retrofitting of vessels to connect to shore-power9 
and the testing of shore-connections in ports10, and support to deploy two commercially 
operated hydrogen fuel cell vessels11, to name but a few. Further measures to facilitate 
the access to funding and finance for such projects are welcomed, including for deep-sea 
vessels. As such, the EU has an opportunity to serve as test and demonstration centres 
for the deployment of new fue l types and technologies  and support a critical mass of 
existing solutions and promote innovation. All with a view to be up -scaled to a global 
level. This will have the potential to provide the full breadth of the EU maritime sector a 
competitive advantage in new green technologies, creating op portunities for jobs and 
growth and providing a first-mover advantage to European shipping companies.

are able to compete on an equal footing with international industry. This means that the

Shipping should remain not only an environmentally -efficient means of transport for 
shippers but also cost-efficient. Short sea shipping in Europe is a strong EU asset, moving 
goods in an environmentally -efficient way and reducing congestion , pollution and 
accidents on roads. While the sector is already embracing new technologies, r equired 
investments in new fuels and technologies may imply higher freight rates, so an impact 
assessment should fully examine such implications on the modal -choice of shippers, 
ensuring the competitiveness of short -sea shipping. Moreover, the costs of trading via 
EU-ports should not become prohibitive. This would undermine  the strength of the 
European economy and of shipping as a transport mode, potentially increasing the share 
of less environmentally-efficient forms of transport from transhipment ports just outside 
the EU. 
Technology neutral and goal-based 
It could be said  that the maritime transport sector is at a cross -roads. There is no 
certainty on which specific fuels or technology represent the future for the industry and 
it is likely that different shipping trades will require different technologies. We encourage 
the forthcoming proposal to be  subject to an impact assessment and  be technology 
neutral to leave open the possibility of emission-reduction technologies that are not even 
yet known. We therefore welcome goal -based objectives with non -prescriptive 
requirements on the type of fuel or technology used. 
Decarbonisation of the shipping sector must  firstly be an international effort.  The 
inception impact assessment rightly refers to the lack of predictability and  high risk of 
investment choices as  barriers towards shipowner/operator deployment of new 
technologies. A shipping company’s decision to invest in a hydrogen-fuelled vessel, for 
instance, must be accompanied by reasonable certainty that it will be technically sound, 
safe, available widely, cost-effective, and able to meet the regulatory requirements of 
the future – no matter if the regional trading route or pattern of the vessel were to change 
during its 25 -30 year lifetime. As such, regional regulatory measures risk ‘moving the 
goal posts’ which is challenging for a sector that operates internationally. We therefore 
encourage any proposal to be coherent – and not undermine – decarbonisation objectives 
as established via the IMO. Europe should thus continue its leading role in ensuring that 
the reduction targets set by the IMO are met, by promoting high ambition and supporting 
consensus among the IMO Member States.

account specific local circumstances in each port. It is therefore important to keep our 
eyes on the target and not focus on one specific technology  at the expense of oth er 
potentially more effective and cost-efficient methods. Such a technology neutral approach 
may also increase competition and better foster innovation.  
Implementation and enforcement  
Fuel costs represent a significant  share of total  ship operating costs, with expensive 
investments required by all stakeholders for ships to run on alternative energy. To avoid 
risks of non -compliance, thereby providing strong financial advantages, any future 
measures must be fully enforceable without creating complex and difficult to comply with 
(administrative) burdens for shipowners. As it is foreseen that many different alternative 
fuels could be used to comply with future measures, any performance requirements on 
the carbon -intensity of energy used m ust be fully transparent and implementable, 
recognising that operational decisions affecting fuel consumption and the cost of 
particular fuels can be taken by vessel charterers and not necessarily the shipowner.  
In addition, measures should be designed to encourage – and not later penalise - front-
running shipping companies which take considerable financial risks to decarbonise their 
fleet as soon as possible within this century. 
 
About ECSA 
The European Community Shipowners’ Associations (ECSA) was founded in 1965 and represents 
the national shipowners’ associations of the EU, the UK and Norway. The European shipowners 
control 39.5% of the global commercial fleet, contribute €149 billion to the EU GDP and provide 
2 million Europeans with careers both on board and ashore. ECSA promotes the interests of 
European shipping so that the industry can best serve European and international trade in a 
competitive free business environment to the benefit of shippers and consumers.

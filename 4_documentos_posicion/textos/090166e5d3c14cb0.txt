       21 September, 2020  Dear Sir/Madam  EGEC views on the Renewable Energy Directive IIA  EGEC, the voice of the European geothermal industry, is a not-for-profit association representing the entire value-chain of the industry across 28 countries. It is included on the European Transparency Register number: 11458103335-07 Further information can be found at www.egec.org.  We welcome the opportunity to respond to the consultation on the Renewable Energy Directive Inception Impact Assessment and wider process. Our observations and recommendations are:  1. Prioritisation: We agree that one of the central problems to address in this reform is the need for greater and rapid penetration for renewable heating and cooling technologies to address climate pollution from heat consumption.     2. Upward revision of the Renewable Energy Directive: We support increasing the RED target from 32% to 40% though are concerned that this might be too low in the context of the need to accelerate decarbonisation of heating and cooling as well as the emphasis placed on renewable hydrogen.   Given the priority placed on renewable heating and its strong relationship to the flagship Renovation Wave as well as energy sector integration initiatives, Article 23 must now become a binding EU target to ensure half of the EU’s heating and cooling consumption is derived from renewable sources by 2030. The annual percentage point increase in the share of renewable must be changed from 1.3 to 3.1. This is the most optimal means of embedding the Renovation Wave into EU legislation and complements the inclusion of buildings in the EU ETS.   2. Options for the Impact Assessment: We support Option 5. It is essential to marry dedicated financial structures for investments, capacity building for project proposals as well as training professional geothermal installers. Robust target setting and implementation frameworks for Article 23 are essential for a successful reform as well as an effective Renovation Wave and sector integration package.                                 3. Extending support schemes (Article 4): The are intended for renewable electricity. They should be extended to include support for geothermal heating, cooling and electricity. Given the need for sustainable supplies of lithium, they should be extended to geothermal lithium supplies from existing and new geothermal energy systems. Thermal energy storage systems should also be able to access support schemes.

 4. Community-wide risk insurance: Article 3(5) of the Directive compels the European Commission to “reducing the cost of capital for renewable energy projects.”  The upfront cost of drilling for geothermal projects accounts up to 70% of the total cost of a geothermal project. A community-wide risk insurance system for drilling must be established to implement to significantly reduce risks associated with drilling for geothermal energy solutions. The Union renewable energy financing mechanism outlined in Article 33 of Regulation 2018/1999 on the Governance of the Energy Union and Climate Action should be amended accordingly to include a Union geothermal risk insurance scheme.   5. Permitting and licensing: to facilitate the market uptake of renewable energy technologies such as geothermal, a streamlining of the permitting and licensing process is needed. To do so, permitting and licensing for large renewable energy projects in electricity and/or heating and cooling needs to be facilitated by the setting of one-stop-shops at the national level, and the time limit for the final decision on permitting must be 2 years from the date of application.  6. Renewable heat pumps: The need to accelerate the displacement of fossil fuel utilisation in heating and cooling with high-efficiency renewable energy solutions is critical to making buildings, which account for 40% of the EU’s greenhouse gas emissions, industry zero-carbon.   To achieve this and the Energy Efficiency Directive target, the Seasonal Performance Factor threshold to consider heat pumps as renewable (Annex VII) must be updated from 2.5 to 3 to ensure the most efficient heat pumps, which produce the largest volume of renewable heating and cooling at the lowest energy input, are prioritised especially given the latest technological advancements. The associated Delegated Acts which establish the methodology to define renewable cooling, must be issued immediately for rapid implemented.  7. Decarbonisation of buildings: According to the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (2018/844/EU), the EU must decarbonise its building stock by 2050, at the latest. This means all heating and cooling investments will need to be renewable based from now to 2030. The implementation of Article 15 of the Renewable Energy Directive must be reviewed to enable the maximisation of the share of renewables in buildings and prepare the lock-out of fossil fuel technologies from the building sector.  8. Geothermal lithium: Europe is dependent of lithium imports. Conventional mapping of lithium reserves failed to consider lithium residing in brines used in geothermal energy capacity. Four projects in the EU are in the process of extracting this using the innovative, zero-carbon Direct Lithium Extraction (DLE) process, which provides sufficient quantities of raw lithium that can be processed into batteries. This is the foundation of the full lithium-ion battery value-chain in the EU. It is also the most environmentally sustainable means of extracting lithium providing significant technological export opportunities.   Geothermal lithium should be included in Renewable Energy Directive as:  i) Definition: Article 2 should include a definition of ‘Geothermal lithium’ as ‘the direct extraction of lithium from geothermal brines’  in Article 2.   ii) Finance: Support schemes in Article 4 should be amended to include support for geothermal lithium 
   3 
 iii) Multiplier accounting: Investment in geothermal lithium capacity should be accounted with a factor of 3 in the delivery of electricity, heating and transport sub-targets due to its ability to provide renewable heating, cooling, electricity and lithium batteries.   9. Removal of direct and indirect fossil fuel subsidies: Article 7 of Energy Performance of Buildings provides a direct subsidy for the replacement of an inefficient fossil fuel boiler with an efficient fossil fuel boiler. This must be amended to read “high-efficiency alternative systems based on renewables,” rather than “high-efficiency alternative systems”.   Furthermore, the Trans-European Networks for Energy (TEN-E) Regulation must be amended to allow for transboundary geothermal heat reservoirs and basins to be able to access EU infrastructure funding from the Connecting Europe Facility. Terminology and definitions: Reference to fossil fuel industry language such as ‘green hydrogen’ cannot be used as this inbuilt bias undermines the integrity of the European Commission and distorts the policy discussion. If they are used all industry lobby material, definitions and language, especially that from the renewable industry, must be used. If hydrogen is to be referred to, it must be recognised as renewable hydrogen emanating from renewable geothermal, wind, solar, ocean and other recognised renewable energy solutions.  11. Modelling of the energy system: the current EU modelling is not robust enough to assess energy efficiency and renewable energy targets in relation with a higher GHG objective. While modelling the 2018 Renewable Energy Directive (EUCO3232.5), the European Commission assumed a decrease in the production of renewable heating and cooling, directly opposed to the objective of said Directive, as outlined in Figure 2.

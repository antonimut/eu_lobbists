EGEC welcomes the opportunity to contribute to the consultation process on the revision of 
the Energy Efficiency Directive. For the geothermal sector, the EU targets on climate, 
renewable energy and energy efficiency need to be revised upwards to be aligne d with the 
2050 decarbonisation objectives. This for instance means 50% of renewable in heating and 
cooling, and a building sector where efficiency and renewables have been mainstreamed by 
2030. The Energy Efficiency Directive need to be revised to reflect these imperatives. 
 
1) The targets need to be reviewed upward to at least -55% GHG by 2030, 
with a consistent reassessment of the energy efficiency and renewable 
energy targets 
 
We argue in favour of increasing targets in a way that is consistent with at lea st 55% GHG 
emissions reduction by 2030. The energy efficiency and renewable energy targets need to be 
increased accordingly, and the Article 23 of the Renewable Energy Directive, whose 
implementation is directly linked to that of the Energy Efficiency Directive, need to be revised 
to become a biding provision that amounts to 50% of renewables in heating and cooling by 
2030. 
Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy targets go hand in hand: there need to be significant 
investments in both reducing energy needs and decarbonizing the energy supply. These 
synergies are particularly strong within the heating and cooling sector. 
Heating and cooling represents nearly half of Europe’s energy needs, including space heating 
for buildings and process heat for industry. In  both cases, renewable heating and cooling 
solutions such as geothermal energy (via district heating, geothermal heat pumps) can be 
deployed as an energy efficiency measure and a renewable one. Geothermal technologies also 
complement other energy efficiency actions such as building envelope retrofit. 
For the geothermal sector, an upward revision of the energy efficiency target is needed, as is 
a similar change of the renewable energy target. Moreover, the links between the two 
regulations should be strengthened. 
Moreover, the implementation of the Energy Efficiency Directive must be more thorough that 
in recent years. While energy efficiency is the only non -binding target in the 20 -20-20 
framework, it also is the one target that the EU is nearly certain to fail. The energy efficiency 
target must be increased and made binding to ensure its implementation. 
 
2) The revision of the targets and the energy efficiency measures need to be 
consistent with decarbonisation: the lock out of fossil fuels must start now

The period from 2030 to 2050 corresponds to the technical lifetime of a heating and cooling 
equipment. This means that after 2030, all renewable heating and cooling investments will 
need to be renewable based. To get there, the Energy Efficiency needs to accel erate 
renovation rate and mainstream the decarbonisation of the building stock and of the industry. 
An integrated approach between energy efficiency and renewables is the only way for the EU 
to achieve its 2050 climate objective of decarbonisation. The 203 0 framework must set the 
basis for an economy that is efficient and renewable based. High EE and RES targets, 
articulated with one another, is crucial in that regard. Implementing measures must also 
reflect this RES/EE synergy and enable investments that lock-out fossil fuels from the energy 
system. The Renovation Wave 1 is a key measure in enabling such a transition towards an 
efficient and decarbonised system, and its implementation must be aligned with a reviewed 
EED framework that locks out fossil fuels. 
 
3) The Energy Efficiency Directive needs to be revised accordingly 
 
Among the three options proposed in this consultation, EGEC argues in favour of the third. A 
Revision of the EED is needed. 
First, the Energy Efficiency Directive need to be adapted to reflect the priorities of the Green 
Deal, and align the Renovation Wave s with the decarbonisation objective, notably via an 
integrated renewable and energy efficiency approach. 
Second, the Energy Efficiency Directive cannot continue to allow Member States to distribute 
subsidies to fossil fuel equipments on the pretext of marginal efficiency gains2. The rules for 
allowing energy efficiency subsidies, notably under the Article 7a of the Directive, need to 
be revised to make them consistent with the decarbonisation imperative. Only equipment that 
is efficient and renewable should be eligible for energy efficiency support, as these are the 
only technologies that can be supported in the market. 
 
Another key article to consider is Article 14 on the promotion of efficie ncy in heating and 
cooling. This article must be the key enabler of the transition towards and efficient and 
renewable based energy system, by promoting the phase out of fossil fuels and the shift 
towards renewable energy. The current implementation of the  article, notably the Annex IX 
of the EED, gives too much space for Member States to implement policies that benefits fossil 
fuels under the pretext of higher efficiency. To correct this issue, the article must be reviewed 
as follow: 
“2.  Member States shall adopt policies which encourage the due taking into account at local 
and regional levels of the potential of using efficient renewable heating and cooling systems, 
in particular those using high-efficiency cogeneration local renewable heating and 
cooling resources contributing to the annual objective in Directive 2008/2001 
article 23. Account shall be taken of the potential for developing local and regional heat 
markets.” 
 
4) Long term renovation strategies must see their implementation 
strengthened 
 
Long term renovation strategies are an essential piece in enabling the decarbonisation of the 
building stock. The revision of the Energy Efficiency Directive needs to embed the 
decarbonisation imperative in the Long Term Renovation Strategies, and their implementation 
must be guaranteed by the NECPS. Currently, few Member States are implementing robust

5) Modelling of the energy system: the current EU modelling is not robust 
enough to assess energy efficiency and renewable energy targets in relation 
with a higher GHG objective 
 
The energy modelling suite used by the European Commission has proven repeatedly its 
inadequacy in representing the decarbonisation of heating and cooling 4. This substantial 
shortfall is crucial when considering modelling done to assess the energy efficiency target to 
2030. In the building stock for instance, most buildings used in 2030 will still be around in 
2050. They must therefore be consistent with decarbonisation. This means that the buildings 
must be energy efficient and rely on renewables. 
Thus far, the v arious modelling exercises presented by the European Commission (most 
recently those linked to the Long Term Strategy 5 or the assessment of the Clean Energy 
Package6) have not been able to model the decarbonisation of heating and cooling, and 
therefore that of the building sector where most energy efficiency is expected to take place 
by the model itself. The models are ignoring the potential of renewable heating and cooling, 
which is typically developed alongside energy efficiency improvements in the real  world, 
particularly in the building sector. While modelling the 2018 Renewable Energy Directive 
(EUCO3232.5), the European Commission assumed a decrease in the production of renewable 
heating and cooling. This would translate into colossal job losses if it were to actually happen.

There needs to be a better representation of the energy system by the modelling used by the 
European Commission for the review of the Energy Efficiency Target, and more generally in 
the framework of reviewing the greenhouse gas emissions objectives. It means that: 
 
• All modelling needs to be consistent with 2050 decarbonisation strategies. 
By 2030, many heating and cooling infrastructure, and much of the building stock will 
still be there to 2050. This approach allows an optimization of investments that 
guarantees a more cost-efficient decarbonisation. 
• The modelling need to reflect adopted EU policies, notably the REDII Article 
23 for mainstreaming renewables in heating and cooling. Even the modelling 
of EUCO3232.5, representing the application of the Clean Energy Package fails to 
represent this measure. EU energy system modelling needs to represent existing legal 
provisions. 
• The model needs to be transparent in its assumption and the way it comes 
up with results. Many models are considered as “crystal balls” by stakeholders who 
do not understand their limitations. Putting clear caveat to th eir outcomes via 
transparency is a necessary step. 
• Stakeholders need to be consulted in the modelling process, notably sectors 
that are directly involved in the implementation of the targets such as 
renewable energy technologies.

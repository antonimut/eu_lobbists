 
 
 
  
  
 
 
Statement by Deutsche Umwelthilfe as part of the consultation 
of the Efficiency Directive 2012/27 of October 25, 2012 
on energy efficiency, amending directives 2009/125 / EC and 2010/30 / EU 
and to repeal Directives 2004/8 / EC and 2006/32 / EC 
 
 
Berlin, 15.09.2020 
     
 
Deutsche Umwelthilfe (Environmental Action Germany) sees a considerable need to amend directive 
2012/27. The European requirements for combined heat and power generation (CHP) are a 
prerequisite for national CHP support schemes and therefore have an effect far beyond the directive 
itself. Based on directive 2012/27, support schemes were created that cement the use of fossil fuels 
in heating and actively work against a switch to renewable heating. In detail, we note: 
 
1. Article 14 is supposed to promote efficiency in heat and power generation through financial 
support for “highly efficient” CHP. According to Annex II of Directive 2012/27 on energy efficiency, 
“highly efficient CHP” only needs to be 10% more efficient than the separate generation of electricity 
and heat. For small CHP plants, no such minimum percentage is set; any “primary energy savings” 
qualify them as highly efficient. These can be close to 0%. 
 
We note that efficiency losses in the heating networks usually amount to 10%, practically eliminating 
the required efficiency advantage. 
 
Article 14 in combination with Annex II allows the promotion of fossil combustion under favorable 
conditions, and in fact most CHP operators use this option to switch from coal to gas. This hinders 
the switch to renewable energies (RE) and a low-carbon transition in the heating sector more 
broadly. The dilemma becomes apparent with the coal phase-out in Germany: Shutting down coal 
power plants does not lead to a switch to RE but to fossil gas-CHP, due to the massive subsidies 
available. 
 
In CHP plants, power and heat generation are coupled. However, heat demand usually dictates when 
these plants run. Whenever heating is needed, power is produced as well. In Germany and countries 
with similar climate, this occurs more during winter than in the summer months. To ensure heating 
supply, CHP is practically operated as "must run" capacity during winter. While the 2018 EED revision 
removed priority dispatch for “highly efficient” CHP plants by striking Article 15, paragraph 5, this 
only applies to plants that started operation after 4 July 2019.  
 
As a result, subsidized fossil CHP displaces subsidized renewable electricity. This system conflict 
becomes more severe with an increasing share of RE. Even the construction of CHP plants with 
power storage facilities cannot resolve this. These facilities are only designed to increase flexibility 
during daily operation; they are not suitable for compensating fluctuations that extend beyond a day.

In addition, the increasing need for flexibility of CHP comes with a trade-off in terms of efficiency and 
therefore the realized primary energy savings. Simultaneous power and heat demand are occurring 
less and less often. Optimal operating conditions for CHP are thus becoming increasingly rare, which 
means that relying on CHP will not be sustainable in the long term. 
 
The current promotion of CHP in Germany is justified with the environmental benefits of primary 
energy savings. A gain in efficiency is assumed, compared to the conventional, separate generation 
of heat and power. According to Section 2 Clause 8a of the German Combined Heat and Power Act 
(Kraft-Wärme-Kopplungsgesetz, KWKG), systems are considered to be “highly efficient” if they meet 
“… the requirements of Directive 2012/27 / EU of the European Parliament and the Council of 
October 25, 2012 on energy efficiency….”. “Highly efficient” CHP according to this definition receives 
a basic operating subsidy and a bonus to switch from coal to gas and other fuels. 
 
Due to the current definition of efficiency, CHP also receives further advantages in the EU: Since 
2013, CHP plants have enjoyed free allocation of CO2certificates under the EU Emissions Trading 
Scheme. CHP operators can make use of self-consumption privileges and claim negative network 
charges in some member states. Due to these preferential conditions, many CHP plants are 
completely amortized after just a few years. RE installations, on the other hand, are often curtailed in 
the event of negative market prices. In Germany, e.g., renewable electricity is no longer remunerated 
after 6 hours of negative electricity prices, while CHP continues to receive a subsidy. A draft law 
revising the Renewable Energies Act (Erneuerbare-Energie-Gesetz, EEG), which would shorten this 
time to 15 minutes, is currently under consideration. 
 
Recommendation: The preferential conditions for public support of CHP created by Article 14 
should be abolished to cause a revision of national CHP support schemes. Public support should 
only be permissible to renewable heat generation. The European Union should stop creating 
competitive advantages for CHP and disadvantages for renewable heat.

determined based on a comparison with separate heat and power generation in the same fuel

In the example, 100 kWh of fossil energy used in CHP produces only 90 kWh of final energy under 
ideal operating conditions. When using environmental heat via a heat pump, 100-140 kWh of final 
energy are generated. The current method for determining efficiency reference values, on which the 
classification as “highly efficient” CHP is based, systematically favors fossil fuel-based CHP while 
discriminating against renewable heat. This is not compatible with the EU’s climate objectives, given 
that the CO2 reduction achieved with RE is much higher than that of CHP. 
 
Recommendation: Annex II, letter f should be changed so that comparisons can be made across 
fuels, allowing efficiency reference values to be determined by a meaningful comparison between 
different heating systems. 
 
3. The climate impact of fossil gas-powered CHP urgently needs a scientific reassessment. Natural gas 
releases greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions not only at the point of burning, but also causes significant 
upstream emissions resulting from extraction, processing and transport. These emissions are 
currently not included when calculating the GHG emissions produced by fossil gas. The main part of 
fossil gas is methane, which has 80 times the climate impact of CO2 over a 20-year time span. Even 
leakages of small amounts of methane can have a major climate impact. 
  
Recommendation: The European Commission should commission a study to scientifically reassess 
the GHG balance of fossil gas-based CHP, including upstream emissions. 
 
4. Possible uses of the heat produced, especially in biogas CHP plants, are very broadly defined and 
often are not the most efficient. High efficiency losses and absurd uses of heat to occur are not 
uncommon. 
 
Recommendation: The requirements for what constitutes efficient and useful purposes for heat 
generated by CHP plants should be tightened and equipped with possible sanctions. 
 
5. The classification as “highly efficient” CHP is currently based on “expected operation”, rather than 
actual operation. A mandatory annual review should be conducted by the Commission to ensure 
congruence of expected and actual operating conditions. In case of non-compliance, the preferential 
status and any public support received by the CHP operator should be revoked. 
 
Recommendation: As a first step towards establishing an effective monitoring, the EED revision 
should create an obligation for network operators to document the operating conditions and 
energy balances of their plants.

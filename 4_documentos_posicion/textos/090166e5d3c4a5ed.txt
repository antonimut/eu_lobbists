The World Shipping Council 
 The World Shipping Council (WSC or the Council) is a non-profit trade association that 
represents the liner shipping industry, primarily operators of containerships, vehicle carriers, 
and roll-on/roll-off vessels. WSC members carry over 90% of the world’s containerized trade 
and a substantial share of the world’s roll -on/roll-off cargoes. WSC Member companies 
transport over 40 million TEUs of European export and import cargo each year or roughly two-
thirds of the EU’s seaborne trade by va lue. Their operations and investments extend also to 
port terminals, warehouses, truck companies and information technology systems that are 
critical for EU logistics and supply chains. The World Shipping Council is listed on the EU 
Transparency Register under number 32416571968-71. 
Response 
 The IMO’s 4th GHG Study published in August 2020 showed that while the carbon 
intensity or efficiency of the  global maritime fleet improved by 31.8% between 2008 and 
2018, total GHG emissions from shipping increased by  9.6% from 2014 to 2018. Efficiency 
improvements alone will not enable maritime transport to achieve emission reductions 
targets set by the IMO and EU. The sector’s transition to green fuels is indispensable.  
 The more ambitious carbon reduction targets that have been set for 2050 require that 
research and deployment pathways be established to identify, develop, and deploy low and 
no-carbon fuels and vessels .  Ocean shipping faces a particularly challenging set o f 
engineering issues due to the immense energy demands of these ships and their need to carry 
large volumes of fuel over long distances. This needs to be addressed as soon as possible, but 
other fuels can play a role in offering more immediate GHG reductions. 
 In order to maximize reductions in the near to medium term as we continue the work 
to develop transformational fuels for the future fleet,  it is vital that the maritime sector has

much better access to the most sustainable fuels that are available today and are suitable for 
vessels in today’s fleet. However, many of these renewable options face significant issues with 
costs and supply. As opposed to other sectors such as road transport, sustainable 
technologies options suitable for deep-sea shipping  are limited . Biofuels and other viable 
alternatives should be focused towards supporting the transition of hard to abate sectors like 
shipping.  
 Accelerating the uptake of currently available fuels will be particularly important to 
reach the EU’s 2030 decarbonisation targets.  A revision of the Renewable Energy Directive - 
in line with options 3, 4 and 5 of the Inception Impact Assessment document – should ensure 
that Member States are incentivised to improve the supply of such fuels to the maritime 
transport sector, as part of their national renewable energy plans. Today article 27 of the 
Directive privileges the supply of renewable fuels to rail and road transport. However, 
Member States should also be able to count the supply of biofuels and other renewable fuels 
to maritime transport within the overall calculation of a Member State’s renewable energy 
consumption as per the Directive. In short, Member States should be equally incentivised to 
improve the supply of  renewable fuels to the maritime sector as they are when supplying 
renewable fuels to other sectors.  
 Sustainable or renewable energy supply projects that can benefit sustainable shipping 
today must be encouraged more. Taken together with other instruments that should be used  
under the Green Deal to improve the attractiveness of low carbon and low emission fuels, an 
amendment of the RED II Directive to prioritize availability of such fuels to the maritime sector 
can make a considerable difference to accelerate the shipping sector’s green transition.

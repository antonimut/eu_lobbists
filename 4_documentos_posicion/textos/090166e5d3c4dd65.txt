Introduction 
 
VERBUND welcomes the Commission’s intention to revise the Renewable Energy Directive. 
In the Commission’s consultation on a potential revision of the 2030 climate target, 
VERBUND already spoke out in favour of an ambitious upward revision to at least 55% CO2-
savings until 2030. In order to meet this target ambition the 2030 energy targets would need 
to be adapted accordingly. 
 
Based on the difficulties to attain the 2020 energy efficiency target we believe that a majority 
of the contribution for the additional CO2-savings must be made by building out more 
renewables and fostering their end-user uptake. However, only increasing the targets will not 
be sufficient. Due to the fast-paced developments in e.g. the hydrogen sector it will be also 
necessary to amend the renewable energy directive. Furthermore, these regulatory 
measures need to be accompanied by non-legislative measures such as information 
campaigns and trainings. Thus, VERBUND favours Option 5 of the Inception Impact 
Assessment which includes, raising the ambition, amending the REDII and deploying non-
regulatory measures. 
 
In addition to this general policy preference VERBUND would also like to point to some 
concrete regulatory opportunities that might be considered when revising the existing REDII. 
 
 
Create a general classification and definition of renewable gases in particular 
hydrogen 
 
At the moment the REDII mainly defines biogas as a renewable gas, while hydrogen 
produced from renewable electricity falls within the definition of “Renewable Fuels of Non-
Biological Origin” (RFNBO). RFNBOs are almost exclusively used in the context of Article 25 
and Article 27, which are both relating to the transport sector. The current classification and 
definition of hydrogen is therefore far too specific and partially limits the uptake of hydrogen 
in other sectors. 
A revised REDII should take into account the wide-ranging end-use applications of hydrogen 
and should therefore come forward with a definition that adequately covers renewable 
hydrogen and its derivatives in all sectors (electricity, gas, industry, transport etc.). 
 
Create an adequate incentive for the uptake of renewable gases in end-use sectors 
 
End-use sectors, such as transport and mobility or industrial sectors face a lack of proper 
pricing signals to incentivise the development and deployment of renewable hydrogen. It is 
currently difficult to develop sustainable business models for green hydrogen, also because 
counter-productive subsidies are in place (e. g. free allowances in the EU ETS for fossil 
hydrogen production). Thus, either one introduces an adequate CO2-price (vie EU ETS or 
taxes) or a GHG emission savings reward for renewable gases or one could apply specific 
end-user quotas per sector for renewable hydrogen and its derivatives. 
 
For example, at the moment hydrogen can only count towards the attainment of the 14% fuel 
supplier obligation for renewable energy in transport. Contrary to advanced biofuels,

renewable hydrogen and its derivatives do not enjoy a sub-quota, wherefore it is unlikely to 
outcompete the much cheaper yet not sustainable first generation biofuels. 
 
Unburden renewable hydrogen production via electrolysis 
 
The current regulatory framework in the REDII foresees narrow criteria for the production of 
renewable hydrogen from electrolysers connected to the electricity grid e. g. “additionality”, 
“simultaneity” as well as “regionality” (Article 27(3) and Recital 90).  
 
The additionality criterion effectively excludes already existing, renewable generation from 
producing green hydrogen and therefore lowers the competitiveness of renewable hydrogen 
vis-à-vis conventional gases. In addition, depending on the definition of the additionality 
criterion, it might as well as exclude the production of green hydrogen even in cases where 
excess renewable electricity is being used instead of curtailing it. 
The other aforementioned criteria (simultaneity and regionality), if applied strictly, could 
further increase the production cost for renewable hydrogen. This bears the risk of increasing 
the cost of renewable hydrogen relative to less sustainable alternatives such as “blue” 
hydrogen or first generation biofuels. It counteracts the Commission’s overall intention to 
facilitate the creation of a European hydrogen economy. 
 
We therefore suggest to allow for a flexible implementation of these criteria, at least in an 
initial phase until 2030. This would coincide with the EU Hydrogen Strategy’s development 
pathway for electrolyser upscaling.  
 
Ensure trade and traceability  
 
A European-wide certification scheme for hydrogen could provide traders and end-consumers 
with genuine information on the origin of the hydrogen supplied to them. Such a certification 
scheme should be based on a clear methodology taking into account overall green-house gas 
emissions, as well as on a clear terminology for the renewable tra its of the respective 
hydrogen. 
 
Furthermore, the revised RED II should entail a timetable for the introduction of a European -
wide Guarantees of Origin scheme  for all gases and  for hydrogen and its derivatives  in 
particular. Currently the lack of such a scheme creates a lot of regulatory uncertainty for 
potential investors and therefore impedes investments into renewable hydrogen projects.

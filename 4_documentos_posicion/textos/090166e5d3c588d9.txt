 
 
Revision of Directive on the promotion of the use of energy from renewable 
sources - Inception Impact Assessment 
The Views of the Enel Group 
 
Enel welcomes the Commission’s Inception Impact Assessment (IIA) initiative to revise the 
Renewable Energy Directive, in order to support the renewed and higher EU climate ambition and 
implement the transition towards a more integrated energy system. Upscaling climate action 
through the energy transition and the further development of renewables will deliver substantial 
benefits in terms of resource-efficient economic growth, technological leadership, global and local 
pollution reduction, security of supply,  fair transition. Given the global nature of the GHG effect , 
Enel believes that responding to climate change challenge needs to be a coordinated effort at the 
global scale, to be pursued also through enhanced commitments at regional and national  scale. 
Enel itself is committed to decarbonize its energy mix well before 2050. 
In order to achieve the climate neutral ity goal by 2050, it is paramount that the EU continues the 
implementation and modernization of policies  to mainstream the deployment of renewable  
energy source s, direct electrification  of final consumption,  energy efficiency measure s and the 
further integration of different sectors . Against this background, it is necessary to  revise the 
Renewable energy Directive in a holistic and harmonized way together with the other strategies 
and legislative initiatives i n the energy and climate field, in order to provide a clear , consistent 
and stable framework to support the transition. 
Enel believes that an increased 2030 EU climate target of at least 55% of GHG emissions reduction 
is feasible, affordable and due , considering the 2050 carbon neutrality objective . Renewable 
energy should be confirmed as one of the main drivers of decarbonisation. In this respect, the EU 
RES target should be raised to at least 35%.  
As acknowledged in the IIA, Europe has made significant progress in decarbonising its electricity 
production throu gh renewables, especially when compared to other sectors. However , boosting 
renewable energy deployment in the power sector  is still the  crucial and most relevant pre -
requirement and enabler to reach the overall EU’s decarboni sation goals . The revision of the 
Renewable Directive should hence improve and enhance the framework for a massive 
development of additional renewables  (green-field power plants and refurbishment  and 
optimisation works of existing-ones), including: 
- Long-term visibility for investors, to unlock finance at the lowes t possible cost and allow 
Member States to manage the transition . A set of instrument s has to be made available in 
this regard. European Institutions should ensure that (i) RES developers are free to combine 
different routes-to-market (such as different kind of auctions, corporate PPAs, grid services) 
for each RES in vestment (ii) Guarantees of Origin are able to support the development of 
specific business models/technologies (Corporate PPAs or renewable hydrogen).  
- Simplified permitting procedures and clear time limits , coherently with the RED Directive 
targets (Art. 16), to remove all the obstacles to the development of RES, for new renewable 
capacity, refurbishment or repowering initiatives  and plant optimisation  applications. The 
authority for this sits at the Member States level, but  the European Commission must lean

on Member States on this and make use of its powers (e.g. by issuing guiding principles 
aimed at implementing best practices and harmonizing such procedures  and timing at EU 
level). Moreover, also the grid connection process should be simplified. 
- Guidance on how to engage with local communities to raise the awareness on the 
environmental, economic and social benefits of the energy transition and RES development. 
- Promotion of hybrid projects (i.e.  RES coupled with Storage ): this is playing a key role in 
enabling the EU to develop a decarbonised and secure electricity system. These units can 
minimize RES imbalances, supply more flexibility and balancing to the grid, providing a back-
up to intermittent renewable energy, other than enabling higher RES shares and large-scale 
expansion of renewable resources. 
The renewable share in the power sector for 2030 should move from the current 55.6%1  to above 
60%, by adding at least 60 GW of new renewable capacity to the new capacity already foreseen in 
the final National Energy and Climate Plans for the period 2021 -2030, mostly in the form of wind 
and solar technologies. Correspondingly, new policy measure s should be put in place to close such 
gap, by requiring Member States to increase RES  ambitions in their plans and  setting overarching 
mechanisms at EU level. At the same time, an enabling framework for the development and uptake 
of flexibility solutions should be designed, including in the revision of EEAG. 
Coupling the power sector with end-uses sectors through renewable-based electricity (via direct 
electrification and renewable hydrogen, the latter for hard-to-abate sectors) is a key driver to allow 
the positive spill over effect of decarbonisation of the power system into the rest of the economy , 
where the use of other form of renewable energy is lagging. The smart integration of electricity 
with final uses will significantly decrease both GHG emissions  and energy demand  of the EU’s 
transport, buildings and industry sectors, as electric appliances can deliver equivalent services with 
less energy input  and electricity will be increasingly generated by renewables . Moreover, it will 
enable the integration of  larger shares of renewables generation , thus reducing EU import 
dependency and improving security of supply , ultimately making the transition affordable for 
citizens and businesses.  
To achieve the enhanced 2030 and 2050 climate targets, it is essential to leverage on the benefits 
of increasing electrification of end -uses. Thus, it is more relevant than ever to eliminate from the  
electricity tariff those components that unduly penalize it, i.e. to remove from the tariff charges and 
levies not related to the supply of electricity but to social and environmental policies. 
From a general point of view and improving on past exercises, the IIA should take into account the 
different dimensions of a Sustainable Development, focusing on the social dimension and on the 
means of ensuring a Just Transition for Europe.  In particular, the “technology neutrality” concept 
should evolve and be measured not only against climate change, but also against other SDGs such 
as air quality and circular economy. Furthermore, the IAA should explore cross-sectorial synergies 
and not limit itself to optimizing individual sectorial contributions.

In fact, to meet the Union’s 2030 and 2050 climate objectives, while also guaranteeing sustainable, 
secure and affordable energy for consumers, there is a need to ensure that all sectors fully 
contribute to decarbonisation.  
The use of renewable energy  such as biomass, biofuel s (despite the limited availability due to 
sustainability consideration), synthetic fuels produced from renewable sources (such as hydrogen 
or hydrogen derived fuels) could offer a complementary solution  to decarbonise industrial 
processes and economic sectors  where reducing carbon emissions is both urgent and hard to 
achieve through direct electrification only.  To be effective at reducing GHG emissions, such fuels 
must be produced in a sustainable  way, to avoid supporting solutions not sustainable in the long 
term or that could end up in stranded assets  (e.g. first generation biofuel from food crop , 
disproportionate extension of fossil fuel infrastructure). Hence, we firmly disagree with the 
proposal made by the IIA to support low carbon fuels (“Promote further development and use of 
renewable and other low-carbon fuels…”) within the future framework of Renewable Directive. As 
per the Hydrogen Strategy Communication, “low carbon ” can refer to fuel s/energy vectors  
produced from fossil fuel source s. It is paramount to keep a clear separation between what is 
renewable and what is produced from non-renewable sources that could pose sustainability issues 
in the long terms and not contribute to the reduction of the EU‘s import dependency (e.g. hydrogen 
produced from fossil fuels with CCUS). Such fuels should be clearly differentiated from renewable 
production pathways and excluded from the renewable energy directive scope2, to avoid to build 
a framework for the deployment of renewable sources that considers fossil fuels based solutions at 
the same level of renewable ones. 
Against this background, a first priority is to create a clear  and comprehensive terminology 
(taxonomy) covering all renewable s and low carbon fuels , to avoid ambiguity and allow informed 
choices by consumers. Such classification shall be based not only on the climate performance, based 
on a life cycle approach including direct and indirect GHG emissions (e.g. indirect land use change, 
methane leakage, etc.), but also on the origin of the input /feedstock3 (renewable, non-renewable) 
and would constitute a precondition for a new framework seeking for the decarbonisation and the 
promotion of renewable sources . In this respect, policy support and support schemes should be 
limited to truly renewable energy sources and fuels, including Renewable Hydrogen. With specific 
regard to renewable hydrogen and the hard to abate sectors only, in order to be more effective and 
enable a higher level of decarbonisation, Enel believes that is important to operate also from the 
demand side through mechanisms that guarantee the absorption of the production . In particular, 
the Commission policies should be targeted  to support the industrial consumers via market -based 
tools that fill the economic gap between fossil based and electricity based renewable  hydrogen, in 
order to protect their competitiveness and support renewable hydrogen market development. The 
definition of Renewable Hydrogen in the revised Directive and the identification of the conditions 
for which hydrogen from electrolysis can be considered renewable should ensure:

Furthermore, to support the development and use of renewable fuel s, the implementation of a 
trading system for renewable guarantees of origin (GOs) can be a pivotal instrument.  Guarantees 
of Origin for renewable energy play an important role to stimulate final customers’ demand for 
renewable energy. For instance, the current renewable electricity GO system appears to be a well-
designed and suited tool, as it constitutes a guarantee of reliability, while also enabling  the 
development of “green commercial offers” for consumers willing to pay a premium for it. In order 
to preserve this value, the design of GOs shall ensure a clear distinction between GOs for renewable 
energy and, where applicable, GOs for non-renewable energy. 
 
As stated in the IIA, it is paramount that this init iative will also ensure synergies with other 
legislation, such as the Energy Efficiency  and Energy Performance of Buildings  Directives, the 
Electricity Directive and Electricity Regulation, the mobility strategy, directives and regulations   
Refuel Aviation, Refuel maritime, ETS. In particular, Enel would like to stress out the importance to 
consider the close link and the common goal of RES Directive with the Energy efficiency Directive 
to allow a faster and efficient integ ration of renewables in all end uses sectors and increasing 
flexibility, sustainability as well as energy and resource efficiency. The renewable energy directive 
should also acknowledge the different efficiency potential that the use of RES sources has in the 
different sectors, to avoid wasting resources and to promote their use in sectors where activities 
can be decarbonised with a lower energy input, allowing the optimization of their use.

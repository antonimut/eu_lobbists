RESPONSE TO THE RED II INCEPTION IMPACT ASSESSMENT 
Eurogas welcomes the European Commission’s inception impact assessment on the Revision of 
Directive (EU) 2018/2001 on the promotion of the use of energy from renewable sources.  
All options will be needed to reach sustainability objectives in a way that ensures the scale needed to 
decarbonise EU’s economy. This includes renewable gas in the form of biomethane and hydrogen 
produced through electrolysis, and decarbonised gas – hydrogen from reformed natural gas in 
combination with CCUS. Appro priate policies are needed to accelerate and scale its deployment. A 
holistic and technology neutral approach for mature technologies, while fostering the development of 
commercially non-mature technologies, is primary for climate neutrality to be a success.  
The consultation rightly indicates that achieving climate neutrality is a trans-boundary challenge that 
cannot be solved by national or local action alone.  In this respect, Eurogas considers that national 
cooperation and interstate alliances are key facilitators for the developments of synergies between 
sectors.  
Eurogas notes the four policy option scenarios included in the impact assessment. In this regard, we 
would like to highlight the need to also assess renewable and decarbonised gas targets as pa rt of the 
fourth option. This would ensure greater penetration of renewable energy and would give more 
flexibility to Member States to meet their RES targets.  
Recommendations for a stronger RED II 
Eurogas notes that the revision of the directive must envisage and foster a stronger role for renewable 
and decarbonised gas in all sectors of the economy so as to capitalise on the existing gas infrastructure 
and EU’s industrial potential for developing climate technologies.  
Eurogas supports the Green Deal o bjective of increased overall 2030 GHG reduction targets  that 
consider investor certainty, environmental and social responsibility, a fit for purpose regulatory 
framework inclusive of all clean energy technologies.  
Binding EU level target for renewable and decarbonised gas  
Member States provide little incentive for the uptake of renewable and decarbonised gas, which 
hinders investor confidence.  
1. EU level greenhouse gas intensity reduction target 
Eurogas calls for a binding EU target on gas demand for the reduction of the GHG intensity of gas. 
By 2030 the GHG intensity of the gaseous energy* consumed** in the EU shall be reduced by at 
least 20% compared to 2018 levels, through the use of renewable and decarbonized gases. 
To be eligible, each renewable and decarbonized gas typology production pathway must comply 
with appropriate sustainability and emission reduction criteria in line with RED II and future 
European legislation. Eligible gas typologies  account for the  2030 EU binding target for the 
reduction of the GHG intensity of gas in proportion to its greenhouse gas emissions savings (Life 
Cycle, including negative emissions). In order to avoid a disproportionate administrative burden, a 
list of greenhouse gas emissions savings (Life Cycle)  should be laid down for common gas

2. EU level renewable gas target 
In order to develop of a sustainable gas sector Eurogas supports the introduction in European 
legislation (e.g. revision of the Renewable Energy Directive) of a binding 2030 EU target on 
demand for renewable gases of at least 11 % in terms of energy content of gas consumed*. 
Renewable gases contributing to the “EU level renewable target” account at the same time also 
for the “EU level greenhouse gas intensity reduction target” in line with the eligibility criteria set 
out for “EU level greenhouse gas intensity reduction target”. 
3. Design of the greenhouse gas intensity reduction target and renewable gas target 
It should be possible to comply with the greenhouse gas intensity reduction target and renewable 
gas target  building on existing RED II mechanisms. These should be extended to cover all 
renewable and decarbonised gases. This includes, but is not limited to, the possibility to comply 
with the targets though a harmonized EU wide system of certifications, inter alia, the GO system 
(extended to include GHG intensity information) and sustainability certificates. 
Member States shall be free to define their individual national contributions  towards the “EU 
level greenhouse gas intensity reduction target” and “EU level renewable gas target” no later 
than June 2024.1 2  
When setting their national contributions towards the “EU level greenhouse gas intensity 
reduction target” and “EU level renewable gas target” in their (revised) NECPs, Member States 
may do so, inter alia, by means of measures targeting volumes, energy content or greenhouse gas 
emissions as well as being free to establishing differentiated sectorial targets and obligations (e.g. 
ETS/non ETS). 
The European Commission should facilitate coordination between Member States in areas where 
a common approach is necessary to enable effective implementation (e.g. gas quality standards, 
blending standards, transport, distribution and refueling infrastructure).

EU blueprint for Guarantees of Origin covering all forms of renewable and decarbonised gas 
A harmonized framework for Guarantees of Origin (GO) is necessary to ensure transparency on the 
source of the molecules and improve market signals for renewable and decarbonised gas. The national 
standards for GO should be harmonised according to a European blueprint which would enable GO 
tradability across the EU.  
Member States should also issue GO for decarbonised gases  to facilitate energy disclosure to gas 
consumers opting for renewable and decarbonised gas products. This would ensure a swifter transition 
by increasing consumer awareness and willing ness to demand these products. An obligation for 
Member States, to issue one standard GO for all renewable and decarbonised gases, including GHG 
abatement information should be introduced. It is important to maintain a clear distinction between 
renewable and decarbonised gases. 
A comprehensive terminology for all renewable and decarbonised gases and liquids 
The Renewable Energy Directive defines a set of gases which can be used to generate heat or power. 
Building on considerations included in the gas di rective and regulations, the renewable energy 
directive recognised the existence of: 
(1) biogas, biomass fuels (such as biomethane) which can be injected into the grid or consumed as 
such in Natural Gas Vehicles (NGV) 
(2) renewable fuels of non-biological origin (ReFuNoBios) such as hydrogen produced from power 
(3) other gases produced from waste for instance.  
This approach seems restrictive for certain end uses therefore we propose a more comprehensive set 
of categories that would also cover low-carbon and  decarbonised gas produced from low -carbon 
power or fossil fuels with the application of CCS. These would have to respect strict thresholds in order 
to be considered. The proposal  would require the inclusion of new production pathways in the 
renewable energy directive and in the revision of the internal gas market legislation in order to 
leverage the potential of renewable and decarbonised gas to its full extent. 
These new categories should cover production pathways that include low -carbon electricity and 
reforming/pyrolysis processes linked to natural gas and complemented by carbon storage and 
utilisation processes. The hydrogen produced would then be categorised as decarbonised or low 
carbon depending on the carbon footprint of the overall process. 
Consistency across policies – primary to driving decarbonisation across sectors 
RED II and other revisions and new legislative initiatives should follow the same terminology and build 
synergies in a way that drives decarbonisation. Consistency is primary to ensure that initiatives such 
as the implementation of the sustainable finance regulation do not undermine the rollout of 
renewable and decarbonised gas, infrastructure expansion and retrofitting where more cost-efficient. 
To this end, existing legislation should be implemented, thereby ensuring covering all forms of 
renewable gases included in RED II as well as decarbonised gasses that can enable the energy transition 
by providing solutions to reduce GHG emission as foreseen by the Taxonomy regulation.

Renewable and decarbonised gases should be recognised as an option for decarbonisation 
throughout all sectors.  
The Renewable Energy Directive (RED) II sets an appropriate framework for the mobility sector. Its 
quick implementation into national law is now decisive. However, additional policies are needed, for 
instance for th e building sector, where renewable and decarbonised gases often lack proper 
recognition in national law (see Gebäudeenergiegesetz in Germany or draft Climate Change and Energy 
Transition Law in Spain). Additionally, all renewable gases should count towards RED target and sub-
targets (currently applicable only to hydrogen in transport).  
The revision of the RED II must recognise and value positive externalities of biomethane, which can 
justify the implementation of support policies . The competitiveness of bi omethane is gradually 
increasing, but the missing valorisation of positive externalities related to its production is hampering 
its large-scale development potential in the short term. Positive externalities include additional income 
for farmers as well as  the significant potential for methane emission reductions in the agricultural 
sector. Where technically and economically feasible, t he use of biogas should be reoriented from 
power generation to biomethane injection. 
Additionally, t he revision of RED II should aim to maximise the potential of sustainably produced 
biomethane. The Annex IX of the REDII lists  a number of  feedstocks that can be used for biogas 
production. Eurogas believes this annex should be revised to broaden the scope  of different types of 
feedstock. All biowastes that fit the definition of the Waste Directive (2018/851) shall be included  as 
well as catch crops (also called sequential crops, cover crops, intermediary crops) as they clearly 
provide numerous environmental benefits. 
Deep decarbonisation does not imply increased electrification

consultation suggest increasing the deployment of renewables in the power, heating and cooling, and 
transport sectors. Decarbonising electricity or electrifying processes alone will not enable the deep 
decarbonisation of activities in a timely, just, and cost -effective manner or ensure the reliability and

Gas at the core of alliances for climate leadership 
In this context, Eurogas welcomes European Commission’s proposal for alliances on clean hydrogen 
and low carbon technologies. These could serve as a platform to discuss and design support schemes, 
joint communication efforts and policy recommendations. In doing so, they could eliminate barriers 
and ensure a faster scale-up of technologies.  
In parallel, the Commission should include representatives of the biomethane, power-to-gas, pyrolysis 
and CCUS industries in the new European Commission Industrial Forum . This would offer the 
opportunity for different actors to cooperate on solutions and create a platform for sector integration. 
The development of renewable and decarbonised gas comes with major benefits  
Economic. Europe is a global leader in renewable and  decarbonised gas technologies; ITM Power for 
example, operates the world’s largest electrolyser factory and supplies solutions to customers in 
transport, industry, and the power sector around the world. Europe is also a market leader in anaerobic 
digestors, pyrolysis, carbon capture and storage, and LNG engines and turbines. These technologies 
will revolutionise the way in which we produce energy and goods around the world and Europe must 
maintain this industrial advantage and lead the way towards climate neutrality. 
 
Eurogas considers important that the consultation analyses the  benefits of renewable electricity 
production as well as the  associated costs of integration and distribution  that inevitably arise. New 
infrastructure for the transportation of ren ewable electricity will cost society around 130 billion EUR 
annually. This amount could be saved only by continuing using gaseous energy. Additionally, molecules 
use reduces the cost of extensive renovation of the building sector and power grid expans ion to 
accommodate for all-electric heating.  
Social. Beyond creating economic growth, the development of renewable and decarbonised gas has 
tremendous job creation potential. The Gas f or Climate employment study 4 shows that scaling up 
renewable gases in the EU can create 1.7 million to 2.4 million jobs by 2050, of which 600,000 to 
850,000 would be direct jobs.

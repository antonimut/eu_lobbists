 
Consultation REDII Roadmap 
Feedback from Fern on policy options for forest biomass  
 
Introduction 
Fern is a Brussel based NGO organization with 25 years of experience on EU policies related forests and the 
protection of peoples’ rights. We have four campaigns on consumption, development, trade and climate. 
Fern welcomes the ambition of the Commission’s European Green Deal initiative, its commitment to increasing 
ambition on climate change mitigation, as well as its plans to review the 2030 greenhouse-gas reduction target, the 
renewable energy target and existing climate regulation. We appreciate its recognition of the need to ensure 
policies contribute to the cost-effective deployment of renewable energy sources and to use natural resources more 
efficiently, as well as its Green Oath to do no harm by preserving biodiversity and reducing air pollution. We’ve also 
welcomed the Biodiversity Strategy, notably its initiatives to restore forest lands and assess the sustainability of 
forest biomass use for energy. 
We welcome recognition in the Climate Target Plan Communication of the need to increase efforts to mitigate 
climate change, increase renewable energy consumption and enhance the EU’s terrestrial carbon sinks by 2030, 
while protecting forests and safeguard the environment. It is helpful in that context, that the Communication 
recognises that the role of bioenergy - and particularly forest biomass - towards 2030 renewable energy goals is 
limited. 
Following the Inception Impact Assessment, the review of the Renewable Energy Directive (RED) aims to (i) ensure 
renewables contributes to the achievement of higher climate ambition; (ii) the RED contributes to reaching climate 
neutrality by 2050, while promoting resource-efficient economic growth, job creation, technological leadership, 
pollution reduction and biodiversity preservation; and (iii) find synergies with the energy efficiency directive. 
This briefing gives (a) recommendations for assessing policy options on forest biomass  (in a broader policy 
framework) and is followed by (b) a note to justify the recommendations provided.  
Recommendations for assessing policy options 
The policy assumption that bioenergy is carbon neutral is central to the way this form of 
renewable energy is regulated through the REDII, and how it is supported through financial 
mechanisms.  
As shown below, scientists agree that this assumption is not generally valid for forest biomass. 
Negative impacts on the climate, biodiversity, resource efficiency or air quality are to a great 
extent associated with the scale of deployment and type of feedstock use, rather than with the 
sustainable production/mobilisation of biomass resources – this makes the current 
sustainability approach in the REDII largely irrelevant for ensuring the (increasing) use of  forest 
biomass for energy is sustainable (further explained below).  
The role of forest biomass under the REDII is becoming more difficult to justify, especially in the 
context of the need to urgently reduce emissions in the next 10-15 years. Significant policy

revisions are needed, and we advise the Commission to holistically assess policy options in 
three areas: (i) Reducing negative impacts through sustainability criteria (REDII), (ii) Ensure 
emissions from biomass burning are fully accounted through the EU’s carbon accounting 
framework (EU ETS / LULUCF), (iii) Reduce financial incentives for forest biomass through the 
REDII, Energy Taxation Directive (ETD), Energy Efficiency Directive (EED), related State aid rules 
and various EU funding programmes. 
For the REDII, we advise the Commission to assess policy options to address the scale of 
deployment of forest biomass for energy and its eligibility for support, regulate the type 
biomass feedstock that can be used, and direct a more optimal end-use of biomass as a limited 
resource, including:  
1) Exclude forest biomass as a form of renewable energy (except industrial / mill residues) 
or introduce a phase-out trajectory for its role towards reaching renewables targets;  
2) Count forest biomass as renewable but discontinue its eligibility for financial 
incentives, including the zero-rating for biomass combustion emissions under the EU 
Emissions Trading System (ETS), carbon/energy tax exemptions, renewable support 
schemes (notably operating aid), support for high-efficient co-generation, and direct EU 
funding; 
3) Count forest biomass as renewable, but introduce a quantitative cap on the role of 
forest biomass to achieve renewable energy targets, in combination with additional 
sustainability criteria, including on feedstock type (no roundwood use and limited 
residue harvest) and restrictions on end-use (NB ending use of forest biomass for 
power-only and restricting the role of forest biomass for heating and cooling (H&C)). 
 
▪ When assessing the nature and level of the renewable energy target or subtargets (e.g. 
for renewable H&C or advanced biofuels), we urge the Commission to assess ways to 
restrict the role that biomass can play for different end-uses. In this context, it would 
also be helpful to review Annex IX and the role of forest biomass for advanced biofuels, 
to avoid excessive use of harvest residues. 
▪ In light of these points, we urge the Commission to broaden the scope of the review 
beyond Article 29 and include Articles 4, 23 and 25 (support to renewable electricity and 
role of biomass in H&C and transport) and Annex IX. 
Justification  
The use of forest biomass is out of control. Since the introduction of the Renewable Electricity 
Directive in 2001, support to forest biomass has rapidly increased. Today, forest biomass 
provides around 60-70 per cent of bioenergy feedstock, and over 35 per cent of the total EU’s 
renewables mix. Today’s levels of forest biomass use have surpassed projected levels and 
estimated levels of sustainable supply (see table 1).

Today, EU Member States spend billions on the use of forest biomass for heating and power 
each year, mostly in the form of operating aid, while its role for the climate and a sustainable 
energy transition is limited or counterproductive. 
As was already recognised by the Impact Assessment on bioenergy sustainability for the recast 
of the Renewable Energy Directive, the assumption of carbon neutrality of the use of forest 
biomass used for energy is not generally valid. The climate impact of biomass use is understood 
to depend on many factors, including scale of deployment and resulting harvest levels, type of 
feedstock use, counterfactuals, and conversion efficiencies.2 There is a threat biomass burning 
will lead to an increase in smokestack emissions and contributes to a rapid decrease in the 
ability of EU forests to remove carbon-dioxide from the atmosphere (see Figure 1) – which may 
annul (or even exceed) the emission reductions from avoided fossil fuel use. The combustion of 
forest biomass leads to more smokestack emissions than coal, while the ‘carbon payback-
period’ depends on many factors and can take decades. The role of biomass thus becomes 
more problematic considering the need to reduce emissions in the next 15 years to stay under 
2 degrees warming.

Unlike other forms of renewable energy, bioenergy relies on the combustion of carbon and the 
use of a limited natural resource and is associated with relatively high external / environmental 
costs. The use of forest biomass as an alternative for fossil fuel has increased air pollution since 
2005 and has a negative impact on biodiversity (both in the case of roundwood and residues 
use) and resource efficiency.3 Scientists have raised the concern that ‘targets for renewable 
energy have resulted in a surge in the consumption of woody biomass’, while today energy 
accounts for almost half (48%) of total reported wood use. At the same time, its use is 
increasing primary energy consumption.  
In this light, it is concerning that the Commission’s assessment of the National Energy and 
Climate Plans (NECPs) found that Member States have not sufficiently assessed the negative 
impacts of expanding bioenergy on carbon sinks, biodiversity, water and air pollution and that 
they lack detail on ‘how to supply the required sustainable biomass, by feedstock and origin and 
trajectories of forest biomass, and how they are aligned with measures to maintain and 
increase the carbon sink.’ 
Negative external costs from the use of forest biomass are currently not adequately mitigated 
through EU policies or priced-in to current State aid assessments, which leads to a skewed 
image of its cost-effectiveness as a renewable energy option. Not factoring in external costs of 
forest biomass may result in an inherently preferential treatment of biomass and hinder the 
development of other – more innovative and cleaner – technologies, undermining the principle 
of technology-neutrality embedded in the RED and the Guidelines for State aid for

environmental protection and energy (EEAG). This is further reinforced by the design of the

All factors combined, the inclusion of forest biomass in the REDII is difficult to justify, both from 
its predominant aim of reducing greenhouse gas emissions and for coherence with other 
environmental and State aid objectives. The revised REDII does very little to mitigate negative 
impacts (see below). It is therefore crucial that radical policy changes are considered, in the 
REDII and beyond, to ensure its role of biomass achieves positive climate effects in the short 
term and does not lead to negative environmental impacts or market distortions in the energy- 
and raw material markets.  
Why the current policy framework is not adequate 
Member States have a large discretion on allocating State aid, deciding on their energy mix and 
regulating forest management practices. Combined with the current policy framework for 
forest biomass, there is a risk that Member States inefficiently over-invest in biomass 
mobilisation and combustion (substitution), while allowing for forest carbon sinks to deplete. 
Indications from Member States on planned biomass use and Land Use, Land Use Change and 
Forestry (LULUCF) sinks already show this risk is real, undermining the EU’s broader climate and 
environmental objectives.  
There are broadly three flaws in how the current policy framework regulates the use of forest 
biomass for renewable energy: (i) external costs are not fully mitigated through other EU 
policies (REDII) (ii) external costs such as from greenhouse gas emissions use are not adequately 
priced-in (EU ETS Directive/LULUCF Regulation); and (iii) forest biomass receives financial 
incentives through a wide range of mechanisms, including through the EU ETS, the REDII, the 
EED, the ETD and several EU funding programmes (CAP, RD&D, et al).  
There are four main reasons why the REDII is inadequate for pricing in / mitigating 
environmental impacts:  
1. The REDII confirms the policy assumption that forest biomass is carbon neutral. The 
calculation for the greenhouse gas savings threshold for the use of forest biomass 
ignores both emissions from forest harvests and emissions from the smokestack. 
2. The REDII still includes forest biomass as a RES option on an equal footing with other 
RES technologies, while not fully mitigating its external costs.  
3. The REDII does not adequately mitigate negative impacts (costs) on the climate, 
biodiversity, air pollution, or resource efficiency. Its ‘risk-based approach’ focuses on 
‘sustainable production’ of biomass harvests, in the sense that it relies on existing rules 
in Member States on Sustainable Forest Management (SFM) and implementation of the 
LULUCF Regulation. This fails to address the questions whether an increase in the use of 
forest biomass for energy is climate-friendly, sustainable, or resource efficient.

4. Criteria on end-use are very weak and do not provide guidance to Member States on 
where biomass could play a (limited) role in decarbonisation. They still allow for biomass

installations as well as in large-scale deployment in the heating sector.

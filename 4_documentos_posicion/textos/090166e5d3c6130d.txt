EBB Position on the 
Increase of the EU climate ambition for 2030 
 
 
Background 
The European Green Deal aims at making the European Union (EU)  the world’s first climate -neutral 
continent. To this end, the European Commission  (EC) intends to  increase the EU’s 2030 target for 
greenhouse gas (GHG) emission reductions to at least -50% and towards -55% compared to 
1990 levels (1). 
 
The EC is currently working on an Impact Assessment (IA) study looking at the economic, social , and 
environmental implications of the plan. This IA is due in September 2020 . Looking for feedback from 
stakeholders, the EC has launched a public consultation asking for stakeholder views. This EBB position 
paper aims at responding to this consultation by detailing our policy proposals. 
 
Summary of EBB main policy asks 
Biodiesel (2) represents a significant renewable energy source in the EU. With more than 11 million tonnes 
of biodiesel produced per year, the EU is the world leader in the production and use of biodiesel 
for transport (3). To continue the role of biodiesel in a meaningful and long -term decarbonization of the 
transport sector, it is critical that upcoming EU policy actions to increase the EU climate ambitions consider 
the following EBB policy asks:

Why biodiesel 
The production of this  “made in Europe” green diesel helps reducing a yearly EU diesel deficit of around 
25-35 million tonnes of imported fossil diesel.  Biodiesel avoided costs of imported fuels of € 7.6 billion in 
2010 by using domestically produced  biofuels, of which biodiesel alone amounted to as much as €  5.8 
billion of savings ( 4). Forecasts project that avoided fuel import costs could reach at least € 50 billion in 
2020 in 2010 prices (5). The EC itself estimated that “a 30% share of renewables in 2030 would help create 
about 600,000 jobs and save €258bn in fossil fuel imports” (6). 
 
Conventional biodiesel, as well as waste-based and advanced biodiesel, are the most widely used biofuels 
in Europe today, and are a cruci al part of Europe’s decarbonisation path towards carbon neutrality. The 
most recent statistic put forward by the International Energy Agency (IEA) concludes that the production 
of transport biofuels grew by just 4% in 2017 (7). The IEA considers that, to achieve its Sustainable 
Development Scenario (SDS) 2030 target, use of biofuels needs to triple , driven by cost 
reductions of advanced biofuels, widespread sustainability governance and more adoption in aviation and 
marine transport. This reinforces the argument that all sustainable biofuels are essential to meet the 
Paris Agreement , and therefore it is essential that upcoming EU measures to increase its ambition 
incentivize changing to a less carbon-intensive fuel such as biodiesel. 
 
Another additional ben efit from biodiesel production is the production of feed. A s the EU is still heavily 
dependent on protein imports, European biodiesel production helps the food and animal feed sectors 
through the production of valuable co-products such as proteins for animal feed, hence decreasing imports 
from third countries . Mostly from European produced rapeseed, every tonne of biodiesel produced 
from crops generates two tonnes of EU -made vegetable proteins. This supports EU 
independency in food and feed supply and balances the 70% EU reliance on imported proteins. 
 
Moreover, downstream, biodiesel’s main co-product, green glycerine, replaces chemicals in a wide variety 
of fields such as medical disinfectant, cosmetics, food, feed, etc.

A. Decarbonisation of the transport sector is fundamental to deliver on the European Green 
Deal objectives – higher renewable transport targets are crucial 
The EBB welcomes and fully supports an increased EU decarbonization ambition for 2030, with 
a view of achieving a climate neutral EU by 2050, or even earlier. Achieving this objective is critical to 
reduce GHG emissions from all sectors of the Euro pean economy, keeping a global temperature rise this 
century well below 2.º C above pre-industrial levels, and pursuing efforts to limit the temperature increase 
even further to 1.5.º C. 
 
The transport sector remains the only EU sector where GHG emissions keep increasing every 
year and could reach close to 45% of all EU economy emissions in 2040 -50 (8), contrary to all other EU 
economy sectors which have experienced falling GHG emissions.  
 
Moreover, consumer embrace of electric cars on a wide -scale is likely to take time, and dela y a broader 
roll-out and use of these vehicles. Therefore, while that does not happen, biofuels are a readily available 
alternative to immediately decarbonize the transport sector. 
 
Therefore, if the existing target for renewables in transport in the EU Renewable Energy Directive (RED II) 
is not strengthen, biofuels incorporation is likely to decrease, and a corresponding increase in fossil fuel 
use would be inevitable. Similar reasoning should be done in the GHG emission reduction target in the EU 
Effort Sharing Regulation (ESR). 
 
Policy proposals: 
 
1. Ensuring the stability and the long -term predictability of the 2050 EU legislative framework will 
unlock industry investments in renewable fuels . The EU legislative framework up to 2050 
should reflect this predictability and ensure that all sources of renewable energy are 
used in a sustainable and cost-effective way. 
 
2. To facilitate the achievement of the 2050 climate-neutrality objective, the EBB supports higher 
renewables and GHG emissions targets for 2030. 
 
Only by putting in place ambitious specific efforts in transport that the EU will be able to avoid an 
exponential increase in GHG emissions in this sector.  Therefore, an ambitious target for 
renewables in the transport sector should be part of these higher targets, with at least 
a 20% effective incorporation of renewables in the transport energy mix by 2030. 
 
This number could be further increased, subject to an assessment on feasibility and cost -
effectiveness to be included in the IA performed by the EC. 
 
3. To match this increase ambition in the uptake of renewables in transport, similar strengthening 
should happen by setting higher GHG emissions targets in the EU ESR sector . 
 
4. Moreover, the obligation to decrease the carbon intensity of transport fuels ( 9) set by 
the EU Fuel Quality Directive (FQD) should be maintained and progressively increased 
to at least 16% to match the increased efforts of the other economic sectors.

B. Increase in EU ambition should be based on tangible and evidence-based policies 
While an enhanced EU ambition is warmly welcomed by the EBB, it should be translated into concrete and 
pratical actions to ensure an effective and successful decarbonization of the European transport sector .  
 
Therefore, while the abovementioned increase in ambition is welcomed, increased EU new targets should 
only be set (and updated) after the detailed IA study is concluded by the EC, in particular looking at the 
feasibility and cost of a higher GHG emission reduction target by 2030. 
 
Policy proposals: 
 
1. The quality and completeness of this IA will be crucial for the EU to be able to prepare, alongside 
ambitious and long-term plans for its decarbonization and climate neutrality by 2050, a credible 
plan with concrete actions  to translate these ambitions into reality in the upcoming 
revisions of the relevant EU legislation towards 2030 (10). 
 
2. While electrification is likely to experience a significant development in relation to passenger cars 
in urban areas, it will need to be combined for long distance transport with higher blends of biofuels 
in Heavy-Duty Vehicles (HDVs), aviation and the maritime sector. 
 
This electrification should respect the principles of additionality and ensure that the 
electricity used comes from renewable sources. 
 
C. B10 blend replaces the B7 as standard diesel fuel  
Commercially available biofuels are the most efficient and viable means to achieve green and low emissions 
in transport. As a minimum across the EU, to ensure higher use of renewable fuels in transport, it is crucial 
to continue the work on new fuel standards and eliminate regulatory hurdles.  
 
In addition to higher blends, an easy and efficient way to incentivise biodiesel and biofuels development 
would be by recognising the GHG emission savings of biofuels in the EU CO2 emission standards 
for vehicles . This approach would replace the existing “tank-to-wheel” approach, which  misleadingly 
labels Electric Vehicles (EVs) as having zero emissions. 
 
Policy proposals: 
 
1. To replace the current B7 by B10 blend across the EU The upcoming revision of the EU Fuel 
Quality Directive (FQD) is the right tool to implement B10 across the EU as diesel standard.  
 
2. The increase in EU ambition should also provide the right framework to promote the 
development of higher biodiesel blends  such as B30, B100 and HVO100, especially in those 
sectors where electric mobility is not a viable option  such as long -distance road freight, and 
dedicated fleets.

D. New deployment of biofuels should be promoted in sectors such as Heavy-Duty Vehicles, 
Aviation, Off Road transport, and Maritime transport 
Reducing GHG emissions from transport is challenging, especially in the case of long-haul buses and 
Heavy-Duty Vehicles (HDVs) (11), off-road transport, aviation, and the maritime sector, where 
electrification is not a viable option. In fact, contrarily to passenger cars, these sectors are foreseen to have 
an increase in fuel consumption. 
 
Therefore, and while electrification is likely to experience a significant development in relation to passenger 
cars in urban areas, for long distance transport it will need to be combined with higher blends of biofuels 
in HDVs, planes and ships. The reduction of emissions in these specific transport modes will rely significantly 
on the promotion and uptake of the use of biodiesel and biokerosene. 
 
Policy proposals: 
 
Up to 2030, the EC should consider implementing specific measures to decarbonize the abovementioned 
sectors, including by: 
 
1. Developing separate targets for biofuels and CO 2 emission standards for Heavy and Light Duty 
Vehicles (vans, buses and trucks – all keeping a full diesel mobility in next decade at least); 
2. A specific target for bio-kerosene in aviation; 
3. A specific target for renewable fuels in the maritime sector. 
 
These specific measures should protect existing and future investments in renewable fuel pathways , and 
respect an integrated approach for designing incentive policies  that considers possible disruptions that 
outcompete existing biofuels producers. 
 
E. Biofuels’ contribution to the decarbonization of transport should be recognized in the 
framework of EU Vehicles CO2 standards regulations, in particular for Heavy Duty Vehicles 
Using a Life Cycle Approach (LCA) to assess fuels GHG emission footprint would incentivize vehicle 
manufacturers to put on the market vehicles that accept higher blends of biofuels.  Therefore, the EU 
assessment of GHG emissions of fuels should transition into an LCA , or “Well-to Wheel”, approach instead 
of a tail-pipe methodology. 
 
Policy proposals: 
 
1. The EU should work to recognize the GHG emission savings of biofuels in the existing EU 
CO2 emission standards regulations for HDVs, and envisage a similar approach for passenger 
cars.

F. Progressive and realistic deployment of advanced biofuels on top of conventional biofuels 
The European biodiesel industry was born from a political ambition that is still ready to serve: develop an 
EU-made green fuel to improve energy security, progress towards a low carbon economy and strengthen 
the independence and revenue of European farmers.  Our industry continues committed to these values, 
with a vision to continuously improve its performance  and to contribute for the EU’s long -term vision to 
decarbonize the transport sector and achieve a carbon neutral Europe by mid-century. 
 
While not ideal, the post-2020 EU Renewable Energy Directive (RED II) agreement kept EU incentives and 
measures supporting biodiesel, and determined that only biofuels with a high risk of Indirect Land -Use 
Change (ILUC) should be phased -out, but this phase out is not applicable to  sustainable crop-based 
biofuels. 
 
Therefore, to respond to the goals of COP21 , meet the EU’s overall decarbonisation goal s in the non-ETS 
sectors (12), of which transport is the most challenging sector, as well as to achieve a more ambitions EU-
wide goal of GHG emission reductions, biodiesel has a key role to play. 
 
Policy proposals: 
 
1. The EU cannot roll back its ambitions and should build upon the RED II for the period up to 2050. 
To this end, the revised EU policies on biofuels from arable crops, as a share of 
renewables in transport, must provide a long-term stable legislative framework. 
 
2. In addition to advanced, waste-based, and renewable biodiesel, sustainable crop-based biodiesel 
has an immediate and cost-effective role to play in the EU’s increase in ambition for 2030 and the 
decarbonization of the European transport sector. 
 
To this end, the existing cap on crop-base biofuels should be reassessed upwards, taking 
into consideration the current crop-based biofuels market share, higher renewables and emissions 
reduction targets, and the fact that high ILUC-risk biofuels should be fully phased-out by 2030. 
 
Also, the certification criteria for low ILUC-risk biofuels are important to be developed 
without delay, and being as practical as possible. 
 
G. Improve effectiveness of sustainability criteria and traceability requirements for biofuels 
Sustainability is a precondition for any form of renewable energy to be able to contribute to decarbonisation 
objectives. The European biodiesel chain complies today with strict sustainability criteria laid out in the 
existing RED and is committed to continuous improvements. 
 
European biodiesel already abide s by the most stringent sustainability rules in the world, which prevent 
feedstocks from being grown on deforested land, peat lands or areas with a high biodiversity value.  
 
Policy proposal: 
 
1. For the sector to continue its leadership in sustainability certi fication, the quick and effective  
implementation of the single European transparency and traceability database for all 
biofuels (as foreseen in the RED II) is crucial to have existing national and voluntary 
certification schemes interlinked to avoid any kind of potential fraud.

H. Road transport should not be included in the EU ETS 
Reducing GHG emissions and promoting renewables in the transport sector is comparatively more difficult 
and costly than in any other sector. 
 
Policy proposal: 
 
1. Therefore, proposals for including EU road transport sector in the existing EU Emissions 
Trading System (ETS) should be rejected. 
 
Decarbonisation of land/road transport is driven by several other means, incl. RED II, Effort Sharing 
Regulation (ESR), Fuel Quality Directive (FQD) article 7a, and taxation policies. They have proven 
to be somewhat successful in decarbonising transport, although they could step -up their level of 
ambition. 
 
RED II has the intention to drive the adoption of emerging new feedstock s and advanced biofuels 
based on raw materials listed on RED II Annex IX, part A. A cross -sectoral ETS approach would 
eliminate the incentive to invest in first-of-their-kind production units, as ETS allowance price levels 
are very unlikely to provide the economic attractiveness needed to operate these units profitably. 
 
Such inclusion, given present and future levels of ETS allowance  prices, would be very unlikely to 
trigger the important efforts that need to be done to decarbonise the EU transport sector. This 
would also mean that, under the same emission cap, other ETS sectors would have to reduce more 
their emissions as well as pay more f or emissions allowances they would need. Transport sector 
would continue to use fossil fuels instead of reducing their emissions more than there is a possibility 
to have electrification in place.

The European Biodiesel Board (EBB)  is a non -profit organisation established in January 1997. 
Today, the EBB gathers 65 members across 21 Member-States, which represents 75% of the European 
biodiesel output. Biodiesel, including both FAME and HVO,  is the main European solution to reduce 
emissions from transport and dependence on imported oil. EBB aims to promote the use of biodiesel in 
the European Union and is committed to fulfil International standards for sustainability in GHG emissions 
and sustainable feedstock.  The EBB is constantly wor king towards the development of improved and 
greener technologies.

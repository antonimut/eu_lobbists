In December 2019, the EU member states agreed that climate neutrality by 2050 the latest is 
necessary for the EU to contribute its part to the Paris Agreement from 2015. This is soon to be 
enshrined by law. This ambitious long-term climate target is at the heart of the European Green 
Deal, that sets out a plan to revise all necessary regulations and directives in order to reach cli-
mate neutrality, including the Renewable Energy Directive (2018/2001/EU) (REDII).  
 
Danish Energy highly welcomes the European Commission’s initiative to review and revise the 
REDII in order to promote renewable energy production and utilization in the EU. The directive 
is based on the EU’s current 2030 climate and energy framework aiming at a 40% GHG-reduc-
tion in 2030 and will thus fail to contribute to the new and increased 2030 and 2050 climate tar-
gets of the EU. 
 
Our feedback is divided into the following 3 headlines: 
1. Increase overall RES-target 
2. Definitions of renewable energy 
3. Renewables in non-ETS sectors 
 
An accelerated build-out and deployment of renewables into our energy systems and sectors is 
absolutely vital. We hope this feedback will provide valuable input for the revision of the REDII. 
 
1. Increase the overall RES-target in line with the 2030 Climate Target Plan  
The current target of a share of 32% renewables in the final energy consumption is outdated 
and not aligned with the Paris Agreement nor the Union’s target of climate neutrality in 2050.  
 
Danish Energy supports the Commission’s proposal to increase the Union’s GHG-reduction tar-
get for 2030 from the current 40% to at least 55%. In order to reach this target, the share of re-
newables in our energy system must increase accordingly. We urge the Commission to in-
crease the target from 32% to at least 40% in 2030.

Being the largest emitter, the energy sector has enormous potential to reduce GHG-emissions 
in the EU. At the same time, renewable energy represents the key to unlock the green transition 
towards a net-zero emitting society in 2050, as many sectors cost-efficiently can be decarbon-
ized by replacing fossil energy with renewable-based electricity. 
 
The Commission’s 2030 climate target impact assessment predicts a RES share of between 
38% to 40% in EU’s gross final energy consumption in 20302.  
 
This increased share of renewables is mainly driven by the large-scale electrification and green-
ing of electricity. The Commission’s own estimates predict that the share of electricity in final en-
ergy consumption will grow from 23% today to 30% in 20303. At the same time, the share of re-
newable electricity in the electricity mix will more than double to around 65%4. 
 
The estimated share of renewables in final energy consumption assumes that the EU will need 
12-14 GW renewable hydrogen electrolysers in 2030 to reach the -55% climate target. That is 
significantly lower than the Commission’s political ambition to install 40 GW electrolysers by 
20305. The political ambition is almost three times bigger and will require significantly more re-
newable energy. A share of 40% renewables in 2030 should thus be considered an absolute 
minimum. 
 
In the Commission’s public consultation on the new 2030 climate target, almost 70% off all re-
spondents supported a target higher than 40% renewable energy share in 2030. This underlines 
that the European population, organizations and businesses are capable and willing to deliver 
on a much-needed higher renewables target. 
 
Currently, the main tool for reaching the RES-target are the National Energy and Climate Plans 
(NECPs). However, the recently adopted plans are developed according to the current 2030 cli-
mate and energy framework. The Commission’s assessment shows that the plans will reach a 
range of 33.1% to 33.7% renewables in 2030 at Union level6. To reach more than 40%, member 
states must step up their efforts.  
 
The Commission should encourage member states to develop plans aligned with the needed 
development towards climate neutrality, meaning a GHG-reduction of at least 55% in 2030. Ad-
ditionally, a better implementation and monitoring of national efforts must be ensured, and the 
Commission should consider introducing national RES-targets.

2. Definitions of renewable energy 
One of the directive’s major purposes is to adapt definitions for different renewable and low-car-
bon energy sources. Clear definitions are important to create stability and send the right signals 
to investors and companies. Currently, the REDII lacks a clear definition of renewable hydro-
gen. 
 
We support the Commission’s definition of renewable and low-carbon hydrogen in the recently 
published Hydrogen Strategy7. The strategy defines renewable hydrogen as hydrogen produced 
via electrolysis with electricity stemming from renewable sources, e.g. wind or solar, while low-
carbon hydrogen represents fossil-based hydrogen combined with carbon capture.  
 
It is important to develop a clear and comprehensive terminology of hydrogen consistent with 
other classifications, definitions and taxonomies in EU legislation to create certainty and to steer 
investments in the right direction. Market participants demanding and using hydrogen must be 
able to trust and verify that the product they buy is in fact renewable. We therefore strongly rec-
ommend that the definition of hydrogen in the revised REDII is aligned with the EU Hydrogen 
Strategy. 
 
The Hydrogen Strategy states that hydrogen-derived synthetic fuels are to be considered re-
newable if the hydrogen is renewable. However, the carbon source should also be considered. 
Carbon for these fuels can come from renewable sources such as sustainable biomass or bio-
gas as well as non-renewable sources. For these fuels to be truly renewable both the hydrogen 
and the carbon should be renewable. 
 
Furthermore, a European system of certification for renewable and low-carbon fuels, based no-
tably on full life cycle green-house gas emission savings and sustainability criteria, should be 
established. 
 
3. Renewables in non-ETS sectors 
As the Commission’s consultation roadmap rightfully states, renewables have not been de-
ployed in sectors such as transport, heating and cooling and industry as much as in the electric-
ity sector. The renewables directive should promote electrification of these sectors.  
 
We urge the Commission to realise and implement the energy system integration strategy an-
nounced in July. Creating a more inter-connected energy system with a holistic view on energy 
systems is one of the main pillars of the green transition.

Over the past decade, the electricity sector has shown a significant decarbonization trend de-
creasing its CO2-emissions by 29 percentage-points from 2013-2019. In the meantime, emis-
sions in the transport sector are on the rise and the industry sector has kept a more or less flat 
reduction curve with slight CO2-reductions8.  
 
To support the decarbonization of these sectors, sectoral targets in REDII should be revised 
and strengthened. 
 
The current RED II sets a 2030 target of at least 14% renewable energy within the final energy 
consumption in the transport sector. As the electrification of road transportation continues to 
grow and with the expected introduction of fuels based on renewable hydrogen, this target 
should be revised upwards. The Commission estimates that the share of RES in the transport 
sector in 2030 should be 24% in order to reach the overall climate target of 55% GHG-emis-
sions reduction9. Even then, road transport, which accounts for a fifth of the EU’s total emis-
sions, will only see a decrease in GHG-emissions of 20%. To accelerate the decarbonization of 
the transport sector, the sectoral RES-target should be increased to at least 24%, preferably 
higher.   
 
Heating and cooling make up 50% of the EU’s annual energy consumption. Electrification of 
heating systems by replacing fossil fuel-based heating systems with electrical heat pumps will 
contribute substantially to the green transition. To accelerate the green transition in buildings, 
the current target of 1.3%-points annual increase in the share of renewable energy in the heat-
ing and cooling sector should be revised upwards in line with the 2030 target of 55% GHG-
emissions reduction. 
 
The current REDII exempts ‘efficient district heating and cooling’ from the application of the re-
quirements regarding annual renewable share in the sector. Defined in Directive 2012/27/EU in 
article 2(48), efficient district heating or cooling are systems using at least 50% renewable en-
ergy, 50% waste heat, 75% cogenerated heat or 50% of a combination of such energy and 
heat. This will e.g. allow gas-fired CHP plants to be exempted from transitioning, slowing the de-
carbonization of the sector. The review of REDII should take this issue into account.

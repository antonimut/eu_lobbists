Mytilineos is one of the largest industrial companies in Greece, with activities in numerous sectors 
(including Metallurgy, Electricity generation and supply, Gas trading, and EPC works). One of our key 
lines of business concerns the operation of the largest vertically integrated alumina/aluminium 
production facility in Europe, where roughly 185,000 tonnes of primary aluminium are produced 
annually. The company is als o making significant strides in the field of aluminium recycling, having 
recently completed the takeover of ‘EPALME’, the largest independent producer of secondary 
(recycled) aluminium in Greece. This enables Mytilineos to produce another 11,000 tonnes of 
recycled aluminium each year, with plans to further scale up the production capacity so as to reach 
50,000 tonnes by the year 2022. 
 
Our company  recently announced an ambitious pledge to ensure that 100% of its aluminium 
production will be based on carbon -neutral electricity by 2030 1. The primary aluminium production 
process is already electrified (in line with the European Commission’s Long-Term Strategy2), meaning 
that the EU’s primary aluminium industry is a  genuine frontrunner in the fight against clima te 
change. A decarbonised electricity supply would reduce our aluminium plant ’s carbon footprint by 
over 80% . However, consuming renewable electricity remains a significant challenge f or primary 
aluminium producers, as well as for electro-intensive consumers more generally. This was confirmed 
in a report that was published by DG ENER last year, which specifically mentions that “it is apparent 
that more can be done to facilitate corporate sourcing of renewables in the aluminium industry”3.

electricity consumption , removing these barriers is a pre requisite to achieving a carbon -neutral 
electricity system . Facilitating these ‘hard to abate ’ consumers  in their attempts to consume 
renewable electricity would pave the way for a rapid  decarbonisation of the European power sector

Industrial RES Sourcing: Barriers and Solutions 
 
Electro-intensive industrial processes (such a s prim ary aluminium smelting) tend to require an 
extremely steady and uni nterrupted supply of massive volumes of electricity.  Indicatively, our 
company’s aluminium plant consumes around 2.85 TW h of (baseload) electricity each year, which is 
equal to around 5% of the total consumption in Greece . This makes it very difficult -and expensive- 
to co ver this demand exclusively using renewable electricity generation, which tends to be much 
more variable and unpredictable.

In order to ensure an uninterrupted supply of electricity, the variable renewa ble production has to 
be ‘firmed up’ using some form of controllable generation, which es sentially covers the gaps in the 
renewable generation.  Thus, consuming renewable electricity leads to numerous additional costs 
(so-called ‘firming costs’, which are also discussed in the Industrial Transformation Masterplan 8). 
This is particularly problematic for electro -intensive consumers, who are incredibly sensitive to 
electricity costs . For example, electricity tends to account f or around 40% of the  overall cost of 
producing primary aluminium 9. Indeed, the Commission identified firming costs ( which ar e 
sometimes also referred to as ‘shaping costs’) as the most relevant obstacle preventing industrial 
consumers from signing RES PPAs10.  
 
This is the reason why, until now, the only cases of renewable PPAs in the primary aluminium sector 
are in the Nordics,  where the high levels of market liquidity and access to massive volumes of  
captive hydropower generation enable the ‘firming up’ of variable w ind production in a cost -
competitive and low-carbon manner. The European Commission’s study on industrial RES sou rcing11 
notes that even companies that have been able to c onsume renewable e lectricity in Norway have 
not been able to source renewab le ele ctricity for their plants in the EU. For this reason, the 
Commission concludes that further policy action is required in order to facilitate the corporate 
sourcing of ren ewables in the alu minium industry, and -by extension - European industry more 
generally.

The most efficient -and effective - way of facilitating industrial RES sourcing would be to provide 
compensation for the additional costs incurred by electro -intensive consumers when consuming 
decarbonised electricity . This could be achieved within t he con text of the Important Projects of 
Common European Interest (IPCEI) framework. The Strategic Forum for Important Pro jects of 
Common European Interest has already identified12 ‘Low CO2 Emissions Industry’ as a strategic value 
chain and called for the es tablishment of IPCEIs in this field ( although the Strategic Forum’s report 
focused almost exclusively on the reduction of emissions that occur directly from industrial processes, 
whereas the  issue of decarbonising industrial electricity supply was largely -and unjustifiably - 
overlooked). 
 
Providing compensation for firming costs would enable industrial consumers to procure the required 
volumes of decarbonised electricity at competitive prices, an issue that was specifically identified in 
the Industrial Transformation Masterpla n13 as a key enabler for the decarbonisation of E uropean 
industry. As noted above, the main reason why  renewable electricity is now relatively cheap to 
produce is the massive support that renewable electricity generation has received over the past two 
decades. The same strategy that has already proved successful in boosting the generation of 
renewable electricity (i.e. targeted support) should now be applied to the consumption of renewable 
electricity, in order to ensure the full integration of renewables i nto our electricity system . This is 
also important to ensure that the decreasi ng cost of RES production actually translates into lower 
electricity prices for consumers (as claimed in the Inception Impact Assessment). 
 
Compensation for firming costs could partly replace the current RES support schemes, in a way 
that actually ensures the full integration of variable RES generation into our electricity system (i.e. 
without overlooking the consumption si de), while also minimising the total system cost . Despite 
the crucial role that support schemes have played in kick -starting the transition towards a 
renewables-based electricity system, it is becoming increasingly apparent that support schemes also 
create a strong disincentive for renewable producers to sign PPAs with industrial consumers . This 
issue was also identified by the European Commission in its report on industrial RES sourcing14.

Support schemes enable renewable producers to receive guaranteed revenues at a level that tends 
to be significantly higher than the average market price for electricity. Renewable producers have no 
economic incentive to sell their electricity to an industrial consumer (via PPA) at a price that is lower 
than the one they could receive under a support scheme . This constitutes a massive barrier to the 
signing of industrial RES PPAs, which Member States are r equired to remedy in line with the 
provisions of RED II 15. Of course, the decreas ing cost of renewable electricity generation -and, in 
particular, the fact that RES units are increasingly able to compete against conventional generation 
technologies on market terms 16- also calls into question whether the co ntinued operation of 
renewable support schemes is still justifiable.  As also noted by the European Commission 17, in the 
absence of support schemes, renewable producers would have an incentive to accept a lower PPA 
strike price in exchange for the long-term visibility that a PPA provides with regard to the producer’s 
future revenues (as opposed t o being exposed to fluctuating market  prices). Ensuring a guaranteed 
revenue stream greatly reduces the risk associated with the pr oducer’s (capital-intensive) 
investment in new RES capacity and therefore reduces the cost of capital, a key factor in ensuri ng 
the bankability of the investment18. In this way, RES PPAs would be an attractive proposition for both 
the producer and the consumer, and the long-term certainty that a RES PPA provides with regard the 
producer’s revenues  can replace the long -term certainty that is currently provided by renewable 
support schemes.

What is more, compensating electro-intensive consumers f or the firming co sts that are crea ted 
when consuming renewable electricity would be significantly cheaper than compensating the same 
electricity under a renewable support scheme. This is particularly important when viewed in light of 
the troubles that numerous Member States have been having in funding their RES support schemes 
(the account dedicated to funding RES support in Germany is running a deficit of over €2 billion19, 
whereas, in 2018, RES charges accounted for over 40% of certain co nsumers’ overall electricity costs 
in both Germany and Italy 20; this is clearly not su stainable). Data from recent RES auctions suggest s 
that the LCOE of solar PV projects that w ill be commissioned from 2021 onwards has already 
dropped to a  global average of just 39 $/MWh, i.e. around 33 €/MWh21. T he International 
Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA)  also notes that  an LCOE of 30 $/MWh (i.e. 25.50 €/MWh ) is 
already possible “ in a wide v ariety of national contexts ”. These figures are consistent with the 
European Commission’s 2019 report on corporate RES sourcing, which notes that in Spain the 
“generation costs for solar power are slightly below 30 €/MWh”22. 
 
However, according to the European Commission’s official statistics, the average reference price for 
solar PV projects that successfully compe ted in auctions across the EU during 2019 amounted to 80 
€/MWh23. When compared to the average wholesale electricity market price in Europe during  Q1 
2020 (33.50 €/MWh24), this translates into an incredible subs idy of around 46.50 €/MWh on 
average, for a technology that is already mature and -in most cases - out-competing fossil fuels 25. 
These subsidies are being funded by European consumers, and this situation (if not remedied) will 
end up jeopardizing societal acceptance of the energy transition.  Conversely, providing 
compensation for firming costs at a fragment of the cost borne by consumers currently , could lead 
to a massive upsurge in industrial RES PPAs and therefore the wide-scale decarbonisation of both 
European industry and the European power sector.

Security of Supply 
 
The crucial role that electrification wil l play on the path towards climate neutrality also mean s that 
Europe’s strategy is dependent on the procurement of massive volumes of renewable electricity. For 
example, around 628 TWh of renewable electricity will be required each year in order to 
decarbonise the German chemicals sector33. To put this figure into context, this is significantly more 
electricity than Germany as a whole currently produces each year (518 TWh in 2019, of which ‘just’ 
238 TWh corresponded to renewable generation) 34. The International Energy Agency also estimates 
that around 13,750 TWh of decarbonised electricity will be required each year (i.e. twice the amount

Given these demands, it seems unlikely that we even have enough physical space in Europe to build 
all of the RES units that would be require d in order to reach climate neutrality, and the success o f 
this strategy will depend on our ability to import massive volumes of renewable energy.  For this 
reason, it is becoming increasingly appa rent that low-carbon power generation ( natural gas ) is 
absolutely necessary as a transition fuel . This was recently acknowledged by First Vice President 
Frans Timmermans, who stated that “in some areas of transition, the use of natural gas will probably 
be necessary to shift from coal to sustainable energy”36. However, recent developments in EU policy 
are making it increasingly difficult to invest in natural  gas projects. The EIB’s new energy lending 
policy37 (and specifically the emissions performance standard of 250 gCO2/kWh) effectively rules out 
funding for any new natural -gas fired electricity generation plant  without CCS/CCU ( which despite 
being promising, is not yet a commercially viable technology ). Meanwhile, the TEG’s proposal 38 for 
the EU taxonomy of environmentally sustainable e conomic activities foresees an even stricter limit 
of 100 gCO2/kWh for electricity generation. 
 
Although it is und oubtedly i mportant to ensure that the EU’s energy and climate polic ies are  
consistent with our long-term goals, it is equally (if not more) important to ensure security of supply 
in the energy sector as we transition towards climate neutrality . Without security of s upply, it will 
simply not be possible to implement all the investments that will be required in order to decarbonise 
our economy . The electricity system in particular requires controllable generation in order to 
prevent black -outs during hours of insuff icient renewable production  (which is precisely what 
happened in California this summer, when hundreds of thousands of resi dents were left without 
electricity39). Decarbonised fuels such as green hydrogen offer interesting opportunities in this 
regard (and should therefore be supported ), but we are still a long way from being able to rely on 
such fuels to power our  energy system. Hydrogen currently accounts for less than 1% of Europe’s  
total energy consumption40, whereas just 4% of global hydrogen production is based on electrolysis41 
(and even then, it is unclear how much of this electricity is renewable ). The Commission’s Roadmap 
for an EU hydrogen strategy 42 outlines the significant obstacles that will have to be overcome in 
order to scale up our production of  green hydrogen, including high costs and the low efficiency of 
this process. 
 
Unless and until we scale up our production of decarbonised fuels to a point where they are able to 
cover a significant portion of Europe’s  demand for energy in a cost -efficient manner, natural gas is 
by far  the most sustainable way of ensuring controllabl e, flexible electricity generation .

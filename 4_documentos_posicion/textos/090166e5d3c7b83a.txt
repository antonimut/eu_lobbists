Review of EU renewable energy rules – Roadmap consultation 
Feedback from Eni S.p.A. 
 
 
We welcome the upcoming review of the EU policy and regulatory framework for renewables as we see a 
strong potential in supporting a fast yet economically efficient renewables deployment pursued at Union level. 
Respecting the trajectory that will be required for Europe to achieve climate neutrality in 2050 and meeting 
the subsequent revised 2030 targets will require large investments. Therefore, a balanced approach should 
combine a stable regulatory framework with a fast uptake of renewables in the short to medium term. In the 
current circumstances of economic downturn linked to the COVID crisis, this means paying specific attention to 
the cost effectiveness of measures, to the principle of technology neutrality, and focusing on the delivery of 
immediate results. Finally, the Green Deal calls for an overarching transformation of the EU economy and 
production system, which demands a just transition and an integrated policy approach, going beyond sectoral 
silos. This principle o f integrated policy making, already enshrined in the Sector Integration Strategy and 
deriving from the principles of the circular economy, should be carefully reflected in the expected revision of 
the Renewable Energy Directive and in other related initiatives, for example by recognising the contribution of 
low-carbon gaseous options to the decarbonization objectives. 
Based on these general reflections and on the more specific considerations here below, we support a 
comprehensive revision of the EU framework for renewables, which should reflect the need to adopt a more 
ambitious 2030 target, strengthen the enabling conditions for the integration of renewables across all sectors 
and translate into legal measures the actions proposed in other energy strategies as part of the Green Deal, 
namely Option 5 per the Commission Inception Impact Assessment (IIA). 
Building the key elements of a functional market for RES and low-carbon products 
In order to support the development of a functional and transparent market for RES and low-carbon products, 
an integrated and holistic approach is required and the decarbonization potential of all the available products 
and technologies should be leveraged. 
As referred to in the Commission’s IIA, the revised framework should promote the development and use of 
low-carbon and decarbonized gasses  (namely hydrogen produced from natural gas with the application of 
CCS), in line with the approach foreseen by the Smart Sector Integration and Hydrogen Strategies. Such policy 
should necessarily be based on a comprehensive classification covering renewable, decarbonized and low-
carbon fuels based on a technologically neutral standardized life-cycle analysis assessing the product’s GHG 
emissions and resulting contribution to decarbonization.  
Such classification should also be used to develop one standardized system for Guarantees of Ori gin (GO), 
which should be linked to a certification scheme for renewables and non- renewable carbon products, 
including clean hydrogen (produced via all available decarbonized technologies). This mechanism should be 
consistent with the GO system currently in place in the power sector and should further build on the principles 
already enshrined in the RED  II, i.e. for GO to also cover renewable gas products and opening to the 
opportunity for the extension to non-renewables gas to fall under the system (an option left to the Member 
States in the current Directive). This approach would promote a neutral  and healthy competition between 
different energy sources and technologies, thus supporting, for example, the further uptake of biomethane 
and helping kick-start a hydrogen market in Europe.

Decarboniz ing the existing generation 
The Communication on the 2030 Climate Plan acknowledges that the achievement of an ambitious revised 
2030 target enabling climate neutrality should be pursued in an economically efficient manner. This is key for 
public acceptance and in the aftermath of the current crisis. This means that a holistic approach towards 
decarbonization should be adopted, on the one hand continuing to pursue high penetration of renewables in 
the energy system, while also leveraging other measures aimed at reducing the carbon footprint of existing 
generation, e.g. through CCU S applications, also in the power sector. The electric option should not be 
considered the default option for decarbonization, especially in sectors such as transport and heating, where 
other climate-neutral options (i.e. renewable and low-carbon gaseous and liquid based technologies) could be 
more easily and economically adopted by consumers. 
Linking renewables investment to the CO2 displacement potential for higher impact 
In the same cost -effective spirit, RE S deployment should be planned by  closely considering the  project’s 
decarbonization potential, and supported accordingly. This could be done by establishing the principle of a 
CO2 displacement criterion recognizing  the value of RES projects built in areas characterized by a carbon-
intensive power mix (e.g. with coal generation). This way, EU investment in renewables would have a direct 
impact on CO2 reductions, linking renewable energy and decarbonization targets in a direct way, achieving as 
much CO2 reduction as possible in the least possible time. 
A stronger EU push to remove red-tape and fast track renewables installations 
The revision of the RED2 should also be used an opportunity to further push Member States towards 
streamlining national authorization processes, and removing administrative barriers slowing down permitting, 
which undermines the viability of project timelines, increasing risks and costs. 
Consistent and integrated rules for fuels 
We believe that the RED II revision should foresee measures to align the obligations on the fuel supplier 
included in the current Regulation with the obligations established by the Fuel Quality Directive (FQD). In this 
regard, clear targets and trajectories in RED II for the transport sector are key to enable the penetration of low 
carbon solutions, leveraging in particular those already implemented, such as biofuels, for immediate results. 
A comprehensive approach to transport decarbonization 
As acknowledged in the newly presented Communication on the 2030 Climate Plan, it has proven hard to 
achieve emission reduction in the transport sector. In light of that, it should be recognised t hat sustainable 
decarbonisation should foresee a synergistic mix of technological solutions. Consistently, the EU framework 
should enable all technologies (including hydrogen production ones) which can deliver significant GHG 
emission reductions to compete on a level playing field. 
Electrification alone is currently not an economically and technologically viable solution to reach the required 
amount of renewable penetration in transport (which should reach 24% by 2030 according the Commission’ s 
estimates). The costs associated with the switch to electric and the consequent pace of uptake, together with 
the average age of the fleet, indicates that no significant growth will take place in the short terms, which yet 
will be required by the revised target. 
On the contrary, the development of biofuels represents an immediate and effective response to the need of 
reducing transport emissions, while resulting in further deployment of renewables. With regards to the 
deployment of biofuels, it is generally expected that increasing quantities of raw materials listed in Annex IX,

Furthermore, as recognised by the EC 2030 Climate Plan, biofuels represent the soundest and most immediate

renewables framework 
Finally, a technology neutral approach to fuels which recognises and values the contribution of the circular 
economy for the production of renewable energy vectors should: 
• Promote the use of waste-based feedstock for the production of advanced biofuels. 
• Acknowledge and support all hydrogen production paths, including those leveraging circular economy 
processes and supporting sustainability in other sectors, such as hydrogen and fuels obtained from 
solid recovered waste (waste to energy solutions). 
• Include an EU-level obligation for Member States to account for the contribution of RCFs (recycled 
carbon fuels) – where available – towards the renewable target, removing the current possibility to 
opt-in for  Member States as in the case of RFNBO (renewable liquid and gaseous fuels of non-
biological origin).

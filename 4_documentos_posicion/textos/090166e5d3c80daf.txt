EU renewable energy rules – review 
About Hydrogen Europe 
 
Hydrogen Europe is the European association representing the interest of the hydrogen and fuel cell industry and its 
stakeholders. We promote hydrogen as the enabler of a zero-emission society. 
With more than 160 companies, 78 research organisations and 23 national associations as members, our association 
encompasses the entire value chain of the European hydrogen and fuel cell ecosystem collabo rating in the Fuel Cell 
Hydrogen Joint Undertaking. 
We are a Brussels-based association fostering knowledge and pushing for fact -based policymaking ensuring that the 
European regulatory framework enables the role of Hydrogen in our society.  
For more information, please visit www.hydrogeneurope.eu. 
Introduction 
 
Hydrogen Europe welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback related to the review of the European Union’s 
(EU’s) renewable energy rules. Hydrogen Europe members support the EU’s objective to achieve climate neutrality 
in 2050. As such, we believe there is  a need to review upwards the existing 2030 targets for greenhouse gas 
reduction. Consequently, there is a need to assess and rethink the role of the Renewable Energy Directive (RED) 
in contributing to achieving these objectives and accelerating the transi tion to a more integrated system with 
hydrogen being a key part of this effort. Significantly more ambitious GHG reduction targets in line with the EGD 
shall be accompanied by the possibility to count the contribution of renewable and low carbon energy in order to 
achieve the desired climate impact.  
The RED should promote further development of renewables, and especially renewable hydrogen. However, the 
current iteration of the directive  (so-called REDII) was drafted and agreed on in a different context and spirit to 
that of today.  The European Green Deal (EGD) has set a clear pathway towards climate neutrality , and firmly 
underlines the importance of a well-balanced RED and a concerted regulatory environment that enables a positive 
investment environment for existing players and market challengers, to enable the scaling-up and deployment of 
hydrogen and hydrogen technologies.  
Furthermore, the European Commission’s Hydrogen Strategy 1 (published on 8th July 2020) clearly highlights and 
underlines the importance of hydrogen in achieving energy system integration and climate neutrality across the 
EU economy, with hydrogen expected to meet between 14 -24% of energy demand in 2050. The EU Hydrogen 
Strategy sets two clear objectives for hydrogen in 2024 and 2030. Specifically, by 2024 the EU has an objective of 
producing 1 million tonnes of renewable hydrogen and at least 6GW of installed electrolyser capacity. By 2030, 
the objective is to reach an installation capacity of 2x40GW to produce renewable hydrogen (40GW in Europe and 
40GW outside EU2). In order to achieve these objectives and support the decarbonisation of our economy, there 
are a number of challenges posed by the REDII which need to be addressed. Our  climate objectives as a Union 
have evolved, technological breakthroughs and advancements continue to evolve together with the decline in 
costs of renewable hydrogen production. In the same vein, our legislation should evolve and adapt as well.

The production of hydrogen and derived renewable and low carbon gases from renewable energy contributes to 
decouple the deployment of renewable energy from grid bottl enecks unlocking the full potential of renewable 
energy to replace fossil energy carriers in all sectors of our economy, beyond the power sector.  Hydrogen and 
derived renewable and low carbon gases, liquids and chemical feedstocks allow integrating ever growing amounts 
of renewable energy into so called “hard to abate” sectors  such as steel, chemicals, and transport, including 
refineries, maritime and aviation . As highlighted by the EU’s Hydrogen Strategy and the Communication on 
Energy System Integration, climate-neutrality across all sectors of the economy requires hydrogen and derived 
renewable and low carbon gases, liquids and chemical feedstock as one of many solutions. In order to enable the 
achievement of EU’s climate targets in 2030 and 2050, hydroge n needs to start being deployed, at large scale, 
across different sectors as soon as possible. 
The Renewable Energy Directive (RED)  could be part of a framework to turn renewable solutions into business 
cases. However, it must be part of a wider policy fra mework that also provide fiscal support for first plants and 
fuels, support for infrastructure, supply side measures to encourage OEMs to produce alternative fueled vehicles 
and demand side support for consumers. Industry is ready to invest.   
The proposed Revision of Directive (EU) 2018/2001 on the promotion of the use of energy from renewable sources 
proposes four options in part B.” Objectives and Policy options”. Our opinion that option 1 (no policy changes) 
and option 2 (Non -regulatory measures) will not be sufficient to promote development of renewables in the 
hard-to-abate sectors in the spirit and objectives of the EGD. A combination of option 3 (Raising the ambition 
level of the REDII targets and sub-targets in line with the 2030 Climate Target Plan) and option 4 (Amend REDII 
to translate into legal measures the actions proposed in other energy strategies of the EGD) will be necessary 
to overcome the current investment hurdles for our members.  
Further elaborated hereunder, Hydrogen Europe would like to highlight critical challenges and undue barriers , 
that should be addressed as part of the review of renewable energy rules. In particular: 
- We support an increase of the overall renewable energy target to promote renewable energy production.  
- On top, it is critical to define a specific RFNBO sub-target and RFNBO multipliers. 
- We will also discuss guarantees of origin, and important feedback on the principles of additionality and 
other criteria, that are unlikely hurdles.  
- Import of hydrogen is also an important topic, that we would like to flag. 
RFNBO sub-targets and multipliers 
The 14% renewable fuel obligation (Art. 25) should be significantly revised  upwards and underpinned by a 
specific dedicated target for Renewable Fuels of Non-Biological Origin in the transport sector (similar to the 3,5 
% target for advanced biofuels provided by Article 25.1 (b)) to level the playing field.  
o 14 EU Countries have already implemented, as part of their NECPs higher RES targets in transport 
(Over 40% in Sweden in Finland, 33% in the Netherlands, 20% or more in Spain, Germany, 
Luxembourg, Italy, Slovenia, Portugal and Denmark and between 14% and 20% in Greece, France, 
Malta and Lithuania) 
 
o The application of multipliers (Art. 27.2) (without a corresponding upward revision of the target, 
when the Directive was negotiated) has made the 14% a target almost irrelevant or very easily 
attainable (statistically) in many EU countries from the moment  the RED II was adopted. As will 
be explained below, we advocate for a revision of the multiplier system.  
 
Renewable energy su b-targets and multipliers are essential instruments to (i) incentivi se the uptake  of 
renewables in the energy system and (ii) to close the financial gap between the cleaner yet  more expensive 
option and its  cheaper fossil alternative for end -customers. However, both instruments are interlinked. Sub -

targets will result in an obligation to produce RFNBO and create a “push” in supply. In an emerging market , 
multipliers will create a stronger demand for RFNBO relative to the fossil alternative and create a “pull” effect. As 
such we advocate for an integrated framework, within the RED whereby both instruments are aligned with each 
other and are mutually reinforcing.  
 
Specific sub -targets for hard to abate sectors. (e.g. steel production, aviation and maritime) may also be 
considered in the upcoming revision of the RED to further incentivize and speed-up deployment and adoption of 
renewable energy in specific sectors and industrial segments. When developing legislation and proposing targets 
and incentives, it is important to avoid duplication of efforts; as such if specific sectoral targets - e.g. in industry, 
maritime or aviation - are proposed within the context of RED revision, they should always be coherent with any 
other more specific and targeted sectoral legislative initiatives e.g. RE Fuel Maritime or RE Fuel Aviation. 
Guarantees of Origin 
In order to enable a robust and credible guarantee of origin system,  hydrogen should be treated as a separate 
and distinct energy carrier. In the present reading of the REDII, Article 19.7 is interpreted by some to mean that 
hydrogen should be viewed as a subsector of gas. This is a wrong assumption. A robust system of Guarantees of 
Origin requires that energy products which are not interchangeable have to be treated as distinct energy carriers 
with clear rules to be applied for conversion, otherwis e the system will be open to misuse and abuse . Hydrogen 
and Hydrocarbon gases are distinct energy carriers, with different production methods, uses and characteristics 
which have varying implications on end users.  
Energy Carrier conversion, between one ca rrier to another (e.g. from electricity to hydrogen, from Hydrogen to 
methane, etc.) must entail the cancellation of a GO of the product being consumed and the issuing of a new GO, 
corresponding to the energy value of the product that has been produced.  
 
As a general rule, only by clearly separating energy carriers within a GO system:  (i) electricity (ii) hydrocarbon gas, 
(iii) hydrogen and (iv) heating and cooling, (as it being proposed by the current version of the FastGO project) can 
a robust GO system be designed. 
Principles of additionality, geographic and temporal correlation 
Climate-neutrality requires (much) more renewable energy. Increased demand for renewable energy (including 
for the production of  hydrogen) should and will (inevitably)  be met wi th growing renewable energy capacity 
both domestically and through imports. Despite this economic reality, the RED II imposes on Hydrogen ( RFNBO) 
producers the obligation to prove “additionality” and “other appropriate criteria” (e.g. temporal and geographical 
correlation).  
 
Additionality (Article 27.3 and Recital 90) 
 
There are significant challenges posed by the requirement to prove additionality as prescribed by the REDII. For 
example, the fact that the lead time for investments into some renewable electricity generation assets (e.g. 
offshore wind – up to 7 years) does not coincide with the time needed to construct an electrolyser (less tha n 2 
years). Until new electricity capacity is available, electrolyser project developers will therefore have a very low 
incentive to build, as the main demand driver is precisely the renewable character of the hydrogen and its 
contribution towards REDII targets.

To avoid delaying the rollout of electrolysers, which would ultimately have a negative impact on achieving our 
climate goals, we have engaged in detailed technical discussions with our members, other industry associations 
and the European Commissio n to find cross -industry consensus and practical solutions in preparation for the 
Delegated Act (DA)3. We remain fully committed to work with the European Commission to find technical solutions 
within the current framework of RED2 in case those criteria remain in full force after a revision of the Directive.  
 
However, the process of having to find technical solutions on how to implement the requirements related to 
additionality and other appropriate criteria for grid connected electrolysers have shown jus t how difficult (if not 
impossible in certain cases) these legal barriers are to overcome in practice. 
Additionality is not needed if the growth in demand is met with an equal amount of RES capacity. A revised 
Renewable Energy Directive should support the uptake of renewable energy in all sectors of the EU economy 
and ensure that the responsibility to add to the existing renewable energy capacity is shared by all energy 
consumers and not only by RFNBO producers.   
As mentioned previously, the lead time for installing and commissioning an electrolyser is much quicker than the 
time it takes to install and commission extra RE -production, which means that, in the context of the application 
of the additionality principle, an electrolyser must “wait” until the RE-production site is ready. This is a severe risk 
for the investor s and an unnecessary delay towards deployment  and achievement of EU energy and climate 
targets. 
“Other appropriate criteria” (Recital 90) 
 
The requirements to ensure “geographical correlation” between renewable power generation and hydrogen 
production also creates major obstacles and ambiguities .  Firstly, grid congestions are not static during the 
lifetime of an electrolyser, which means that investors cannot have any guarantees that congestions will not 
appear during the lifetime of the project. In any case, even if there are no congestions between the RES plant and 
the electrolyser, this does not mean that the e lectricity physically consumed comes from the RES plant  For 
investment stability, this geographical correlation criteria could be based on a snapshot. 
RFNBOs “cannot be counted as fully renewable if they are produced when the contracted renewable generatio n 
unit is not generating electricity ” (Recital 90). This “ temporal correlation” condition imposed by the RED also 
creates uncertainty and is a large barrier to investments due to the intermittency of RES generation . For the 
operational implementation such condition, the possibility to use electricity GOs should be considered, as it is 
possible for any other electricity consumer, and complex operational processes and expensive IT systems should 
be avoided. 
It is important to underline that a new and lengthy legislative process linked to the revision of REDII could create 
further risk to investor certainty and clarity in this field. It could also inadvertently undermine the efforts and the 
pace at which EU en ergy and climate targets are met. As such, we believe that the issues related to Article 27 
should be clarified, as a matter of priority, within the context of discussions related to the DA to be finalised by 31 
December 2020 (per the directive).

Subject: EU Renewable Energy Rules - review    
 
 
POSITION PAPER  
 
Comments EU Renewable Energy Rules – review 
Answer to Roadmap - 21/09/2020  
 
EDF welcomes the opportunity to comment on the Roadmap related to the review of the EU’s 
renewable energy rules. As the European leader of carbon free electricity with a close to 90  % 
decarbonized generation mix, EDF strongly supports the target of net zero emissions by 2050 
and an increased GHG emission reduction target by ‘at least 55%’ in 2030.  This is a critical 
milestone to enable a cost efficient pathway to climate neutrality, giving longer term visibility to 
investors. The revision of EU’s renewable energy rules is an opportunity regarding the following 
points:  
 
An opportunity to further recognise the key role of electrification and increase 
consistency between climate and energy policies   
 
 The European Green Deal (EGD) has set a clear pathway towards climate neutrality, and 
reducing CO2 emissions in all the sectors of the economy should therefore be the priority. 
A meaningful and predictable EU ETS carbon price should remain a key driver for 
investments to enable a cost efficient transition to a low carbon future. But achieving 
climate neutrality will also require massive electrification of transport, building and industry 
sectors based on decarbonised and renewable power generation. 
 
 EDF recognizes the positive effect of Renewable Energy Targets on the deployment of 
renewable sources and the need to increase the pace of development of these technologies 
in the coming years. Nevertheless to ensure all interactions between climate and en ergy 
policies are carefully addressed, the process leading up to a revision of all targets 
(GHG, RES and EE) needs to be handled in a coordinated and structured way. 
 
 To decarbonize the building sector, the revision of RED II should foster the development of 
renewable energy especially for heating and cooling. There is a crucial need for sufficient 
financial support and enabling permitting measures to ensure the develop ment of 
green DHC systems and heat pumps to replace old fossil based boilers.  
 
 
An opportunity to consolidate existing measures and ensure proper transposition in the 
Member States 
The existing renewable directive includes several concrete measures to pro mote the deployment 
of renewable energies, but today the main issue remains implementation in the different MS: 
 
 Simplifying and shortening permitting procedures. Today reaching the existing 2030 
RES target is put at risk because in some MS national administrations cannot process the 
amount of permit rapidly enough – for new deployment and repowering projects despite the 
provision in the Renewable Energy Directive.  
 
 Giving visibility on the timing and volume of auctions and the possibility for MS to 
choose energy mix and technology specific auctions in order to reach that mix.  To 
ensure a cost effective and timely development of RES, industrial players of the entire value 
chain need market perspectives as stable as possible to be able to plan ahead and operate 
efficiently. It is of utmost importance to consolidate the possibility for MS to carry out 
technology specific tenders. Indeed, these tenders are the most efficient way to deploy RES 
both to increase the needed visibility for investors and to develop a  balanced mix of 
production allowing a smooth grid integration.

 Allowing revenue stabilisation mechanisms especially Contract for Difference (CFD). 
CFD are crucial a financing tool to de-risking financing of renewable energy projects. They 
provide the necessary confidence for investors which results in a lower cost of capital and 
ultimately guarantees affordability for the final consumer.  
 
 
An opportunity to create a meaningful regulatory framework for hydrogen, including a 
classification based on a CO2 criteria to effectively deliver on climate neutrality   
 The key role of hydrogen produced through electrolysis from both 100% renewable 
electricity and from a low carbon electricity mix should be fully recognized to tackle GHG 
emissions in hard -to-abate sectors. The regulatory framework, needed to incentivize t he 
deployment of hydrogen technologies, should include a common classification at EU 
level based on a clear CO2 emission criteria throughout the production process. 
 
 The renewable energy directive imposes on fuels producers for transport to prove there are 
“physically” using additional renewable capacities to produce renewable electrolytic 
hydrogen. There are significant challenges to comply with the so called additionality 
principle and the temporal and geographical correlation criteria, especially when us ing 
electricity from the grid. This means that a direct line connection between renewable 
capacities and electrolyzers is clearly favored in the current regulation. Not only is this 
constraint (i) detrimental to the pursuit of the electricity sector integr ation and 
decarbonation, it also (ii) inflates the cost of hydrogen projects (increasing the level of public 
support needed) and (iii) induces the need of hydrogen imports from outside of Europe, 
creating new dependencies. On the contrary, the benefits of electrolyzers connected to 
the electricity grid should be recognized as a key enabler of sector coupling.  
Compared to off -grid systems and direct line connection schemes, the overall business 
case of an electrolysis facility connected to the grid is beneficial in terms of running hours, 
additional revenues from flexibility services and reduced storage needs for the power 
system. 
 
An opportunity to provide a clear strategy for storage deployment and ensure a level 
playing field between all technologies  
 The Renewable Directive includes several references to storage as a strategic asset for 
the energy transition. Indeed energy storage – alongside flexible demand assets and 
dispatchable generation – will be an essential source of the flexibility needed to incre ase 
the share of renewables in the power system while maintaining reliability and security of 
supply.  
 
 Energy storage requires a clear strategy and a comprehensive regulatory framework 
to ensure a cost -effective deployment and a level playing field betwee n all 
technologies. Various storage technologies play complementary roles in the system 
depending on the use case and the timescale of the flexibility needed - from seconds to 
seasonal needs.  
 
 The lack of business for storage should be addressed especially for existing assets. Today, 
positive externalities provided by storage, such as system flexibility and stability, are not 
properly valued via market price signals. Specific economic support should be granted to 
energy storage to fully recognize its crucial role in the energy transition.

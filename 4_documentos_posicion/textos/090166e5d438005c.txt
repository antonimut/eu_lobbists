 Cologne, 2 October 2020  Open-Xchange contribution to the online consultation on the EU digital ID scheme for online transactions across Europe  Open-Xchange, as the leading European open source software company in the email and DNS space, and as the service provider of choice for some of the biggest Internet access providers in Europe, would like to thank for the opportunity to provide additional comments on the consultation on a European digital identity scheme and on possible revisions to the eIDAS Regulation. We note that Open-Xchange is not an economic player in the digital identity space; we do not provide eID software or services as part of our business. However, we think that digital identities are key to the future development of any Internet service and market, and we are concerned by the dominance of the big American platforms in this sector. Thus, in 2016 we were one of the founders of the ID4me project1 to establish an open, federated European alternative to the dominant online identity systems by Google, Facebook and other Internet platforms. Our continued participation in this project gave us the first-hand experience on which we build our comments. We would like to start by commending the long-standing efforts of the European institutions to establish a scheme for public eIDs. We note that the eIDAS scheme has been successful in promoting simpler online access to public services in many European countries; while implementation still needs to increase, as not all public administrations seem to support eIDs yet, and while European-wide integration could still enhance its usefulness (though only for a small subset of “nomadic” European citizens), the positive results are already quite visible. At the same time, we note that the eIDAS scheme does not meet the user demand for simpler, faster, unverified (often pseudonymous) online identities for everyday use in website authentication in lower value or more private contexts, such as social media, online shopping, discussion forums, app registration and so on.  National eID schemes are very useful for highly verified authentication in sensitive contexts, but are overkill for ordinary, everyday use for the average website. People do not like the idea of having their government know all the websites they visit; they want to pick their identity provider, not necessarily within a small government-validated set, or even manage their identity themselves; they do not want to use their full, verified identity when logging into ordinary websites, as they often prefer pseudonymity and do not want all websites to learn highly verified information about themselves; they want to hold multiple online identities and use different ones  1 See https://id4me.org/ .

for different activities, so to keep – for example – professional and personal accounts completely separate.  Moreover, the procedural requirements of eID schemes, inherently connected with validating identities for use in strongly authenticated contexts, make them inevitably more complex and less user-friendly than systems not meant to provide such a high level of assurance over the identity. It would be very hard to “tweak” the eIDAS framework and implementations to make them usable in this completely different case, and even if you did so, the result would always be at a usability disadvantage in respect to identity solutions conceived and optimized since the beginning for everyday unverified usage; it is likely that such a system would not gain widespread adoption among users. This demand for simpler and less verified online identities for website authentication has rather been caught and met by the social identities provided by the big American platforms, which were perfectly designed for that purpose. In just a few years, three providers in particular – Facebook, Google and Apple – have dominated the market, using the network effect deriving from their immense user base and, in some cases, through the adoption of self-preferencing practices. Any website operator that wished to provide easier sign-on for users would add support for some of these providers, relying on the fact that most Internet users already had a Facebook, Google or Apple account because of the dominance of these companies in other services and products, and so those users would gain immediate access to that website as well.  These companies, relying on their financial power, gave away the identity service completely for free, both to users and to website operators; the actual business model is unknown, but it may be assumed that it relies on cross-subsidization for strategic reasons and/or on user tracking and data monetization. Managing a user’s digital identity allows the identity provider to track each and every service that the user logs into, profiling the user’s online activities and general preferences with extreme precision.  In this way, dominant identity providers can acquire significant advantages for their other services and products, especially if such products are related to targeted advertising. Digital identity services are extremely sensitive for the user’s privacy, and thus need to be carefully assessed under this aspect; we think that introducing alternative identity providers to the dominant platforms, especially if located in Europe (under the protections of the GDPR) and having different business models not based on user data monetization, would clearly be beneficial to the privacy of European citizens. Technically speaking, all these identity platforms are based on the same open standards, OpenID Connect and OAuth 2.0. However, while these standards are in themselves open, the implementation by the big platforms is explicitly designed not to interoperate; for example, a “Sign in with Google” button is designed to only allow usage with OpenID Connect identities provided by Google, and not with technically similar identities provided by other parties. This lack of interoperability promotes and preserves the dominance of these identity providers. Users could in theory “multi-home”, and acquire identities from multiple providers, even if this is quite inconvenient for services whose purpose is to let users manage all their logins in a single place. However, website operators are limited in the number of login buttons they can add to their login page, as this clutters their interface, fills up the screen and risks driving users away.  If – as in the current, “siloed” implementation model – website operators need to add one new button per each additional identity provider, they will naturally end up supporting only the 2-3

This makes it very hard for any competing identity provider to enter the market successfully, as users will not acquire identities that are not widely supported by websites, and websites will not add support for identities that do not already have a broad user base. For all these reasons, we think that a regulatory intervention that mandates interoperability and support for an open identity standard is necessary if the European Union wants to restore competition in digital identities for everyday usage, and promote the adoption of alternative identity providers freely chosen by the users among a much bigger number of options, including self-hosted identities.  We thus recommend the European Commission to adopt a complementary approach based on: 1. a uniform European eID based on the eIDAS framework, either as a collection of reciprocally interoperable national eIDs or as a single new standard, for access to public services, banks, health services, and other very sensitive services; 2. mandating support in other online services for an open standard allowing any public and private party (incuding non profits and individuals) to provide interoperable eIDs with lower levels of assurance, technically and functionally similar to the ones currently provided by the dominant Internet platforms.  We think that there is a very simple rule that could be introduced to accomplish point number 2: any online service that supports one or more of the dominant social media logins must also add support for login through an open, public, federated standard of choice that allows any party to provide identities. This approach was proven effective by Apple; when they decided to catch up with Google and Facebook and introduce their own single sign-on digital identities, they simply added a requirement that any app supporting Google and/or Facebook logins also had to add support for Apple logins as a requirement to be allowed into Apple’s app store2. This self-preferencing approach, which in our opinion is abusive in policy terms, worked in practice quite well and could be reversed and exploited for the public interest, to require the adoption of an open system. Such a rule would be accompanied by the need to formalize and update a list of the “dominant social media logins” and the choice of the mandatory standard to support; these needs could be met by implementation guidelines deferred to an appropriate European regulatory agency. Anyway, stressing the importance of picking a proven, reliable and scalable technology (no blockchain please), we suggest that the initial technical standard of choice should be OpenID Connect, the same already used by the social media platforms, coupled with the recent technical “federation” extensions that allow the establishment of multiple identity providers; we stand ready to share the technical and operational expertise acquired through the non-profit ID4me consortium on issues such as security, detection and prevention of bad operators, exchange of trust information and so on.

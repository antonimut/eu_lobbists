 
 
 
 
Position Paper on 
 
European Commission’s inception impact assessment on corporate sus-
tainable governance 
 
I. Overall position 
 
The German insurance Industry strongly supports the efforts of the EU-
Commission to promote the transformation to a global sustainable economy . 
We believe that the numerous cross-sectoral (such as Regulation (EU) 2019/2088 on 
sustainability‐related disclosures in the financial services sector or the review of the 
Non-Finance Reporting Directive) and insurance-related legislative initiatives (e.g. 
the EU-Commission’s proposals amending Delegated Regulation (EU) 2015/35 as 
regards the integration of sustainability risks in the governance of insurance and rein-
surance undertakings and amending Delegated Regulations (EU) 2017/2358 and 
(EU) 2017/2359 as regards the integration of sustainability factors and preferences 
into the product oversight and governance requirements for insurance undertakings 
and insurance distributors) already set in motion have the potential to provide ad-
equate financial and behavioral  incentives to foster this process.  
 
Naturally, a transformation of this magnitude will take some time to succeed. That 
is why the GDV encourages the EU-Commission  to monitor the impact of the 
measures enacted so far instead of considering incisive regulatory interventions at 
this early stage. The policy options set out in the inception impact assessment would 
not incentivize corporate boards to better align sustainability interests but impose a 
binding corporate governance regime in order to establish a sustainability agen-
da. They may redefine the current understanding of the freedom to conduct busi-
ness as guaranteed by Article 16 of the charter of fundamental rights of the European 
Union. From the perspective of the insurance industry, which is subject to a legal 
framework dedicated to protecting the interests of policyholders , they may raise 
serious consistency issues with prudential regulation  and expose both supervi-
sors and insurance undertakings to a conflict of objectives. 
 
Sustainability is not only a European but a global challenge. While we welcome the 
ambition of the EU-Commission to take a vanguard role in this process, it is impera-
tive that the global markets agree on a set of standards. Pushing forward unilaterally 
may end up in an unlevel playing field  and expose European undertakings to a 
competitive disadvantage. 
 
Given these fundamental issues at stake and in order to avoid unintended conse-
quences, we request the European Commission to refrain from rushing into a legal 
process without a thorough impact assessment. More serious consideration should 
be given to non-legislative options. Recognizing the transformation already under-
way, more confidence should be placed to the mechanisms of the markets. Exploring 
options to refine existing transparency and reporting requirements may guide the

If, however, the EU-Commission comes up with binding legislation, we would promote

II. Problems the initiative aims to tackle 
 
The GDV believes that the European insurance sector does not suffer from mar-
ket and regulatory failures. 
 
The increasing focus on short-term financial performance is identified as the most 
detrimental development for sustainable transformation. While this conclusion is cer-
tainly accurate in general terms, the underlying studies lack a deliberate and com-
parative analysis about the exposure of economic sectors to short-termism. 
 
Short-term performance is contrary to the DNA of the insurance business model. 
Life insurance companies often cover financial protection for contracts with a duration 
of decades. Casualty insurers settle claims over many years in long-tail business 
lines. Insurers must maintain a required amount of capital to meet their contractual 
obligations at any time and under consideration of even remote stress scenarios.  
 
In addition, the inception impact assessment justifies legal action by blaming indiffer-
ence and an impaired awareness of climate change and other environmental, social 
and human rights risks and impacts in their operations. This conclusion does not 
meet the reality in the insurance sector . Insurers are legally required to establish 
risk-management system  comprising strategies, processes and reporting proce-
dures necessary to identify, measure, monitor, manage and report, on a continuous 
basis the risks, at an individual and at an aggregated level, to which they are or could 
be exposed, and their interdependencies. This all-embracing risk concept does of 
course include sustainability risks . The EU-Commission’s proposal on amending 
Delegated Regulation (EU) 2015/35 as regards the integration of sustainability risks 
in the governance of insurance and reinsurance undertakings would only clarify this. 
 
We would also challenge the conclusion that insurers generally fail to integrate poten-
tial new opportunities either for investment or for building resilience. A growing num-
ber of market participants have increased their efforts, not least in anticipation of 
changed customer preferences and strengthened product governance require-
ments. 
 
It should also be borne in mind that sustainability transformation requires a solid and 
resilient economy and stable financial markets. Insurers have a key function to se-
cure this foundation  by assuming risks from consumers and businesses. The rele-
 
 
 
vance of the insurance sector was just recently underpinned by the EU-Commission 
in a communication on COVID-19 related financing issues.  
 
III. Objectives and policy options 
 
Measures to address adverse sustainability impacts in companies’ operations 
and value chains (due diligence duty). 
 
• While we understand the EU-Commission’s motive to envisage this option, it 
remains unclear how generic binding due diligence requirements should be 
designed in a feasible and proportionate way. 
 
• As respondents to the accompanying study on due diligence requirements 
through the supply chain accurately pointed out, the current legal landscape 
does not provide companies with legal certainty about their human 
rights and environmental due diligence obligations. 
 
• Thus, the establishment of a corporate obligation may go along with unin-
tended consequences, as companies may be confronted with incalculable 
legal risks arising from possible civil law liabilities to administrative or even 
criminal sanctions which may result in a complete withdrawal from markets 
with questionable sustainability frameworks.  
 
• Absent globally agreed standards , competitors not subject to due diligence 
duties may step in, ending up in a situation where European companies suffer 
competitive disadvantages while the violation of sustainability goals continue 
to exist. 
 
Taking into account all stakeholders’ interests which are relevant for the long-
term sustainability of the firm or which belong to those affected by it and inte-
grate these interests with measurable and time-bound, science-based targets 
where relevant. 
 
 
• Facilitating dialogue between stakeholders and companies would be helpful 
to better understand the sustainability implications of business activities. 
 
• However, integrating stakeholders’ interests into the decision-making process 
including decisions on corporate strategy would raise difficult legal ques-
tions. 
 
• It is unclear how directors are supposed to resolve conflicts of interests be-
tween stakeholders and shareholders. 
 
 
 
 
• The same situation may arise if stakeholders are not in line  with the interests 
of policyholders 
 
• As a result, the reconciliation of different interests is likely to make every com-
pany decision subject to appeal, hence compromising the director’s capac-
ity to act. 
 
Establishing of appropriate facilitating, enforcement and implementation 
mechanisms, including possible remediation where necessary. 
 
• As indicated above, remediation mechanisms will expose companies to signif-
icant liability risks. 
 
• Therefore, potential legislation should include safeguards, such as an indem-
nity clause if companies have complied with due diligence requirements. 
 
 
Other possible corporate governance arrangements for example regarding 
director’s remuneration. 
 
• Insurance  regulation for the remuneration of directors and certain staff levels 
(key function holders and individuals whose professional activities have a ma-
terial impact on the undertakings' risk profile) is already aligned with long-
term and sustainability goals. 
 
• Article 275 of the Delegated Regulation (EU) 2015/35 requires insurance un-
dertakings to take account of both financial and non-financial criteria when 
assessing an individual’s performance regarding variable remuneration com-
ponents which is subject to a deferral period of no less than three years.  
 
• The EIOPA-Opinion on the supervision of remuneration principles in the in-
surance and reinsurance sector explicitly names Environmental, Social and 
Governance criteria as a benchmark  for the qualitative performance as-
sessment. 
 
• Furthermore, the EU-Commission’s proposals amending Delegated Regula-
tion (EU) 2015/35 aims to ensure that remuneration policies  of insurance 
and reinsurance undertakings contain information on how those policies take 
into account the integration of sustainability risks  in the risk management 
system.

Raising standards and facilitating their assessment 
Enhancing traceability  
along supply chains 
In recent months, the debate on the environmental, social and human rights responsibilities of business enterprises 
has regained momentum: in some European member states, including Germany, the  calls for a mandatory legal 
framework are growing increasingly loud. At the end of April, the European Commissioner for Justice Didier Reynders 
announced his intention to introduce a proposal in 2021 for a European supply chain legislation. Not least the 
coronavirus pandemic illustrates the need to make supply relationships responsible and resilient.  With the 
globalisation of world trade and the associated increase in consumers’ expectations of companies, the compliance of 
goods and services with safety and quality standards along the entire supply chain has become a central challenge for 
all involved. This applying to virtually all sectors – the food, manufacturing, processing and automotive industries. 
Compliance with defined social and environmental standards is increasingly being required along the entire supply 
chain – in short, for the production conditions from start to finish. 
The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs) call on states to contribute to compliance with 
internationally recognised human rights conventions, the labour standards of the International Labour Organization 
(ILO) and the core elements of the International Environmental Agreements (IEA) in their own business activities 
through a mixture of voluntary and mandatory measures. In some industrialised countries such as the USA, United 
Kingdom, France, Australia and the Netherlands, laws to comply with human rights and environmental supply chain 
requirements have already been passed. Other countries, including Germany,  have so far relied on voluntary 
commitments by industry. A study1 published by the European Commission concludes that just one in three 
companies in the EU carefully reviews its global supply chains in terms of the human rights and environmental impact 
and proposes various regulatory measures. According to the study report, 70 percent of the companies surveyed are 
in favour of a possible harmonised EU legislation for general and mandatory due diligence.

The TÜV-Associaion´s assessment 
Many companies are already willing to assume greater responsibility for the conditions under which their goods and 
merchandise are produced and the effects these have on people and the environment. However, in a number of 
producing countries, observing human rights and environmental protection does not appear to be a matter of course 
yet. It is often not immediately apparent to or even enforceable by contracting or purchasing companies if and how 
these rights are observed. However, in a global economy, the responsibility of companies – including suppliers and 
providers of raw material  – should not end at national borders. In order to ensure that human rights protection and 
the awareness of environmental responsibility do not present a competitive disadvant age, the same conditions must 
be established for companies all across the EU. The TÜV-Association therefore advocate a mandatory legal 
framework that obliges companies to comply with internationally recognised social  and environmental standards in 
their value chain. A European supply chain legislation must be developed to avoid a patchwork of legislations across 
Europe and to create a level playing field within the European Single Market.  
Merely developing mandatory requirements is not enough though. In ad dition to increased standardisation and the 
associated specification of requirements to comply with, impartial confirmation of conformity with applicable laws, 
guidelines and standards is particularly crucial to ensure reliable compliance with human rights  and environmental 
standards along the entire supply chain. Through their testing, inspection and certification activities, the assessment 
bodies can reliably contribute to documentation of the stages in the process chain of a product or material, from the  
raw material to the shop counter. Trust in the declarations of the individual links in the supply chain is created 
through confirmation and certification

Central demands  
1. Introduce a European supply chain legislation 
A robust legal framework is needed to make compliance with human rights and environmental standards 
mandatory. A European solution should be the primary objective, for supply chains do not stop at national 
borders. Only if all participants in the European Single Market work together effe ctive human rights and 
environmental protection can be achieved. 
2. Develop new standards and improve the quality of existing standards for conformity 
assessment 
The European regulators are called upon to define clear and mandatory requirements for complianc e with social, 
environmental and economic criteria along the entire supply chain. The clearer and more transparent the 
requirements are, the better tracking and tracing of the supply chain can be organised. High and uniform 
standards must be enforced to maximise the credibility of certificates attesting compliance with social and 
environmental standards. 
3. Introduce independent third-party assessments for supply chain requirements 
In order to ensure the necessary effectiveness of regulations and standards, i ndependent reviews to provide the 
necessary confirmation of conformity must be made mandatory. The European regulators are called upon to 
define minimum social, environmental, human rights and technical standards for conformity assessment and to 
set uniform quality standards for certification. The system of accreditation and conformity assessment already 
forms the basis for the global exchange of goods and services  today and can ensure the required transparency of 
the entire supply chain. 
4. Strengthen European market surveillance 
Global supply chains are today more extensive and complex than ever before. The EU therefore needs well -
coordinated and effective market surveillance by national authorities to identify non -conforming products at the 
earliest possible stage and withdraw these from the market. The new market surveillance regulation 
(2019/1020/EU) is just one step in the right direction. The authorities of individual member states devote 
different amounts of resources in varying intensities to market su rveillance. Particularly uniform Europe-wide 
requirements on the number of samples are lacking.

The measures in detail 
 
1. Introduce a European supply chain legislation 
 
The TÜV-Association advocates a mandatory legal framework that obliges companies to also c omply with 
internationally recognised social and environmental standards in their value chain.  
> Develop European legislation 
The TÜV-Association welcomes the proposal of the European Commissioner for Justice Didier Reynders to 
introduce a legislative proposal in 2021 for a Europeansupply chain legislation. It is important here that 
mandatory measures are introduced, rather than voluntary commitments. A growing number of global 
enterprises  are also demanding a mandatory instrument to comply with human rights and environmental due 
diligence obligations to achieve a level playing field. 
> Include human rights, social and environmental criteria in public procurement  
The European Union should assume a pioneering role and define clear human rights, social and  environmental 
criteria in public procurement legislation. 
 
2. Develop new standards and improve the quality of existing standards for 
conformity  assessment 
 
The quality and significance of conformity assessment services always depend on the applicable standards and 
criteria. The clearer and more concrete and measurable the requirements are specified, the better the basis for 
conformity assessment. 
> Define standards for compliance with human rights and environmental standards along the 
entire supply chain 
The European regulators are called upon to define mandatory specifications and requirements for compliance 
with social and environmental criteria along the entire supply chain. Existing international standards such as 
those of the ILO, UN, ISO and IEC should be considered during development of the catalogue of criteria. 
Existing standards like the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises, the UN Guiding Principles on 
Business and Human Rights and the core elements of the International Environmental A greements (IEA) 
should also be implemented. The clearer the requirements, the more reliable the assessment of compliance 
can be – and thus also the organisation of tracking and tracing along the supply chain. .  
> Support standardisation bodies worldwide and enforce standards 
While countless systems are already in place to verify and organise corporate due diligence obligations,

uniform standards are lacking. With the “chain of custody” international standard (ISO 22095), a standard is 
currently finalised that creates a framework for traceability of products and materials in all sectors. Until now, 
such standards have only existed for specific industries, such as the timber or cocoa industries. ISO 22095 is the 
first standard to provide generally applicable definitions, overarching approaches for design, implementation 
and organisation, five models for the design of traceability in supply chains. The traceability of products and 
materials is not only important at the end of the supply chain for marketing, but a lso for processing companies. 
The textile manufacturer must for example know whether the shredded plastic scraps are actually fishing nets. 
The TÜV-Association is of the opinion that international standards should always be aspired to as they are 
most conducive to the free movement of goods. Global standardisation work should be supported and the 
enforcement of global standards fostered. 
 
3. Introduce independent third-party assessment for  supply chains 
 
International standardisation of the requirements and impartial confirmation of conformity with applicable laws, 
guidelines and standards is crucial so as to verify compliance with human rights and environmental standards along 
the entire supply chain. Conformity assessment conducted by independent testing, in spection and certification (TIC) 
organisations – for example, during on-site inspections and audits, tests in laboratories or calibrations of measuring 
equipment – confirm conformity with the requirements through attestation of conformity or certification.  
Independent assessment service providers such as the TÜV companies regularly prove their competence and 
impartiality during a competence assessment and continuous surveillance by public authorities (accreditation and/or 
government recognition). The accreditation procedure is defined in the ISO/IEC 17000 international standards series. 
This creates a sound and uniformly recognised basis for private conformity assessment bodies to champion public 
protection interests. The necessary trust in the products, ser vices, facilities, processes, systems and information to be 
assessed is thus ensured.  
> Make independent assessment along the supply chain mandatory 
In order to ensure the required effectiveness and efficiency of legislation and standards, mandatory 
independent assessments to provide the necessary confirmation of conformity must be made mandatory. The 
system of accreditation and conformity assessment already forms the basis for the global exchange of goods 
and services today. It can therefore also ensure the necessary transparency of the entire supply chain. 
> Set uniform quality standards for conformity assessment 
In this context, it is also necessary to define minimum social and environmental standards for conformity 
assessment, and to set uniform quality standards for certification and audit reports. The international 
standards for conformity assessment and accreditation systems should be taken as the basis for this.  
> Facilitate access to data

the complete traceability and consistent quality of products. A prerequisite for this is non -discriminatory access

> Provide orientation for consumers 
Marks of conformity based on certification offers consumers valuable guidance in their purchasing decisions. It 
creates transparency by summarising and comprehensibly documenting the characteristics relevant to the 
respective product – for example, compliance with human rights and environmental requirements. This allows 
consumers to compare products and at the same time compensates for the sellers’ information advantage over 
the buyers. 
 
4. Expand market surveillance 
 
Global supply chains are today more extensive and complex than ever before. The EU therefore needs well organised, 
coordinated and suitably effective market surveillance by the authorities more than ever to identify non -conforming 
products at the earliest possible stage and instigate appropriate measures. Market surveillance aims to ensure that 
products comply with the applicable requirements relating to public interests such as health and safety in general, 
health and safety in the workplace, consumer protection, environmental protection and public safety.  
> Strengthen European market surveillance 
Market surveillance must ensure the uniform enforcement of harmonisation legislation throughout the EU so 
as to create a level playing field among economic stakeholders. In practice, market surveillance within Europe 
currently still demonstrates considerable shortcomings though, particularly since the authorities of individual 
member states devote different amounts of resources in varying intensities to market surveillance. Market 
surveillance should be strengthened by introducing comprehensive Europe -wide guidelines, increasing the 
intensity of checks and standardising the number of samples throughout the EU.  
> Establish international market surveillance 
In addition to strengthening European market surveillance, international market surveillance should be 
established to effectively address the complex global nature of supply chains. .

VdTÜV (Association of Technical Inspection Agencies) advocates technical safety and trust in 
products, plants and services by independent testing in Berlin and Brussels. Its members are the TÜV 
organisations as well as industrial companies which are affiliated with the objectives and tasks of the 
association. 
With its positions on safety in the industrialised society, on mobility as well as on products and 
services, VdTÜV is a sought-after contact for politicians, companies, associations and authorities. The 
association is committed to adapting rules and standards to the latest technological standards at all 
times. With its experts, VdTÜV pursues the aim of safeguarding the high level of technical safety 
within our society and developing secure frameworks for technological change.

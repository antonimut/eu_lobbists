UNI Europa reply to EC Consultation on Sustainable Corporate Governance 
 
UNI Europa, the European Services Workers Union and EU sectoral social partner, has 
read the Commission’s Roadmap on Sustainable Corporate Governance with great interest 
and welcomes the attention being paid to an range of topics that we as trade unions have 
long been raising as problematic in the current corporate setup of our economy. 
Short-termism and a pervasive focus on shareholder dividends have, as the Commission 
correctly points out, had deep effects on the structure of many companies and caused a 
range of unfortunate knock -on effects in these companies’ ability to deal with chall enges, 
be they short or long -term. Many of the issues are not new but have been addressed by 
other organisations in the past, e.g. G20/OECD Principles of Corporate Governance and 
Basel Committee on Banking Supervision Guidelines on Corporate Governance principles 
for banks. Yet, the uptake of these suggestions have been slow and in many cases 
inadequate to bring about change in a holistic way that would mitigate the many problems 
currently plaguing our economies. We therefore support Option C outlined in th e 
Commission’s Final Report, page VII (Study on directors’ duties and sustainable corporate 
governance).  
UNI Europa therefore looks forward to the Commission working on the following topics. 
Due diligence duty 
UNI Europa’s feedback on the proposal of a du e diligence duty is joined by UNI Global 
Union, the global trade union federation for the skills and services sectors. 
We have felt the impact of the pressure for short -termism and the corporate failures to 
address human rights impacts. As such, we support the introduction of a European directive 
on mandatory human rights due diligence and responsible business conduct to address 
these issues. We have worked extensively both together with multinational businesses and 
holding them to account on their global h uman rights’ impacts. We will be providing 
examples of lessons from this experience as a resource for the Commission.  
Based on this experience, we make the following specific calls for the directive: 
Companies covered  
The directive should cover all compa nies which are established or active in the EU, 
regardless of sector, size or legal form. 
Much of the due diligence debates have focused on issues in the value chain of sectors 
considered high risk such as mining or manufacturing. However, we have also obs erved 
widespread and persistent serious human rights issues within companies’ own global 
operations in the service sectors. As such, it is critical that the initiative cover all sectors.

It must also recognise the need for due diligence requirements to apply to a company’s own 
operations, including their own employees, outsourced workers, and to corporate 
subsidiaries or joint ventures. 
Importance of freedom of association and collective bargaining 
The directive should cover all human rights. Within this, i t is critical that the fundamental 
labour rights of freedom of association and collective bargaining are recognised as central 
components. These rights are fundamental rights in international standards, including as 
core ILO conventions.  
In addition to being fundamental rights, they are considered enabling rights, as respecting 
these rights often leads to fulfilling other human rights. The ability for workers to freely 
organise themselves into a trade union and collectively bargain provides workers a means 
to monitor and protect their human rights’ when states or businesses may be unable or 
unwilling to do so.  
Involvement of trade unions in the process 
To ensure that the initiative is effective, it is essential that workers, worker representatives, 
and trade unions at national, European and global levels have an integral role in the due 
diligence process. They have a stake in companies and their long-term success beyond the 
role of other stakeholders. They also have expertise that can strengthen the due dil igence 
process from in-depth understanding of their companies’ practices and labour rights risks.  
This should include: 
• the right for trade unions at the national, European and global levels to negotiate with 
the company over the due diligence policy and process;  
• the right for workers’ representatives to be informed and consulted throughout the 
process;  
• an early alert mechanism should be developed in partnership with the trade unions at 
the national, European and global levels in the companies concerned. 
Effective remedy and meaningful enforcement 
Effective remedies and access to justice must also be available for victims, including trade 
unions. Steps to improve access to justice should be incorporated, such as fair rules on 
disclosure of evidence.  
Given the failure of a voluntary approach, the directive must ensure meaningful 
punishments and enforcement to create accountability. This should include a range of 
consequences including exclusion from public support. A strong liability regime must be 
introduced for cases where companies fail to respect their due diligence obligations, without 
prejudice to joint and several liability frameworks. 
Directors’ duties 
As the Commission itself points out in the impact assessment, there is a problem with the 
current incentive structure in place in many larger companies, both public and private, with 
the average CEO remuneration package having grown exponentially over the last decades, 
while the average salary in the same companies have almost stagnated. This discrepancy 
in itself is a problem because it drains resources from the company that should normally 
have been used to pay for salary increases for the rest of the employees, finance training 
and allow the company to plan long -term investments. We would therefore be in favour of 
defining maximum levels of remuneration for top-positions in companies and to look at how 
the focus of top-management in companies can be moved from short-term gains to the long-
term development of the company. This would include KPIs with lo ng-term priorities, 
promoting cooperation with all concerned stakeholders in the company (chief among them 
the employee representation), development (of skills and infrastructure) and a realignment 
of company operations to develop sustainable value chains and play a beneficial role in 
their respective countries. For multi-national companies this would include promoting labour

rights and trade union cooperation within all the company’s branches and suppliers, not 
only within the HQ. We are however cognisant of the fact that CEO remuneration packages 
are not the only problem to be addressed and is often directly linked with the continued 
demand from shareholders for high annual pay -outs, even in times of difficulty for the 
company or society. Addressing this i ssue is therefore of equal importance and while 
shareholder activism is doing its part to remedy the problem, a legislative helping hand is 
needed. This is especially the case since a majority of publicly listed stocks today are held 
by institutional inves tors, who often have different investment profiles than individual 
investors due to competition among institutions to provide best pay -outs and the ability to 
acquire large parts of a company’s stocks. For this reason, we support developing criteria 
for sh areholder pay -out, which would incentivi se a focus on long -term priorities. Or 
potentially bring back one the Commission’s own ideas, of providing “loyal” shareholders 
with extra voting influence, to disincentivi se the almost pump -and-dump like behaviour 
exhibited by the current average holding time for stocks in the EU in 2020 falling to a 
staggering 5 months. 
Employees as a resource 
While often not recognised externally, employee and their representatives have a deep 
knowledge of the various links in a co mpany’s value chains and they are therefore ideally 
placed to point out any weaknesses or discrepancies in these chains. But often raising these 
weaknesses in front of management or the Board will tend to not be well received, since 
management will, as pre viously mentioned, be remunerated based on short -term 
performance, rather than subsequent performance, no matter how high the risks for future 
problems to arise are. We often encounter this type of short -sightedness in cases of 
outsourcing or off-shoring, where the plan looks promising on paper, but does not take into 
account the loss of implicit/tacit knowledge, differences in incentive structures between the 
parent and outsourcing company, language and time-zone differences, to name but a few. 
This in the end can result in irreversible damage to the company over the long-term. Yet in 
most cases, the employees working in the parent company have been able to predict many 
of the problems that would later occur but were either never consulted or their warnings  
were dismissed as scaremongering. We therefore hope that the Commission will take this 
opportunity to strengthen requirements for employees and their representatives to be heard 
in decision-making in companies and to properly enforce the Information and Consultation 
rights of employee representatives (COM(2017) 250 final – Establishing a European Pillar 
of Social Rights, p.34-36). 
Board composition 
We along with many other stakeholders have since the financial crisis in 2008, been calling 
for increased diversity in the composition and recruitment for company Boards. With the 
impending challenges, caused by the Corona crisis, facing the European economies, there 
is an urgent need for a multitude of competences on company Boards, which should reflect 
the local society and culture within which the company exists. In the finance sector we often 
see Boards and management being populated with people having very little connection to 
the countries where they operate and with little interest and understa nding of how labour 
relations are structured in the country. A diverse composition of management and the Board 
prevents ‘group -think’ and ‘confirmation -bias’, which is essential to ensure company 
development and the ability to deal with unforeseen situations. Diversity in terms of gender, 
ethnicity, background, and professional skills has been shown to improve innovation, growth 
perspectives and the strategic opportunities on the long-term for companies. 
Continuing on the note on professional background and competences, it is important in our 
view, that the competence requirements of top -management and the Board does not 
impinge on the diversity of management and Board. While ensuring that potential Board 
members have the knowledge and experience to assume t heir responsibility, the 
requirements should not restrict the field of potential candidates so much that only 
candidates with a similar professional background pass through the ‘eye of the needle’, to 
the point where there is little diversity of thought provided even if all the ‘diversity boxes are

ticked’. It is also important to ensure a high level of diversity of competences and 
independence for key positions, e.g. in finance it would be the AML responsible, that 
oversee the company’s compliance with important rules. 
Furthermore, it is in our opinion important to address another issue which is ‘overboarding’. 
In times of crisis, a Board position is extremely time and effort consuming. This is even more 
the case for the position of Chair of the Board, but applies to all positions on Boards, when 
the survival of the company is at stake. Yet, we often see members of Boards, holding 
positions on several different Boards, even in some cases having the chairmanship of 
several Boards simultaneously. This hardly seem like a solution which benefits anyone else 
but the person in question themselves, could potentially lead to conflicts of interest. We 
therefore think the Commission should take a closer look at this issue in its work going 
forward. 
Risk assessment 
It i s important for company management and Boards to develop an informed and 
sustainable risk assessment, that impacts the company’s daily operations in relation to the 
changes brought about by the company’s ecosystem, especially in terms of business 
development, potential for innovation and ability to survive in the long run. It is vital for a 
company’s chances of survival, that the management and Board have a true picture of the 
company’s risks, including the correlation between different categories of risks in the 
company’s global value chains, in order to make the best decisions. Hence why it is 
imperative to involve the employees and their representatives to a much higher degree than 
has happened so far, in identifying, evaluating and preventing those risks that develop in a 
company’s value chains and restructurings. Sustainable corporate governance is the glue 
that binds risk management in the company, and this starts with the ‘tone from the top’ and 
requires commitment, understanding and enforcement by man agement and employees 
alike. 
Non-financial risks 
A revised perspective on company risk profiles need a systemic focus on company’s non -
financial risk factors, and consideration for the links between different categories of risks, 
where the deep knowledge o f employees should be leveraged. Company organisation of 
work, culture and governance structure, inducement structures and overall vision is 
important factors for a company’s handling of risks. I.e. Labour conditions in all countries 
where the company is a ctive, should be seen as a non -financial risk, with absence 
especially of social dialogue and collective agreements being considered a risk. These 
factors have an impact on the relationship with local society, customer relations, supplier 
relations and in the long term a company’s general reputation. Furthermore, these factors 
have an impact on the transparency of a company, both in its behaviour internally and 
externally, and hence for the employee motivation, well -being, and satisfaction with their 
work.  
Renewing the social contract 
Employee representation in a company’s formal decision -making process is in our 
experience important to balance out often contradicting concerns and wishes in the daily 
operations of a company. A shared responsibility between employee and management 
ensures a balance between decent working conditions and good economic results. This in 
turn ensures the company’s reputation, survival and sustainability profile in the long term. 
Democratisation of responsibility ensures a long -term perspective on the company’s 
objectives, increases the confidence of all involved parties and ensures continuous dialogue 
both internally and externally of the company, thereby avoiding sudden development of 
problems which can then escalate and cause long-term negative consequences.

Social responsibility 
As we have previously stated, the surrounding society is important for a company’s survival 
in the long run, and while some company’s seem to consider it a good option to simply move 
operations when they become too unpopular, we encourage the Commission to help them 
become more socially responsible. This responsibility extends to the company’s willingness 
to invest in possibilities for the future; be it product development, technological 
development, employee training and wages, improvement of local society and environment. 
A company’s social responsibility must extend to all areas of its operations, which also 
includes all countries where it operates. No longer should companies be allowed to freely 
speculate in where to place their operations based on where wages and trade union density 
are the lowest. Nor should the tax loopholes currently siphoning off millions of Euros from 
country tax revenues, be allowed to stay open for exploitation as they currentl y are. If we 
truly want to develop the social Europe promoted by President von der Leyen, the 
Commission must address these issues to the best of its abilities in order to keep the faith 
of the European citizenry in the European project alive. We as always are ready to help the 
Commission with this work and look forward to providing our comments in coming 
consultations. 
Whistle-blower protection 
While not mentioned by the Commission in its impact assessment, we see the aspect of 
whistle-blower protection as being quite important when discussing corporate governance. 
By ensuring that whistle-blowers can come forward with information about companies hiding 
or misreporting data, legislators will promote truthful reporting in the first place. This 
however requires that rules and a structure is set in place, which protects the identity of the 
whistle-blower as much as possible, encourages a corporate culture where concealing 
wrongdoing is not incentivised and which has clear channels that whistle -blowers can 
approach when wanting to report. 
The importance of taking a comprehensive approach 
UNI Europa  would like to emphasize the importance we see in thinking sustainable 
corporate governance into the larger framework of due diligence and sustainable finance. 
Many of the points currently being raised as important to consider in the due diligence 
debate also apply to the corporate governance discussion. By requiring companies to 
submit due diligence reports and data, their corporate governance approach would also be 
affected in a positive direction. However, this leads to another important point, which is the 
need for coherence in rules. Given the numerous different proposals currently being 
developed on the topic of reporting, it is important that the Commission ensures that these 
files are developed together rather than on top of each other. This way loop -holes can be 
avoided, as can unnecessary double -reporting. One example of such a move toward 
coherence, would be to use the NFRD review process to prepare the NFRD to be integrated 
with possible future due diligence and corporate governance reporting. Finally, we would 
also like to express our support for the reply sent in by ETUC and their appeal f or a 
European directive on mandatory human rights due diligence and responsible business 
conduct. 
About UNI Europa, the European services workers union 
As the European trade union federation for 7 million service workers, UNI Europa speaks 
for the sectors that constitute the backbone of economic and social life in Europe. 
Headquartered in the heart of Brussels, UNI Europa represents 231 national trade unions 
in 50 countries, including: Commerce, Banking Insurance and Central Banks, Gaming, 
Graphical and Packaging, Hair and Beauty, Information and Communication Technology 
Services, Media, Entertainment and Arts, Postal Services and Logistics, Private Care and 
Social Insurance, Industrial Cleaning and Private Security, Professional Sport and 
Leisure, Professionals/Managers and Temporary Agency Workers. UNI Europa 
represents the largest region in UNI Global Union.

UNI Global  
UNI Global Union, based in Nyon, Switzerland, represents m ore than 20 million workers 
from over 150 different countries in the fastest growing sectors in the world – skills and 
services. A total of 90% of new jobs are expected to be in these sectors in the next decade. 
UNI and our affiliates in all regions are driven by the responsibility to ensure these jobs are 
decent and workers’ rights are protected, including the right to join a union and collective 
bargaining.

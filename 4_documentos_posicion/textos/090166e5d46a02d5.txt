EC inception impact assessment: Sustainable corporate governance  
The Federation of Finnish Enterprises (FFE) is pleased have an opportunity 
to provide feedback and comment on the inception impact assessment of a 
sustainable corporate governance framework. 
General views 
It is a fact that capitalism and the market economy have recently been eval-
uated and criticized at an accelerating pace. For example, in fall 2019, 
leading American corporate executives (Business Roundtable) argued that 
companies must look after the interests of non-shareholders as well. In Fin-
land, at the latest survey made in January 2020 by one of the biggest 
banks (OP Financial Gropu) 72 per cent of business leaders of large com-
panies believed that their company's task is also to solve social problems.  
It is also clear, that businesses play a vital role in the economy by creating 
jobs, fostering innovation and providing essential goods and services. Busi-
nesses make and sell consumer products; manufacture equipment and ve-
hicles; grow and produce food; provide health care; generate and deliver 
energy; and offer financial, communications and other services that under-
pin economic growth. As well it is clear that businesses need to generate 
long-term value for shareholders, who provide the capital that allows com-
panies to invest, grow and innovate. FFE sees that responsibility is already 
today deeply included in increasing shareholder value - as repatriation gen-
erally cuts the company's growth and value. 
The good governance of corporation today is mainly based on shareholder-
centricity which refers to a system of company law in which shareholders 
exercise ultimate control and in which shareholders may, within the frame-
work of special legislation make decisions in their interests. A key form of 
exercising power in such a system is the election of the company’s board of 
directors, and the tasks of the board ultimately aim at - and in the long run - 
to create value for shareholders. FFE sees ultimate control over the corpo-
ration should rest with the shareholder class; the managers of the corpora-
tion should be charged with the obligation to manage the corporation in the 
interests of its shareholders.

It should also be noted that a company is usually (not always) established 
to serve the founders ’own interests, and the company operates primarily at 
the risk of the founders. In case any changes to this basic principle – there 
are multiple aspects to consider. For example, alternative models regarding 
to the corporate governance to the shareholder-centered model would need 
to decide when (and why) the company’s goal changes in the company’s 
development curve so that: it must be in the interests of non-shareholders. 
The greater the change relative to the shareholder-centric model, the 
higher the threshold for the company's development is formed. 
About the EY study on directors’ duties and sustainable corporate governance 
The objective of the study referred to in the inception impact assessment 
was to assess the root causes of “short termism” in corporate governance, 
discuss their relationship with current market practices and/or regulatory 
frameworks, and identify possible EU-level solutions, also with a view to 
contributing to the attainment of the UN Sustainable Development Goals 
and the goals of the Paris Agreement on climate change. The aim clearly is 
a statement having been implied at the outset of the study. 
However, the study seems biased towards producing preconceived results 
rather than containing a dispassionate, impartial and comprehensive analy-
sis regarding different aspects and views on the matter. The study pro-
ceeds by unsupported assertions such as that managers and investors are 
short-termist and corporate law is responsible for it, rather than any rigor-
ous demonstration. The study also rests heavily on reviews of existing liter-
ature but overlooks non-supporting contributions and the choice of literature 
appears surely biased towards the preconceived outcomes. 
Given the substantial risk of unintended consequences, it is essential to 
base any reform on the highest-quality evidence. In places, the evidence 
presented in the study is very one-sided and low-quality. At times, it reads 
as if the authors have already decided the outcome and then have 
searched for all evidence – regardless of quality – to support this conten-
tion, while ignoring even high-quality evidence that suggests the opposite. 
As a conclusion, the study suffers from some serious methodological as 
well as analytical shortcomings which by themselves render it unfit as a ba-
sis for EU-level legal intervention. 
Objective - corporate governance framework  
A key economic rationale for the shareholder-centric model regarding also 
the good corporate governance relates to the clarity of the goal set for

management, which in turn makes it possible to control the activities of 
management, both financially and ultimately also in a legal sense. The 
more stakeholders are defined for the company, the more difficult it be-
comes to control management, and the more goals the more the manage-
ment can promote its own interests. 
FFE also points out that no one is forced to invest in the shares of a limited 
company or other securities. Models where the real goal - or obligation – is 
other than generating a profit for shareholders, would most likely affect in-
vestors ’willingness to invest in. Also any non-shareholder-centered models 
would most likely involve the risk of seeking capital outside the EU, a re-
duction in foreign investment and thus a loss of competitiveness. A reduc-
tion in the shareholder surplus or becoming more uncertain would also 
likely affect funding for the development of environmentally friendly technol-
ogies, for example. 
Another key element to the shareholder-centered model is that the involve-
ment of other stakeholders such as customers, creditors, employees as 
well as the status of the environment are regulated by specific legislation 
and / or agreements. In general, shareholder-centricity does not mean that 
shareholders are a better or more important stakeholder than others, but 
that shareholder protection is the most socially sensible way to organize 
shareholder protection, while other stakeholders are the most socially sen-
sible to organize in other ways, such as through special legislation. 
It should also be noted that from an environmental point of view, there 
should probably be no reason to assume that stakeholder-centric models 
would be substantially better than shareholder-centric models. For exam-
ple, promoting the interests of an employee, customer, financier or munici-
pality does not, at least obviously, have a more positive effect on the envi-
ronment than promoting the interests of a shareholder. It has also been 
suggested that other stakeholders may be shorter-term than shareholders. 
It is said at the inception impact assessment that “the initiative aims to en-
sure that sustainability is further embedded into the corporate governance 
framework with a view to align better the long-term interests of manage-
ment, shareholders, stakeholders and society”. However, there are multiple 
question regarding this aim that should be addressed before any further ac-
tion: 
o The essential question is how a corporate governance model 
based on strong corporate social responsibility defines a com-
pany’s operational responsibilities?

o A key question on the content of regulation would be how to as-
sess whether the CSR criterion is being met and how this should 
be changed to make it so.  
o Which body determines the content of the criterion in each 
case? 
o Which body assesses corporate social responsibility in practice? 
o Should an assessment be made before or after the investment 
or other transaction? 
o How often the estimate should be made (for each significant 
transaction or at least at certain intervals)? 
o What resources and expertise are used to carry out the assess-
ment, given that companies cover all possible sectors and often 
cover many countries? 
o What are the penalties for violating the criterion? 
o How much would the functioning of the economy and society be 
affected by that the key property rights of the main economic op-
erators would not (obviously) be clearly defined and would the 
impact after all be in the interests of the environment? 
o Would the proposed arrangement lead to an increase in man-
agement power and thus inefficiency? 
o How would the described regulation and the associated uncer-
tainties affect the willingness to invest in joint stock companies, 
the operation of companies in practice and the costs of compa-
nies, and indirectly for example in public sector tax revenues? 
o Would delegating CSR decisions to corporate management be 
the most effective and democratic way to promote CSR? 
FFE highlights that before any legislative action is taken, efforts should be 
made to answer the above questions. We also see that the objectives men-
tioned in the inception impact assessment such as helping companies’ di-
rectors to establish longer-term time horizons in corporate decision-making 
and companies to identify, assess and mitigate adverse impacts in the 
value chain should in any case rather be organized through special legisla-
tion.

The level playing field 
 
As it is stated at the impact assessment, many companies are growing and 
becoming EU-wide or even global, and even smaller companies 
havevcross-border supply chains. This is true. However, as the national in-
tervention might be likely to result in market fragmentation, which increases 
the costs for companies as well as for their investors and other stakehold-
ers, it should also be noted that even though EU-wide legislation would 
maybe create leverage and enable building a global level playing field for 
EU companies to operate on sustainable terms, the impact assessment 
does not take into consideration the fact how this kind of legislation would 
impact on the competition between companies operating in EU and outside 
EU. Also, there are no mentions on the impact assessment regarding the 
impact on companies based outside EU, but which have operations in EU 
member states. 
FFE sees that before taking any legislative action, the competitive effects 
on EU companies operating in the Single markets as well as the effects on 
non-EU companies with activities in the Single markets should be thor-
oughly examined. 
Finally, it is said at the inception impact assessment that “in the baseline 
scenario, without any policy change at EU level, Member States will con-
tinue to apply or allow a variety of solutions as regards corporate and board 
duties. If left to voluntary market action, short-termism is unlikely to de-
crease. FFE sees that this base line ignores the impact of the market and 
consumers on businesses, even though it is mentioned that studies shows 
that companies performing well on sustainability factors outperform their 
peers and are more competitive. Regarding this question it is good to notice 
that recently published The Deloitte Global Millennial Survey 2020 stated 
that one the main messages by young people (millennials and generation 
Z) is that “the successful management of one's own company is not 
enough, but managers must show responsibility for wider societal chal-
lenges through their practical actions.” The results of the questionnaire 
study showcase that the behavior of consumers is changing and consum-
ers, especially young people are more aware than ever. Also knowing the 
level of environmental, social, and economic impact and viability of vendors 
and customers is becoming increasingly common as all industries move 
towards more sustainable future due to regulatory requirements and grow-
ing consumer demand for sustainability. Therefore, it is a fact that compa-
nies as well as their management need to take that into consideration to be 
able to maintain the company’s growth and to increase the shareholder 
value in the long run.

The impact on SMEs needs to be considered more broadly 
It is good that it is clearly stated at the inception impact assessment that 
“due consideration will need to be given to limiting and alleviating the bur-
den for SMEs, for instance by the inclusion of exemptions from certain sub-
stantive and possible reporting obligations and/or by simplified standard(s), 
according to the proportionality principle based on the size and impact of 
the company.” However, FFE highlights that possible impacts on SMEs 
need to be considered more broadly. For example regarding the due dili-
gence duties that would require companies to address and mitigate the ad-
verse impacts of their operations in the supply chain one must take into ac-
count that SMEs have less capacity, information and resources to build on 
diversified supply chains. In addition, they often have weaker bargaining 
power on the aspects of supply-contracts than large companies have. 
Equally, SMEs are dependent on the information provided by their suppliers 
as they often do not have the means or capacity to do an extensive re-
search on suppliers’ backgrounds’. It must be kept in mind that the first duty 
of an entrepreneur and business management is always to ensure the busi-
ness continuity and liquidity of the company. 
Conlusion 
As a conclusion, FFE does not see any need to modify the company law 
Directive (2017/1132) or the consolidated Directive on Shareholder rights 
(2007/36) at this point. If any measures are taken at the EU-level to meet 
the objectives mentioned at the initiative they should be non-legislative.  
In addition, the outset based on which the possible initiatives are made 
needs in any case to be re-evaluated and any legislative actions, which 
may impact European companies and their competitiveness need to be 
based on a much more thorough research and facts and all measures need 
to be done by limiting and alleviating any additional burden for SMEs.

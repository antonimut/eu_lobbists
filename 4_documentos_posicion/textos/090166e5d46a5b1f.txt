H&M	Group’s	position	on	mandatory	due	diligence			October	2020	  H&M	Group’s	initial	contribution	 to	 the	European	 Commission’s	inception	impact	assessment	on	Sustainable	Corporate	Governance			H&M Group welcomes the opportunity to comment on the inception impact assessment ahead of the public consultation on sustainable corporate governance in 2021.  H&M Group was one of the first companies that made a public commitment to the UNGP through our Human Rights Policy, and our work to constantly improve our effort are annually described in our Sustainability Report and Modern Slavery Statement. We share the European Commission’s vision to establish a mandatory due diligence framework at European level to create leverage and enable building a global level playing field for companies to operate on sustainable terms.    Undeniably, the mandatory due diligence discussion is an important topic to many, the public and business, civil society and governments alike, hence, as a large global company H&M Group finds it important to bring clarity to our position and recommendations which we believe need to be considered to successfully arrive at a legal framework that is practically feasible, yet supports  comprehensive implementation of human rights due diligence.   H&M	Group’s	initial	recommendations	 Based on our experience, any European mandatory initiative shall:  1. Ensure a harmonised and common approach at European level, addressing the multiplication of distinctive national initiatives and legislations,  2. Build on existing reporting frameworks as well as the need for coordination with the revision of the Non-Financial Reporting Directive; 3. Be risk-based following existing international frameworks as the UN Guiding Principles on business and human rights (UNGPs), and OECD due diligence guidelines, including sectorial OECD guidelines such as the ones addressing due diligence in the garment and footwear sector; 4. Reflect the dynamism in the concept of a ‘smart mix’ of measures – as defined under the UNGPs -  by states, which demands an ongoing interplay between new regulations, policy measures and support to voluntary efforts by business, with each strengthening the effect of the others in driving a measurable increase in business respect for human rights over time; 5. Be cross-industry and relevant to both SMEs and large businesses alike. This demands a pragmatic framework and content that have some flexibility in order to be applicable to different industries and business sizes, as there is no such thing as one size fits all; 6. Recognise the different actors’ roles along the value chain, including governments, and clearly define roles and accountabilities accordingly;  7. Provide legal certainty for businesses through a common language and definitions; 8. Apply a practical approach. Practical realities should be carefully considered as successful implementation relies on the understanding of how risks can be efficiently addressed in specific contexts which varies a lot from one another. This will help avoid unforeseen and potentially harmful outcomes. Good due diligence should be judged not on impacts only but on efforts. As some areas demand contributions from several sectors to sustainably change, one company cannot be held accountable for lack of or slow impact when tackling complex topics in challenging environments; 9. Recognise industry schemes and existing tools addressing due diligence. The textile industry has developed tools to implement and track progress with the above international standards through the Social & Labor Convergence Project, as well as the Higg Brand & Retailer Module. Both industry tools went through an OECD alignment assessment against the OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Supply Chains in the Garment & Footwear Sector.

H&M	Group’s	detailed	position	on	Mandatory	Due	Diligence	 Our commitment  H&M Group was one of the first companies that made a public commitment to the UNGP through our Human Rights Policy, and our work to constantly improve our effort are annually described in our Sustainability Report and Modern Slavery Statement. A mandatory due diligence legal framework would have the advantage of creating a level-playing field for all businesses and their operations to account for how the commitment to UNGP and scaling of human rights due diligence practices is improving the lives of people across the value chain. This could also increase transparency and understanding for the due diligence processes we conduct, and hence help us account for our business impact on people’s life but also future proof positive impact on the value creation of our business.   A practical framework supporting a level playing field for due diligence  We strongly agree with the encouragement that can be found in the UNGPs where states are recommended the use of a “smart mix” of mandatory and voluntary approaches to properly address human rights towards business operations. Any mandatory due diligence framework should align with the UNGPs and OECD Guidelines. Those guidelines reflect best practice in relation to the process of human rights due diligence, including the identification, prioritization and addressing of salient human rights issues.   However, if not properly constructed and based on the best practice of implementing the UNGPs, a legal framework in itself will not ensure a more efficient and effective and it will not automatically imply a better outcome in the communities and for the people that we impact throughout our operations. Currently, there are a few examples of instances where lack of clarity in legal language and content have caused confusion rather than clarity on what the requirements mean, especially when translated into practice and complex global value chains.   Potential positive effects of a mandatory due diligence process is that it can generate engagement across business functions internally, spread knowledge on human rights and business for stakeholders and the public, and reward companies based on their efforts, hence incentivizing even better implementation.   Moreover, whilst welcoming a legal definition and process we see that two particular routes have developed: International mandatory legislation addressing due diligence (through cross-national constellations such as the EU for example); and National mandatory legislation addressing due diligence.  As a multi-national company, we are interested in having legal obligations coordinated between states to the largest extent possible, and therefore support ongoing discussion on EU level for harmonizing efforts.    Clarity regarding scope of liability  It would be necessary to clarify the depth and scope of liability. The UNGPs provide guidelines in relation to the definition of cause, contribute and linkage, to be reflected in the liability to be adopted by the European Commission.  Companies/businesses are dealing with complex value chains with several levels of suppliers all the way up to raw material. Human Rights due diligence processes include preventative procedures as well as remediation measures across the value chain. In relation to these processes and measures clarity is also needed, on what is deemed responsible and appropriate action in terms of preventative measures and remedial action. The scope of liability should be reasonable and not be exclusively focused on individual businesses/companies at the top of complex value chains. We expect legislators to balance the consequences for failure to take action in certain business relationships with the right incentives that will lead to creativity, partnership and innovative thinking in addressing systemic and more distant value chain issues.  Linked to scope is also ways to achieve transparency and public’s requirements on transparency for different supply chain models. This would enable independent review and support level-playing field as well as to facilitate collaboration and collective action and leverage.

Reasonable accountability  Mandatory due diligence should not undermine sovereign states and governments and their duty to protect especially in countries with challenging dynamics and need for economic and social development.   There needs to be a consideration for potential negative consequences that this could have for investment in challenging countries/contexts, to avoid “de-risking” of investment in countries where economic development and inclusive growth is much needed to enable positive social outcomes.   The scope of accountability and liability should be clearly defined and should not be created in a way that discourages business partners, independent of size, to be accountable for their own operations. Here, it is important all business actors throughout the value chain are kept accountable for respective activities and impact, ensuring learning and growth through such ownership and responsibility.   Governments, international institutions and civil society should look at how it can enable and support collaboration and action in an efficient way. Particularly when it comes to business’ linkages and contribution to systemic issues in challenging dynamics in operating contexts’ which requires various actor’s involvement including collective business leverage.    Way forward  We encourage an inclusive process and aim to be actively engaged in the discussion. With presence in more than 70 countries and a history dating back more than 70 years we believe we have a lot of contributions to make, with lessons learnt, expertise and insights to inform discussions towards a framework that is pragmatic and feasible. We remain open and recognize that our position will evolve over time and through exchange with others.

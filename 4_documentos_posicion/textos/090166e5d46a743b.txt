1. Sustainable corporate governance includes sustainable innovation  Fraunhofer welcomes the Commission’s new EU strategy on sustainable economic growth as included in the Action Plan on Circular Economy3 and the European Green Deal.4 Addressing sustainable corporate governance as part of this, and moving away from short-termism as an investment paradigm focused solely on shareholders,5 is considered an important foundation to achieve the overall EU strategy.  As the Commission has delivered the EU Industrial Strategy as the core enabling mechanism to drive the twin green and digital transition6, Fraunhofer proposes that the frame of reference for sustainable corporate governance also include a focus on sustainable innovation.  It is considered that digitalisation and the ICT sector can only deliver results beneficial for European society in the context of sustainable innovation.     It is further noted that fostering innovation is one of the UN Sustainable Development Goals7 which the Commission aims to achieve.8    2. 3. The EU Charter on Fundamental Human Rights protects sustainable innovation  Fundamental rights are protected in Europe under the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (Charter),16  which constitutes primary European law, holding the same legal value as the Treaty of the European Union and the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU) – the basis upon which the European Union (EU) is established.  It is suggested that sustainable corporate governance be framed with reference to the Charter. By way of example, IP is recognised as a fundamental right pursuant to Article 17 of Charter, and we consider that this is only protected as a fundamental right if there is sustainable innovation that is supported by long-term strategies and investment – which would appear to be in line with what is proposed by the Commission’s sustainable corporate governance initiative. Both sustainable corporate governance and sustainable innovation thus appear to have an important and interconnected role for ongoing improvement in quality of life for society and competitiveness for industry.  Noting the findings of the Commission’s Charter report of June 201917 and of the European Agency for Fundamental Rights18 that the Charter is still not used to its full potential and improvements can be made in order to raise awareness of this document, Fraunhofer respectfully suggests contextualising sustainable corporate governance within the EU Charter and the fundamental rights being fostered through this initiative.   4. Potential impacts are that it is not possible for reinvestment in the ecosystem to occur, and that IP assets lose value or are no longer created. Investment in innovation and contributions to developing should thus be protected from a corporate governance regime which is driven by short-term, individual company financial goals.   Ambitious goals such as achieving the Digital Single Market and the adoption of 5G can only be accomplished by ensuring that all parties to a licensing negotiation arrive at the bargaining table in good faith and with all legal rights intact, with no one party overburdened by administrative red tape – which itself could lead to litigation or legal challenges to the right to protect IP assets.   In that sense, allowing short-termism to dominate corporate governance and create barriers to trade and innovation could have a direct effect of disrupting the achievement of goals set by the European Commission. As the time lag is considerable – an innovation cycle can easily exceed 10 years – barriers created today, if not countered, challenged and corrected, will be having an effect on the innovation for a decade, affecting various stages of the innovation cycle and, most importantly affecting the decisions on investment in innovation several years from now. Any slowdown in the area of innovative economy could be expected to have a negative effect on employment, GDP and trade balance in Europe.  In addition, this could diminish the return on investment in R&D for the European governments – and their taxpaying citizens.  In order to preserve incentives to invest in innovation and further trigger dynamic competition for and in the market, we respectfully invite the Commission to acknowledge that the real danger for innovation nowadays appears to be the scale reached by IP infringement based on the assertion that it is good to have cheap products available to consumers.  To counter such conduct - which only serves to provide infringing companies with an unfair advantage in the market compared to legitimate enterprise - sustainable corporate governance could include an obligation on companies to uphold the rule of law and incorporate the intent of supporting sustainable innovation within their corporate governance.  In order to demonstrate leadership in sustainable corporate governance consideration could be given to DG JUST working in conjunction with other DGs (such as DG Connect, DG RTD, DG Comp, DG GROW, DG Trade), so that a joint statement affirming the respect for rule of law, the applicability of EU law and CJEU jurisprudence to IP (including standard essential patents) can be prepared.  5. Short-termism poses risks to international standardisation potentially negatively affecting development of new standards in ICT  a. Sustainable corporate governance supports business model neutrality within the standard developing organisations (SDOs)  The European Commission recognises standards as solutions that are mutually beneficial for all actors in the market. Standardisation consists of defining through consensus-based decision making the technical or quality requirements with which current or future products, production processes, services or methods may comply. Standards enable the EU to function across Member States, with ICT standards being assured to be backward compatible and interoperable. Collaborative standards provide a framework for encouraging the development of new and improved products or markets and improved supply conditions.25   25  See European Commission, Guidelines on the applicability of Article 101 TFEU to Horizontal Cooperation Agreements (hereinafter referred to as the EC Guidelines on Horizontal Co-operation Agreements”, 2011/C 11/01, paragraphs 257 and 263, available at https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:52011XC0114(04)&from=EN.

 Sophisticated technologies such as those included in standards, are made up of various forms of IP, with contributions made by a variety of businesses, universities and research organisations.   It appears that some SDOs are being used as ‘soft targets’, to impose certain business models on all participants. This has been witnessed in other jurisdictions,26 to the detriment of innovation and in turn reducing society benefit. The impact of imposing one particular type of business model on all those desiring to participate in SDO activity is exclusionary, as it prevents the return on investment by contributors such as research organisations, universities and SMEs.    This could be either by virtue of a limited mandate or limited resources.  By way of example, universities and research organisations are most times prohibited from manufacturing or selling services to consumers. If an SDO required that technical contributions are to be made on a ‘royalty-free use’ basis, these SDO participants could be excluded from obtaining any return on investment from those contributions. Unlike other participants, there is no possibility to generate revenue further along the value chain. Failing to protect a ‘business model neutral’ approach to participants could thus have a real impact on innovation levels, and the broader innovation ecosystem.      Sustainable corporate governance includes supporting good governance in SDOs. The World Trade Organisation (WTO) Agreement on Technical Barriers to Trade (TBT Agreement)27 provides the international framework for SDO governance.  The WTO elaborates on the principles governing standards development, and establishes a link between standards development, openness and policy development, non-discrimination, and meaningful opportunities to participate. 28  Fraunhofer respectfully suggests that meaningful principles of sustainable corporate governance include upholding good governance in SDOs as set out in Annex 3 of the WTO TBT Agreement, as recently affirmed by the US Department of Justice, which has stated:  [T]he United States is also committed to ensuring that standards development does not create barriers to international trade, consistent with our treaty obligations.  Ensuring that the entire process of developing standards, including developing the intellectual property policies related to standards, remains balanced, open, and consensus-driven is a critical safeguard against standards development advantaging a particular country, region, or interest group.29  Fraunhofer humbly proposes the Commission include as a sustainable corporate governance principle respect for business model neutrality in the SDO setting, particularly for official European Standardization Organizations (the European Committee for Standardization (CEN), the European Committee for Electrotechnical Standardization (CENELEC) and the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI). Systemic changes require time  A systemic change such as redefining good corporate governance as sustainable corporate governance could require a change in corporate culture and thinking, which would take time.  In the meantime, Fraunhofer respectfully observes that there are ways for the European Commission to support sustainable corporate-decision making in the context of innovation immediately.  This could include:  i. Emphasising the importance of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights, highlighting the relevance and applicability of international rules, such as the WTO TBT Agreement, and reinforcing the role of the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU).   ii. Leading by example regarding sustainable governance by, for example, including sustainability criteria and innovation impact assessment criteria in any new initiative adopted by the European Commission.  iii. Sustainable policy development, meaning that new initiatives are checked for impact on the existing framework for protecting fundamental rights.  7. Sustainable corporate governance supporting innovation requires an wholistic approach and implies the need for policy coordination with the European Commission  Taking the long-term view and addressing sustainable corporate governance in conjunction with sustainable innovation ecosystems calls for a holistic policy approach, within the framework of international, EU and Member State national laws.   It is considered important that consistency be retained between the aims of sustainable corporate governance, EU IP policy and wider EU policy and practice. Ambitious goals such as achieving a Digital Single Market and the adoption of 5G can only be attained by encouraging investment in R&D and standardisation and safeguarding IP rights. Ultimately these elements are important for the sustainable, dynamic and international competitiveness of Europe, along with improvement in quality of life.  Fraunhofer encourages the European Commission to consider the implications of policy decisions in a coordinated manner, perhaps with input from relevant Directorates-General.  It is respectfully suggested that DG JUST’s initiative should support and align with the EU Industrial Strategy and its goals, along with the ‘Next Generation EU’ policy goals, highlighting the essential role played by IP and standardisation in the European Economy – especially during the current recovery from the economic and societal impacts of the Covid-19 pandemic.  Fraunhofer respectfully encourages DG JUST to heighten its engagement with other DGs (such as, DG Connect, DG RTD, DG Comp, DG Trade, and DG GROW), to have polyfacetic yet consistent measures adopted across Europe for the advancement of its industry and society with respect to sustainable corporate governance.

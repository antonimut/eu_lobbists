AMICE Note on EIOPA First Holistic Impact Exercise 
Executive Summary 
A significant number of AMICE members took part in EIOPA´s first data collection exercise and the 
two EIOPA’s Holistic Impact Assessment exercise s undertaken between March and August 2020. 
This note focuses on the first HIA exercise. AMICE expressed concerns with some of the proposed 
amendments which had been tested as part of the first HIA exercise. Some policy options which were 
part of the first data collection exercise were not tested in the HIA. We would have preferred a 
comprehensive approach by which the combined impact of all the different measures which will be 
part of the final advice of the 2020 review of Solvency II were tested.  
Our main concerns are detailed further below: 
Volatility Adjustment 
EIOPA’s proposed amendments to the VA would not fully help achieve the desired outcome and will 
introduce undue complexity. In particular, AMICE does not support the changes to the calculation of 
the risk correction and the introduction of liquidity penalties through the proposed AR5 or adjustment 
for illiquidity of liabilities. 
AMICE disagrees that the VA had been designed to take into account the illiquidity characteristics of 
the liabilities. Recital 32 of the Omnibus Directive clearly states that the volatility adjustment aims at 
preventing procyclical behaviour and mitigat ing exaggerations of bond spreads on own funds . The 
Application ratio 5 introduces double counting in the valuation of technical provisions and the solvency 
capital requirements as the SCR already captures 1 in 200 events in the mass lapse risk sub-module. 
Liquidity is already addressed in the Pillar 2 requirements of Solvency II. The adjustment for illiquidity 
of liabilities should therefore be removed. 
A risk correction determined as a crude proportion of spot spreads would be subject to exaggerated 
market movements and would strongly and unduly penalise undertakings by limiting the amount of 
artificial volatility captured by the VA introducing pro- cyclicality as well as massively overstating the 
risk. 
Long-term equity investment 
We strongly regret that EIOPA has specifically r equested an exclusion from the reduced equity risk 
of equities backing own funds. Insurers should be able to apply the LTE capital charge on their equity 
investments backing insurance liabilities as well as own funds provided, they have a sound ALM 
enabling them to gather enough cash-flows to cope with short term liquidity needs, longer term needs 
being addressed by solvency requirements. 
Moreover, EIOPA requires life entities to have liabilities with a duration in excess of 12 years  which 
undermines the LTEI approach and is a step back to a very conservative approach.  For non-life 
entities, EIOPA requires insurers to cover all their best estimate liabilities by High- Quality Liquid 
Assets. The liquidity ratio is unfortunately designed in a way that overstates liquidity needs as it is 
rather conceived as if minding solvency needs while also restraining what liquid assets are.  
We therefore propose an adequate liquidity ratio that deals with liquidity issues rather than solvency 
ones. A liquidity ratio should test the capacity of the undertaking to have sufficient cash inflows to 
cover outflows in a timely manner to avoid sales of assets with possible losses. A liquidity ratio should 
focus on the short-term outflows and the means with limited market exposure at the disposal of the 
company to make those payments.

Extrapolation 
Any change in the way the risk -free interest rate is derived should be assessed in conjunction with 
the other proposed changes. In is olation, AMICE recognises the negative impact of any change on 
the capital position of its members. 
The alternative method should not only be assessed in detail for the e uro but also for the  non-euro 
currencies such as the Swedish Krona and more work needs to be done especially regarding the use 
of the DLT criteria (i.e, the residual and the matching criterion) in deriving the first smoothing point (i.e 
FSP) and last liquid forward rate and appropriate weights wh ere the “alpha” parameter becomes 
particularly important.  
AMICE does not concur with the removal of the matching criterion . The alternative extrapolation 
methodology should incorporate the residual bond criterion and the matching criterion  and both 
criteria should also be appropriately calibrated for all currencies outside the Euro. For doing that the 
risk characteristics of each currency and specificities of its bond market should be taken into account.  
Another point of consideration is the inclusion of the Volatility Adjustment. If market data beyond the 
FSP is used, that data point on the discount rate should also include the Volatility Adjustment.  
Other alternative discount rates methods proposed by the industry should be assessed (see also the 
comments made by AMICE as feedback to the consultation). 
 
Risk Margin 
EIOPA’s proposal for a “tapering” approach”  - i.e. applying a tapering adjustment to the projected 
SCRs - is a step in the right direction. However, we disagree with EIOPA’s decision not to introduce 
further amendments to the risk margin calculation. The current design of the Risk Margin is extra 
interest rate risk sensitive, the CoC factor should therefore be a function of the risk-free interest rate 
in such a manner that a more anti -cyclical outcome is obtained. Furthermore, the MA or VA should 
be allowed when projecting future SCRs and diversification benefits between  life and non -life 
business within the same entity should be granted. 
Interest rate risk 
EIOPA´s proposal assessed in S cenario 1 remains in essence the proposal which was part of the 
2018 Solvency II review and it was deemed unsuitable by the industry back then. AMICE is not 
supportive of the HIA alternative calibrations 1 & 2 and believes that these do exaggerate the interest 
rate down risk especially for the short maturity points for which little evidence has been provided to 
support the calibration. We also do not believe that the amendments tested in the HIA take into 
account the industry concerns regarding the treatment of the illiquid part of the discount curve in the 
interest rate risk module.  
 
AMICE finds that the advice on interest rate risk published by EIOPA IRSG on June 2020 has the 
structural components and rationale to review the interest rate down risk and encourages EIOPA to 
continue to improve its proposal for interest rate risk.  
Countercyclical buffer 
EIOPA’s proposed Own Funds buffer is unnecessary. The inclusion of an additional discretionary 
buffer to be applied when credit spreads are excessively compressed would effectively create 
additional capital add-ons to deal with systemic risk and would basically increase the confidence level 
above 1 in 200. It is also worth noting that such a proposal would act in contradiction with the VA.

VOLATILITY ADJUSTMENT 
BACKGROUND 
The volatility adjustment has been established in order to limit the excessive and undue impact of 
volatility on the solvency position of undertakings. During market turmoil spreads may increase 
sharply and decrease the market value of fixed income assets to levels that are not justified 
by tangible default risk. This loss in market value may induce a decrease of the net asset 
values and solvency positions beyond true economic levels and spur non- desirable pro-
cyclical investment behaviours.  
Such will be the case where the net asset value results from assets and liabilities valuations that are 
not consistent. The VA mechanism was introduced to improve the consistency of the valuation of the 
best estimates with the dynamics of fixed income assets where marked to market so that the net asset 
value remain s meaningful and avoid undue volatility. For that, the volatility adjustment should 
adequately mitigate the effect of exaggerations of bond spreads. The VA adjustment should achieve 
the above prescribed goal of  full compensation net of default risk. Hence a special care should be 
given to volumes of fixed income assets and encompass them all to avoid an undershooting impact 
of the VA when applied to the RFR curve used to discount BE cash-flows.

ANALYSIS OF THE NEW VA FORMULA 
1. The calibration of the risk correction 
EIOPA’s motivation for the review of the risk correction 
In CP-19-465, EIOPA is concerned that risk correction is insensitive to changes in credit spreads  
which may lead to underestimating the risk in times of spreads increases.  
AMICE reiterates its reaction to CP -19-465 (see §2.291, page 33) where EIOPA claimed historical 
evidence linking spreads increases with defaults increases. That statement is in contradiction with the 
paper quoted by EIOPA itself (K. Giesecke et al.) where the authors find that there is little or no 
evidence that credit spreads respond to current or lagged default rates. Authors conclude that 
“Taken together, these counterintuitive results support the view that corporate credit spreads 
are driven largely by factors such as illiquidity”.  
Moreover, in CP -19-465 (§2.442), EIOPA states that “the risk correction is everything that is not 
liquidity premium” without enough substantiation. F or instance, prices are also driven by market 
features such as supply and demand beyond sole liquidity or risk issues. For instance,  institutional 
investors buying long-term bonds to back their liabilities also pay for a “term premium”. Even risk may 
be related to various factors, not all of them being of importance in this context, namely downgrade 
risk not associated with default. 
Current spreads cannot be used for the determination of the risk correction of investment 
grade fixed income assets 
EIOPA would like to move from a risk-correction based on long-term averages of spreads and defaults 
to a risk correction based on current spreads. Under the new approach, the higher the spread, the 
higher the risk correction and the lesser the portion of spreads that can be translated into the VA. 
We would like to refer to another context where AMICE proposed to replace external ratings by 
spreads during the 2018 review discussions. EIOPA (see EIOPA-BoS-17/280) argued against it and 
stated that "a proposal to use spr ead as a risk indicator instead of ECAI’s mapping may increase 
pro-cyclicality and incentivise (re)insurance undertakings to focus on the short-term credit risk”. 
We would raise the same concern in the matter of risk correction. A risk correction determined as a 
crude proportion of spot spreads would be subject to exaggerated market movements and would 
strongly and unduly penalise undertakings by limiting the amount of artificial volatility captured by the 
VA introducing pro-cyclicality as well as massively overstating the risk. 
The change in the risk calibration proposed by EIOPA is only relevant where increases in spreads 
may be correlated to increases in risks. In times of distress we found evidence that such a link exists 
for high yield instruments only and not in the case of investment grade instruments that yet represent 
the essential composition of the reference portfolios underlying the computations of the VA.

This contrasted link between spreads and defaults behaviours for high yield and investment grade

For investment grade, current spreads are not a suitable driver on which to base the calibration of the

To summarise, the risk correction proposed by EIOPA could be a suitable candidate for high 
yield investments only. There is no need for a change in the risk correction for investment 
grade.

Focus on own funds  
AMICE has a long- standing position on own funds and claimed several times that own funds allow

One of the objectives of VA was to limit pro- cyclicality. However, if own funds are not accounted for

situation where pro-cyclicality comes from own funds which are supposed to dampen pro-cyclical and 
herding behaviours.

Long-term equity investments (LTEI) 
1. General Comments 
EIOPA has introduced several adjustments to the criteria established under Article 171a and most 
are welcome and adequate. Nevertheless, the revision proposed by EIOPA would eventually 
unfortunately reduce the scope of this new asset class even further.  
Several criteria which govern the application of the long- term equity risk submodule have been 
removed or replaced. The removal of the requirement to identify the specific portfolio of liabilities 
(criteria b and c) and the change to the average holding per iod requirement (criteria e) are welcome 
changes. However, the newly proposed approach under revised criteria g) is undermining the 
approach and is a step back to a very conservative approach. 
2. Requirement to avoid forced sales for at least 10 years (criteria g): 
Life 
For life entities, EIOPA has proposed to use the eligibility to the bucket I of its flawed illiquidity criteria 
from the VA calculations and requires liabilities to have a duration in excess of 12 years. We regret 
that this approach does not ac count for the various risk profiles, management actions and going 
concern. Namely, insurers should be able to demonstrate that a significant portion of their liabilities 
remains in their portfolio over long horizons allowing and even requiring them to adopt long term 
investment stances. 
Non-life 
For non-life entities, EIOPA requires insurers to cover all their best estimate liabilities by High-Quality 
Liquid Assets.  
The above requirement is excessive. An insurer cannot be required to cover all its liabilities by liquid 
assets as, on a going concern, a sound ALM shall invest in liquid assets to cover the cash flows of 
the first years of the liabilities and on more illiquid assets on a longer term. Additionally, the scope of 
liquid assets as prescribed by EIOPA is overly restricted when excluding bonds of financial institutions 
and listed equity. Finally, the binary application (i.e. if the insurers meet the HQLA test or not) is also 
likely to lead to restricted application.  
On the numerator side, the list of assets to be retained as HQLA is not complete. For instance, equity 
investments seem to be considered as illiquid, while they have a secondary market much more alive 
than fixed income instruments, at least for the constituents of the main equity indices. An appropriate 
haircut could be set in order to account for the volatility of these investments but without discarding 
them completely. 
Moreover, the haircuts level may be deemed to be overly conservative, especially for level 2A bonds 
and covered bonds with CQS 0 or 1, whose quality are supposedly very high.  
On the denominator side, EIOPA’s does not delineate the scope according to liqui dity issues but 
rather accounts for the whole number of best estimates. Liquidity issues represent a risk that is needs 
to be appreciated on a horizon of one year maximum. The capacity to address outflows on an horizon 
longer than one year is not a liquidi ty but rather an ALM concern or a solvency concern addressed 
through the solvency requirements. As an example, let us refer to the cash flows of life obligations 
stemming from non-life and which are rather predictable and may very legitimately be backed by real 
estate or other illiquid investments.

Alternative methodology 
While EIOPA’s analysis in the latest consultation paper on the Solvency II review tended to focus 
mainly on the euro, the technical information published as part of the HIA shows that the shortcomings 
of the alternative methodology imply that, in practice, it cannot be fully applied for the vast majority of 
non-euro currencies. 
EIOPA’s analysis of the residual volume criterion and the matching criterion demonstrates that market 
conditions have not changed sufficiently to justify an extension of the LLP or  a change in the 
methodology, neither for the euro nor for any of the non-euro currencies. As such, the implementation 
of this proposal would require the removal of the matching and residual criterion which were 
introduced to ensure that the S olvency II framework was based on practical, rather than theoretical 
considerations. Another example of this is that the proposal has an unclear (arbitrary) method for 
compensating for different speeds of convergence, as demonstrated for example by the choice of 
mean reversion parameter for the Swedish krona in the technical specifications (40% mean reversion 
of “alpha”, instead of 10% mean reversion, as is used for all other currencies in the HIA).  
The adoption of an alternative extrapolation approach may bring potential risk management benefits. 
However, for such a methodology to be acceptable, it must be calibrated to respect the principle that 
insurers can match their liquid cashflows with bonds. It is not sufficiently clear whether EIOPA has 
considered this principle when putting forward  their alternative method . We have also noted 
previously that some  areas of the discount rate curve design which are flawed and may act 
counterintuitively to how insurers manage their market-based interest rate risk in practice. We would 
like to avoid situations where the solvency discount rate and the handling of market-based interest 
rates diverge too much, creating the need for different handling of risks for solvency and economic 
reasons. Also given this, we believe other alternative extrapolation approaches with potential greater 
risk management benefits should also be considered.  
AMICE had proposed in its comments to EIOPA’s consultation paper an Alternative 6 which is based 
on the Cardano-method, which  is applied in the Netherlands and Sweden to the IORP s. We do  
recognize the potential risk management benefits of an alternative extrapolation approach.  On the 
other hand, the Cardano method as commented in earlier papers, has a different methodology when 
applying weights between model/UFR data and market-based data, which we also feel sets the right 
incentives for good risk management of economic/market-based interest rate risk.

Interest rate risk 
EIOPA has retained its overly theoretical, uneconomic and extremely damaging proposal on the 
design and calibration of the interest rate risk submodule. We believe there are two key deficiencies 
in EIOPA’s proposed formulation;   
• the level of the effective lower bound of interest rates in the model and  
• the use of factor-based stresses for both the liquid and extrapolated parts of the interest rate 
term structure. 
As seen in the Scenario 1 of the HIA; EIOPA’s proposal will result in excessive capital requirements 
for interest rate risk. AMICE put forward, during the public consultation of the 2020 Solvency II review, 
a credible and economic  sound proposal which prov ides a better reflection of the effective lower 
bound for interest rates and results in more realistic capital charges.  
 
As part of the HIA, EIOPA has also requested, on a voluntary basis, information on two alternative 
IRR formulations.  
 
• The first alternative risk calibration is about the inclusion of an explicit -1.25% floor across all 
maturities. This floor level  has only a very marginal impact in the capital requirements at 
YE2019 and therefore the requested information is of negligible value. A similarly muted 
impact is expected at other times which renders the calibration of a floor ineffective. 
 
• The second alternative risk calibration provides some of the interest rate shocks which affect 
the illiquid part of the curve for currencies where the first smoothing point (equivalent to the 
LLP) is less than 15 years. This proposal partially addresses the issues caused by applying 
factor-based stresses to the illiquid part of the curve. However, in order to fully rectify this 
issue, the illiquid part of all shocked curves should, as stated in the AMICE’s response to the 
public consultation on the 2020 Solvency II review , be determined using the standard 
extrapolation methodology and parameters. Please refer to the Swedish IORPs Stress, which 
handles the stress of the illiquid part of the curve differently (i.e SFSA Regulatory Code, FFFS 
2019:21 for further information).

Countercyclical buffer 
Regarding some of the new elements being tested, our view is that EIOPA’s proposed Own Funds 
buffer is unnecessary. The inclusion of an additional discretionary buffer to be applied when credit 
spreads are excessively compressed would effectively create additional capital add-ons to deal with 
systemic risk. 
It is also worth noting that such a proposal would act in contradiction with the VA. There is no need 
for a buffer when the risk of default is correctly assessed under the VA. In the case of compressed 
spreads, almost no adjustment is added to the RFR curve through the VA. Additionally, there is no 
need for the release of a buffer accumulated in times of compressed spreads since the VA is acting 
as a compensation of exaggerated spreads when market conditions change and prevents the artificial 
volatility of Own Funds. When spreads increase a higher proportion of the spreads can be affected to 
the RFR curve. 
 
Non-proportional reinsurance 
There are a number of considerations and limitations in t he EIOPA´s proposal for the recognition of 
the risk mitigating effect of non-proportional reinsurance in the SCR standard formula.  
• Only applicable to a “single group of insurance policies 
Reinsurance programs may be defined in varied ways and notably in accordance with how 
risks are managed and perceived in reality and based on experience and practice. 
Reinsurance schemes exist independently of the way the standard formula splits the risks 
between segments and modules and sub-modules: 
EIOPA requires that a non-proportional reinsurance treaty is only  applicable to one single 
group of insurance policies and that such a group should only be defined within a Solvency II 
segment. In practice, a group of insurance contracts has more than one cover. For applying 
the reduction for non-proportional reinsurance loss, the insurer has to choose one among the 
covers; this would also mean that the mitigating effect of multi-LoB reinsurance covers which 
are very common in practice are not taken into account. In most cases the transfer of risks is 
done at a different granularity than the Solvency II segments. Non-life products often offer 
several guarantees that belong to different lines of business  or segments and that yet a 
reinsurance scheme can address at product level. Insurers will have to choose the group with 
the largest impact. Thus, the risk mitigation effect will only be partially recognized at best of 
times which would underestimate the e conomic benefit that the reinsurance covers would 
bring. In some worse cases, other restrictions such as the non-recognition of different covers 
on different perimeters will lead to a total inapplicability of any non -proportional cover of the 
insurer’s outwards RI. 
• A non-proportional reinsurance contract is not be eligible to non- life premium risk 
mitigation if that cover is also used to mitigate part or all catastrophe risk in the 
standard formula 
Often non-proportional reinsurance programs address risks that can either arise from the non-
life premium risk or the non- life catastrophe sub- module of the standard formula. This

• The methodology proposed is not suitable for facultative reinsurance covers.  
The methodology used in Paragraph 108 is not suitable for facultative reinsurance covers as 
the assessment that needs to be made regarding the average severity and average frequency 
are highly uncertain and difficult to make; There is usually no reliable historical information to 
base that assessment which means that the assumptions used are likely to be arbitrary and 
contain a high degree of uncertainty.  
• Limitations in risk mitigation calculated amounts 
Where it is possible to apply a cover according to what is left after the restrictive criteria 
presented above, the calculated risk mitigation effect under the HIA is low due the very 
conservative formulas proposed. The formula in paragraph 117 of the technical specifications 
can be extremely conservative depending on how premiums are computed. Such is the case 
with low RoL. Such low rates are provided by reinsurers who can benefit from ver y strong 
diversification at their level both across risks and regions but also over time.  
For the reasons mentioned above, t he new approach leads to an underestimation of the non-
proportional reinsurance adjustment. In addition, we believe that any proposals to measure the effect 
of non-proportional reinsurance should avoid introducing undue burden/complication to the standard 
formula calculation. The Delegated Regulation approach which derives the net volatility for some 
LoBs by multiplying the gross risk factor by a non- proportional factor should be maintained.  We 
reiterate the need for simplicity in the Standard Formula and a better consideration for NP reinsurance 
covers in the context of the Undertaking Specific Parameters (i.e USP).

Supporting document to response to the European 
Commission’s public consultation on the inception impact 
assessment of the Sustainable Products initiative 
 
 
 
Climate Action Network (CAN) Europe is Europe's leading NGO coalition fighting 
dangerous climate change. With over 170 member organisations active in 38 European 
countries, representing over 1500 NGOs and more than 47million citizens, CAN Europe 
promotes sustainable climate, energy and development policies throughout Europe. 
 
CAN Europe welcomes the Sustainable Products initiative, and considers it a further step 
towards filling a considerable and important gap in the EU policy framework addressing various 
aspects of environmental and social challenges – from the climate and biodiversity emergencies, 
to resource over-consumption, pollution and substances of concern. It is also a further step in 
addressing unsustainable production and consumption patterns, which continue to be at the 
source of many environmental crises, alongside the relentless quest for growth. Until these two 
underlying drivers of unsustainability are addressed appropriately, a narrow focus on efficiency 
will not help achieve political objectives of climate neutrality, living within planetary boundaries, 
zero pollution in a toxic-free environment or a regenerative economy. 
Nonetheless, developing EU sustainable product policy potentially provides orientations 
addressing several important economic elements. First, it can provide key sectors with clear 
product requirements that the market alone cannot provide. The key products and sectors 
identified are indeed priority ones due to their resource intensity, and CAN Europe particularly 
draws attention to: intermediate products of cement, chemicals and steel; electronics and ICT; 
buildings and construction; and batteries and vehicles. Ambitious product requirements, linked to 
sustainability strategies where relevant, can help drive innovation that provides economy-wide 
benefits and makes many environmental and health targets easier to meet. 
We also welcome the Commission’s firmer positioning on the development of sustainability 
principles. These principles are essential not only to provide a harmonised starting point for 
approaches to be taken across a range of products, but also to help develop a more harmonised 
understanding of sustainability beyond companies and sectors directly addressed by the 
Initiative. Analysis on EU Ecolabel criteria has shown that companies not applying for the label 
use the criteria nonetheless when assessing how to improve their product’s environmental 
performance.  
We also agree with the identified market and regulatory failures from the roadmap document, 
particularly those of product-related externalities not being fully internalised and EU initiatives 
and legislation only partly addressing product sustainability aspects, whether on a mandatory or 
voluntary basis. The Sustainable Products initiative must correct these regulatory failures, and 
we encourage the Commission to continue to work towards building market measures that 
penalise poorer performance rather than driving premium pricing on better performing products.  
Although we also agree that reliable – and verifiable - sustainability information along value 
chains must be more easily available, we caution against a policy approach that places too 
much focus on information as a mechanism for behavioural change. Such an approach does not 
reflect results of studies undertaken since the past 15 or so years, also in the context of the 
European Commission’s sustainable consumption and production agenda – studies consistently 
show that purchasing behaviour is more complex and less rational than simply having access to 
information. Choice editing – removing unacceptable products from the market – works more 
effectively, as has been seen from results of the Ecodesign Directive. 
Finally, we highlight two continuing gaps and weaknesses in recent cornerstone policy 
developments relating to the European Green Deal. The first is the Circular Economy Action

Plan’s narrow plastics focus on micro-plastics and on packaging. This is a significant missed 
opportunity to continue to implement and build upon the Strategy for Plastics in a Circular 
Economy, particularly in the design and use of plastics for lower environmental impact. 
Addressing chemicals in the Sustainable Products initiative could have extended to plastics as 
well, but it does not appear to do so. We hope that plastics will be addressed horizontally, 
alongside the other intermediate products of cement and steel.  
As the energy-intensive industry sector having the highest final energy consumption, the 
chemicals sector requires particular attention – from its production processes to its products, 
including plastics. Strong links need to be made between sectoral measures still awaited through 
the 2020 Industrial Strategy processes and the Sustainable Products initiative. 
The second is the unfortunate gap that appears to be becoming systemic between climate 
efforts and efforts on other environmental emergencies, particularly those coming under the 
circular economy agenda. This gap can already be seen in the impact assessment on the 2030 
climate target where circular economy features only in descriptive text and not at all in 
calculations towards GHG reductions. It is repeated in the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability 
which features little to no links to the sector’s considerable carbon intensity – in its feedstock and 
its production processes. Particularly in this strategy, climate efforts are relegated to public 
funding for research, development and deployment. Carbon neutrality, embraced by the 
chemicals sector, should not depend on public funding, but can definitely be supported by a 
clear regulatory framework.

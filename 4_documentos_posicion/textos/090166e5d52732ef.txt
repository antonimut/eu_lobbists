Taking a holistic circular approach: IKEA’s input to  the European Commission’ s request for feedback 
for the Inception Impact Assessment on the “Sustainable Products Policy” initiative [Inter IKEA and 
Ingka Group] 
 
 
As a global home-furnishing brand, IKEA has a big responsibility – and a big growth opportunity – to make a large-scale, 
positive impact on climate and resource use by adopting a circular business model. IKEA is  committed to become a 
circular and climate positive business by 2030.i  We are taking a total value chain approach, including shifting to use only 
renewable or recycled materials, and designing all products with circular capabilities. Our Circular Product Design Guide 
is our guide to navigating the pr oduct design process. In our own journey toward circularity, we have and continue to 
learn a lot and realize that many questions remain. Disrupting existing models is always difficult.  A successful circular 
economy requires many actors to play a role – from industry to experts to consumers to government. W e are still in  
“circularity’s early days,”  but things are moving fast; consumers are becoming more conscious of their consumption 
behaviours and setting new demands, companies are working to transform t heir business models, and here in the 
European Union, policymakers are developing bold, new legislation to enable the movement. The challenge now, is to 
recognize the interdependence of these efforts so the result can be a lasting difference for our planet.  
 
We welcome the opportunity to provide input on the roadmap for the  Sustainable Products Policy . We  also see the 
interdependencies with the other initiatives related to the Circular Economy Action Plan (including the “Empowering the 
consumers to the green transition” and “Substantiating Green Claims”), and the parallel movements within initiatives like 
Waste Shipment, Textile Strategy, revision of G eneral Product Safety Directive and the EU Chemical strategy. 
Recognizing that the European Commission is still assessing the best way forward, we offer the following  
recommendations to support your process: 
 
Ensure alignment: At the EU level, there is an unprecedented amount of legal file re -openings for improvements and 
new proposals under discussion aiming to impact the circular agenda . This is an exciting and historical opportunity to 
create a modern, comprehensive and clear regulatory framework. But, to truly enable meaningful change, we need 
to get it right from the beginning. We need to be efficie nt and smart in our  efforts, avoiding competing, complicated , 
and bureaucratic legislation that could undermine the final goal. We need a clear and consistent distribution of tasks, 
where topic areas such as consumer information, chemicals, etc. are regulated under the most relevant existing 
legislation, without duplication. With this, DG ENV, DG GROW, DG ENERGY and DG JUST can establish a more streamlined 
approach to make the necessary systemic changes.  
 
Harmonize (from the start) : We applaud EU action that provides a strong framework for Member States and avoids 
fragmentation within the internal market. To ensure we set the best foundation, we urge leading policymakers  to 
consider how requirements can best be implemented consistently, without differences across the EU countries.  
 
Build on what exists: We don’t need to start from scratch. We should use what we have, and improve upon it. We 
recommend using the existing legal framework to encompass new ideas before introducing new legislation : we need 
clear, modern rules adapted to the circular economy, not a mix of old and new rules.  
 
Recognize sectorial differences, when needed : Through the journey of assessing our thousands of products, we’ve 
discovered that the same circular product development “recipe” cannot be applied uniformly across all product types 
(see Appendix 1). These insights have triggered immediate actions and adjustments  in our own internal work aimed at 
delivering truly circular products. For this reason, we share the view of the European Commission that it is key to begin 
with circular design principles and that a policy tool that allow s us to incorporate product -relevant differences is 
needed.

Adopt a common language: For multinational organizations like IKEA, our ambition to become a fully circular business 
goes beyond national borders. This means we need a shared understanding when referring to “circular” terms. Today, 
that’s missing. At IKEA, we have created our own circular loops definitions (reuse, refurbishment, remanufacturing and 
recycling) to support our circular business transformation. We have also built an “IKEA dictionary” to define the criteria 
under renewable, recycled, and compostable materials. We are working on a definition of “ recyclability”. While work is 
ongoing, we have understood how difficult it is to define some concepts, such as product “lifespan” and “durability” (see 
Appendix 2). We therefore urge caution in introducing terms into legislation before they are  properly assessed 
and defined. 
 
Communicate what is needed and meaningful: Consumer information, when provided in an easy-to-understand way, 
is important for and wanted by customers to help them make informed decisions. Much of what can be communicated 
in the future will depend on the language and methodology decided today , and will help to accurately judge the 
feasibility of consumers’ information requirements. Some important areas of consideration: 
 Information must be readily available for consumers  at the time when  and where  they need it . To do so, 
legislation should account for different ways to inform consumers , including both physical and digital solutions.  
Consumer communication will continue to be accessed through a number of touch points, but will be needed 
throughout the different stages of circular product use. Reducing labelling information on products to what is most 
needed and relevant as well as promoting standardised international symbols over text will help reduce costs and 
optimise material use (such as the almost globally used ISO symbols for washing textiles). 
 To ensure information is reliable and can be compared  between products, information requirements must 
be well defined, measurable, and harmonised through the use of European and international standards.  
 From a c ustomer’s perspective, the information must be relevant , convenient to access,  and easy to 
understand. Information requirements need to be clear, concise, and add value for the customer to help them make 
a purchasing decision. 
 
Based on the above, we offer the following policy recommendations: 
 
1. The EU Eco-Design Directive has been one early approach to provide minimum eco-design principles on energy 
products in Europe. A potential extension of the Eco -Design Directive to non-energy related products - as 
proposed in the Sustainable Product Policy Initiative – is a smart approach to ensure that both horizontal 
requirements and product -related characteristics can be taken into consideration. The Eco-design framework 
would allow for common and specific measurements, test methods and standards that are technically applicable 
to the product in question.   
2. We strongly recommend that the European Commission  consolidate the discussion on all product -related 
consumer information requirements. Product sustainability and information on products go hand-in-hand; to 
communicate relevant information, we need to look at what is relevant for that product group and vice versa. It 
may help to move this discussion from the “Empowering the Consumer” initiative into the “Sustainable Product 
Policy” Initiative, as there will likely be the need for more product-specific evaluation.  
 
We are optimistic that policymakers will leverage this unique time in history to work holistically toward a commonsense 
regulatory framework for a circular transition, agree to a harmonized regulatory framework for the EU, and ultimately, 
lead the international community into a sustainable future. We stand ready to support European policymakers based on 
our experiences as a global company moving toward a circular business model.

Appendix 1: Product Circularity Assessments Lessons Learned 
At IKEA, we have defined Circular Design Principles and have assessed the IKEA product offer against these principles. 
Thus far, we have learnt the following: 
 There is no one-size-fits-all solution for circular product development. It changes based on product type and its 
intended circular potential (reuse, refurbishment, remanufacturing, recycli ng), as well as functionality and 
intended life length of the product 
 Material choice and separability are a big contributor to the product’s circular capability – many materials that 
are difficult to separate hinder possibilities for repair, refurbishment, and recycling 
 Standardized fittings, separable components (allowing for disassemble/reassembly) and spare parts availability 
are key contributors to enabling repair and refurbishment 
 Intended use, in terms of single or multiple uses, ne eds to be determined from the beginning of the design 
process to establish relevant circular design principle for the product.  
 
Appendix 2: Lifespan  
 
In traditional consumption patterns (or “linear” economy), “lifespan” is built on the expectation that a certain 
product exists for a certain amount of time, serving a specific function, under certain conditions of use and then 
is disposed. Sometimes, the concept of “lifespan” is associated to the one of the “durability” of a product, or 
“lifetime”. Among other factors, the concrete “lifespan” of a product changes considerably depending on the use. 
For example, a ceramic plate can last for centuries, but will be difficult to repair if broken into pieces. At the same 
time, the ‘lifespan’ of many products can be greatly expanded through proper care and repair solutions. In a 
circular economy, the product “lifespan” takes on a new meaning, which brings additional challenges in 
capturing its definition. In a circular world, what is produced today should be able to live many lives, by being 
repaired, refurbished, and remanufactured, etc. Trying to define or measuring “lifespan” in this context is an even 
more complex exercise, resulting in multiple possible outcomes, depending on many variables. In addition, 
through durability research conducted by IKEA, we discovered that defining quality and durability through a 
customer’s eyes is complex and includes many different factors depending on the customer’s personal point of 
view or economic background.

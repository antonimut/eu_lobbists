ReFuelEU Aviation 
How the EU can reduce CO2 emissions of aviation 
 Reducing aviation is the most important measure for reducing climate im-
pacts. 
 Only residual flights shall be refuelled with synthetic fuel. Th e required hy-
drogen should be produced 100% from additional renewable sources ; CO2 
should preferably come from direct air capture, and possibly other interim 
CO2 sources subject to sustainability safeguards. CO2 from steel production 
should be excluded.   
 Crop-based biofuels are not an option. The potential for advanced biofuels 
is very limited. 
 Costs for support instruments for synthetic fuels must be born by the users. 
 
Aviation is the fastest growing source of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the European Union (EU). 
The EU has failed to address these emissions effectively in the past. Yet  the transport sector needs to 
become climate neutral by 2050 at the latest in order to be compatible with the 1.5°C limit. Aviation emits 
CO2 emissions but also produces non-CO2 climate impacts through contrails and cirrus clouds, which for 
longer flights (distances exceeding 600 km) are 2-4 times higher than the mere CO2 emissions.  
Reducing aviation is the most important measure 
Reducing aviation  is the m ost important measure  for reducing the climate impacts of aviation. This 
can be achieved by reducing the need for travel (e.g. business trips), shifting intra-European flights as far 
as possible to rail, and managing remaining flights more efficiently.  
Residual flights that cannot be avoided or shifted need to be come climate neutral. Non-CO2 impacts of 
these flights can partially be reduced with flight route management . To reduce CO2 emissions,  direct 
electrification from renewable sources is generally the most efficient option for decarbonising 
transport devices. Renewable e lectricity is still a scarce resource, and a ny indirect use of electricity 
(power-to-X) implies significant energy losses. Yet in the case of aviation , direct electrification is very 
challenging due to the size and weight of batteries. Although the market is developing fast, battery elec-
tric airplanes will probably only be available for ultra -short haul flights in the coming decades. Most of 
these ultra-short haul flights should better be shifted to other modes, except for e.g. island destinations.

Only residual flights shall be refuelled 
For flights that cannot be avoided, shifted or directly electrified, synthetic fuels based on green hydro-
gen (e-fuels) currently look like the most promising option . Aircraft technology for the direct use of hy-
drogen is still in the very early stages of development.  
Synthetic fuels require both hydrogen and CO2. I t is essential that strict sustainability safeguards  are 
put in place which ensure that the production of synthetic fuels is CO2 neutral and that it has no negative 
impacts on e.g. water availability at production sites, biodiversity etc. The following principles should 
apply to the production of synthetic fuels: 
 Hydrogen produced through electrolysis requires consider able amounts of electricity. To guar-
antee a positive climate impact, this electricity should be 100% based on  renewables. Social 
and economic impacts of different hydrogen production methods should be regularly evaluated 
to define and readjust future pathways.  
 The production of hydrogen will create additional electricity demand, on top of what other sec-
tors will need for decarbonisation through direct electrification. There is a risk that fossil power 
production will be extended to cover parts of these electrici ty needs, both in the EU and in ex-
porting countries. Safeguards need to be put in place to guarantee that the renewable electric-
ity capacity used for hydrogen production is additional to the planned renewables roll-out. 
 The CO2 used to produce synthetic fuels should in the long-run preferably come from direct air 
capture, to guarantee a closed CO2 cycle .  CO2 from biogene sources might be an interim op-
tion but only if strict sustainability and human rights safeguards are in place. It needs to be care-
fully analysed whether industrial process emissions, that currently do not seem avoidable (e.g. 
cement production), could be a sustainable interim source of CO2.  CO2 from steel production 
sites shall not be used as this could create incentives for these industries to delay decarbonisa-
tion, although alternative production technologies are available 
 As the EU’s own renewables potential is limited, hydrogen is expected to also  be imported from 
regions with high potential for cheap renewables (e.g. North Africa, Gulf region). Safeguards 
need to be put in place to guarantee that these new energy relationships are not detrimental 
but beneficial for the local population. For instance, hydrogen production companies could be 
obliged to improve electricity access, provide renewable electricity also for local demand, guar-
antee access to grievance mechanisms etc. 
 Those fuels shall be prioritised which minimise non-CO2 climate impacts of aviation. 
Crop-based biofuels are not an option 
Crop-based biofuels in turn are not an option  for reducing aviation’s climate impacts , not even as an 
interim solution. Food and feed crops are a scarce resource and imply land use changes  (destruction of 
forests, grasslands, peatlands,wetlands, and other carbon -rich ecosystems) that result in substantial in-
creases in GHG emissions. This makes the climate impact of crop-based biofuels worse than that of fossil 
fuels.  
Biofuels from waste and residues (advanced biofuels) might be to a limited extent an interim option. 
Yet, the availability is also limited and they might be used more efficiently for other purposes. There  is 
also a risk that feedstocks  are classified as waste but have already other uses.  Advanced biofuels only 
achieve emissions savings of up to 80%, in contrast to 99% in the case of power -to-liquid fuels.

Costs for support instruments shall be born by the users 
To sum up, reduction and shifting of aviation are the first best option  for bringing aviation closer to cli-
mate neutrality, followed by synthetic fuels from renewables. Currently the level of synthetic fuels is still 
close to zero. The main obstacles are limited installed renewable electricity capacity, too small electro-
lysers with high costs, high costs for direct air capture, and a low fossil kerosene price. As a result, the 
production of synthetic fuels is currently 16-30 times as costly as the production of fossil kerosene. Exist-
ing EU and member states policies have not created sufficient drive yet. 
To overcome these obstacles, the EU should consider introducing new and additional instruments. This 
could, for instance, come in form of a synthetic fuel quota for fuel providers to stimulate demand, or/and 
a fixed market premium on top of market revenues to support the production. Such instruments need to 
be combined with a strict certification scheme to guarantee the sustainability. It is also key  that support 
instruments reflect the polluter pays principle: the costs shall be born by the users (airlines and air pas-
sengers) and not by the general public/tax payers. For instance, a support instrument could be financed 
through a synthetic fuel surcharge raised on EU-wide fossil kerosene use.

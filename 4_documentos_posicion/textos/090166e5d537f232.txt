Policy considerations: 
Overview 
Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) is more expensive than traditional jet fuel, with estimates ranging 
from 1.5X to 3X for commercially plausible options today.  Some options under development such as 
synthetic fuels using carbon capture are even more expensive This is mainly due to the small 
production runs and scarcity of the product. However, further production facilities are being 
constructed and actively planned.  
Several leading airlines are signing significant forward purchase agreements as part of their 
environmental strategy and protection against future regulatory risks.  These agreements help 
provide offtake certainty to new energy suppliers, allowing them to finance new plants (or retrofits to 
existing fossil fuel refineries). This helps bring down the cost of SAF in the mid- to long term.  
IATA modelling, supported by ATAG analysis through the WayPoint 2050 publication indicates that 
very large quantities of SAF will be necessary to achieve the industry’s long-term decarbonisation 
targets of a 50% reduction in net CO2 relative to 2005 levels.  ICAO analysis and agreements support 
this view.  Extensive feedstock assessments have been performed under appropriate sustainability 
constraints and indicate that adequate feedstock options exist to technically satisfy the long term 
SAF fuel requirement for the aviation industry out to the year 2050.  Other energy transition options 
such as electrification and hydrogen are being developed in parallel. To achieve large system 
penetration of SAF, policy must play the lead role over the next decades to enable the aviation 
energy transition and ensure that aviation can continue to deliver the significant social, economic 
and societal benefits.  Furthermore, the opportunity to decrease reliance on liquid fuel imports, 
improving energy security, becomes a significant global economic advantage.  
1.1. Policy options – IATA considerations: 
IATA recognises the EU objective of increasing SAF use from the presently low 0.05% of the total jet 
fuel demand to a more substantial figure over the next decade.  IATA also regognises that this will 
play an important role in curbing substantially emissions from the air transport sector. 
 
IATA appreciates the policy options being considered and provides some additional context and 
consideration to these:

gradually increase over time, to be supplied to airlines and/or a minimum share of SAF to be used by

airlines. The aim is to create a stable policy framework over a sufficient time horizon to provide 
investors with the necessary confidence to invest in the production of sustainable aviation fuels and 
for airlines to pursue an efficient fuels policy. Sub-options to be considered could include a 
mandatory volume-based minimum share of SAF uptake by a certain time horizon or a minimum level 
of reduction of GHG-intensity of jet fuels used. 
 
IATA considerations:  While the supply of SAF is immature, IATA believes a mandate is premature.  In 
the event that a mandate as a policy option would be considered there are some important design 
characteristics that can substantially reduce economic and environmental risks associated with such 
a policy.  The major risk that a mandate creates is a distortion in competition.  Globally the highly 
competitive aviation sector produced on average a profit of $6.12 US per ticket in 2019.  COVID-19 
means the industry will sustain significant economic losses in 2020 (estimated at $84 billion US) and 
the operating environment during the recovery (to at least 2025) appears fragile. 
 
It is essential to minimize the risk of distortions and not provide an unintended economic incentives  
to tanker jet fuel by establishing a clear system boundary to the policy.  This must be defined as intra-
EU.  Appropriate consideration and analysis need to be performed on the impact for non-EU carriers 
performing 5th freedom flights.   
 
The time frame and potential percentage of SAF (net of the emissions reduction factor (ERF)) need to 
be realistic and achievable without causing the SAF market potential including associated feedstock 
markets to become distorted.  IATA believes 2% by 2025 (assuming neat SAF at 100% ERF) is 
achievable with appropriate policy incentives.  For this to be achieved in a non-distortive fashion, all 
EU States must be aligned. Policies such as those being developed in Sweden and Norway need to 
be paused while a whole   EU approach is developed.  IATA will not support a patch-work approach to 
SAF introduction within the EU.   
 
IATA recommends a phase-in approach where a mandate framework is being considered.  A 
conservative but smooth introduction is favoured, rather than an over ambitious but disorderly 
introduction of SAF.  It is vital that the appropriate systems are in place including a functioning book 
and claim accounting framework – something IATA is participating in the development of.  IATA also 
has nearly 10 years of experience concerning collecting fuel emissions and reporting data, including 
linking with verifiers and States.  A clear system to demonstrate the chain of custody, fuel used and 
flow of emissions value is vital to ensure confidence that claims are accurate and made only once. 
 
An independent cost-benefit assessment needs to be performed by a multi-stakeholder group.  This 
can help inform the level and value of economic support attached to the introduction of larger 
volumes of SAF.    
 
IATA believes there should not be a ‘new’ set of sustainability criteria developed.  Rather, using work 
that has been agreed in ICAO is a sensible template.  All feedstock should be allowed if the criteria 
are met.   
 
Protection mechanisms need to be established to avoid the risk of markets (SAF supply / resellers or 
feedstock) becoming inelastic and inefficient which could allow inefficient and excessive pricing.

A significant package of policy support needs to be attached to any potential SAF quantitative 
obligation.  This will allow the market to truly develop beyond the floor of a quantitative obligation.  It 
is essential in the long run that SAF demand and supply fundamentals develop into a traditionally 
functioning marketplace.  
 
Transparency and reporting structure are overarching features that will define the integrity of such a 
policy.  Quantities, source and sustainability attributes must be transparent and available to airlines.  
A functioning book and claim system will be necessary to ensure integrity and confidence. 
Revision of the multiplier under the Renewable Energy Directive: Currently, 
with a 1.2 multiplier for aviation, Member States where biofuel is supplied to the aviation sector can 
count towards their national target 20% more than the SAF volumes provided. The approach could 
be further specified ensuring a harmonised implementation and the multiplier could be increased. 
 
IATA considerations:  IATA modelling indicates that a multiplier of at least 2x is required to level the 
playing field for a producer to assess whether to direct his production towards the ground transport 
sector or aviation.  Today, most HEFA renewable fuel production facilities are optomised to direct 
maximum output towards renewable diesel, rather than SAF due to economics.  This is a missed 
opportunity to leverage existing production infrastructure and grow SAF production over the short to 
medium term, while the total market expands.  IATA would be pleased to share modelling on the SAF 
multiplier with the EC. 
 
A central auctioning mechanism: SAF producers would be invited by a central auctioning 
authority to bid at the lowest price to supply a certain volume of SAF to the aviation market over a 
certain period. Such schemes have been used in the field of renewable electricity. 
 
IATA considerations:  Increasing the functionality of a SAF market is welcome.  Such a marketplace 
should be available to all airlines and all suppliers to facilitate the most competitive SAF supply and 
price. 
 
Funding mechanism: The EU would channel funds through one or more EU financial 
instruments with the aim of encouraging the deployment of SAF production facilities in the EU and of 
accompanying the gradual uptake of SAF by the aviation market at competitive prices by helping to 
bridge the cost gap and upscale production. 
 
IATA considerations:  Funding support mechanisms will be important to grow and genuinely mature 
the supply of SAF including the financing and development of numerous new SAF supply facilities in 
Europe.  
 
Prioritisation: As feedstock used to produce sustainable transport fuels are a scarce resource, 
sustainable transport fuels should be directed to transport modes where they are the most needed, 
i.e. transport modes with limited or no alternatives to decarbonise in line with the EU’s climate

IATA considerations:  Feedstock prioritisation must be given to “hard to abate” sectors.  A solution 
IATA favours is to adjust the multiplier allowing a higher value for the same feedstock to be directed 
towards aviation rather than ground transport.  This naturally incentivizes the ground transport 
sector to seek the superior alternatives (such as batteries and electrification) they have, while 
directing the finite feedstock responses to the sectors that require it the most. 
 
Voluntary agreements: setting up of a collaborative platform to facilitate purchase 
agreements between SAF producers and airlines. It could also issue guidance on how to conclude 
efficiently purchase agreements. 
 
IATA considerations:  Any voluntary platform or support mechanism to facilitate the efficient 
transition of SAF is welcome.  Guidance and best practice documents can help improve logistics and 
distribution coordination.  IATA is happy to share material and experience to support the EC in 
establishing such guidance material. 
 
Technical facilitation and support initiatives: a dedicated facilitation initiative for SAF 
approval could be put in place in the EU. The objective would be to accompany SAF producers along 
the approval process, by providing the necessary technical support. The idea of an EU coordination 
platform could also be envisaged to bring closer together aviation stakeholders, SAF producers and 
regulators with a view to communicate and engage towards the common objective of developing the 
SAF market. 
 
IATA considerations:  Support activities that can make SAF availability more homogenous and 
streamlined, with the overarching objective of ensuring the most efficient market pricing can exists is 
a positive initiative and IATA would willingly lend input and expertise into this process. 
 
Monitoring: a dedicated data stream could be put in place to better monitor the production and 
use of SAF in the EU. Key Performance Indicators could be designed to monitor the effects of SAF 
policies. 
 
IATA considerations:  Transparent and verified monitoring of SAF use is vital.  This includes a 
transparent monitoring, reporting and verification system and a functional and secure data 
repository for airlines to record and report SAF use.  IATA is willing to share experience gained from 
developing the fuel reporting and emissions data base which has been used by the majority of 
airlines over recent years.

ACI EUROPE response to the EC public consultation on ReFuel EU Aviation 
 
ACI EUROPE welcomes the opportunity to provide input into the EU ReFuel EU Aviation 
initiative. Our members have been engaged in climate action for many years, in particular 
through Airport Carbon Accreditatio n, the only global carbon management standard for 
airports, launched in 2009. Building on its success, in June 2019, European airport operators 
committed to reach Net Zero carbon emissions for operations under their control at the latest 
by 2050, while calling on the whole air transport system to do the same.  
ACI EUROPE fully supports the ReFuel EU Aviation initiative. Without substantial use of 
Sustainable Aviation Fuels (SAF), it will not be possible for the air transport system to 
decarbonise in line with EU climate goals. To achieve a significant uptake of SAF, a dedicated, 
supporting policy instrument is required. 
This pape r provides some complementary information to some of our questionnaire 
responses: 
- Question 3:  
o Synthetic fuels have the potential to be almost completely em issions-free (if 
produced with renewable electricity and direct air capture), do not have the 
same limitations in terms of fee dstock availability and do not entail the same 
sustainability risks as biofuels.  However, there are still technological and 
commercial challenges to be addressed to enable their deployment at a large 
scale, in particular a very high production cost. 
o Therefore, advanced biofuels complying with strict sustainability criteria should 
not be discarded. They will be important to support aviation decarbonisation in 
the shorter-term. 
o We therefore believe that a n EU SAF policy framework should support both 
advanced biofuels and synthetic fuels , while also encouraging R&D in new 
types of feedstock and conversion processes. 
 
- Question 5:  
o SAF are a key lever for the decarbonisation of aviation in the short- to medium-
term and thus the sector’s contribution to the EU climate goals for 2030. Eight 
SAF pathways have already reached advanced technological readiness levels 
and are ASTM-certified. Drop-in SAF produced based on these pathways and 
blended with conventional kerosene can be certified as Jet-A1. As such, it has 
the advantage of not requiring any modifications to existing aircraft systems or 
fuel supply infrastructure on the ground. With adequate policy support, a 
broader commercialisation and uptake of SAF can be achieved. In comparison,

- Question 6:  
o The current EU regulatory  framework for SAF is insufficient for the following 
reasons: 
▪ Fuel producers have been mainly directing their alternative fuels 
production to the road transport, where less stringent and costly 
certification requirements apply than in aviation. Furthermore, demand 
from the road sector has been higher than from aviation, in particular 
due to a mandatory target imposed by the Renewable Energy Directive 
(RED). Aviation has been lacking similar policy drivers. 
▪ The RED multiplier for SAF has only been introduced in the RED II from 
2018, so it is yet too early to assess its impacts. In general, many 
experts in the aviation sector concur that the magnitude of the multiplier 
(1.2) is too low to incentivse SAF production, all the more as the same 
multiplier also applies to the shipping sector. In addition, aviation is not 
part of the 14% renewable energy target established for transport in the 
RED II, it’s at the discretion of Member States to opt it in. 
▪ Large-scale inve stments in energy production often have a long 
payback time of around 15 to 20 years. To provide the necessary 
certainty to investors, a stable, long -term policy framework is needed. 
Such a framework is currently not available – the RED II (which 
addresses aviation to a limited extent) only covers the timeframe until 
2030, while some related national arrangements have even shorter 
timelines (e.g. in the Netherlands only until 2025 there is certainty of 
opt-in for aviation fuels). 
 
- Question 7:  
o A regulatory framework for SAF at EU level seems preferrable for the following 
reasons: 
▪ The EU has ambitious climate objectives and the aviation sector needs 
to be enabled to decarbonise accordingly.  
▪ Compared to measures at Member State level, a ction at EU level can 
help ensure a level playing field for the air transport stakeholders 
operating in the EU. 
▪ Action at EU level can help make the most out of the potentially varying 
availability of feedstock across Member States. If action is taken at 
national level only, some Member States might have more difficulties to 
support SAF than others because of limited feedstock availability. 
o That being said, it is also important to ensure that an EU regulatory framework 
does not introduce any distortion of competition for the European air transport 
sector compared to non -European aviation. This is all the more important as 
such distortion of competition can also have negative environmental impa cts, 
e.g. through fuel tankering in countries with less stringent environmental 
requirements and lower fuel prices and/or longer routes being flown to enable 
refuelling in such countries.

- Question 12:  
o In the short to medium term, the most promising measures to be further 
explored are an EU-wide SAF blending mandate, funding in support of SAF 
production deployment and a central SAF auctioning mechanism, because 
they can all be used for already approved SAF pathways. When considering a 
SAF mandate, it is critical to ensure that only SAF meeting th e highest 
sustainability criteria are eligible to meet it. Likewise, it is important to consider 
all possible side -effects of a blending mandate , in particular distortion of 
competition for European air transport stakeholders and fuel tankering. 
o “European trading system for fuel carbon credits across transport modes”: we 
suggest exploring an accounting and trading system (book & claim) for SAF 
only first. It would be a mechanism similar to the one in place for green 
electricity, e.g. through Renewable Energy Certificates. Such a system would 
be based on the trade of SAF certificates, thus dissociating the purchase of 
SAF from the  physical consumption of SAF. Certificates would be issued for 
uplifted SAF, but any airline, regardless of whether it effectively tanks SAF or 
not, would be able to buy them  and thus claim the associated emission 
reductions. Such a system could help best stimulate demand, use the available 
feedstock and production capacities, and ultimately reduce emissions. For 
instance, it will be preferable to supply SAF at airports located in the vicinity of 
production plants, rather than aiming to distribute it to all airports in the EU, 
thus generating additional emissions. Such a system will also allow airlines to 
purchase and claim varying amounts of SAF depending on their capacities and 
ambition. 
o “Support and facilitate SAF approval processes for fuel producers”: it would be 
important to ensure easier and faster ASTM certification of synthetic SAF 
pathways that are promising in terms of cost a nd energy efficiency. ASTM 
certification is a major barrier to commercialisation of alternative SAF pathways 
and it should be a top EU policy priority to coordinate and ensure fast 
certification. 
o Other possible measures not listed in the questionnaire include: 
▪ Reconsidering EU State Aid guidelines, so fully or partly State -owned 
companies have the flexibility to proceed with investments and financial 
incentives in support of SAF . This type of support can currently be 
challenging due to the complexity of the State Aid provisions.  
▪ Introduce contracts-for-difference: if the production costs for SAF are 
higher than the price users are willing to pay, this mechanism would 
provide a public subsidy to cover the differential and thus create 
certainty for fuel producers that they will be able to recover their costs. 
This type of mechanism has been used in the past to support renewable 
electricity generation.

- Question 15:  
o The possibility of defining a sub -target for synthetic fuels should be explored . 
Given the high costs of synthetic fuels , dedicated support measures are 
required so their production can be scaled up. 
o However, if a such a sub -target is set, it will also be necessary to ensure that 
this does not disincentivize R&D in new SAF feedstock and pathways. 
 
- Question 16:  
o It needs to be reassessed whether the sustainability criteria and types of 
feedstock defined in the RED II are fit -for-purpose. When doing so , an 
integrated approach across different sectors should be adopted , so as to 
ensure that if a feedstock is used for SAF, this does not lead to an increased 
demand for less sustainable raw material from other sectors. 
 
- Question 17:  
o The suggestion that “modulation of airport charges” could serve as an 
instrument to create a fund for SAF reveals a misunderstanding of what an 
“airport charge” is, and what purpose “modulations” serve. 
▪ An airport charge is a price set for a service provided by a business to 
a purchaser. It is no different than the prices charged by airlines to 
passengers, ground -handling companies to airlines, or management 
consultants to clients. Airports are commercial businesses in their own 
right. 
▪ It is therefore arbitrary to choose the a irport as the economic entity 
which should be mandated to modulate the prices for the services it 
provides, simply because the act of re -fuelling happens at the airport 
platform. 
▪ It is true that a very small share of European airports have their airport 
charges approved in some way by an independent regulatory authority 
(6 – 10% of airports in Europe are economically regulated, depending 
on the definition of regulation).  It is likely that the misunderstanding 
arrives from this fact. Broadening economic regu lation to include 
additional policy objectives, such as promoting a specific type of fuel 
used by the customers of the airport, is imprudent as it will harm the 
regulated entity (the airport), its customers and the framework of the 
regulation itself. 
▪ Finally, it is worth noting that a modulation to encourage SAF use is 
feasible, but can only be developed and implemented by the airport 
operator as part of its corporate strategy. However, modulation of 
airport charges cannot as such be used to create a fund to support SAF, 
because modulations are by definition revenue -neutral for the airport. 
For more information, please see ACI EUROPE information paper on 
the use of modulations of airport charges for environmental reasons. 
o The ETS Innovation Fund is a very relevant funding source for SAF, in 
particular in the light of a possible reform of the ETS for aviation through the 
revision of free allocation of emissions allowances. It offers the opportunity to 
ring-fence revenues from the auctioning of allowances and then reinvest them 
to support the decarbonisation of aviation.

- Question 19:  
o Drop-in SAF blended with convention al kerosene is certified as Jet -A1. This 
means that the existing fuel supply infrastructure and logistics at airports can 
be used without any modifications to handle such blends. 
o In some cases, fuel suppliers might choose to store blended SAF separately 
from conventional kerosene. In such instances, airport operators will need to 
support the set-up of relevant facilities, e.g. by providing land. 
o If SAF production is taking place in the vicinity of the airport or directly on the 
airport site, airport operators might also have to facilitate the connection of the 
production site to the fuel farm at the airport. 
o EU policy action should recognise the above and support airport operators  in 
initiatives taken to facilitate SAF supply. 
o For the longer term, it is critical to bear in mind that non -drop-in SAF, such as 
hydrogen or electricity, will require significant changes to airport infrastructure. 
These will need to be supported by an appropriate policy framework as well.  
 
In conclusion, we would like to stress the importance of the ReFuel EU Aviation initiative 
specifically in the context of COVID -19. Given the dramatic impact s of the crisis, the air 
transport sector will not be able to decarbonise in line with EU climate goals without strong 
policy support. The latter should include both a clear and stable regulatory framework but also 
funding mechanisms, including through EU recovery instruments, such as the EU Reco very 
and Resilience Facility and InvestEU.

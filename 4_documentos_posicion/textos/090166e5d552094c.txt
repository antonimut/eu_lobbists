 
 
Policy Hub: Contribution “Sustainable Products Initiative” 
02 November 2020  
 
The Policy Hub welcomes the opportunity to comment on the European Commission’s Inception 
Impact Assessment "Sustainable Products Initiative". The transition towards a circular economy in 
which materials can be used and reused at their highest potential, is one of the priorities of the apparel 
and footwear industry. For many leading companies, it is already a key pillar of their sustainability 
strategy. 
 
First and foremost, given the various initiatives currently being launched by the European Commission 
which will be interlinked and will be impacting the apparel and footwear industry at various levels, we 
urge the European Commission’s services to coordinate, align and harmonise efforts across initiatives 
targeting sustainable products launched in 2020: 
o At a product level with DG ENV’s initiatives on Green Claims and DG JUST’s initiative on 
Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition.  
o At brand /organisational level with DG ENV’s initiatives on Green Claims, DG JUST’s 
initiative on Sustainable Corporate Governance and DG FISMA’s r eview of the Non -
Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD). 
 
We share with the European Commission the belief that an EU policy framework is needed to promote 
sustainable products. We recommend the following for this European Policy framework: 
 
1. Identifying appropriate policy tools for the apparel and footwear sector  
 
The Policy Hub shares the Commission ’s belief that products on the EU market should become 
increasingly sustainable. The Policy Hub recommends carefully assessing and identifying the impacts 
of any proposed existing or future policy instrument. Given the complexity and specificities of 
apparel and footwear value chains we strongly believe that a sector specific approach is needed for 
the sector. In light of this , we recommend an impact assessment of the Eco -design Directive on the 
apparel and footwear sector.  
 
We support the European Commissi on’s initiative to provide clear guidance on substantiating green 
claims and have commented on the Commission’s inception impact assessment (available here). We 
believe that this is an important focus area to enable the market uptake of more sustainable products 
through customer behaviour. However, certain existing tools are not fit for purpose and should be re-
evaluated. The EU Ecolabel , for example , has not been recognised by the apparel and footwear 
industry as a reference label: only very few products are certified with EU Ecolabel. It has proven to 
be misaligned with existing standards, too complex and burdensome to implement and not recognised 
by consumers, especially since consumers care for very different causes (from the sustainability of the 
material, to product processes with less water, less chemicals or animal welfare). We strongly 
encourage the EU Commission to consider digital labelling o ver traditional physical labels as digital 
communication vehicles enable companies to provide more detailed information, that can easily be 
translated, corrected and adapted if needed. 
 
2. Clearly defining the attributes of circular and sustainable products and developing a holistic 
approach for our industry to enable and support innovation 
As a baseline, a harmonised definition of a circular and sustainable product for apparel and footwear 
is needed. The Policy Hub is willing to lead the discussion in detailing this proposal, on a harmonised 
definition of a circular product in the textile, apparel and footwear industry  considering its purpose 
and use . The Policy Hub recommends taking a holistic approa ch recognising and support ing the

different contributions to minimising waste  as each brings different but clearly tangible benefits . 
These include:   
• Designing for more sustainable production  (e.g. through minimising waste & making use of 
by-products such as material scraps), 
• Designing for duration of service and increased use, having a beneficial impact on the ability 
to rental, resell-sale, swap, etc.  
• Designing for repairability, 
• Designing for cyclability (e.g. recycling and compostability), 
• Design for holistic impact (e.g. materials with lower environmental impact).1  
 
It is key that an approach on design provides companies with the agility to innovate and evolve their 
processes in order to optimise the design of their products and enable the shift towards circularity. 
Therefore, a European  Product P olicy Initiative should be flexible enough to adapt itself to future 
innovations, which may influence the definition of circular and sustainable products in coming years . 
Rather than being prescriptive on detailed implementation, the EU could consider setting concrete 
objectives that could apply to the vast variety of products, materials or production processe s. This 
approach could potentially fit better with the ambition to build an EU PEF or equivalent schemes (such 
as the Higg Index) to assess a product’s environmental footprint holistically. 
Waste reduction hierarchy, design waste out , promoting reuse and diverse access models , overall 
environmental impact reduction and generally increasing sustainability features in apparel and 
footwear products should be the key considerations.  
 
Finally, while some companies have already committed to sustainability targets of their own (e.g. on 
the usage of recycled content), the industry is open to further discuss if and how such targets should 
be applied. Meeting these targets can only be achieved when the necessary and adequate collection, 
sorting recycling, infrastructure and technologies are in place (see section 3).  
 
3. Incentivising the transition to more sustainable products through the development of 
appropriate infrastructures and the rewarding of better performing products through EU 
funding.  
A fundamental priority of the transition to a circular economy with more sustainable textile products 
is to develop appropriate collection, sorting and recycling infrastructures. This can only be achieved 
by stimulating the market  through increased funding of innovation in developing scalable solutions 
and provide dedicated fund ing to scale innovations that were already proven at pilot scale for 
recycled/ secondary raw materials .  Additionally, investing in and scaling of automated sorting and 
fiber recognition technologies  to ensure the availability of a feedstock at scale as a viable input for 
recycled materials is needed. A key enabler in in this respect is the free movement of post -consumer 
textile waste and secondary raw materials across the EU to allow for an optimal efficiency of secondary 
raw material markets. The framework shall also support educational partnerships  and adaptable, 
company-specific training modules for designers.  
 
Finally, the Policy Hub recommends setting the overall right incentives for companies to produce more 
sustainable products. This can be achieved by rewarding more sustainable products on the European 
Market through various tools. One example of such incentives would the implementation of a VAT 
reduction for textiles demonstrating circular at tributes, such as recycled materials or increased 
duration of service. This should be discussed and harmonised at Member States level in order to 
guarantee the well-functioning of the European single market and would provide a positive lever to 
encourage the industry towards this transition.

4. Enabling (digital) infrastructures to share reliable and trustworthy information about 
products.  
The Policy Hub calls for public-private partnerships to develop the necessary infrastructure for digital 
solutions that will enable product traceability  and thus recycling related operations (collection, 
sorting, recycling).  Given the importance of sharing  reliable and trustworthy information about 
products to serve a Business to Business purpose, we would strongly recommend exploring t he 
digitalisation of evidence. This will ensure that information remains available throughout the entire 
life cycle of the product and gives the needed flexibility to the industry to update information as new 
policy requirements are developed.  
 
Jointly with the industry, the content and methodo logy behind different solutions, such as digital 
passports, watermarks and tagging should be explored. A joint effort of the industry and government 
bodies is needed to develop an enabling framework in this area through new funds, partnerships, and 
pilot projects.  
 
Hence, the Policy Hub recommends building on existing projects to advance traceability and digital 
product passport, such as the “Enhancing Traceability and Transparency for Sustainable Value Chains 
in Garment and Footwear” project of the UNECE. Digital passports for the apparel and footwear sector 
can be enablers for a circula r economy and waste reduction through sharing relevant information 
between actors in the value chain (e.g. producers, retailers, consumers, sorters, recyclers). While 
pilots are still needed to explore the impact of digital passports, the Policy Hub is willing to engage in 
these upcoming conversations.

The EU Sustainable Products Initiative (SPI) 
 
EDRA’s key messages on this issue are the following: 
  
1. General remarks 
 
• Firstly, we would like to stress that we thoroughly support this initiative as we feel it is time for 
the EU to upgrade rules on product sustainability for the benefits of all. 
 
• Consistency with existing and future EU legislation: EU initiatives should be consistent to ensure 
that there are neither overlaps nor contradicting measures among current EU proposals. This is 
particularly important as regards the New Consumer Agenda or the Sustainable Chemicals 
Strategy for instance.  To av oid confusion, measures should build upon current legislation 
depending on their scope and intended actions: product sustainability (Eco -design Directive), 
chemicals legislation (REACH legislation), safety (Products Safety Directive). This would ensure 
appropriate implementation by economic operators.  
 
• Defining “sustainable”:  it is important to define clearly what ‘sustainable’ means, in a way that 
it is easy to understand and to measure. This should be specific not only to the different product 
categories, but actually tailored to the different product families.   We should also bear in mind 
that the impact on sustainability of a product depends very much on the stage of its lifecycle 
(manufacturing, use or end -of-life). For example, cement is consuming a lot energy and 
resources at the manufacturing stage. 
 
• Sustainability and life cycle: the work of the EU on sustainability should  cover the full life cycle 
of the product, including raw materials extraction, production impact (including energy & water 
use), customer use and disposal. In this respect, the SPI should also cover end-of-life  (ex: 
designing for end of life, customer reuse, re -manufacturing). On designing to reduce waste, it 
should be noted that many construction materials & products are carbon intensive and waste 
at the installation phase can be a significant issue. A good example of this is plasterboard where 
cutting to size results in waste. 
 
• What about consumers?  The Sustainable Product Initiative aims are all directed at 
producers.  We think EU effort should also go towards changing the habits of consumers (they  
are the polluter in many cases) along the lines of the Japanese legislation on consumer waste & 
Japan's home appliance recycling laws. In this respect, for customers, guidance should be made

2. Raw material sourcing and manufacturing 
• The scope of m easures on production processes should be more precise  (e.g. facilitate recycled 
content or remanufacturing and to track the use of hazardous substances in such processes): we 
welcome that the Roadmap on the Sustainable Products Initiative references the environmental 
impacts and working conditions of raw material sourcing and manufacturing . However, we 
request that. These measures cover energy and water usage to reduce carbon emissions (e.g. 
requirements for renewable energy, on -site water recycling / reuse ). Besides, w aste 
management at factory level is also key, encouraging cross-industry collaboration to make use 
of manufacturing waste or by-products and creating secondary markets for materials 
  
  
3. Durability 
• Extension of the scope of the Eco-Design Directive: the Commission intends to extend the scope 
of the Eco-design Directive to non-energy related products (e.g. textiles, construction products). 
Questions: does the Commission intend to extend the scope to construction in the sense of 
construction products covered by the Construction Products Regulation or to any products related to 
the building? 
What about overlaps with the Construction Products Regulation? 
 
4. Reparability 
 
• Flexibility is needed : c onsidering the amount and large variety of products sold  and to avoid 
unintended negative environmental and economic effects, requirements regulating the 
availability and delivery of spare parts must be flexible. 
 
• Spare parts are not a panacea. There are other options out there to make repairs: there are other 
mechanisms/processes that allow customers to fix their products. They should also be taken 
into account. There are many products which if damaged, may not require spare parts but still 
require repair (e.g. flooring, furniture and home textiles). Customer r epair kits should be more 
available for straightforward repairs. For instance, wax repair kits exist for customers to fix 
damaged wooden flooring without needing to replace the whole plank. Repair is essential to 
keeping products in use and circulation for longer. If retailers and manufacturers cannot provide 
repair kits for all products, they should help customers to connect to local businesses that can 
provide this service. Also, repair kits offset production of spare parts which makes them more 
environmentally-friendly. Also, beyond repair kits , many repairs do not require either spare 
 
 
parts or a specific kit (e.g. tapes, repair putty, glues) that can be used to repair a wide range of 
products. 
• Timeframe for delivery of spare parts : the timeframe for delivery of spare parts in the current 
Eco-design Directive (15 Working days) seems appropriate.  
 
5. Recyclability 
• Incorporation of recycled content : t he notion of incorporation of recycled content  should be 
better defined as it can refer to different materials. The markets of recycled are not equally 
developed. For instance, metals are more developed than wood products. Also, in order for the 
definition of recycled content to be applicable and encourage uptake of recycled content, the 
waste source (post industrial, post consumer..) should be defined as well. Using post consumer 
waste will be more impactful than post industrial waste.  Finally, we agree this should be at a 
material level and should reflect existing best practice as the minimum requirements, to ensure 
no industry reverts on their current position.  
  
6. Information to consumers 
• A scoring system for reparability : A separate label with the reparability score of the product is 
not an acceptable option because 1) it will be burdensome to affix it on products on display 
(similar to the energy efficiency label), 2) certain products that are not easily reparable will be 
discriminated against. Using the scoring system in existing rules (e.g. Energy label, eco -label) is 
acceptable. For instance, having the reparability score on the energy label. In this case, the 
reparability score will not be as prominent as the energy efficiency label and the information on 
reparability will not appear as distinctively to the customers. This could matter in the case of 
product with a low reparability score. Having this information on the energy label has also the 
advantage of centralizing all information on the sus tainability of the product in one location.  
However, unlike energy efficiency repairability scoring / labelling is likely to be subjective. More 
precisions are needed on how the EU intends to make it objective. Some products unsuitable 
for customer repair, but instead needs specialist repair - labelling should take account of this.   
• Digital passport/Provision of sustainability information through digital means : EDRA is part of 
an alliance of stakeholders that aims at promoting digital labelling at EU level (“Digital Consumer 
Information Alliance”). Concretely speaking, it means allowing mandatory consumer 
information to be provided digitally.  This digital passport could be an opportunity to achieve 
that. However, our initiative goes beyond mere sustain ability information.  Maybe the 
Commission should think about making this dig ital passport more comprehensive and include 
other types of consumer information in it? The key element here is to define what kind of 
information should or can be mentioned within the digital passport.

• Challenges around green claims : there are discussions ongoing on the use of PEF or other 
lifecycle analysis tools. Although we agree that green claims should be based on scientific facts,

The European DIY Retail Association (EDRA) is the voice for the home improvement industry in 
Europe. It was founded in 2002 by the French (FMB) and German (BHB) DIY retail associations. 
Today EDRA represents 140 home improvement companies in Europe either through its country 
associations or as direct members. These members include the top 3 European players by net 
turnover: ADEO, Kingfisher & OBI as well as 27 of the top 30 players in Europe.

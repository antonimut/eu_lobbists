 
16 November 2020 
Feedback on the Inception Impact Assessment on the Sustainable Products Initiative 
 
Introduction 
For 25 years, eBay has enabled millions of consumers and businesses to give a second life to goods. 
Whether through individuals selling items online that they no longer need, or by facilitating online 
access to spare parts for repair, eBay can be considered a key player in the circular economy.  
As we collectively take conscience of the climate challeng e ahead, consumers move towards more 
sustainable online shopping options. According to eBay data, the number of search queries for 
"sustainable" and "environmentally friendly" offers has increased by almost 100 percent from 2018 
to 2019.  
During the econom ic recovery from Covid -19, European SMEs will have a great interest and 
opportunity in responding to the ever-growing demand for second-hand, refurbished, repurposed, 
ecodesigned or repairable goods. The ability for them to count on a stable, protective and clear legal 
framework in this context will be crucial.  
We welcome the opportunity to comment on the Commission’s planned Sustainable Products 
Initiative which we believe may have an important impact on the way SMEs use online commerce to 
distribute more sustainable products in the EU market. With the comments below, we hope to provide 
constructive views on some of the policy options that the Commission intends to explore as per this 
Inception Impact Assessment. 
 
On information requirements, such as mandatory sustainability labelling and/or disclosure 
of information to market actors along value chains in the form of a digital product passport 
As consumers increasingly expect to be comprehensively informed on a product’s environmental 
qualities, we believe that the creation of EU rules can support greater clarity of that information.  
About 98% of the European businesses  using eBay engage in export ; they sell to an average of 21 
markets every year. To support their competitiveness on the global market, these SMEs must be able 
to rely on a European harmonized yet flexible standard for providing consumers with information  
on eco-design characteristics  such as  product durability, repairability  and energy consumption.  
Currently, certain Member States  have been developing national rules regarding sustainability 
labelling, which have created additional barriers to the Single Market  and compliance risks for SMEs 
offering such products for sale , in a particularly challenging post-crisis period. A recent example can 
be found in the repairability score adopted by France to be applied as of 1 January 2021. We support 
the Commission’s initiative to harmonize these rules at European level and believe that a 
moratorium should be imposed on national rules in the meantime in order to avoid further 
fragmentation.  
We recommend that the Commission follows two other important principles when designing 
mandatory labelling rules – for the benefit of both consumers and the SMEs that serve them:

- Compactness. Prescription related to new environmental product information should be 
flexible, taking into consideration technical and design challenges with respect to small screen 
sizes.  
- Flexibility. In connection with the above, new requirements should remain adaptable to the 
great diversity of user experiences existing both in the online and the offline world, while 
ensuring clarity and readability for consumers. For instance, we  believe that prescriptions as 
to the size, color and placement of such information should remain limited, in order to enable 
businesses to deliver the information in a  way most adapted to each particular user 
experience. 
Finally, a digital product passport could support SMEs and the sales channels they use (incl uding 
marketplaces like eBay) to accurately and efficiently relay the information to the end -consumer. It 
would also allow competent authorities to control product compliance. At the same time, we would 
caution the Commission against creating new liabilities for members of the value chain that were not 
initially targeted by this instrument. At least, coupling it with adequate legal protection seems 
paramount to ensure trust both in SMEs when deciding to sell online and in consumers who  look for 
their products. We provide more detailed comments on the challenges presented by complex retail 
chains in the following part of this document. 
 
On making producers responsibl e for providing more circular products and intervening 
before products can become waste 
When the Eco -Design Directive was first adopted in 2009, cross -border B2C e -commerce had not 
reached the scale it has today. Within the last decade, it  has become more common for small 
businesses to reach customers globally, thanks to technology and online marketplaces. The Internet 
has lowered barriers to entry that blocked SMEs from addressing customers remotely and has 
increased consumer choice as well as international growth opportunities for companies of all sizes.  
As a result, when considering revising  the Eco -Design Directive, the Commission should take into 
account the new profiles of businesses that might be covered by the traditional definitions of 
“manufacturer”, “importer” or “producer”. In fact, small and even micro-businesses might be covered 
by one or more of these defin itions, to the extent they offer for sale a product made outside of the 
EU, visible online to EU consumers , for which they still depend on  the information that the “true” 
manufacturer will, or not, provide to them.  
In this context, we believe that new eco-design and product passport requirements should remain 
clearly aimed at the player in control of these elements and that other members of the retail chain 
should be protected against a manufacturer’s failure to comply with them. 
In the event of a dispute, a seller should therefore benefit from a legal protection against a 
manufacturer (or supplier / distributor) having provided inaccurate or incomplete product 
information. As many online resellers are SMEs with little capacity to e ngage in legal conflicts with 
manufacturers, the Commission  should support SMEs against harm from inaccurate or incomplete 
product information, for example by imposing participation of manufacturers in accessible dispute 
resolution channels such as specialized B2B mediators.  
 
This protection should be enforced at every stage of the contractual chain (manufacturer, distributor, 
seller), in particular if additional EU rules, such as the period of legal guarantee, are to be connected 
with sustainable characteristics of the product, such as its expected lifespan. 
In a similar fashion, pure third -party marketplaces, like eBay, must not be held directly liable for the 
absence or inaccuracy of such informa tion. Rather, they can support with appropriate take- down 
measures when authorities identify infringing products on their platforms, as well as with preventative 
education campaigns and other forms of public-private collaborations. 
In 2019, the EU adopted a new Market Surveillance Regulation (2019/1020). Under Article 4 of the 
Regulation and from the date of its entry into force in July 2021, products placed on the EU market 
will have to be connected to an economic operator established in the Union, responsible for ensuring 
the conformity of the product as per applicable EU legislation. The Commission could explore relying 
on the same economic operator as appointed under this Regulation, to ensure compliance with eco -
design requirements. 
 
On requirements to address social aspects throughout the product lifecycle as part of 
sustainability principles and requirements, where appropriate and feasible 
There is a need for clarification of  what is inferred by the te rm “social aspects” and how it might 
concretely translate into practical requirements. 
 
On measures to ban the destruction of unsold durable goods 
Although eBay does not directly provide storage services to its third-party sellers, we suggest adopting 
a careful approach with regards to such measures. In fact, many small businesses in Europe t rading 
online rely on the services of third -party logistics and warehousing providers to meet their delivery 
needs. It seems  important that these SMEs retain independence in the management of their stock, 
even when delegating some operational functions to external providers. When applied to 
warehousing providers, measures banning the destruction of unsold durable goods may translate into 
a challenging choice of options being offered to those SMEs that use them. For example, SMEs may 
be forced by the provider to either have their unsold stock returned to them in exchange for a high 
return shipping cost or  given away to charity on  their behalf. Adequate safeguards to protect the 
independence of SMEs should therefore be considered in this area. 
 
Conclusion 
Despite its recent revision in 2018, the  Waste Management Framework and connected  Directives, 
which impose  significant duties related to “ extended producer responsibility ” remain very 
fragmented. P roducer Responsibility Schemes operat e on a national basis, creating registration, 
reporting and payment obligations on producers (in the extended meaning of the term) varying in 
each of the Member States where the product may get placed. Combined with the new dynamics of 
cross-border online commerce , this creates a challenging situation where SMEs are faced with 
fragmented and burdensome obligations for each EU Member State where they may sell even just  
one product. We therefore encourage the Commission to have a maximum harmonization approach 
in the context of this new initiative  which will ensure better compliance of smaller players with the 
new rules, while allowing them to seize the growth potential of the new digital and green economy.

Hydro welcome the Sustainable Product Policy Initiative (SPPI) and strongly supports initiatives aiming at driving 
demand towards low-carbon and circular products. We support establishing sustainability principles and common 
criteria, as well as improve and make available reliable information on sustainability. Transparent and reliable 
sustainability information along the value chains are key to enhance companies’ offer of greener products and to 
enable consumers to choose products with the lowest carbon footprint.  
 
For a new SPPI to be effective in making products fit for the climate neutral and circular economy, we believe the 
following principles should be considered: 
 
Broadening the scope of the EcoDesign Directive  
 
- If the Commission choses to extend its scope, it will be crucial to ensure that such an extension does not 
create legal uncertainties or overlap with existing product-specific legislation. 
- Although having some common minimum criteria under an umbrella legislation may be useful, we believe 
product specific legislation should always have the priority over horizontal multi-product ones, like the 
EcoDesign Directive. 
- It will also be important to clarify the exact definitions, options and thresholds it is considering defining the 
circularity criteria, and how this would all feed into the broader discussion on applying a life cycle 
approach to products. 
 
Life cycle assessment  
 
- A common framework, based on harmonized LCA methodologies, should be developed to disclose the 
environmental footprint of material and products. This methodology should cover all the stages of the 
product lifecycle, including production and energy use, use-phase, end-of-life recycling and the integration 
of recycled materials into new products. 
- A common LCA methodology is welcome and it can provide a reliable, standardized and harmonized view 
at sector of activity level on the sustainability of products, tailoring it to sector-specific needs. Continued 
refinement of existing tools such as the product environment footprint (PEF) as well as continued industry 
involvement are paramount to deliver on this.  
- The Environmental Footprint methodology is a good starting point but needs improvements and 
corrections. For instance, the Circular Footprint Formula should further be refined by aiming to reflect the 
physical material streams and balance between recycled content and end-of-life credits. 
- However, it is important that any LCA methodology must be able to differentiate based on the actual 
energy source used to manufacture the material and should aim to reflect physical realities as closely as 
possible. 
- Furthermore, consumers should be given the opportunity to be informed and educated about the full 
lifecycle of products (including carbon footprint) in order to have a clear view of their eco logical footprint 
and performance. One example to do so are digital product passports. Such passports could very well 
work, but the goals, feasibility and effectiveness of such passports need to be assessed first in close 
cooperation with industry stakeholders.

Well defining sustainability and circularity  
 
- Secondary vs Primary material - An important consideration is that policies to define sustainable 
products should also evaluate products based on the footprint of the primary material. This is a particu lar 
concern for aluminium, where - due to long lifespan of aluminium products (50 years in construction and 
15 years in transport) – the amount of aluminium reaching its end-of-life and being available as post-
consumer scrap to be recycled is limited. Today, post-consumer scrap represents approx. 20 % of the 
aluminium metal supply in Europe and most of the material ever produced is still in use (more than 75%), 
ref. European Aluminium. Therefore, material longevity – added to the growing demand for aluminium – 
means that we will still need primary production in the foreseeable future. As there are substantial 
differences in the carbon footprint from primary aluminium, it is important to establish clear and common 
definitions on what is low-carbon primary aluminium, and how to measure its environmental footprint. 
 
- Multiple recycling - Circularity cannot be defined in one metric only, but one of the ways in which it could 
be defined is through the concept of multiple recycling, already present in the revised Waste Framework 
Directive where priority is given to products that are suitable for numerous recycling cycles without loss of 
quality, which is already a common practice in aluminium. The role of multiple recycling should be 
considered as the preferred waste management option in the waste hierarchy. Furthermore, the use of 
permanent materials with high recycling potential should be promoted and recognized in future legislation.  
 
- Differentiate between pre- and post-consumer recycling - It is imperative that any definition of 
circularity should clearly and correctly define what is recycled material. In fact, “recycled material” will 
usually be perceived as post-consumer scrap by consumers, meaning scrap coming from end products 
e.g. cars, buildings, that have previously been used. However, recycled material may also refer to pre-
consumer (or process) scrap, i.e. waste that arises during the manufacturing process that is routinely re-
melted in the aluminium industry and should be minimized. Although they are both recycled materials, it is 
important to note that in the case  the carbon footprint of process scrap is higher than the one of 
post-consumer scrap. Process scrap carries a footprint ranging from 4 kg CO2/kg alu to more than 20 kg 
CO2/kg alu, depending on the primary metal origin, while the post-consumer scrap typically has a footprint 
of 0,5 kg CO2/kg alu, after remelting.  
- It is thus crucial to ensure full transparency when evaluating the different scrap sources which compose 
the recycled material.  
- The policy framework should therefore acknowledge this distinction because the ultimate objective should 
be to incentivize recycling of more post-consumer scraps. In fact, what it matters for a truly sustainable 
‘recycling’ is to improve the recycling efficiency of end products containing aluminium, as this would allow 
more post-consumer scrap volumes to be available on the market. This can be achieved via improving 
collection systems, as well as investing in innovative sorting and recycling technologies.  
 
- European Standards (EN) to measure the recyclability of products should be further developed (See e.g. 
the recently published EN 45555:2019, under the Ecodesign Directive, which does recognize the 
difference between pre and post-consumer scrap) and criteria should be developed to promote recycling-
friendly products and multiple recycling. 
 
Recycled content  
 
- In the case of aluminium, introducing requirements for minimum recycled content is not necessarily a 
driving factor for more circular products and should not be used as a stand-alone criterion to measure the 
sustainability of materials. Recycled content must be considered in a context with other criteria such as 1. 
the carbon footprint, 2. it must be seen in a lifecycle perspective.  
- Secondly, due the long lifespan of the material and volumes available (75% of all aluminium ever 
produced is still in use, parts of this recycled multiple times), criteria for minimum recycled content will not

- Aluminium is used in most cases as an alloy together with other metals to achieve the desired properties. 
Such alloying elements are kept in the loop as valuable ingredients in the next life cycle together with the 
aluminium itself.  Recycled content requirements would reduce the ability to apply recycling material in a 
way fitting best to the metallurgical demand in future life cycles. 
- The objective should instead be to improve the recycling efficiency of products containing aluminium, 
ensuring they are collected, sorted and recycled effectively, using the best available techniques, at their 
end of life. In this way, products will be brought back into the loop and more post-consumer scrap volumes 
will be available on the market.  
 
Product design 
 
- Products should be designed in a way that creates economic value for manufacturers (and other 
businesses), as aluminium producers, to collect, sort and recycle the various materials used at the end -of-
life.  
- New products put on the market should be automatically designed in a life cycle perspective and with 
material efficiency requirements, i.e. recyclability, durability and repairability at the forefront.  Existing 
horizontal standards addressing such requirements should be widely applicable. 
- These are important elements that should be better addressed at the design stage, along with economic 
and technical viability, in order to anticipate for their repair and sustainable end-of-life treatment. The 
Ecodesign Directive should evolve to fully include these requirements in product design  
 
Green Public Procurement 
- Requirements for public procurement are an effective tool to achieve climate and environmental goals. We 
therefore would welcome the introduction of minimum sustainability requirements as part of the mandatory 
criteria for a public tender process towards more greener products. 
- Such mandatory sustainability requirements, if introduced, should be based on clear definitions , should 
cover the whole life cycle of products, and should be required at all stages of the procurement process.  
Further, they should also include clear conditions for quality treatment of products at the end-of-life, so as 
to ensure that materials are duly recovered and brough back into the loop.  
- Personnel responsible for public procurement very often does not have the competence in setting good 
purchase criteria to attract the products with least environmental or climate impact based on the 
production processes, need for maintenance, duration and recirculation/reuse. Further, the knowledge of 
what “green” products are available, is limited. We therefore propose the establishment of mandatory 
programs and courses for personnel responsible for public procurement to increase competence and 
knowledge to secure that more greener products become part of public procurement .

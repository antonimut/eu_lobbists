 
The ELV Directive, which came into force 20 years ago largely delivered on its initial objectives due 
to the significant efforts made by the automotive and the recycling industry. The European 
Automobile Manufacturers’ Association (ACEA) fully supports the  European Commission’s 
initiative to ensure a better consistency with the objectives of the European Green Deal, including 
the Circular Economy Action Plan and the European Climate Law.    
 
Product Longevity 
The approach selected by the European Commission  to favour circularity, prioritises the reduction 
of waste and reuse of materials/articles before recycling. It has always been the automobile 
industry’s goal to design vehicles as long -time usable products, while ensuring they are reusable, 
repairable, and recyclable to the highest possible degree. After multiple ownership, the average age 
of an ELV (15-22 years – Trinomics report 2020), is much higher than most other consumer products 
and clearly demonstrates the success of the automotive industry’s appro ach. Unfortunately, this 
extended product life makes it impossible to assess if a design choice implemented today delivers 
the expected positive impact in 15-22 years when the time comes for the ELV to be recycled. This is 
especially true when it comes to shredder technology and other recycling technologies (e.g. chemical 
recycling) which have certainly improved over such a period. For example, the reduction of SVHCs in 
today’s new vehicles may not result in simplified material recycling as knowledge about substances 
and their classification, will continue to evolve over the next decades.  
 
Dismantling Information 
At some considerable cost and effort with the International Dismantling Information System (IDIS) -
developed in close cooperation with the economic partners- vehicle manufacturers provide detailed 
information to ATFs to ensure the environmental treatment of End -of-Life-Vehicles, safely and 
economically. Automobile manufacturers are requested to deliver relevant information within 6 
months after launch of a model. This dismantling information is provided free of charge by 77 car 
brands on 1000 car models and about 2200 variants in 40 countries and is available in 31 languages. 
IDIS includes links to the repair & maintenance information (RMI) websites including e.g. spare part 
catalogues and training information, of the vehicle manufacturers. ATF are welcome to use the same 
reference as any independent operator/workshop with regard to finding relevant information to 
dismantle or reuse parts.

ATF which will depend on the market situation in a specific region or Member State. A level p laying 
field must be guaranteed for all market operators. It is a business decision of the recycling industry

of spare parts from ELVs:

• Treatment of a large number of ELVs of a single vehicle line will result in the availability of 
many spare parts and reduce demand  
• Illegal dismantling activities and related online sale lead to an oversupply of spare parts in a 
“grey sector” which reduces the business profitability of legal ATFs. It is therefore crucial to 
force online marketplaces to require from sellers a valid ATF registration number and 
contact details to ensure the spare parts originate from an actual ATF. Furthermore, this 
would also be a benefit for the customer. 
• In some cases, fraudulent spare parts (e.g. body parts) made with cheaper material are 
available on online marketplaces. Beyond the evident safety risk, these parts should also be 
banned unless evidence of conformity, certified by the Original E quipment manufacturer 
can be provided. 
• In some EU member states the whole ELV is considered as waste and every part or material 
dismantled from it shall automatically be classified as waste, which can generate confusion 
and legal uncertainty on the status (waste or non -waste) and transboundary shipment of 
parts dismantled for reuse. Clarifying that parts dismantled from ELVs in legal ATFs for reuse 
purpose (with all due traceability) are not waste and can be handled, transported and sold 
as products could prompt legal ATFs to further develop this activity. 
• In some Member States lower insurance fees for clients accepting repair with second life 
parts or obligations for automotive repair workshops to offer “circular economy repair 
parts” (i.e. remanufactured o r reused spare parts) are proven to be effective to stimulate 
growth of the second life parts market. Such examples demonstrate that this can be 
achieved by stimulating demand rather than setting mandatory targets. 
 
 
Repair, Re-use, Remanufacturing 
Extending product Life 
Increasing the longevity of vehicles is actively supported by the members of ACEA through the 
comprehensive remanufacturing programmes, among others. These programmes exist for many of 
the mechanical and electronic components.  
Design for sustainability 
The automobile industry is actively participating in the Circular Plastic Alliance and is currently 
supporting discussion on the untapped potential for recyclates in vehicles. Waste reduction, 
including eco-design of cars to facilitate re-use, repair, remanufacturing, and recycling is a key goal.  
Recycled content 
On top of the Circular Economy Action Plan, the European Climate Law defines clear climate and CO2 
targets for 2050, suggesting carbon reductions efforts from all sectors. With this in mind, car 
manufacturers measure environmental performance (e.g. carbon intensity) internally to determine 
the most appropriate material for use in specific parts and components. In some instances, the use 
of a recycled material instead of a more pe rformant and lightweight virgin material can contradict

this approach, resulting in higher carbon emissions over the whole vehicle lifecycle. ACEA would 
therefore like to highlight that no one-size-fits-all solution can help to drive circularity and deliver on

different performance requirement. 
Car manufacturers are already using a high volume of recycled steel and recycled NF metals in new 
vehicles and are actively seeking solutions to increase recycled content in their products in line with 
Article 4(c) of the current ELV Directive.

Nevertheless, ACEA members will continue to increase the usage of secondary materials in EU 
products, including materials which are processed for a second time (e.g. recycled plastics from pre-
consumer waste) as this prevents the consumption of primary raw materials such as crude oil in case 
of polymers or ore for metals. It is ACEA’s opinion that making a distinction between post-industrial 
(pre-consumer) or post -consumer recyclate is therefore not required as it is likely to lead to 
additional administrative burden with little benefit for the environment. 
Achieving higher recycled plastic will require the development of innovative recycling technologies. 
Chemical recycling for instance, can represent a great opportunity to expand the use of recycled 
plastics to more demanding applications in the mid -term, as a complement to more traditional 
recycling methods.  
Several car manufacturers have published interesting studies of flagship vehicles (actually large 
premium vehicles with a high amount of plastics) which include a high rate of recycled plastics. Such

concepts are filled with good intentions and will deliver innovative recycled materials on production 
vehicles in due time. It is however too early for these materials to become mainstream across all EU 
car segments due to material availability and strategic choices made by car manufacturers to reduce 
vehicle CO2 emissions, such as the use of innovative lightweight virgin materials which do not have 
any recycled equivalent on the market providing the same CO2 benefits over the full vehicle life cycle. 
Market forces play an important role and a level playing field must therefore be guaranteed to all 
market operators in order for car manufacturers to increase the recycled content in their products 
in a fair manner. A shortage in supply of recyclates, triggered – for instance – by increased demand 
from other sectors, must not lead to artificial price hikes. 
In addition, it takes at least five years to develop a new vehicle. Materials are selected several years 
before start of production after undergoing long and complex validation procedures to guarantee 
that they meet the high quality, durability, and safety standards of the carmaker and from legislation. 
Once all materials are selected, plastic injection moulds and stamping tools are designed and 
produced accordingly and will be used during the whole industrial life of the vehicle. Any material 
change occurring after this initial materials selection and validation phase, such as the substitution 
of virgin plastics by recycled ones, would require to redesign and replace the injection moulds of all 
the concerned parts and go through the whole validation process again including a series of crash 
tests on the whole vehicle.  
Considering this long, complex and costly materials selection and validation process it is essential 
that any new requirement such as a mandatory recycled content on new vehicles (which ACEA does 
not support on its principle) should apply to new types only and at least five years after such new 
requirement is published, and not to vehicles which have already come or will come into production 
until five years after this publication. 
 
Hazardous substances 
Since the year 2000, the ELV Directive has successfully restricted the use of the four heavy metals in 
the motor vehicles.   This has been possible because the dedicated review procedure is industry 
specific and takes into account the unique physical and technical requirements for automotive 
materials and components, as well as development time scales and Type Approval dates for vehicles. 
Substance restrictions in vehicles are becoming increasingly regulated by a growing number of 
separate pieces of legislation, e.g. REACH and RoHS.  These different regulations frequently overlap 
with the ELV Directive and in many cases, they even contradict each other, making complying 
confusing and unnecessarily complex. 
Given the success of the ELV regulatory process, ACEA believes that the regulation of the four heavy 
metals in automobiles should be solely in the scope of the legal framework of the ELV Directive. 
The ELV process could further be approved by taking the learning from other legislative processes, 
for example by including a socio -economic evaluat ion as to not overly burden the automotive 
industry for little to no improvement to the environment or consumer. Taking note of the fact that 
the auto industry has managed to phase out these heavy metals in the vast majority of applications

and that there are only very minor applications left where further substitution becomes increasingly 
burdensome, the frequency of the Annex II revision should be reduced. 
To provide better cohesion between the ELV Directive and the REACH Regulations, ACEA 
recommends that Annex II of the ELV Directive is introduced as a sector specific Risk Management 
Option (RMO) for the restriction of these heavy metals under REACH, which is the approach that has 
already been taken for similar sector specific legislation (e.g. RoHS). 
 
Better enforcement of the provisions designed to ensure that all ELV are collected. 
It is critical to implement a consistent EU wide obligation for the last owner to provide a Certificate 
of Destruction (CoD) to complete the final de-registration of a vehicle and to link it to the cancelation 
of the car insurance fees In such a system, the last owner of a vehicle would not be able to cancel 
the fees of its car insurance without an appropriate document which indicates the transfer of the 
ownership or an appropriate CoD which can only be issued by ATFs. The legal obligation for vehicle’s 
last owner to hold its vehicle at the end of life and, to hand it over to an ATF must remain. ACEA does 
not support schemes that would financially incentivise the last owner or economic operators to fulfil 
their legal obligations as such measures would financially reward the fulfilment of a legal duty instead 
of penalising misconduct.  
Today deregistration and submission of a CoD  is mainly a time-consuming process with a high rate 
of human made errors  and suffers a lack of transparency . Automating these processes and the 
delivery of certificates has enormous potential for innovation and business development and should 
be supported by the EU Commission as part of its Digital Transformation strategy. Such automated 
process would deliver better enforcement of the existing legislation by preventing the business of 
unauthorized operators and of illegal export and recycling activities. Valuable resources which are 
disappearing today will find their way back to legal markets and will ensure a self -sustaining ELV 
business model over the long term. 
As part of the ELV Directive, all economic operators must set up systems for the collection, 
treatment, and recovery of end-of-life vehicles (ELVs). Vehicle manufacturers take responsibility for 
the substitution of hazardous substances and for the cost -free take back of all ELVs by authorised 
contracted treatment facilities (ATFs), i.e. dismantlers  and/or recyclers. ATFs organise the vehicle 
take-back and ensure the environmentally sound treatment of ELVs and together with the shredder 
companies ensure that recycling and recovery activities are also met in an environmental sound way.  
For informatio n ELV compliance cost figures collected by the automotive industry in early 2020 
indicated that the automotive industry already invested 2,7€ billion for set -up of IMDS, IDIS, take 
back networks and consumer info and pays almost 160€ million on an annual basis. 
Such a take-back and treatment system for ELVs delivers benefit to all stakeholder and is generally a 
self-sustaining business model as the value of an ELV sufficiently covers the cost of all necessary 
treatment steps throughout the entire recycling value chain. This includes automotive and industrial 
batteries that are collected with the ELV.

Batteries under the scope of the ELV directive 
It is ACEA’s opinion that a vehicle must be considered as one single product, in line with customers’ 
expectations when they purchase a complete product including all functions and characteristics. It is 
therefore expected that the recycling industry would treat vehicles as a whole as well, without 
discrimination for specific components or materials while still gen erating profit. ACEA therefore 
believes that all batteries which have been taken back as part of an entire ELV have to be considered 
as officially taken back. 
 
The scope of the ELV Directive  
 
The ELV Directive should not be extend to heavy duty vehicles (HDV), especially with the circular 
economy and the competitiveness of the automotive industry in mind because: 
• The complexity of heavy -duty vehicles – Each of them is tailored to perform a specific 
commercial need and thus there are thousands of different HDVs. In addition, they are not 
finished vehicles when they leave the manufacturers’ gate as they are led to the body 
builders to complete these vehicles with a variety of different specialized equipment; 
• Their market volume, mileage and lifetime  – Trucks and buses are sold in low volumes 
compared to passenger cars (1 HDV for 35 cars). HDVs have a longer lifetime and are usually 
sold multiple times after their initial purchase. Moreover, since they are often re -sold and 
used in other world regions, a small number of HDVs are scrapped in Europe; 
• Their durability and reparability – From a design perspective, HDVs are more reliable and 
easily reparable than passenger cars; 
• Their remanufacturing and recycling – The HDVs are remanufactured many times an d are 
sold as new, which creates complexities with their EPR. In addition, these vehicles exhibit 
high recycling rates as they are made of mostly valuable materials; 
• HDV manufacturers already phase -out hazardous substances and provide dismantling 
information – Manufacturers of HDVs follow the requirements of the REACH Regulation and 
customer demand and are thus already phasing out heavy metals on a voluntary basis 
whenever that is technically and economically possible. In addition, HDV producers provide 
manuals on a voluntary basis for the depollution and dismantling of trucks and buses. 
 
ACEA support the conclusion in the Trinomics report 2020 regarding the need of a thorough 
environmental risk analysis if considering extending the scope of ELV to HDV. 
The relatively low numbers of vehicles (implying a smaller scale of environmental risk than for cars), 
a high share (compared to cars) of SMEs involved in the manufacture (implying a large impact on 
SMEs, who have less resources to comply with the directive), longer life vehicles compared to cars 
and more legitimate export of used vehicles out of Europe are important factors to be assessed.

ABOUT THE EU AUTOMOBILE INDUSTRY  
▪ 14.6 million Europeans work in the auto industry (directly and indirectly), 
accounting for 6.7% of all EU jobs. 
▪ 11.5% of EU manufacturing jobs – some 3.7 million – are in the automotive sector. 
▪ Motor vehicles account for €440.4 billion in taxes in major European markets. 
▪ The automobile industry generates a trade surplus of €74 billion for the EU. 
▪ The turnover generated by the auto industry represents over 7% of EU GDP. 
▪ Investing €60.9 billion in R&D annually, the automotive sector is Europe's largest 
private contributor to innovation, accounting for 29% of total EU spending.

Feedback on Roadmap for:  
End-of-life vehicles – revision of EU rules 
 
Plastics Recyclers Europe (PRE) represents the interest of the plastics recycling industry. Several of 
PRE members are specialized in treating the shredder light fraction resulting from the ELV treatment 
to produce plastics recyclate for use in the traditional plastics compounding and converting industries. 
A fraction of this material is already being used in the automotive supply chain.  
It is with great interest that we read the inception impact assessment. In our response we will provide 
suggestions on how to improve collection of ELV, and why this would be beneficial to increase the 
recycling activity, we will provide suggestions for improvements in ELV treatment, comment on 
recycling targets, and suggestions for a system to define recycled content targets. These comments 
are a start, and we would be available to provide more in later stages. Furthermore, we would be 
happy to facilitate contact between any desk officers and/or consultants to our members for the impact 
assessment. In summary: 
• A reimbursement should be provided to last holder of a vehicle, to address the proper end-of-life 
treatment of vehicles and to remedy the “missing vehicle” issue.  
• Delegated power should be given to the European Commission to amend the provisions on 
treatment to ensure separate collection of certain parts for recycling.  
• Recycling targets should be set for thermoplastic material 
• Overall vehicle recycled content levels may be set, but this must be complemented by a procedure 
whereby the European Commission is given delegated power to set recycled content requirements 
per specific vehicle components.  
 
Collection 
At present many old vehicles do not reach authorized treatment facilities as they are exported, legally 
or illegally, to countries outside of the EU. Such exports are a natural consequence of economic 
incentives. While it is beneficial that the holder of an old vehicle can dispose its vehicle free of charge, 
as otherwise this may or may not come at a fee depending on the state of the vehicle, value of spare 
parts, and subsequent gains from recovery. However, the holder of such an old vehicle is normally 
able to find a buyer willing to pay small amount for the vehicle. These buyers then deregister the 
vehicle and export the vehicle to countries outside of the EU where they can get a higher price for the 
old vehicle. Outside of the EU, such vehicles may be given a short second life, whereafter they 
become waste there or may be scrapped for parts and the remaining husk of the vehicle treated in a 
non-environmentally sound manner.

The inception impact assessment indicates that digital solutions can be found to the issue of “missing 
vehicles”. While further improvements in digital tracking of vehicles may be part of a solution, the 
provision of an economic incentive would be a more potent way of ensuring that the end of life stage 
of the vehicle does not transpire outside of the EU. The provision of a fee to the holder of an old 
vehicle, would provide such an incentive and ensure that vehicles sold in the EU are treated in the 
EU. Such a payment to the holder of an old vehicle can be financed though Extended Producer 
Responsibility mechanisms.  
The height of the fee should not be decided in the National context, but at EU level in order to ensure 
that there are no intra-EU transfers of old vehicles to benefit from the differences in the fee provided. 
Otherwise, the EPR systems in the country with the highest reimbursement would start bearing a 
disproportional part of the burden of financing the reimbursement. 
The measure would be beneficial to the absolute amount of plastics recycled within the EU.  
 
Treatment 
Another point for consideration would be greater specification to be included in Annex I on treatment. 
With regards to plastics this is currently worded as follows: 
removal of tyres and large plastic components (bumpers, dashboard, fluid containers, etc.), if these 
materials are not segregated in the shredding process in such a way that they can be effectively 
recycled as materials, 
Currently dismantling of plastics components is frequently not done as they are either not considered 
to be “large” or because the polymers can be recovered from the shredder light fraction. While the 
latter is quite acceptable, the former is something that can be discussed.  
To give a practical example, car headlights are made from transparent polycarbonate. Transparent 
polycarbonate is a high value thermoplastic material that is at present not recovered from the shredder 
light fraction. Disassembly of car headlights would result in a waste stream that would be highly 
recyclable. Yet this is only rarely done, and one could argue that it is not needed as the component is 
not “large” (especially when compared with the examples mentioned).  
Of course, such a dismantling step would result in costs for ELV treatment and it is uncertain whether 
such cost would outweigh societal benefits. As these kinds of considerations would be part specific 
and may evolve over time, a process could be established within the directive whereby the European 
Commission is empowered to amend Annex I section 4 through delegated acts.  
Such measures could positively affect the recycling rate within the EU.

plastics from the ELV treatment operations. Here we would like to note that it is important to 
determine the yield of the recycling process and establish either average loss rates or shift the 
calculation point towards these facilities, as it is not the case that 100% of the shredder light fraction 
sent for recycling is actually recycled by the specialized plastics recyclers. 
The specialized recycler will utilize density sorting to produce a polyolefin fraction (PE and PP) and 
styrenics fraction (PS and ABS). Subsequently these polymer family fractions can be further 
separated by electrostatic separation or simply used as is in the traditional plastics compounding and 
converting industry as these polymers have a degree of compatibility with one another that does not 
result in too inferior physical/mechanical properties. The denser fraction contains carbon/glass fiber 
reinforced plastics, other denser polymers, and polymers containing brominated flame retardants and 
may be send for energy recovery. More advanced technology may become available in the future to 
recover more polymers from the fraction that is currently waste output, but these are not necessarily 
available for the time being.  
Setting targets per material could result, though the EPR mechanism, in the development of new 
recycling technologies and make these more economical.  
 
Recycled content 
When it comes to recycled content, certain parts within automotive applications may be more suitable 
to incorporate recyclate, while in others it may be more difficult or impossible at present.  
For example, a variety of thermosetting polymers are used within automotive applications because 
such thermosetting polymers have certain physical/mechanical properties that cannot be (easily) met 
with thermoplastics. The incorporation of recyclate into such thermosets will be difficult, if not 
impossible because thermosets are not readily recyclable and therefore there is no recyclate to 
introduce into these materials. One could argue that such thermosets should then be replaced by 
thermoplastics, however this is often not feasible as thermoplastics cannot meet the requirements of 
the part.  
In other cases, recyclate simply is not able to meet the same physical/mechanical properties required 
of a certain part. This is important, especially when such parts affect the safety of the vehicle (e.g. 
seat belts, fuel/breaking fluid lines). In other less critical parts (e.g. dashboard trim) this is less 
important.  
This does not mean that vehicle overall recycled content targets per material category should not be 
set, but this practicality will impact the feasibility of setting higher overall vehicle level targets. 
However, for those parts where recycled content can be introduced without negatively affecting the 
performance of a vehicle a procedure should be put in place to make the use mandatory. The European 
Commission can be provided delegated powers to amend an annex specific to recycled content 
requirements for specific parts within vehicles. The option of establishing such a procedure should 
be evaluated in the impact assessment.

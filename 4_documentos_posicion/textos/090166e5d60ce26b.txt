EuRIC Position Paper on the revision of Directive 2000/53/EC on 
end-of-life vehicles  
19 N ovem be r  2020  
 
The European Recycling Industries’ Confederation (EuRIC) is pleased to share comments on the revision of 
Directive 2000/53/EC on end-of-life vehicles (ELVD). 
 
As highlighted in the Final Report 1, 258 million passenger cars were registered in the EU in 2016 out of which 
11.21 million light commercial vehicles (category M1) and passenger cars (category N1) were deregistered in 
2017. Of these, 6.57 million were reported as ELVs and 0.87 million were reported as exports of used cars to non-
EU countries which leaves the whereabouts of 3.77 million vehicles unknown. This makes end -of-life vehicles 
(ELVs) an important waste stream in terms of generated volumes but also growth rates, embedded valuable 
materials and illegal flows intra- as well as extra-EU. Consequently, the proper management of ELVs in accordance 
with the ELVD is of paramount importance to tackle those issues and move towards a circular economy as defined 
in the European Green Deal and the Circular Economy Action Plan (CEAP). The following is of utmost importance 
for EuRIC in light of the ELVD revision:  
 
 
1. Streamlining requirements of the ELVD to improve implementation & enforcement  
 
As already highlighted in our previous position paper on the ELVD revision2, EuRIC fully supports the goals of the 
ELVD and calls for measures aiming at strengthening its implementation & enforcement. The revision of the ELV 
Directive is the right opportunity to implement urgently needed measures to solve practical challenges linked to 
the Directive, such as: 
 
 Improving registration and de-registrations systems: In order to tackle the problem of “missing vehicles” more 
effectively, EuRIC strongly calls for substantially improving and harmonizing the current de -registration 
process. Additionally, temporary d e-registrations also require attention. For instance, a temporary de -
registration should also be accompanied by information about the fate of the vehicle to prevent abuse. 
 
 Financial incentives to deliver a vehicle to an ATF in exchange for a CoD: It is absolutely vital for the ELV 
recycling sector to ensure that the certificate of destruction (CoD) is properly enforced – incentives play a key 
role in that respect. Therefore, EuRIC strongly suggests to consider an incentive system based on an insurance 
premium as being done in the Czech Republic:   If the car is not validly de-registered, the insurance cannot be 
terminated and a penalty has to be imposed.  Such a simple framework incentivizes legal operators and 
sanctions illegal ones. In addition, it can b e implemented in complement to other incentives based for 
example on refundable tax or fees’ schemes. 
 
 Distinction between used cars and ELVs: It is essential to have a clear distinction between a used car and an 
ELV thanks to clear definitions and  practical implementation measures  to prevent  substantial loophole s. 
EuRIC strongly supports the introduction of a roadworthiness test or a technical control. Such an obligation 
has been introduced in Italy with the effect that only a car which has passed such a roadworthiness test can 
be exported to another country.  
 
 Increasing the responsibility of car manufacture rs: Should an approach based on extended producer 
responsibility (EPR) be chosen to increase the responsibility of car manufacturers in the end of life stages of

2. Improving circularity from product design to End-of-life vehicles (ELVs)  recycling and 
recycled content 
ELVs have long been recognised as a valuable resource of readily recovered ferrous and other metals (almost 
100% of these materials are recycled thanks to mechanical treatment, shredding & post-shredding technologies) 
and for the last decades also as a valuable resource for recycled plastics, yet with very much untapped potential 
for this polymeric material.  While ferrous and other metals can be readily recovered, non -metallic automotive 
waste (incl. plastic)  are more difficult to recover and may pose a burden to the  environment. Thus, proper 
treatment at the end of life in compliance with the ELVD is of the utmost importance.  
   
In terms of ELV recycling, 70-75% of automotive waste is generally easily recovered, including the metal skeleton 
of vehicle, lubricants, tyres, etc3. Also, although not a generalized practice, some plastic parts can be dismantled 
by authorised treatment facilities (ATFs) for reuse, and this adds to the profit generated in the value chain. 
However, plastic recovery by manual dismantling remains marginal because, although it allows the segregation of 
specific and pure plastic grades, its productivity is very limited and ill-adapted to industrial scale recovery, its costs 
elevated, and automatization hampered by the diversity and composite structures of car components. As cars are 
evolving so do the challenges, therefore, the other 20-25% (considering 5% end waste) of the automotive waste 
(which include plastics, glass, rubber, fabrics and other materials) present a significant challenge to be recovered.  
 
2.1 Technical issues stemming from ELV plastics recovery 
A large part of the plastics from cars can be separated after the shredding of the car in post -shredder stages. 
Different steps of float/sink separation are most commonly used in the separation of mixed plastics on the basis of 
density differences; other techniques for plastic separation have been developed such as froth flotation or laser 
and infra -red systems ( Fourier technique) used to separate plastics based on colour 4 and different types of 
polymers by comparing their spectra and being significantly more efficient than dispersive spectrometers5.

Optical sorting of plastics by near infrared is a state-of-the-art technology – however, there are certain limitations 
concerning the Automotive Shredder Residues (ASR) such as the presence of humidity, oil residue, coatings and 
colouring of plastics with carbon black.  
 
In order to promote and expand optical sorting, researche rs have proposed to add chemical compounds in 
components as a fingerprint signature (i.e., phosphor markers) that the optical sensors can easily and unbiasedly 
identify and next sort6. However, this is not an option on short notice because of the long development phase for 
cars and their long lifetime. Detectors based on mid infrared spectrometry and laser spectroscopic fluorescence 
detection7 are also aiming at filling that gap.  The final recycling steps consist in shredding and extrusion, which 
results in the production of post-consumer pellets by polymer-types meeting industry specifications for their re-
incorporation, for example into new cars or to be used in other plastic sectors.  
 
The complexity of multi-materials use in vehicle design has created several challenges for vehicle recycling. These 
challenges have also risen in the metal range where high strength steel and a variety of aluminium grades have 
been incorporated into the design of cars. Here x-ray-based detection methods, laser-based methods and density-
based sorting allow recycling companies to retrieve material grades that were previously inseparable. In a similar 
manner for the wide variety of plastics used in automo tive (incl. a large number of resins, different additives, 
etc.), or the presence of reinforced plastics (containing fillers such as glass fibre and glass beads) technologies for 
improved identification and selection, are underway.  
 
Nowadays, the European technical ELV plastic recyclers, represented by EuRIC Plastics Recycling Branch (EPRB) , 
thanks to the highly sophisticated processes able to sort mixed post-consumer polymers into single polymer 
fractions, can recover ELV plastics back into recycled polymers meeting manufacturing quality and technical 
requirements (e.g., safety and longevity, emissions, flammability etc.) similar to virgin polymers. 
 
For the residual fractions, which cannot be  currently recovered, chemical recycling may in the future offer 
complementary solutions for some polymer -types with legacy additives. Yet, currently  chemical recycling 
processes are still at pilot stage and need to prove they can be viable at industrial scale both from a technological 
and a n economic standpoint 8. In addition, ensuring adequate capacity for energy recovery of non -recyclable 
fractions with high calorific value is essential both for the competitiveness of the recycling industry and proper 
treatment of residual waste9. 
 
 
Examples 
 
Front runners in the automotive industry  which some of them have partnered - for more than a decade  - with 
leading technical plastics recyclers represented by the EPRB, have proven that plastic incorporation into new cars 
is technically feasible as they are already incorporating post -consumer recycled plastics for several parts of new 
cars, primarily interior and drivetrain components.  
To quote only a few publicly -available examples, Mercedes, for the base version of the new E -Class launched in 
2015, proved that it is possible to produce a total of 72 components, with a total part weight of 54.4 kg10, with a 
proportion of high-quality recycled plastics. Even though there is still a lot of untapped potential , it shows that 
car manufactur ers are shifting from a linear to a more circular economy  for plastics .  Renault, another car 
manufacturer, increases the use of recycled plastics on its vehicles from generation to generation11. For example,

2.2 Binding recycled content targets to increase circularity of ELV plastics  
EuRIC entirely supports the new Circular Economy Action Plan  which foresees the setting up of mandatory 
recycled content targets for certain materials, including plastics, as part of the revision of the ELV Directive.  
 
As documented in EuRIC substantiated call for binding post-consumer recycled plastic content int o new cars 17, 
such targets for plastics in cars are vital to:  
 Increase circularity of plastics used in the automotive sector;  
 De-correlate prices of recycled polymer prices from virgin ones which are driven by oil -prices but fail to 
internalize negative externalities; 
 Scale-up recycling capacity in Europe by securing long-term demand; and  
 Maximize resources’ as well as greenhouse gas emission and energy savings and internalize these benefits 
in prices, which the market so far fails to internalise.

In line with the Plastics Strategy, it is vital to scale up plastics recycling in the automotive sector by securing long-
term demand through binding recycled content for thermoplastics used in new cars. As witnessed with binding 
recycled content targets se t by the SUP Directive, such targets are the only regulatory means to scale -up 
capacities, foster investments along the value chain and de-correlate recycled polymer prices from virgin polymer 
prices when oil prices collapse, as the crisis stemming from the COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated18. 
 
Based on industry experience, gradual and fully achievable recycled content targets for post -consumer 
thermoplastics in new cars should be set as follows:  
 25% by 2025,  
 30% by 2030 and,  
 35% by 2035.  
 
The recycled content targets shall be calculated on the basis of the total weight of thermoplastics used in each 
car. Such recycled content targets , would directly support the objectives set in the Plastics Strategy of reaching 
10 million tons of recycled plastics annually used to make products in the EU from 2025. 
 
 
2.3 Design for end-of-life collection and sorting: a long- and short-time required steps to 
increase recyclates  
Cars contain a wide number of valuable materials and, when they have reached end -of-life stage, the most 
sustainable option in terms of resource efficiency is material recovery. Taking into consideration at design stage 
the recovery of materials which are difficult to separate using Best Available Techniques (BATs) is overly important 
to increase circularity along the automotive value chain. There are two main drivers in that respect: 
 
Further development and implementation of Post -Shredding Technologies (PSTs) : Vehicles are comprised of 
multiple materials: metals (71 -74%), polymers (12 -15%), elastomers (6%), glass (3%), others (2-8%)19,20. The 
widespread use of PSTs is paramount to increase circularity since it will not only address the problems in shorter 
time than increasing design for recycling/sustainability but also , for example in plastics, (Fig. 1)21, managing this 
automotive shredder residue ( ASR) - through PST - addresses the biggest quantities of this material found in a 
car. For this waste stream it is necessary to take into account that most of the metals and plastics present into a 
car -after turning into an ELV - become the metals and plastics that need to be recovered from  the ASR . 
Technological advancement has allowed the creation of a number of PST which are employed to recover materials 
from ASRs. In the past, cars were mostly made of steel. However, with the development of lightweight technology, 
high-strength steel and plastic are – nowadays – widely used in the automotive industry and not only because of 
its smaller weight than that of other materials but also due to its high safety, low cost and durability. Thus, 
improving PSTs grants plastic recycling an opportunity within the ELV Directive to improve - further than plastic 
recoverability – recycling and, in this way, foster the circular economy of plastics in the automotive sector.

Eco-design (i.e., taking into account sorting strategies at earlier vehicle design stages could foster effective material 
recycling). In Europe, the automotive value chain  faces the challenge of  anticipating and responding to  ELV 
management legislation, a challenge that comes from the fact that there are almost 20 years between the design 
of a car and  its end-of-life phase . Therefore, it is not easy to estimate in the design phase which recycling 
technologies or legislative requirements will be in place  20 years later when the car will become an ELV.  
Nevertheless, producers should take into account how the choice of materials (e.g., polymers, additives …) 
influence their recyclability based on currently available best available technologies . In this regard, it is possible 
to establish a link between ca r design and current recycling technologies. Therefore, taking into account that 
most materials will end up in the automotive shredder residue (ASR), automotive design should be based on the 
state of the art and short-term evolution of PST. The polymers rate in next generation vehicles is not expected to 
decrease (on the contrary), so that without corrective actions the production of ASR  could further grow in the 
future. In this regard,  it is possible to distinguish between curative actions (e.g., improve ment in recovery 
processes, with the automotive value chain developing innovative sorting and recycle methods to recover a higher 
share of materials) and preventive action (e.g., improvement of vehicle design in order to favour its recyclability); 
this latter can be defined as environmental conscious design  methods and are referred as  Design for 
Recycling/sustainability activities. Generally, the change to sustainable design for automotive products has the  
aim of encouraging dismantling of components22. However, this needs to go beyond the idea of tackling parts for 
dismantling and therefore a higher focus needs to be laid on the processability of the ASR (e.g., reduce the number 
of different polymers, reduce polymer share (e.g., PVC) or label plastic  parts in order to facilitate the 
identification).  
 
For this, measures at design stage, are important to increase the amount of quality plastics sorted and sent for 
recycling in particular, whenever technically feasible: 
 the reduction of polymers /coatings/additives combinations impacting the recyclability at end-of-life stage;  
 the use of recycled materials in new vehicles , in order to support both, increase the recyclability of 
components using plastic and the uptake of recycled materials;  
 the substitution of SVHCs with safer alternatives which don’t alter the prime functionality of the component 
where these substances (flame retardants, heat resistant, etc.) have been added , is also strongly supported 
in order to improve recyclability.

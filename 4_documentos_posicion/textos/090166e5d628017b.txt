 
 
EFOW’s position 
EC INCEPTION IMPACT ASSESSMENT ON THE REVISION OF THE EU GIs SYSTEMS 
 
 
EFOW, the European Federation of Origin Wines  (www.efow.eu), is the Brussels -based 
organisation representing Protected Designati on of Origin (PDO) and Protected 
Geographical Indication (PGI) wines towards European institutions. 
 
The EU is the world’s leading producer of wine at more than 160 million hl, representing over 
60% of world production. Nowadays, there are approximately 3. 2 million ha of area under 
vines (45% of the world’s total) and 2.5 million agricultural holdings grow vines. Moreover, 
up to 83% of the EU vine area is dedicated to the production of GI wines . One could 
say that without GI wines there is no European vitic ulture. European wine was by far the 
largest EU agricultural export , this data will need to be reassessed following the COVID-
19 crisis.   
 
EFOW welcomes the Commission’s roadmap on the EU’s food quality scheme. The EU is a 
renowned leader in the production o f quality products. This positioning has helped shape 
and maintain sustainable rural areas. The on-going reform of the Common Agricul tural 
Policy reinforces a number of tools for the GI sector and EFOW calls on the European 
Commission to support these inno vative el ements. EFOW consider s that the 
European Commission to strive to further help GI wines  in their sustainability effor ts 
in the framework of the Fa rm to Fork Strat egy. To do so,  EFOW considers that the 
following issues should be taken on board by the European Commission:  
 
1. Allowing GI wine producers to respond to growing societal demands for 
sustainable wines 
 
The European GI wine sector is the example of a modern, value -added business rooted in a 
skill-rich tradition which cannot be outsourced or im itated on the cheap. Its current model of 
production supports a network of small, often family -owned businesses and provides key 
jobs directly and indirectly in rural areas. There is just no alternative to wine production in 
many regions across the EU. Add  to that the thousands of jobs generated thr ough 
oenotourism and you begin to see a more complete picture of the sector’s contribution to the 
economy. Many of Europe’s wine regions are UNESCO World Heritage Sites, offering 
unique environments and fostering biodiversity. 
 
The maintenance and development of the GI wine sector is therefore of crucial importance to 
the sustainable development of many areas across the EU. This can only be done if GI 
producers can meet the expectations of consumers. Consumers are  nowadays more and 
more interested not only in the origin of the product but also on how it is being produced (i.e. 
environmental impact, sustainability of resources, farmers remuneration etc). This is shown 
by the proliferation of private certification la bels set up by many companies. This process is 
creating consumer confusion as there is no consistency on and sometimes reality behind 
several private claims. EFOW believes it is indispensable to allow GI producers with more 
flexibility to include in their GI specifications provisions tha t will meet the society’s 
expectations in terms of quality and environmental, economic and social sustainability  (ex. 
the possibility for PDO wines to test hybrids and commercialise these wines etc.).

2. Strengthening GI governing bodies  
 
GI wines, compared to table wines, create a n added value for producers. This pro cess 
ensures not only a positive investment dynamic but also the commitment by producers to 
work together towards common goals, better protection of their GI, better promotion, better 
management of their area and wines  and greater investments in the sust ainability of their 
terroir. Moreover, this guarantees to a certa in extent a generational renewal as wine 
producers have a clear incentive to build their business and pass it on to future generations. 
These communities ov er the years have established themselves in governing bodies to 
manage together their product(s). 
 
Maintaining a viable and competitive wine production is challenging, especially in a 
globalised market. That is why, operators united in governing bodies nee d tools at their 
disposal to manage the growth and the development of their vineyards  as well as respond 
timely to crisis . These bodies have a wide variety of tasks that go from monitoring the 
market, to promoting and defending the product(s), to governing  the specification(s) and to 
advising members. These tasks are extremely costly and need the support of public 
authorities. 
 
Nowadays, one of the greatest challenges, is to find a way to allow GI governing bodies to 
plan the future of GI wines. For example, discussions relating to additional costs and sharing 
the burden of these co sts deriving from  changes in the specifications  (ex. inclusion of 
environmental measures) or to respond to major crisis (ex. COVID-19) are today prohibited. 
Indeed, the introducti on of cost indicators and the adoption of value allocation clauses are 
considered as anti-competitive practices by public authorities. This nullifies the role of these 
bodies in the collective management of GIs. Moreover, there can be no quality policy if the 
producers cannot be sure of covering the costs necessary to comply with the product 
specification.  
 
That is why EFOW asks the EC to: 
• Clarify and enhance the role and  powers of GIs groups. Provide a legal framework 
that will recognise the key role played by these groups in the management of the GI. 
• Help strengthen these groups, for instance some control and legal costs should be 
co-funded by public money and subsidies for promotional activities heightened. 
• Modify EU competition law to allow GI groups to  discuss and adopt value sharing 
clauses.  
 
3. Improving GI protection 
 
The abuse of a GI name by operators threatens the very existence of that GI. In fact, 
usurpations, imitations and evocations all contribute to the dilution of the reputation of a 
given GI  and create mistrust and confusion in consumers. As stated in the EUIPO study 
“Infringement of protected geographical indications for wine, spirits, agricultural products and 
foodstuffs in the European Union – 2016”, the adverse impact of counterfeiting on  GI is 
relevant as it amounts to 9% of the total market for GI products (an estimated loss of €4.3 
billion). European consumers are also directly affected with a damage of €2.3 billion per 
year. 
 
The European Commission has put forward in the reform of the  CAP currently under 
scrutiny, some interesting proposals (i.e. strengthening the protection for goods in transit and 
online sales). Nevertheless, EFOW bel ieves there is room for further improvements. In fact, 
GI governing bodies must be able to better def end themselves against third parties who 
want to use, weaken and dilute the reputation of GI products. It is of outmost importance to 
develop more stringent measures regarding the usurpation of GIs in Internet domain names, 
as well as when GIs are used as ingredients. Moreover, it is also fundamental to improve the 
controls these bodies can carry out, which currently can only be performed in their Member 
State. It would be extremely positive for traceability purposes, to ensure that these bodies 
may also control products outside of their national borders. 
 
That is why EFOW calls on the European Commission to enhance: 
• The protection of GI products to combat ab uses of the GIs’ reputation: GIs need to 
be protected against exploitation, but also against the weakening and dilution of their 
reputation.  
• The protection afforded on the internet: GIs shall be protected against any bad faith 
registration in second-level domain names.  
• The power of GI groups to perform controls outside of their national borders.

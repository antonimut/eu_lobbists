Copa and Cogeca contributions to the 
European Commission Roadmap regarding the 
revision of the EU geographical indications systems in 
agricultural products and foodstuffs,  
wines and spirit drinks 
Copa and Cogeca welcome the revision launched by the European Commission that covers 
Geographical Indication (GI) systems in agricultural products and foodstuffs, wines and spirit 
drinks. 
Geographical Indications (GIs) help to preserve and boost rural communities through their 
tradition, history and taste. They are also an important tool when exporting high-quality 
products to third countries. Indeed, the current schemes showcase and foster the diverse, rich 
and unique nature of agricultural production in the EU. Moreover, by preserving regional 
landscapes, strengthening rural communities both socially and economically, they contribute to 
increasing EU agriculture’s sustainability. However, as is the case with any good system, some of 
its aspects could be improved to further enhance their positive impact. Therefore, Copa and 
Cogeca believe that a revision of the GI systems that would improve their accessibility and 
positive impact on EU rural areas while increasing consumer trust and awareness is a great 
initiative.  
Given that farmers and agri-cooperatives, as producers of GI products, are the first ones 
concerned by this revision, we believe that it is essential to include them and put them at the 
centre of the revision process. We thus welcome our inclusion in the general consultation 
process and hope that farmers and agri-cooperatives will also be fully involved in targeted 
stakeholder consultations that will take place in the context of the impact assessment. 
More specifically, we would like to share our views regarding each of the following main 
objectives of this revision as presented in the roadmap: 
1. Improve protection and enforcement of GIs in the Member States, 
notably on the internet, to ensure effective and efficient use of 
producers’ rights  
Copa and Cogeca believe that it is essential to improve the protection and enforcement of GIs in 
the Member States and thus welcome this objective. Indeed, given their higher economic returns 
compared to regular food and drink products, GI products are more likely to be targeted by 
fraudulent activities. However, GIs are not sufficiently protected through concrete measures on 
the ground by the public authorities. Indeed, consortia and associations have had to invest their 
own resources in order to guarantee a minimum level of safeguarding comparable to what is 
guaranteed by public authorities for EU trademarks. Public authorities should strengthen the 
protection of GIs by carrying out more controls. In parallel to the need for an increased number 
of controls, we would like to underline the need for an EU harmonisation of these controls that 
often differ too much between Member States in their quality and number. We would also like to 
stress that trademarks using GI terms should be banned. 
Online trading is constantly increasing and the current COVID-19 crisis has only strengthened 
this trend. We thus fully agree with the need to improve GI protection and enforcement on the 
internet.

Moreover, while we commend the European Commission’s efforts and achievements in 
protecting GIs in trade agreements with third countries, we believe that generalised protection is 
still not granted and the results are unsatisfactory. In some cases this leads to a legitimate use of 
evocative names with a negative impact on the economic, environmental and social 
sustainability of some sector in particular. Therefore, we hope that this revision will also aim at 
providing tools to improve the protection of GIs and enforcement in third countries. Indeed, it is 
essential that the competent authorities in third countries apply the rules stipulated in the trade 
agreements in order to guarantee a decent level of protection against imitations. It is possible to 
use the European logo on third country products if they are protected in their country of origin 
and if the principle of equivalence is respected. Nevertheless, we must pay attention here in 
order to protect consumers against fraudulent and misleading practices where European GIs 
that are already recognised are used. It is of the utmost importance for the EU to be able to 
obtain guarantees from the third country authorities that a truly effective control system will be 
established in order to avoid fraud and reassure consumers. Furthermore, the use of the 
European logo should only be allowed for products that fall under a bilateral agreement. The 
Commission and Member States must also pay close attention to ensuring that these products 
are fully recognisable to consumers by using clear and transparent labels that do not generate 
any doubt about the product’s true provenance. If these products are to be able to use the 
European GI logo, it must be made clear that they are not European products. The agreement 
should establish clear and effective mechanisms aiming to provide legal certainty for trade in 
GIs and to prevent future conflicts. In addition, the agreement should include a review clause on 
extending the legal protection provided by this agreement to other GIs if necessary. 
Moreover, it is essential to better define at EU level the concepts of “agri-food fraud/crime” and 
“sounding”, in order to prevent quality EU products from continually being copied. This 
phenomenon is currently causing significant economic damage to European farmers and their 
cooperatives. A legal definition of food fraud would be the first step required to implement any 
governmental clampdown on fraudulent activities and to develop a harmonised approach and 
framework for tackling the issue, as was already highlighted by the European Parliament in 
2014.  
Finally, we would like to underline that a comprehensive policy to enhance typical and 
traditional agri-food products should focus not only on the protection of intellectual property 
rights, but it should also make Rural Development Policies more effective. When it comes to GIs 
enhancement, rural development should be more incisive, supporting more the whole food 
chain, in order to stimulate the entire GI’s production system. 
 
2. Clarify legal framework, while maintaining wine and spirit drink 
specificities, and increase efficiency of GI registration procedures 
to make GI legislation fit for purpose 
We strongly welcome any initiative that strives to simplify GI registration procedures and the GI 
legal framework in general. Indeed, Copa and Cogeca strongly encourage simplifying 
administrative procedures to a certain extent, as long as this benefits producers. For instance, 
we welcome the fact that the harmonisation of the scrutiny procedure (six month deadline) is to 
remain consistent. We believe that applying the same system to all products could speed up the 
process. The same is true for the opposition procedure. We believe that the same deadline 
should apply to all agricultural products. In general, Copa and Cogeca believe that all of these 
administrative procedures could be revised both at EU and national level in order to analyse the 
possibility of establishing a more efficient registration procedure at EU level. Furthermore, we 
welcome the proposal to allow the Commission to express its tacit consent to the amendment of 
specifications. Providing that these specifications do not call into question the founding 
principles of the designation itself, it must be possible for them to come into force straight away, 
under the remit of the national authorities in charge of interpreting and implementing EU law. 
If there is no opposition at EU level, the specifications should be considered valid unless the 
Commission proves that they do not comply with EU law, whether a complaint has been lodged 
or not. On the other hand, it is vital to preserve the specific characteristics of each sector. Rules

on the origin of agricultural products and their quality characteristics, which are currently 
defined according to each sector’s specificities and specifications, are of the utmost importance. 
3. Encourage GI producers to place on the market environmentally 
and socially sustainable GI products to meet societal demands and 
consumer expectations  
Farmers and agri-cooperatives believe that GI products possess an intrinsic ability to promote 
sustainability. By focusing on specific geographical areas, their terroir and their human factor, 
they help to enhance agricultural sustainability in all three of its aspects: environmental, 
economic, and social. Indeed, they guarantee economic sustainability by providing an 
interesting economic return for producers. This is because on average the price of a GI product 
is 2.11 times the price of a non-GI product equivalent. They are also socially sustainable because 
they boost activity in local rural areas. Finally, they are environmentally sustainable as they 
ensure the preservation of local landscapes and biodiversity through their specific criteria. These 
promote local species and varieties, which would otherwise disappear because of their low 
yields, as well as specific agricultural practices that preserve these local landscapes. Moreover, 
thanks to an interesting economic return, GIs foster small-scale types of production with a lower 
environmental impact. 
The three pillars of sustainability are interlinked in the GI system. They form a virtuous circle 
where the higher economic return of GIs allows producers to implement specific requirements 
and fosters the environmental and social sustainability of their production. Therefore, we 
believe that we could introduce new requirements in GI products that would further boost their 
environmental and social sustainability as long as the additional economic return necessary to 
support the cost of these new requirements is guaranteed. Economic sustainability is the 
prerequisite for social and environmental sustainability. If the latter is not guaranteed, 
producers might turn away from GIs and favour other types of production that do not allow for 
the same level of environmental and social sustainability. 
Thanks to its focus on local rural areas that encompasses both the human and environmental 
factors, the GI system is, by nature, a great tool to foster the environmental and social 
sustainability of agricultural production. Indeed, it guarantees the economic sustainability 
required to sustain environmental and social sustainability. 
4. Make the GI instrument more attractive to producer groups across 
the EU by empowering them to better manage their GI asset, 
including in enforcement, and so help them improving economic 
sustainability  
Copa and Cogeca welcome any measure that would empower producer groups and help them 
better manage their GI assets. Empowering them could also help to better distribute the GIs’ 
added value across the food chain and ensure that producers can secure a fair return for their 
products. However, we believe that producer groups should not have to bear the burden of 
enforcing GI protection, as this task should be carry out by the Member States’ authorities. 
Regarding the attractiveness of the GI instrument to the producers, we have observed a 
considerable discrepancy between the costs linked to GIs and the benefits for primary 
producers. This varies depending on volumes, market shares and the number of producers, as 
well as accreditation systems implemented by national accreditation bodies and the controls 
performed by national authorities to tackle food fraud. Therefore, as detailed in our comments 
under objective two, we believe that a simplification of the administrative procedure could really 
help to make GIs more attractive to producers. 
Within the Member States, EU quality schemes coexist with national and regional ones. 
Although they have some similar requirements, national and regional quality schemes include 
additional elements, e.g. more ambitious animal welfare or environmental standards, which are 
more aligned with consumer demands in their specific geographical region. In addition, the 
administrative burden of national and regional schemes is in some cases much easier to handle 
than that of the EU quality schemes. Moreover, these schemes are more readily recognised by

local consumers due to direct local interaction. Consequently, these national and regional 
schemes are sometimes more adapted to the producers’ need and we thus believe that they 
should be allowed to continue.  
However, in some cases, producers would still wish to benefit from GIs and TSGs, but applying 
for them is sometimes too costly for those regional and local food products produced in small 
and limited quantities. This is an issue that needs to be addressed to ensure that these products 
can also benefit from the same proper legal protection. 
 
5. Enable consumers to make informed choices on GIs by giving clear 
information on GIs through the logo and labelling information 
Specifications and visual logos related to EU quality schemes are not immediately recognisable 
to consumers and should therefore be easier to recognise. The EU and Member State authorities 
need to invest in additional resources to communicate the specificities of GIs and TSGs to 
consumers through awareness campaigns. We suggest including an explanatory note within or 
next to the GI/TSG logos that allows consumers to immediately understand its meaning. The 
wine sector is a notable exception to this. Some 65% of wine produced in Europe is protected 
under the GI framework, which has created broad awareness and appreciation among EU 
consumers. However, for food products and spirits, this is not the case and we thus welcome any 
initiative that could boost consumer awareness and understanding of GI and TSG logos. 
Providing clearer information through the logo is a positive step that we fully support. 
Nevertheless, this initiative should be supported by major awareness campaigns. While it is also 
important to underline the role of the Promotion Policy in helping to raise consumer awareness, 
this tool alone will not be sufficient. 
6. Review TSG scheme to better valorise traditional products and 
traditional production methods.  
In order to support the market positions regional and micro-regional farmers and food 
producers, as well as to make traditional products widely known, we recommend maintaining 
and strengthening the TSG category. To do so, TSG logos and terminology should be more self-
explanatory. If we want to make TSGs more attractive to producers, an important aspect is to 
ensure that consumers are aware of them and understand their qualities. If consumers know 
about them and their value vis-à-vis their expectations, then producers will see an incentive in 
applying for TSGs. As consumer awareness also depends on the visibility of TSGs in the 
supermarkets, it is clear that the promotion of TSGs to both consumers and producers should go 
hand in hand. 
Additional comments 
In addition to the objectives presented in the roadmap, Copa and Cogeca would like to comment 
on two additional points regarding the revision of the GI systems.  
The Farm to Fork Strategy plans to introduce a mandatory EU front-of-pack nutrition labelling 
system. As farmers, we welcome the opportunity to support a measure that improves consumers’ 
health and we believe that the high nutritional quality of the food we take pride in producing can 
contribute to this. However, we believe that the currently used colour-coded systems end up 
presenting an over-simplistic classification of food products differentiating between those that 
are “good”, in green, and those that are “bad”, in red. Such a dichotomy will stigmatise highly 
nutritious products that are praised for their nutritional value by nutritionists all over the world, 
such as olive oil. Those systems would also discriminate against many products with GIs. Such a 
system would be catastrophic for such products as the only solution for them not to be 
discriminated against and to benefit from a better “colour” would be reformulation. However, in 
order to benefit from GIs or other quality schemes, these products have to respect very strict 
criteria. This means that any reformulation of these products would be very complicated or

simply impossible. Therefore, we strongly encourage adopting a front-of-pack nutrition system 
that will not discriminate against GI products. 
We also would like to underline that we do not support extending the legal protection provided 
by Regulation No. 1151/2012 to non-agricultural products. In our opinion, protecting non-
agricultural products requires a different legal framework, whereas semi-agricultural products 
are already protected by the current legislative framework.

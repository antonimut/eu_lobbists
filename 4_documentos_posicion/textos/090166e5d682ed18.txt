COPA AND COGECA’S FEEDBACK ON THE 
ROADMAP  
“Protecting biodiversity: nature restoration targets 
under EU Biodiversity strategy” (Inception impact 
assessment) 
 
It is very important that any legislative initiative that arises from the objectives set in the 
Biodiversity Strategy for 2030 is accompanied by a thorough and convincing impact 
assessments, based on the latest and robust scientific data, to show that policy measures put in 
place are likely to be effective for biodiversity as well as facilitating viable rural areas in 
coherence with other policies and their objectives. This is in line with the recently agreed 
Council Conclusions1. Therefore, we welcome the Commission’s intention to prepare inception 
impact assessment on the EU nature restoration targets and the opportunity to comment on this 
roadmap. We agree that the focus should be on sustainable use of ecosystems/resources that 
equally recognises all three pillars of sustainability – environmental, social, and economic.  
In terms of the general context, besides pressures, the Commission should also consider and 
recognise:  
• Positive results that were achieved over time - e.g. over the short term between 2012 and 
2017, the grassland butterfly index has grown by 2.7%; the short-term trend for the 
common birds index has been more positive since 2010 and common farmland birds 
index remained stable during this period; gross nitrogen balance per hectare of utilised 
agriculture area for the EU decreased by 10 % from 2004-2015; the phosphorus surplus 
on EU agricultural land more than halved from 2004-2015; forested area of the EU is 
increasing etc. 
• Data limitations in assessing and interpreting the state of nature in the EU - e.g. majority 
of data on habitats and species is not based on complete surveys, while their or status of 
the water bodies is based on one-out-all-out principle which is masking the 
improvements made.  
• The fact that it is not only the pressure from the human activities that is having an 
impact on biodiversity, but also the lack of human intervention – in particular land  
abandonment (which is the main pressure on grasslands habitats, impacting in the same 
time pollinators), increased risk of forest fires and spread of invasive alien species, pests 
and diseases.

One of the main challenges and starting elements of the impact assessment will be to give an 
overview of definitions of restoration and restoration efforts. In this regard, it is crucial to 
understand that restoration is a process and not necessarily a final outcome and that any 
improvement or effort that moves an area towards a better condition should be regarded as a 
contribution to the restoration target. In addition, restoration should be looked as engaging and 
inclusive process with highly local focus.  
When it comes to the objectives and policy options, it is very important that the impact 
assessment assesses the current state of play – taking into account: 
• Ongoing and planned restoration efforts in all EU Member States, including both public 
and private. 
• Restoration efforts that are integral part of different EU legislation and polices – Birds 
and Habitats Directive, Water Framework Directive, Common Fisheries Policy, Common 
Agricultural Policy, Nitrates Directive, Flood protection and programmes etc. 
• Restoration actions under the LIFE, Interreg and Horizon programmes. 
Due to the above, the impact assessment must consider the possibility of developing an Action 
plan or other non-legal policy tool, instead of the legal instrument, that would coordinate these 
restoration efforts.  
The inception impact assessment will also have to explore the following constraints on the 
potential restoration targets and instruments: 
• How to choose/prioritise “what” should be restored? – e.g. results in the shortest 
amount of time, the lowest costs, voluntary commitments to avoid resistance of 
impacted stakeholders, prioritisation among ecosystems with higher biodiversity value, 
prioritisation among ecosystems with higher climate mitigation potential etc.  This 
choice will also influence the amount that can be restored when other resources are 
given e.g. financial.  
• Where the restoration could take place? – it is important that there is an effort sharing 
between the Member States, between different sectors, and between impacted 
stakeholders. Besides the choice of the ecosystems, the location of the restoration will 
also be impacted by the costs. If the costs are not included, this might result in unequal 
burden sharing among Member States. All sectors should contribute equally, and it is 
also crucial to respect the rights of private land and forest owners.   
• How much should be restored? – this will, besides the choice on what and where the 
restoration could take place, depend on the available resources - e.g. financial or capacity 
of the public administration in different Member States.  
• Another important aspect is acceptability of restoration activities by local communities 
and impacted stakeholders. Local communities usually object to restorations that are not 
voluntary and include e.g. the introduction of large predators. Therefore, the proper 
compensation mechanism must be ensured and voluntary approach to restoration based 
on incentives must be considered.  
• Uncertainties regarding the restoration process itself - because restoration deals with 
complex problems it is not surprising that uncertainties are nearly everywhere:  weather, 
socio-political conditions, climate change, and the uniqueness of the communities and 
ecosystems being restored. Many times these uncertainties (about ecosystem functions, 
species relationships, etc.) are not addressed, and the time-scales set out for restoration 
are unreasonably short, while other critical factors (e.g. social) are either ignored or 
abridged due to feasibility concerns. It must also be considered how these uncertainties

differ between active and passive restoration and whether the restoration actions will 
cause restraints on neighbouring areas. 
 
• Time constrains - restoration is situational and there is no single restoration formula, 
making each project very time-consuming. There is unlikely to be a generic set of 
recommendations that is applicable everywhere because actions need to be matched to 
the particulars of site and situation. In addition, it can take a long time before the 
conditions are reached for certain habitats of plants and animals to revive/return. 
 
All these constraints and questions need to be backed by robust scientific data and any data 
uncertainty must be clearly highlighted. The impact assessment must consider ecological, 
financial, and social constraints on what is realistic and possible when it comes to different 
policy tools.  
Furthermore, in the assessment of expected impacts, it is crucial that the following must be 
considered: 
• Economic impacts – depending on the goal of the restoration, the related costs can be 
very significant. It is therefore important to address who will be bearing the costs and 
how this might impact specific stakeholders (including the right to compensation and 
the potential loss of production capacity). It would be beneficial to have few concrete 
examples (case studies) addressing restoration of different ecosystems. In addition, 
there should be a careful approach on how biodiversity benefits are attributed to the 
restoration objectives chosen not because of their biodiversity value.  
• Administrative burden – it is clear that one of the main challenges is the data gap, which 
might entail higher administrative burden for the public authorities and stakeholders. 
Furthermore, as mentioned above, restoration is situational and there is no single 
restoration formula. Restoration plan, which worked in one area, cannot always be 
applied to another one with the same results expected. This might entail new 
administrative burden in preparation and approval of different restoration projects, 
their implementation and monitoring. Physical modifications to the sites might entail 
burden related to permits.   
• Impacts on fundamental rights - it is of utmost importance to analyse how fundamental 
rights (e.g. ownership rights) are affected by the actions suggested. If any actions against 
the will of land or forest owner would be necessary, the viewpoint of ownership right is 
crucial.

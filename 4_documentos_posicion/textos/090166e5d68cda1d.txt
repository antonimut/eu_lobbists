INHFA Biodiversity Submission 
Introduction 
The Irish Natura & Hill Farmers Association (INHFA) recognise that biodiversity loss is a major 
challenge that has an impact on everyone. In reviewing the EU Biodiversity Strategy launched in 
May 2020 this fact is also acknowledged however, we have major concerns relating to the 
overall strategy as it doesn’t address fundamental flaws in ongoing policy. 
Previous mistakes 
Europe’s first attempts to address biodiversity decline commenced in the early 1990s and led 
to the introduction of our Natura 2000 network which was put in place through the latter part 
of that decade. The aim (we understand) of this European wide network was to protect the 
land areas that were in good condition from a biodiversity perspective. Unfortunately, in doing 
so policymakers never sought to address the dramatic biodiversity decline outside of these 
areas. Looking forward our concern in Ireland is that this flawed policy is set to persist in the 
coming decade through the new Biodiversity Strategy. 
Future concerns 
At the core of the Biodiversity Strategy are proposals that not only maintains the Natura 2000 
network but aims to increase its overall area while also prioritize the new strictly protected 
designation model.  
Currently, we struggle to understand how persisting with this model is going to change the 
outcome in relation to biodiversity - and this outcome from an Irish perspective is quite 
damming.   
Recent reports from the National Parks & Wildlife Service (NPWS) which is the state body with 
responsibility for these sites detailed how 92% of our Natura sites have regressed or stayed 
static since their designation. In simple terms, we have seen improvements in only 8% of the 
listed site’s and while this model continues to fail in relation to biodiversity it has also failed our 
farmers. 
Significant resources allocated by the EU for Natura lands in the past have been redirected to 
other government programmes. Unfortunately, the Natura designation process has 
antagonized many farmers and made them suspicious of almost everything associated with the 
NPWS. This section of the Strategy specifies the percentage (%) of a country’s area requiring 
designation (30%) and “strict protection’’ (10%), without providing any rationale for these

Failure to engage with critical stakeholders 
The imposition of the now despised Natura designations on an estimated 35,000 Irish farmers 
has undermined their farming income, devalued their property, destroyed any opportunity to 
generate alternative income sources and imposed additional costs and burdens.  Through all of 
this the habitats that these farmers delivered in the first instance that were deemed worthy of 
protecting through the Natura designations regress – and the proposal in the Biodiversity 
Strategy is to double down on these designations.  
The people who know how to deliver on biodiversity (namely the 35,000 Irish farmers with the 
burden of these Natura designations) must be listened to on this occasion. If we don’t in ten 
years’ time the situation will be even more precarious than it is now. We must also engage 
positively with the additional 100,000 farmers to deliver improved biodiversity outcomes. In 
doing this we need to recognize how improving our biodiversity is an issue on all farms, land 
types and an issue for the State and the wider public 
Financial benefits 
In the Biodiversity Strategy reference is made to the Nature Fitness Check which valued the 
benefits of Europe’s Natura 2000 network at between €200-300 billion per year. For Ireland, 
this would equate to between €2-3 billion each year. However Irish farmers who manage over 
85% of Irelands Natura network only receive €6m each year for this service.  
A new way forward 
In acknowledging that most of these sites have regressed we need to reiterate that this is on 
the back of imposed restrictions through the 39 Actions Requiring Consent (ARC’S). Instead of 
delivering improved outcomes these ARC’s are responsible for the regression in these sites. 
This has resulted in farmers being restricted from carrying out necessary actions in a timely 
manner that will deliver much better outcomes for our biodiversity.  
Any new proposal or Strategy must be done in consultation with farmers. We need to recognize 
that the practices that were in place up to the imposition of the designations in most instances 
worked. These practices which were passed down for generations are understood by farmers,

Budgetary requirement’s and other considerations 
Instead of imposing more designations with added restrictions, there needs to be direct 
engagement with farmers and their representatives. We also need to recognise that there is a 
cost to farmers in terms or reduced output and in terms of management. Farmers just like 
everyone else need to be paid. This payment must reflect their effort and while they don’t 
expect to receive €2-3 billion there needs to be recognition that the six million currently being 
paid is quite insulting and definitely won't deliver the desired outcomes. 
In addition to a direct payment for the delivery of this service, guarantees will need to be 
provided on future Pillar 1 payments under the CAP Scheme. Farmers will also need assurances 
on these payments if they are forced to re-wet peatlands.  
We also need Member States to put in place a National Voluntary Land Acquisition Fund and an 
Agreement Strategy whereby any lands being considered must offer the landowner the 
opportunity to sell/dispose of these land at pre-designation values to the Member State who 
then takes on the management of the lands. 
Strictly Protected Designation 
Finally, in relation to the possible introduction in Ireland of a Strictly Protected Designation we 
have major concerns relating to its possible impact. This designation type is we understand a 
Category 1a designation type based on the International Union for Conservation of Nature 
(IUCN). When compared against the current Natura 2000 sites which are a category 4 
designations or even the Serengeti National Park in Central Africa this designation where 
applied will end farming or any human activity. This to us is totally unacceptable and will be 
resisted.

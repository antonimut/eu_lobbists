 
Introduction 
On 30 September, the European Commission published its much -anticipated report assessing the availability of SF6 
alternatives in switchgear, including MV secondary switchgear  (the “Report”) . This Report outlines the Commission’s 
current views on SF6 following its stakeholder consultation  and will serve as technical input to the Commission’s F -Gas 
Regulation review, scheduled for 2021.   
 
Eaton welcomes the Report as an important contribution to the available evidence on SF6 gas usage and the opportunity 
for replacing it in electrical switchgear.  The Report correctly signals the need for an early phase- out of SF6 (by 2025) and 
the need for policy intervention to ensure that the right incentives are put into place for a rapid uptake of SF6 alternatives.  
As the leading manufacturer of SF6-free switchgear, Eaton wishes to provide the following comments on certain aspects 
of the Report: 
 
➢ The Commission’s propose d phase-out timeline for MV  is correct, but we should not wait to act : The Report 
suggests th at replacing SF6 in new medium voltage (secondary and primary) switchgear is realistic after a brief 
transitional period of 2 -5 years. This would bring us to 2025. Although Eaton can support this timeline as  a positive 
regulatory signal, we believe that the Commission should not wait to encourage the uptake of SF6 alternatives 
already available on the market .  Given the amount of banked SF6 in the atmosphere and its global warming 
potential, time is of the essence. 
 
➢ The Commission is focused on a phase out by kV level (high/low, primary/secondary): The Report goes in the right 
direction by recognising the possibility for differentiation amongst technologies in the phase out. This differentiation 
between high voltage and medium voltage on the one hand, and different kV levels within each can help accelerate 
the phase out.  It avoids the pitfalls of having the Commission  putting all MV or HV segments in one basket and 
generalising to the most problematic switchgear as it did previously.   
 
➢ Commission acknowledges energy transition potential of SF6-free alternatives:  Importantly, the Report recognizes 
potential advantages of moving toward SF6-free solutions in view of the energy transition (e.g. vacuum breakers are 
more suitable for charging of electric vehicles) .  However, grid operators are seeking EU funding for grid expansion 
to accommodate increasing amounts of renewables.  R enewable applications entail more frequent switching in the 
distribution grid and hence more MV switchgear. The Commission must ensure that EU funds are not used for grid 
expansion accompanied by dirty SF6 switchgear as this will undermine the decarbonization objective of the energy 
transition.   
 
➢ The Report fails to assess the true costs of SF6 switchgear or suggest how to address this differential : The Report 
suggests a difference in costs between SF6 and SF6-free switchgear ranging from 5% to 30%. Our own experience is 
that SF6-free switchgear is on average 15-20% higher in cost that SF6 switchgear, but this difference is largely down 
to the scale of the volumes of units in production and increases in volume units of SF6-free switchgear will see this 
difference continue to narrow further in the future.  Moreover, these costs should not be compared on a product to 
product basis, but rather on a full life-cycle cost basis. SF6 switchgear is more costly if production, maintenance and 
disposal costs are considered, even without reference to environmental costs of its use. (We note the Report 
acknowledges that SF6 -free alternatives present less end -of-life management costs (i.e. recovery, recycling and 
disposal) compared to SF6 switchgear). One major reason more SF6-free switchgear is not in the market today is that 
tenders often require SF6 switchgear or make product cost the sole criterion for success.  The EU should incentivize 
use of SF6-free alternatives through Green Public Procurement.  
 
➢ Certain SF6-free alternatives are less costly than SF6 in terms of maintenance costs : The Commission states that 
SF6-free alternatives are more costly for maintenance as they mostly  use circuit breakers ( e.g., vacuum), whereas 
SF6 switchgear uses load break switches  with fuses  which are normally simpler, cheaper and maintenance free. 
Although the Commission considers this can be overcome, certain SF6-free technologies do exist on the market with 
lower maintenance costs than SF6 .  Consequently, this point should be further elaborated  and clarified by the 
Commission as this statement is incorrect and misleading.

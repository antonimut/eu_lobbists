Executive summary 
In this document Eaton provides the European Commission with additional information 
and context in response to the public consultation  on the F -gas regulation . More 
specifically, the focus of our response is on sulfur hexafluoride (SF 6) and its use in 
electrical switchgear.  
According to the United Nations’ Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), SF6 
tops the list of the most harmful GHG known as it is 23,500 times more potent than CO 2 
and stays in the atmosphere for 3,200 years. The F-Gas Regulation successfully banned 
the use of SF 6 where less harmful alternatives are available. However, the text, which  
entered into force in 2015, provided an exemption to the general ban for using SF 6 for 
insulation purposes in electrical switchgear because  at the time  it was  argued that 
alternative technologies were not ready for the market. 
Today, SF6-free technologies that are cost -effective, technically feasible, energy-efficient 
and reliable exist for the electrical sector. However, market uptake of these solutions is not 
as fast as could be expected. SF6-free solutions are rarely privileged in tenders and 
industry and utilities  overall appear to be unco ncerned that there is still a strong and 
expanding SF6 market. The current F-gas Regulation has arguably supported this status 
quo through an approach that seeks to contain and manage SF 6 gas instead of actively 
encouraging SF6-free alternatives. There is a real need for regulatory impetus here. 
In this context, Eaton welcomes the findings of the recent Commission’s report assessing 
SF6 alternatives in electrical switchgear2. The report provides compelling  evidence for the 
EU institutions to stop unnecessary climate-harmful emissions and  include a ban on the 
use of SF 6 gas in the electrical sector, thereby creating momentum for technology gains 
and giving clear impetus for the available SF6-free technologies to go mainstream. 
For the purpose of this public consultation, Eaton recommends that the Commission: 
• proposes a clear and ambitious regulatory framework with a ban on SF6 use in 
electrical switchgear as decisive and rapid action is needed in this area to achieve 
climate neutrality by 2050; 
• supports an immediate ban for SF6 use in new switchgear up to 36kV, plus early 
phase-out (2-5 years from now) for higher levels up to 145kV, as supported by the 
recent Commission’s report; 
• implements a full y-fledged tracking and tracing system from production and/or 
import to minimise the impact of operation and end-of-life leakages of SF6.  
• stimulates the market demand for SF6 -free solutions through demand incentives 
in Green Public Procurement; and, 
• implements financial incentives (e.g. subsidies, tax rebates)  to encourage 
replacement / retrofits prior to end of life.  
 
The above recommendations are aimed at ensuring the regulatory framework on F-gases, 
and particularly on SF 6, is fit for purpose. A decisive and rapid EU action in this area can 
provide an early and easy win to the EU in its efforts to reduce harmful emissions and 
attain its climate objectives under the European Green Deal and the Paris Agreement.

General comments on aligning F-Gas Regulation with EU’s Green Deal ambition   
1. Action on SF6 is an early and easy win for the Green Deal – we support the 
European Commission's timeline for a phaseout of SF6 in M edium Voltage 
(MV) switchgear in 2-5 years 
We believe that i mplementing an early phase out in the review of the F -gas 
regulation is an ea sy win to help EU meet Green Deal objectives.  The F -Gas 
regulation sent already a strong signal in 2015 to SF6 producers to move toward 
eco-friendly switchgear, and the EU must be mindful  this time of not rewarding 
industry with further delay for their fai lures to heed that warning.  The F -Gas 
Regulation revision must send a clear signal to the market to close the 
remaining loopholes in EU legislation and ban harmful SF6. 
Most m anufacturers of SF6 understand an early phase out is coming and are 
ready for it. However, grid operators seem to be slowing the process by continuing 
business as usual, making the same arguments they made during the last F -Gas 
Regulation revision . In this  context, the Commission needs to  intervene and  
mandate an ambitious  phase out so that manufacturers and grid operators 
accelerate progress against SF6.  Although Eaton can support Commission’s 
proposed phase -out timeline of SF6 in MV (2 -5 years) as a posit ive 
regulatory signal, we believe that the Commission should not wait to 
encourage the uptake of SF6 alternatives already available on the market.   
The only way to achieve an SF6 emission reduction pathway consistent with the 
Green Deal is setting an immediate ban for SF6 use in new switchgear up to 36kV, 
plus an early phase-out for higher levels ( 52 kV), then High Voltage (H V). The 
Commission should also require a progressive phase out of existing SF6 units, 
which have a lifetime of 60 years, to achieve a complete substitution by 2050 and 
achieve the EU’s Green Deal objectives. If necessary, early phaseout timelines 
could be differentiated for MV/HV, as SF6-free solutions for MV are more mature.  
2. The EU cannot achieve climate neutrality without addressing SF6. 
According to recent data released by the European Environment Agency (EEA), 
SF6 production rose  by 6 %  in 2019 compared with 2018 3. This confirms other 
forecasts where the EU SF6 market is projected to grow by 4.9% per year until 
2027, mostly because of electronics, energy, and power industr y demand4. 
Existing electrical growth trends involving SF6 result in increased GHG emissions. 
Driven by the decentralisation of energy generation and storage, the number of 
MV switchgear units is expected to increase by 90% by 2050 5, with higher and 
higher GHG emissions. Banked SF6 emissions are already as high as 196 million 
t CO2e, equating to 20% of annual G HG emissions from the EU power sector 6. 
According to a recent study, allowing the installations of new SF6 units after 2025 
will lead to a 42% increase in the banked emissions by 2050.  If the EU aims to 
achieve climate neutrality by 2050, it needs to act decisively now and 
prohibit SF6 in new MV switchgear while instituting an aggressive 
retrofitting of old switchgear wherever possible.

Moreover, we are witnessing how EU grid operators are seeking significant 
funding for grid expansion to support renewables and decarbonization , which will 
lead to an acceleration in the installation of new switchgear units (estimates 
indicate around 160 thousand additional units will be installed per year). If this new

purposes of climate neutrality cannot be undercut by the expanded use of 
dirty SF6. EU Green Deal funds should not be used for network extension 
that involves dirty SF6 switchgear. 
Specific comments on how the EU can move forward with SF6-free 
3. Alternatives to SF6 exist today that are cost-effective, technically feasible, 
energy-efficient and reliable -- And importantly they also support EU Green 
Deal climate neutrality targets 
 
Early bans on SF6 in MV switchgear are possible because there are no technical 
barriers for using SF6 free switchgear - the technology is mature 7. SF6-free 
switchgear is a proven technology and has been on the market for more than 60 
years, and with some  reasonable extra investment, can become technically 
feasible for nearly all MV applications within 2 to 5 years 8.  
 
While SF6-free alternatives are presently slightly more costly (circa 20%) than SF6 
switchgear on a product to product comparison, they are significantly cheaper on 
a life -cycle basis when maintenance and disposal are taken into account.  
Moreover,  cost-effectiveness is also a function of economies of scale – the more 
SF6-free product  enters the market , the less expensive they will become . 
Therefore, we need a strong and ambitious phaseout timeline s for SF6 use 
in switchgear, which will favourably impact current cost margins and 
facilitate a wider SF6-free uptake.  
 
4. Gas leakage is underreported and will entail new safety concerns in an 
extended network 
 
The Kyoto Protocol lists SF6 as an extremely harmful GHG . No amount of SF6 
should be  necessary or acceptable . Industry often cites its own standard 9 to 
suggest a ‘de minimus’ leakage rate of 0.1% per year during operation. We should 
be c lear that no amount of leakage is de minimus. Moreover, this often-cited 
leakage rate refers to state -of-the-art equipment and the majority of units in the 
market are not state of the art 10. The more active the grid becomes, the greater 
the use of SF6 and the greater the potential for SF6 gas leakage, which can occur 
throughout production, installation, use, maintenance, and disposal. Moreover, 
regulations regarding end-of-life handling are in place but the quality of the actual 
implementation is uncert ain since switchgear producers, users and recyclers 
provide no public information on the scale of leaks.  
 
We estimate that the rate of SF6 switchgear leakage is at least 15% of the fill 
amount over the life of the switchgear. Regulators must consider leakage over the

be possible for qualified maintenance professionals to be present and responsible

Recent estimates indicate that high quality lifetime management  alone, reducing 
end-of-life leakages from 10% to 5%, could reduce the accumulated emissions by 
40%11. Manufacturers of SF6 gas must be held responsible for gas leakages 
throughout the value chain based on polluter -pays-principle. However, the 
development and adoption of such monitoring and reporting system should by no 
means delay the adoption of a clear timeline for a ban on SF6 in both medium -
voltage and high -voltage switchgear.  The monitoring and reporting system 
should be developed and adopted independently from the ban on the use of 
SF6 in both medium-voltage and high-voltage switchgear. It should amplify 
and complement EU policy action, not become a precondition for it.  
 
5. SF6-free alternatives should be incentivized through Green Public 
Procurement 
 
As of today, very few tenders for switchgear require SF6 -free equipment or 
incentive their use. In fact, most tenders support the use of SF6 by either requiring 
it outright or by making price the sole criterion . In the limited cases where SF 6-
free switchgear is incentivised or placed on a level playing field, it has been very 
successful.  
 
To make progress against SF6, the EU should ensure that tenders for new -
- and where possible retrofit -- switchgear incentivise the use of SF6-free 
products under green public procurement. Moreover, the EU should explore 
promoting the use of financial incentives (e.g. subsidies, tax rebates) to 
encourage replacement / retrofits prior to end of life as evidence shows it is 
necessary to achieve climate neutrality by 2050.

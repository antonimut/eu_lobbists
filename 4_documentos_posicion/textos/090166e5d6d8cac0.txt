Insolvency law - Inception Impact 
Assessments - Comments of the European 
Banking Federation 
 
 
EBF comments:  
 
Goal of harmonisation: 
The focus of harmonisation of insolvency laws in Europe should be to achieve the goal of 
insolvency law better. As we see in general, this goal is to contribute to, or at least not 
detract from, the greater economic p rocess, although we readily note that this is not the 
only goal of insolvency law. Important (sub) goals are usually grounded in notions of 
(distributive) justice held within different member states, such as rights of workers, the 
environment etcetera. Not surprisingly, these sub-goals may vary from member state to 
member state. 
 
Suppose we concentrate on insolvency law as a means to achieve an economic outcome. 
In that case, we note that the overarching goal of harmonising insolvency law should be 
to promote law that achieves this outcome. In doing so, a benchmark analysis to assess 
which insolvency regimes in Europe best achieve this outcome should be the starting point 
of the harmonisation effort. Furthermore, it should be noted that insolvency law always  
functions within the broader framework of hard and soft law, institutions etcetera. 
Insolvency law and this more comprehensive framework provide incentives to parties to 
act in certain ways. The totality of insolvency law and this more comprehensive framework 
then results in different outcomes for different member states. The EBA has recently issued 
its report on the benchmarking of national enforcement frameworks. 1 This report shows 
that there are important variations between the member states concerning  recovery rate 
of debt, time to recovery of debt and judicial costs of recovery. Further research is required 
to understand, which drives the differences in the relevant jurisdictions. However, this

It is clear from this report that the broader impact of changes to insolvency is in orders of

Benchmark 
The benchmark for harmonisation should be those EU jurisdictions which have a better 
recovery rate, shorter time to recovery of the debt and lower judicial costs of recovery and 
other economic parameters, such as the degree insolvency law prevents parties from going 
bankrupt in the first place. In addition, as insolvency laws function in a broader institutional 
framework, any harmonisation sh ould still allow member states to adopt a solution that 
fits within their broader framework. Moreover, member states should also have the option 
to adopt (non -economic) (sub)goals of insolvency law. Finally, the  insolvency law of 
member states should be predictable so that the different implications can be more easily 
foreseen ex ante. 
 
In general, we feel that for the moment the best way of harmonising different member 
states’ insolvency law would be to adopt principles and recommendations (best practices) 
to help member states improve their insolvency law. 
 
Suitable subjects 
Given the differences in the legal system over the EU, we suggest a focused approach.  
 
The impact assessment identifies several topics for harmonisation. Our preliminary 
conclusion, but further research is required, is that of the issues mentioned the following 
seem to be appropriate to be included: 
- conditions for determining avoidance actions and effects of claw-back rights; 
- directors’ duties related to handling imminent/actual insolvency proceedings; 
- court capacity when it comes to expertise and necessary training of judges; and  
- asset tracing which would be relevant, in particular in the context of avoidance 
actions. 
 
As an additional and related topic, we list the following topics: 
- the prerequisites for when insolvency proceedings should be commenced (including 
a definition of insolvency and provisions on who is entitled to file for insolvency) ; 
- the incentives insolvency law provides to the Insolvency Practitioner, i.e. the person 
central to most insolvencies. What oversight does insolvency law impose on this 
person and what other incentives does he or she have, for example, from the way 
he is remunerated. 
 
We feel that these topics are all appropriate topics because they mos tly affect the 
behaviour of parties in the period shortly before any insolvency proceedings will be opened 
and of course the behaviour of parties during the insolvency. In addition, in this area of 
insolvency law, in general, the influence of (non -economic) notions of distributive justice 
is relatively small. 
 
Distributive justice goals should not be harmonised 
Finally, the impact assessment identifies the position of secured creditors in insolvency, 
taking into account specific needs for the protection of other creditors (e.g. employees, 
suppliers). The national laws on security interests and the preferential rights enjoyed by 
some creditors differ widely. It will be difficult to align those different positions. Also 
because these different ru les are underpinned by differing notions on distributive justice

Conclusion 
In any event, any attempt to align should once again be aimed to ensure that the 
insolvency laws drives better (economic) outcome in terms parameters such as recovery 
rate, shorter time to recovery of the debt, lo wer judicial costs of recovery etcetera. In 
doing so, member states should have the flexibility to ensure that insolvency law aligns 
with the broader framework of a member state and (noneconomic) goals of specific 
member states. This may best be achieved by promoting principles and guidelines to allow 
member states to identify best practices and harmonise their insolvency law accordingly . 
 
Following the above,  we consider that non-legislative measures should be preferred 
(comparisons between the insolvency frameworks of different member states, drafting 
best practices, Commission recommendations). Inefficiency of insolvency systems is 
sometimes explained by a (dis)function of a wider part of a particular legal system, in 
which case a harmonization of norms w ill not produce the desired result. In addition, the 
directive on restructuring and second chance is yet transposed in many member states 
and its impact on the creation of the CMU should be assessed more thoroughly before 
additional binding regulation is put forward.

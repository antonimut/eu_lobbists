ESHRE’s feedback to Inception Impact Assessment 
on the Revision of the Union legislation on blood, tissues and cells 
11 December 2020 
As the world’s leading professional society in human reproduction, the European Society of Human 
Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) welcomes the initiative of the EU decision-makers to revise 
the Union legislation on blood, tissues and cells (BTC).  
A revision of  the European  Directive 2004/23/EC on  safety and quality of human  tissues and cells 
(EUTCD) was very much needed because the current provisions do not address important aspects 
related to the use of tissues and cells for medically assisted reproduction (MAR).  
We take this opportunity to highlight some of the shortcomings identified over the years by scientists, 
medical doctors, researchers, and specialists working in the field of reproductive medicine: 
• The result of assisted reproduction technology (ART) treatments is the birth of new human beings, 
a major specificity that is not covered in the current legislation. In consequence, the activity in this 
sector is inadequately analysed compared to somatic cell/tissue replacement.  In particular, the 
safety and health of increasing numbers of individuals who are born thanks to these technologies 
must be considered.   
• The implications of having the same classification for “partner donor” and “non -partner donor” 
are not being addressed in most of the legislative texts. In the current legislation on blood, tissues 
and cells, assisted reproduction using own gametes from  a man and a woman who are in a 
relationship as a couple is still considered a treatment between a “donor” and a “recipient”, thus 
excluding any difference in terminology as compared to non -partner (or “third-party”) gamete 
donation. This often leads to confusion in discussions with authorities and other stakeholders 
triggering inappropriate requests during inspections of n ational authorities, such as unnecessary 
testing. Although it is important to include in the directive(s) the handling of partners’ own tissues 
and cells, a differentiated terminology would facilitate a more accurate and clear understanding 
in communication with authorities in the medically assisted reproduction sector.  
• Innovative technologies are often implemented in medically assisted reproduction without having 
sufficient validation in terms of efficacy and safety for patients and offspring. Valuable data and 
information are often missing at the time of implementation. Some of these new technologies are 
also charged to patients as “add-ons” to fertility treatments, even if their safety and efficacy have 
not been proven (at the time of treatment). 
• On the other hand, over-regulation regarding new technologies can also hinder innovation in the 
medically assisted reproduction field, possibly leading to increased dependence on less validated 
technology from areas with less stringent regulations. 
• New reagents (e.g. culture media) are placed on the market while the information about the exact 
content remains undisclosed to users, which precludes optimal evaluation of treatments.

• A strong recommendation for national robust and harmonized data collection, which can be 
shared between countries, is missing. Access to epidemiological information derived from 
improved and harmonized registers is urgently needed.  
• Furthermore, the absence of a European registry in the context of cross-border care presents risks 
of bypassing quality and safety measures, as well as increased health risks for women and a future 
child. The possibility of having a correct tracking system to enable follow-up of these treatments 
is thus urgently needed.  
Problem the initiative aims to tackle 
 
1. Patients are not fully protected from avoidable risks 
Comments:  
One aspect on which the new EU legislation should put more emphasis is creating conditions and 
requirements for all Member states to have good registers of medically assisted reproduction  
procedures. The registers would enable regular evaluation through which safe and efficient practices 
can be identified and kept while the inefficient practices can be identified and discarded.  
The evaluation of data and the decision powers regarding measures should remain in the 
responsibility of scientists  / medical doctors  / health professionals  / patients, both through 
societies/associations (such as ESHRE) and professional boards (such as EBCOG  – European Board & 
College of Obstetrics and Gynaecology ). All these actors are best placed to make a n informed and 
accurate evaluation of the available data. 
 
2. Divergent approaches to oversight cause unequal levels of safety and quality and barriers to the 
exchange of BTC across the EU 
Comments:  
Divergent national interpretations and implementations of the legislation can lead to unequal 
protection and a lack of  mutual trust between national authorities. However, most cross-border 
problems within the MAR sector do not seem to derive from “divergent national interpretations and 
implementations of the legislation”, but rather from: 
 1. diverse prohibition/access to  different assisted reproductive technologies (ART)  procedures (for 
example access to donor gametes, age limits,  access to innovative technologies , number of allowed 
ART attempts, etc) in different countries;  
 2. diverse prices of the different ART procedures in different countries;  
 3. diverse marketing powers of the different ART centres in different countries.

These are mostly national options and may have nothing to do with current or future directives. Simply 
strengthening the enforcement of the directives will not help in solving those specific problems.  
Different national legislation , access and reimbursement systems across the EU countries lead to 
cross-border travelling of both patients and cells, with possible lack of quality and safety measures, as 
well as health risks for women and future offspring.  
A European registry that includes cross-border movements of both biological material (gametes, 
embryos) as well as  of patients would have the potential to monitor those movements , to perform 
follow up analyses and thereby  increase quality and safety . An establishment of such a registry is 
therefore very much needed and should be foreseen in the new, revised legislation.  
As compared to the current EUTCD, the new legislation should seek to  improve and clarify the 
formulation of the provisions to minimise the risk of having different interpretation at Member States 
level due to inaccurate or unclear provisions. 
 
3. Avoidable risks for BTC donors and for children born from donated eggs, sperm or embryos 
Comments:  
In principle, we agree with the problem as described in the Inception Impact Assessment document . 
However, introducing legislative requirements for  genetic testing for third-party donors involves  a  
risk that requirements become outdated in a short time. Furthermore, ethical issues will be involved, 
regarding e.g. extended genetic testing and disclosure of results to the donors . In addition,  an 
unnecessarily extended genetic testing would increase tr eatment costs and r educe availability of 
treatment. 
We therefore consider that genetic testing requirements as per the revised EUTCD should be  
maintained at minimal levels, and all more specific requirements should be left to be defined by 
scientists / medical doctors / health professionals / patients, through both societies/associations (such 
as ESHRE) and professional boards (such as EBCOG) , allowing timely updates taking into account 
evolutions and knowledge in the field 
Overall, we recommend a careful consensus regarding genetic testing. In addition, specific scientific 
and technical aspects should be left in the responsibility of professional associations. 
Furthermore, to identify avoidable risks for BTC third-party donors and for children born from donated 
eggs, sperm or embryos, there is a need for a prospective data collection system on third-party donors 
that avoids underreporting of donor adver se reactions for oocyte donors  as well as pathology in 
offspring.

4. BTC legislation lags behind innovation 
Comments:  
Concerning our views about regulating new technologies and the concept of ‘donation’ we invite you

5. EU vulnerable to interruptions in supply of some BTC 
Comments:  
Although this may be the case in many areas of somatic tissue/cell transplantation, this has not been 
identified as problem in the Human Reproduction area in Europe. Nevertheless, the impact of the 
issue of donor anonymity for the supply in Europe of gametes (sperm or oocytes) used in ART need s 
to be addressed.  
As regards the guidance and recommendations of ECDC, we propose that these remain non-binding. 
They should continue to be followed because of ECDC’s recognition as a highly competent and reliable 
European body, and not because of legal allowances or prohibitions.  
Objectives and Policy options 
The overall objectives of this initiative are to ensure a high level of health protection for EU citizens. 
The EU legal framework should therefore:  
1. Ensure safety and quality  for patients treated with BTC therapies, for donors and for 
children born from in vitro fertilization, and enforcement of safety and quality requirements.   
2. Optimize access to, and avoid shortages of BTC therapies.   
3. Ensure the framework is future-proof and facilitates the development of innovative BTC 
therapies.  
Comments:  
We suggest that under point 1 the term ‘in vitro fertilization’ is replaced by ‘ medically assisted 
reproduction’.  
Policy option 1: Strengthened quality and safety requirements defined by blood and tissue 
establishments with strengthened national inspection, EU audits and classification advice   
Comments:  
With regards to strengthening of national inspections  derived f rom adoption of option 1 , we 
recommend that the new directive includes clearly defined criteria for the selection of inspectors, 
based on their level of competence  within the field . The documentation in the field of assisted

reproduction has many specificities and there is a risk to exclude the real experts from ‘inspector roles’ 
by not having selection criteria  defined in a clear manner . Furthermore, Member States would need 
to increase education, resources and financial support to both competent authorities and inspectorate 
bodies, desirably through a European-based supporting mechanism that is currently non-existent. 
Concerning the emergency supply responses and the granting of prior authorisations, these do not 
apply to our field. 
With regard s to the competent authorities  granting of prior authorization based on data 
demonstrating safety and benefit for patients  in case of  major changes in the steps of collection, 
processing and use of BTC, we consider the measure to be unrealistic and may even have a negative 
impact on innovation. For instance, if the i nnovative technologies/therapies cannot be validated by 
the end-users, no data will be available for assessment by the competent authorities.  
Policy option 2: EU -level safety and quality requirements defined by European Expert Bodies and 
strengthened national inspection, EU audits and classification advice   
Comments: 
This policy option appears unrealistic since if innovative technologies/therapies cannot be validated 
by the end-users, data will not be available for assessment by the European Expert Bodies. This may 
even discourage efforts made for innovation; which would be a highly negative development . In 
addition, it will most likely not allow accurate assessment  of innovative technology , but instead 
presents the risk of raising barriers to innovation. 
Furthermore, giving more authority to bodies such as ECDC and EDQM is a potential risk given the 
constitution of these bodies, namely the possible lack or shortage of ART experts in these groups. In 
addition, efforts should be made to ensure that experts from professional societies and other medical 
bodies are included members of the European Expert Bodies like EDQM and not only being considered 
as stakeholders. 
Concerning the introduction of a framework for joint compliance inspections (by two or more Member 
States), the basis for ‘where appropriate’ should be clarified. Specificities of the ART field should be 
considered, and outmost importance should be given to  specific education of the inspectors in this 
field. Furthermore, this option 2 would greatly increase the need for a European-based supporting 
mechanism (educational, financial, and logistical) to increase education, resources, and financial 
support to Member States, namely for competent authorities and inspectorate bodies, to comply with 
the expected requirements. We emphasise that this mechanism is highly desirable but currently non-
existent.

Policy option 3:  EU -level safety and quality requirements laid down in the BTC legislation with 
improved national inspections systems and classification advice   
Comments: 
Aiming to define common binding technical rules and specifications at the European level  seems too 
complex and almost impossible, except for emergency situations. There would be again the risk of a 
legislation that is not fit for keeping the pace with technical innovation.   
In our field, a more adequate policy option would be a combination of the above 3 options, including 
basic technical rules in EU legislation , combined with an increase in the education, resources and 
financial support to Member States, namely to competent authorities and inspectorate bodies, 
supported by European programmes (educational, financial, and logistic).  
In short, we are in favour of general binding rules  to be required by EU legislation and more detailed 
specifications to be defined at the level of European Expert Bodies, ensuring that the composition of 
these bodies is done in a transparent manner and that a competence-based selection of experts takes 
place. In the selection process, experts from professional societies and other medical bodies should 
be included.   
Preliminary Assessment of Expected Impacts 
Likely social impacts 
Comments:  
We recommend that the update of the technical requiremen ts for third-party donors’ testing and 
eligibility should be carefully assessed. If the requirements are too complex, there will be a risk of 
shortage in donors.  
We also recommend an overview of the terms “donor” and “recipient” for couples using their ow n 
gametes versus couples using gametes from a non-partner (third-party) donor.  
As regards the expected increase in health protection of certain population groups, this cannot be 
achieved only by a combination of the  proposed policy options. Harmonised data collection and 
adequate and complete registries must be put in place in all Member States and coordinated through 
a European registry. Only after Member States share d ata, can meaningful follow-up assessment s 
allow for new rules and achievement of  a real increase in health protection of certain population 
groups, namely children born from assisted reproduction treatments.  
The risk of diverging in safety and quality rules and specifications can be limited by combining policy 
options (see previous comments).

About ESHRE 
ESHRE is the world’s leading society in reproductive science and medicine founded in 1985. Our 
members are scientific and medical professionals with a special interest in and focus on human 
reproduction. The core mission of our society is to help couples w hich struggle with fertility issues to 
have children through assisted reproduction. Infertility has become an increasing area of concern 
throughout Europe.  
Today, ESHRE counts more than 10 000 members from all over the world, with almost 60% from within 
Europe. Every year, we organise the worlds´ largest scientific meeting within this discipline (now over 
12 000 participants) as well as 15-20 workshops dealing with all aspects in the field. More background 
information can be found on our website: https://www.eshre.eu/en.

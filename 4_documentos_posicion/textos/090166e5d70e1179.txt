Revision of the Union legislation on blood, tissues and cells 
 
Feedback on the Inception Impact Assessment 
 
14 December 2020 
 
MedTech Europe - the European trade association for the medical technology industry including diagnostics, 
medical devices and digital health - welcomes the opportunity to contribute to the initial consultation regarding 
the revision of the Union legislation on blood, tissues and cells.  
Donated blood and their components have the potential to save lives and enable many aspects of healthcare, 
including in the fields of trauma, cancer, dialysis, inherited chronic haematological disorders, etc.  
Medical technologies - i.e., medical devices and in vitro diagnostic medical devices, which are in compliance 
with Regulation (EU) 2017/745 and Regulation (EU) 2017/746, respectively - are critical to the collection, 
testing, processing, transport, storage, and administration to patients, of blood, tissues, and cells products  
as well as to ensure pathogen reduction. 
MedTech Europe would like to register as an interested stakeholder to contribute to the upcoming stage of 
the public consultation. 
 
Introduction 
 
In principle, MedTech Europe supports the broad objective concerning the Revision of the Union legislation 
on blood, tissues and cells to ensure a high level of health protection for EU citizens. 
Medical devices and diagnostics are used to save people ’s lives, to support the diagnosis and treatment of 
diseases, and to keep people in good health. The rules for guaranteeing the safety and performance of 
devices have been r ecently overhauled by the adoption of Regulation  (EU) 2017/745 on medical devices 
(MDR) and Regulation (EU) 2017/746 on in vitro diagnostic medical devices (IVDR). 
 
General remarks 
 
As recognised in the Inception Impact Assessment (IIA) subject to this consultation, “the BTC legislation lags 
behind innovation”. Since 2002 and 2004, there has been significant innovation in the sector, especially in 
the areas of blood  testing, blood com ponent and cell processing , as well as  in blood collection, which is 
currently not reflected in the Directive 2002/98/EC on safety and quality of human blood and blood 
components, and in the Directive 2004/23/EC on safety and quality of human tissues and c ells ( BTC 
legislation).  
The development and uptake of new processes and technological advancements must be supported by the 
EU legal framework, for example by addressing the current variability of testing requirements for a safe blood

supply. For example,  donated blood can expose recipients (patients) to life threatening infections. It is 
therefore vital that blood is screened for various pathogens. Antibody screening is effective at identifying 
long-established infections but can miss newly acquired infections. To avoid this, more advanced techniques, 
such as Nucleic Acids Testing (NAT) technology are available, which have the advantage to reduce the 
‘window period’, meaning the time from when a person gets infected to the time when the infection will be 
detected. 
Furthermore, t he lack  of harmonisation in the implementation of the BTC legislation has raised serious 
concerns during the recent evaluation. Experience has shown that the existing pieces of legislation do not 
provide a basis for appropriate harmonisation. In addition, the coexistence of various bodies involved in the 
drafting of provisions on blood safety (e.g., national legislation, EU legislation, publications from the Council 
of Europe, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and C ontrol (ECDC) or World Health Organisation) 
further contributes to a fragmented implementation of the EU legislation. Therefore, the obligation for  
establishments to consider technical rules and specifications that will be defined and updated by authoritative 
bodies is perceived as a positive development.  In particular, t he medical devices technology industry 
supports: 
• harmonisation in the implementation of the BTC legislation; and 
• harmonisation of decision criteria for clear identification of which pathoge ns are mandatory to 
screen for, including: 
o how? - including the performance requirements for the tests; 
o when and why? - e.g., seasonal triggers, public health emergency triggers; and 
o who? -  to ensure clear accountability. 
Lastly, it is important to recognise the EU’s supply chain difficulties to continually meet the needed quantities 
of BTC products, e.g., plasma. Therefore, MedTech Europe encourages regulators to play a more active role 
in addressing this challenge. 
 
Analysis of the policy options 
Policy option 1 
Policy option 1 does not seem to sufficiently address the objectives for revising the legislation. Efforts toward 
harmonisation will be difficult and, at best, protracted under this scheme. The timeliness of incorporating new 
risks or new solutions into regulations is highly dependent on leadership and resources available at the 
country level, leaving gaps in quality and safety, as well as the willingness to adopt innovative technologies.  
Contrary to the European Commission’s assessment, Policy option 1 does not appear to promote innovation, 
as great disparities in the level of innovation can be seen across the region. Additionally, from a 
manufacturer’s standpoint, having differing technical specifications acr oss countries can lead to extensive 
development work, which can be a further deterrent to innovation.

Policy option 2 
Policy option 2 as outlined in the IIA would help protect innovation in the EU while contributing to harmonising 
the requirements and stan dardising BTC processes and products. Although the ECDC is an agency of the 
EU, questions should be raised regarding the legitimacy of the Council of Europe in defining technical rules 
and specifications for EU Member States considering its geographical scope goes beyond the EU. The proper 
enforcement of the guidance documents issued by ECDC and/or European Directorate for the Quality of 
Medicines & HealthCare (EDQM) should also be ensured. 
ECDC and EDQM are highly regarded organi sations with experts continually involved in horizon scanning, 
situation analysis and risk assessment . Harnessing these endeavours to directly impact the legislation and 
ultimately the care provided to individual patients across the continent is prudent and practical. While Policy 
option 2 strikes a very nice balance between harmonisation, incorporation of innovative technologies and 
rapid responsiveness to a changing environment , it still  needs sufficient mandates (presumably in EU 
legislation) in order to ensure proper enforcement of such a model. 
 
Policy option 3 
Policy option 3 would be preferable to Policy option 1, but as the mechanism for evaluating and adopting 
changes to legislation are less clear, it is difficult to comment fully. This option will presumably be less efficient 
than Policy option 2, as an additional group of individuals will need to evaluate recommendations set out by 
ECDC and EDQM before drafting changes. Ideally, harmonisation will be benefitted under this scheme, and 
likely innovation will also be prom oted, but strong responsiveness to a dynamic industry may be difficult to 
achieve. Traditionally, modifications at this level of government have not been able to keep the dynamic pace 
of innovation. 
 
Conclusion 
 
Blood safety should remain at the heart of any future pieces of legislation governing the donation, collection  
and testing of blood and blood components. MedTech Europe understands the expert support provided by 
the ECDC as crucial in achieving this objective given that, as mentioned in the evaluation report, they address 
the risk of transmission of communicable disease by substances of human origin and their advice and 
guidance is widely used by BTC professionals and authorities. 
In any new legislation, a public comment period and opportunity for s takeholder engagement is paramount 
to ensuring objectivity and accountability of regulators.  The final legislative proposal requires an especially 
careful construct of enforcement possibilities versus flexibility to quickly update guidance documents, 
technical rules, and specifications in line with technological advancement. 
MedTech Europe is fully ready to engage with stakeholders in further discussions to develop and implement 
an effective legislative proposal.

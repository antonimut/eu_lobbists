The German confectionery industry has a strong interest in handling the required 
resources in a forward-looking and efficient manner. Packaging material has been 
continuously reduced by the manufacturers for a number of years.  The 
continuous improvement in the material efficiency of packaging saves resources 
and CO2 emissions. 
The key focus is on sourcing sustainable raw materials for confectionery production 
itself, such as sustainably produced cocoa;  but sustainable production and the use 
of sustainable packaging is also of key importance.  Hence the confectionery sector 
is constantly deliberating sus tainable ways of handling water, energy, waste, and 
logistics, and is thus also focussed on the eco-friendliness of the packaging used and 
the conservation of resources.  Since the introduction of the German Packaging 
Ordinance in 1991, German confectionery  manufacturers have been continuously 
reducing the amount of packaging material used.  Plastic packaging has become 
ever lighter since then.  As a result of technical progress in the manufacturing 
sector, the use of plastic packaging is now 35% lower than wh at counted as state -
of-the-art technology in 1991.  The German Association for Packaging Research 
(GVM) has calculated: if Germany had stayed with the packaging of 1991, instead of 
going on to use state-of-the-art plastic packaging in 2013, then 
- this would have generated 955 kilo tonnes more plastic 
and 
- this would have resulted in approx. 2.6 (2.5-2.8) million tonnes more of CO 2 
emissions. 
 
Germany is a pioneer in the field of recycling and also that of plastic packaging.  The 
recycling rates Germany has achi eved, i.e. the share of recycled packaging in the 
overall volume collected, are unparalleled worldwide.  According to the European 
Waste Framework Directive, at least 50% of packaging waste from households or, if 
applicable, other household-like sources mus t be recycled. This target was already 
exceeded in 2010, when the recycling rate reached 85%, and this trend has 
continued over the last few years.  In addition, all BDSI member manufacturers 
participate in one of Germany’s Dual Systems (DSD) for waste avo idance and 
recycling. The recycling systems could even improve if consumers managed to curb 
the flow of materials by not carelessly throwing away plastic packaging but sorting 
it correctly. 
 
 
In many cases the confectionery industry still has no alternativ es to plastic 
packaging. They fulfil various functions to protect food in the interests of the 
consumer and hence enable the pleasure and enjoyment of a food consumption 
that is safe. 
Packaging serves to perform various tasks. The following functions can be achieved 
by making a requirements-optimised selection of plastics for tailor-made packaging 
solutions: 
- Protection of food from contaminants for hygienic reasons 
- Protection against microbial spoilage (e.g. germs/fungi) 
- Prevention of the absorption of undesirable and volatile substances  
(e.g. mineral hydrocarbons) 
- Shelf life 
- Protection from light 
- Protection against oxidation 
- Longer shelf life 
3 
 
- Avoidance/reduction of temperature fluctuations 
- Prevention of the absorption of extraneous odours 
- Prevention of grease transfer to the packaging 
- Protection against water absorption for dry products and also in areas with 
high humidity for other products 
- Protection against insect damage in some areas 
- Transport protection 
- Aesthetics and recognisability of products 
- Increasing the supply of goods oriented to the needs of the consumer 
through consumer-oriented sizes 
 
 
Plastic packaging makes an important contribution to environmental and climate 
protection, as a greater volume of food would spoil without efficient packaging. 
Food often contains many times more resources and energy than the packaging 
material. Food must therefore be packaged in keeping with the requirements so as 
to reduce food losses.  Food packaging prevents the loss of resources and spoilage 
of food.  They the refore contribute to achieving the target of a 50% reduction in 
food waste by 2030, as called for by EU parliamentarians. 
 
 
The BDSI is committed to promoting and accelerating the technical development 
of waste sorting and recycling systems. 
The recognition of the different materials coming from the yellow bag or the yellow 
bin during sorting must be continuously improved so as to be able to recycle more 
materials. According to the today’s state of the art technology, many composite 
materials are not, or onl y to a limited extent, recyclable, although many of these 
materials are essential for the purposes mentioned.  Although packaging made 
using mono-materials can be recycled easily in principle, this is currently often not 
done by waste management companies d ue to their lacking the necessary sorting 
technology. Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) is a thermoplastic that is well suited 
for recycling plastic bottles, for example.  By contrast, PET thermoformed trays, 
used in many food packaging applications and toda y e ven contain recycled 
material, are currently not being recycled.  Only by creating an even better 
infrastructure and improving the recycling structure and technology can the 
recyclability of plastic packaging for food and confectionery be increased and hence 
lead to increased recycling rates. 
 
 
Recyclable packaging must meet the food safety requirements of the respective 
products. 
According to Art.  14 of the Regulation (EC) 178/2002 laying down the general 
principles and requirements of food law , food that  is not safe may not be sold in 
the market.  Foodstuffs are cons idered unsafe if it can be assumed that they are 
harmful to health or unsuitable for human consumption.  Packaging, especially 
plastic packaging, serves to fulfil this legal requirement for food stuffs. To this end, 
packaging must comply with the requirement s of the EU Regulation on materials 
and articles intended to come into contact with food (1935/2004) and the EU 
Regulation on good manufacturing practice for these materials (2023/2006).  In 
achieving the objectives of dispensing with packaging material or only using 
recyclable packaging material, these legal obligations nevertheless have top

priority. Frequently the objective of ensuring the safety of the food and its 
packaging, together with th e currently commercially available shelf lives, can only 
be achieved by using composite materials that are hardly, or not at all, recyclable 
using current state-of-the-art technology. 
 
 
Research into the recovery and recyclability of packaging must be inten sified. The 
energy balance, carbon footprint, and environmental footprint must be taken into 
account in the kind of recycling. 
In Germany, approx.  49% of the plastic waste is thermally recovered and 
approx. 51% is materially recovered, i.e. recycled.  According to the German 
Packaging Act, the recycling rate for plasti c packaging came to 58.5% in 2019 and is 
expected to increase to 63% in 2022.  This means that as early as in 2019, 
Germany’s recycling rate for plastic packaging is expected to exceed the Europ e-
wide target of 55% in 2030 as laid out in the EU Packaging Di rective. Both the 
European Plastics Directive (SUP – Single Use Plastics Directive) and the German 
Packaging Act require packaging to be recyclable.  All plastic packaging throughout 
Europe is to be recyclable by 2030.  In Germany, the fees for participatio n in a dual 
system are to be scaled according to the respective packaging’s recyclability as early 
as of the end of 2019.  A switch to so -called bio-plastics as packaging is not a viable 
solution, as biodegradable plastics usually do not decompose any faste r in nature 
than other plastics do and are hence not returned to the cycle.  Nor are they easily 
compostable. Hence it is rather the packaging industry that is called upon to offer 
solutions to t he users of plastic packaging via which the demand for recycla ble 
packaging can be realised.  Innovation must be the driving motor for achieving 
higher rates of recyclability and improved degradability.  But research must also 
focus on the factors energy bal ance, carbon footprint, and the environmental 
footprint of recycling or thermal recovery.

The sector association: 
The BDSI represents the economic interests of over 200 mostly medium -sized German confectionery 
companies. It is both a trade association and an employers’ association.  The German confectionery  
industry is the fourth-largest sector of the German food industry, accounting for around 10% of overall 
sales. It is particularly characterised by its strong focus on exports.  Germany’s confectionery 
manufacturers have an overall workforce of around 50,000 employees.

Contribution to the European Commission’s r evision of 
essential requirements for packaging and other measures to 
prevent packaging waste  
 
APK AG, an innovative plastics recycling company from Germany, welc omes the initiative of the 
European Commission for reinforcing the essential requirements (ER) for packaging as well as 
revising the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive in order to strengthen waste prevention, 
reusability and high-quality recycling of plastic packaging. We fully support the Commission’s work 
in mobilising untapped potential in the plastics and packaging value chains in order to create a 
functioning market for secondary raw materials through harmonised rules for packaging.   
Achieving the declared goal of the EU Plastics Strategy , which has been reinforced by the Green 
Deal and Circular Economy Action Plan, that by 2030  all packaging shall be reusable or recyclable, 
will require a 360-degree appraisal of the untapped potential in the areas of product design 
and technological innovation.  
 
1. Reflections on the Inception Impact Assessment and the current consultation 
 
In general, t he Inception Impact A ssessment (IIA) as well as the present consultation reflect all of 
the relevant aspects for truly kick-starting a circular economy for plastics in the coming years 
– such as a  harmonised understanding of what constitutes recyclable packaging, consideration of 
recycled content standards and targets, and the issue of hazardous substances.  
The approach to these issues taken by the Commission and reflected throughout the IIA, the 
scoping study, and the consultation, however, poses a challenge to achieving this goal. 
The current approach to a solution for facilitating efficient treatment of packaging waste and high-
quality recycling focuses on standard recycling technology and re-design of packaging applications 
to better fit th ose recycling processes (design for recycling). Indeed , this is one valid pathway for 
ensuring the improved quality of secondary raw materials. Nevertheless, the approach has clear 
limitations when it comes to both the sustainable1 re-design of products and the valorisation 
of mixed waste streams  and complex applications . The options for increasing the quality of 
recyclates are consequently limited, reducing the number of products and end markets in which 
such recyclates can be used.   
To overcome these limitations , the definition of ‘recyclable packaging’ must reflect the 
game-changing influence that innovative recycling processes will be able to contribute in the 
coming years – by 2025 and 2030.2

The current focus on standard technology has resulted in questionable conclusions and statements 
in the current consultation questionnaire , such as that light-weighting and the resultant multi-
material, multi -layer packaging solutions are not recyclable and that the  complexity of 
packaging formats, therefore, must be reduced. Innovative dissolution recycling technology, a 
physical, solvent based process 3, however, is available at industrial scale today and is capable of  
separating different polymer types and different layers of mixed plastic packaging waste to produce 
high-quality recyclates.  
APK would like to strongly urge the Commission to complement its current focus with a solid 
emphasis on technological innovation and infrast ructure development. This will ensure that 
the envisaged legislative proposals do not hamper or delay investments into innovative recycling 
technology and infrastructure during this crucial build-up to the first milestone – the Circular Plastic 
Alliance’s 2025 target. Moreover, it will prevent the initiation of unsustainable re -design efforts. 
Assessing and analysing the relevance of innovative recycling technologies for a circular economy 
for plastics could be integrated in the Commission’s ongoing work in the form of an additional study 
and consultation, leading to the draft of a legislative proposal.4  
 
2. Innovative technology’s contribution to key points of the present consultation:  
Rethinking the definition of recyclable packaging 
The untapped potential of innovation in technology (sorting, collection, and innovative recycling) is 
relevant for all packaging – both established forms and future innovative solutions. The successive 
unfolding of such potential will change our understanding of what is recyclable.  
In consideration of this and within  the context provided, the definition of recyclable 
packaging suggested in the IAA and the current consultation should be slightly revised:  
‘Recyclable packaging is that which can be effectively and efficiently separated from the 
waste stream, collected, sorted , and aggregated into defined streams for recycling 
processes, and recycled at scale through relevant industrial processes such that it is turned 
into a secondary raw material, in line with Article 6a of the PPWD for calculating recycling 
targets, and of a sufficient quality that it can find end markets to replace the use of primary 
raw material.  
Innovative pPackaging placed on the market that requires new  and innovative collection, 
sorting and recycling  infrastructure to be developed shall be recycled at scale within a 
certain period of time.  
At least 95% of the functional unit of packaging shall be recyclable according to this 
definition, with the remaining minor components compatible with the relevant recycling 
process and not hindering the recyclability of the main components, in accordance with CR 
13688.’

To allow the innovative potential of technology to unfold, the relevant portion of the definition must 
apply to all packaging – not merely to innovative forms. Beyond this , in order to sharpen the 
definition and to strengthen its harmonisation effect, the meaning of terms such as ‘at scale’, 
‘relevant industrial processes’, ‘sufficient quality’ and ‘certain time period’ should be specified.  
 
Design for recycling:  
Re-design for today’s standard technology is one of the valid proposed solutions for achieving the 
2030 targets. Nevertheless, it is only feasible for a certain share of packaging. Reduced performance 
of the packaging (especially in the food packaging sector) or increased use of resources (to achieve 
a certain level of performance) would n egate the goals of the circular economy and shift the 
environmental burden from packaging to product. This cannot be the price for achieving 
recyclability within standard processes. 
APK recommends the development of a  set of sustainability criteria that balances re-design 
for recyclability with the impact on other vital aspects of packaging and products packaged.  
Beyond this, the IAA and the consultation do not take into consideration the impact of innovation 
in recycling technology when discussing design for recycling guidelines. Not only physical recycling 
design (mechanical, dissolution) , but chemical recycling  design as well need to be taken into 
consideration. Because this perspective is missing , the IA A’s option 2 , involving negative and 
positive lists for packaging, as well as statements in the questionnaire suggesting the reduction of 
complexity in packaging, must, from a sustainability point of view,  be considered questionable.  
APK would like to once again recommend that the definition of recyclable packaging be 
modified and the work conducted be complemented with a recycling technology angle.   
Recycled content  
APK fully supports measure s that will further the uptake of the use of recy cled plastics in the 
European plastics and packaging market . It is, however, paramount that such measures are 
based on a common European definition of recycled content. The ISO 14021 standard provides 
a sound basis for such a definition, including reflections on the aspect of pre - and post-consumer 
material while acknowledging the definition of recycling provided in the Waste Framework 
Directive. In addition,  harmonised requirements for recycled content within product categories are 
needed. APK further supports the measures mentioned in the consultation, such as a CEN standard 
on assessing the potential for including recycled content and the definition of recycled content 
targets. With regard to the latte r, APK suggests that the definition of recycled content 
targets could take a phase - and product -category-wise approach.  In certain product 
categories, inclusion of recycled content will be feasible in the short term. For these, ambitious 
but initial target s could be defined  within the coming legislative proposal . With expanding 
progress in design and technology , this list could successively grow and initial thresholds for 
recycled content in a particular product category could also be made more ambitious.   
 
Hazardous substances 
APK views the  harmonised and ambitious  management of hazardous substances in plastic 
packaging as highly relevant to produc ing quality recyclates. Along with  legislative measures, 
innovative recycling technolog ies such as dissolution  recycling can play a decisive role in 
ensuring reduction and removal of substances of concern (SoC) and substance of very high 
concern (SVHC) in mixed plastic waste streams. APK’s advanced physical and solvent -based 
recycling process includes a purificati on step in which contaminants, such as organic matter, inks,  
and additives, are removed from the target polymer.5

Enforcement  
The establishment of national packaging registries seems a balanced measure with regard to  
efficiency and administrative burden. Still , here too – these entities should then create minimal 
standards for the recyclability of packaging – a harmonised understanding  of the definition of  
recyclable packaging must be ensured. Moreover, this harmonised understanding must reflect the 
impact of innovative technology on the definition, as previously emphasized.  
 
Emissions 
The consultation does not adequately reflect the emissions-saving potential of differing measures 
and recycling technologies, despite the fact that a net-zero emissions economy is a meta-target in 
establishing a circular economy. Along with  waste prevention an d reduction  efforts, plastics 
recycling will contribute to a considerable decrease  in emissions .  As outlined by the Ellen 
MacArthur Foundation , recycling one tonne of plastics can save up to three tonnes of CO 2 as 
compared to producing the same tonne from virgin fossil feedstock. 6 Emissions savings potential 
and  quality of recyclates, should be  lead criteria in the discussion of ways to mobilise the untapped 
potential of and investments into recycling technology.  
 
Research & Development  
APK welcomes that the present consultation touches upon the R&D potential of technology to 
increase recycling. Unfortunately the presentation of this aspect is far too brief and the wording of 
the statements leaves grey areas ,7 so that, for instance, the innovation of advanced, physical and 
solvent-based recycling (dissolution recycling) is left to the imagination  in this context. Future 
communications from the European Commission should be worded in such a way that  the 
differences between the innovative t echnologies are clearly demarcated , allowing all relevant 
processes to be considered with regard to their full potential. A corresponding statement could 
read, for example:  ‘Advanced recycling technologies – physical and chemical’. Assuming that 
advanced physical processes such as dissolution recycling are to be considered under R&D in the 
consultation, APK would like to state that the potential of dissolution recycling is n ot limited solely 
to the category of ongoing R&D. Scale and quality benefits can already be reaped in the mid-term. 
Additional R&D will further expand the potential of dissolution recycling.  
 
Concluding comment: 
The complementary consideration of innovati on in design and innovation in  recycling technology 
will enable legislators as well as the plastics and packaging value chains to achieve the 2030 goals 
of the Plastics Strategy and pass the milestones on the road, such as the Circular Plastic Alliances’ 
target of 10 million tonnes of recyclate by 2025. In the ongoing discussions, we look forward to 
further clarifying dissolution recycling technology’s contribution to these targets.

In its recently published new Circular Economy Action Plan, the European Commission announced the 
establishment of a sustainable product policy framework based on the concept of circularity. Henkel 
supports the European Commission’s ambitions for a transition towards a more sustainable and 
circular economic system  in packaging . Thus, we are welcoming the  review of the Packaging and 
Packaging Waste Directive in order to fully realize the potential of a Circular Economy. 
With more than 185 production sites worldwide and around 20 billion Euros in sales in 2019, Henkel is 
a globally leading  company in markets for Laundry and Home Care, Beauty Care and Adhesive 
Technologies solutions. Europe is a key region for Henkel , with over 50 production sites and about 
20,000 employees generating roughly half of global sales . Thanks to our broad portfolio, we provide 
our products to both strategic industrial value chains such as construction, packaging, and aerospace 
as well as to consumers. 
Henkel has clearly demonstrated its commitment to circularity from the very beginning, participating 
in the voluntary pledging exercise  of the European Commission  and being an active s ignatory of the 
Circular Plastics Alliance Declaration  and the European Plastics Pact . As an advocate for the Circular 
Economy, Henkel enables product design for recyclability  and provid es solutions for repair and 
maintenance, and has developed ambitious sustainable packaging targets until 2025: 
1. 100 per cent of packaging to be recyclable or reusable1; 
2. 50 per cent reduction of fossil-based virgin plastics by increasing the proportion of recycled plastic 
to more than 30 percent, reducing the plastic consumption, and using biobased plastics; 
3. Progressively moving to n o plastic waste in the nature  by su pporting waste collection and 
recycling initiatives, investing in innovative solutions and technologies,  and providing consumers 
with targeted information on recycling (e.g. through disposal instructions). 
In cooperation along the value chain, such as with the Alliance to End Plastic Waste (AEPW), the New 
Plastics Economy (NPEC) by the Ellen MacArthur Foundation, the Plastic Bank and CEFLEX (A Circular 
Economy for Flexible Packaging), Henkel aims at making packaging more sustainable and addressing 
the global challenge of plastic packaging waste in the environment.

To make a Circular Economy a reality in Europe and support EU industry in their efforts to further 
develop and implement sustainable technologies and business models, a conceptual change to a 
value-chain thinking is needed. Recycling becomes sourcing and s hifts value from the end to the 
beginning of the packaging and chemical industry value chain, enabling true circularity of materials.  
In this context, Henkel specifically calls on policy makers to: 
1. Create a functioning Single Market for secondary raw materials and packaging; 
2. Shape and fine-tune the regulatory framework; 
3. Promote technological progress to improve recyclability and sortability of packaging; 
4. Leverage digital means to facilitate the recyclability of products and materials;  
5. Create secure framework conditions for dangerous goods packaging. 
Please find below a more detailed elaboration on these five key demands: 
1. Create a functioning Single Market for secondary raw materials and packaging 
• A functioning Single Market for secondary raw materials is a fundamental element to establish 
a circular economy in Europe. As a precondition, harmonized rules on packaging requirements 
across EU Member States enabling the free movement of packaging and packaged goods must 
be ensured. The EU Commission must conduct a critical review on the existing legal framework 
to detect obstacles and to provi de clarity for Member States and industry stakeholders.  In 
parallel, national legislation needs a thorough assessment of its compatibility with the 
objectives of the Single Market.   
2. Shape and fine-tune the regulatory framework 
• Harmonised collection and recycling systems across Europe must be established to incentivise 
recycling in Member States. Increased separate collection, better and harmonised sorting and 
investment in the recycling infrastructure are crucial pre-conditions for turning packing waste 
into valuable raw materials. Harmonised systems would also enable producers to provide the 
right packaging design for all geographical areas. 
• Further harmonisation of extended producer responsibility  (EPR) schemes at EU Level  is 
needed. EPR fees should be uniform, independent of the volume  of packaging as well as  
modulated according to the recyclability and use of recycled and biobased plastics. Also, the 
industry should be given ample time to adapt its packaging.  
• National design guidelines and test methods  should be replaced by  binding EU -wide 
definitions on design/recyclability of packaging. The CEFLEX guidelines on flexible packaging 
were developed by stakeholders along the value chain and are a good example to be 
considered as a European standard. Another illustration of our commitment to accelerate the 
transition to a circular economy and our open collaboration, is the 
EasyD4R Tool. This easy-to-
use software can be employed to verify the recyclability of a packaging early in the product 
development phase and is available upon request to economic players along the value chain. 
• Minimum quality standards for recyclates that differentiate between different materials and 
uses, notably concerning non -food applications , should be established . The Recyclates

Characterization Guidance  is a useful tool to evaluate recycled materials and assess their 
quality in line with European standards. These guidelines are accessible for th e biggest 
recycling streams in Europe and use parameters such as origin, colour and technical properties. 
• Free competition must allow producers to choose the packaging that is most appropriate for 
the respective product and distribution system. Functions of packaging must not be limited to 
product protection, hygiene, safety and legally required information, but also address product 
application. 
• The principle of  technology neutrality  should be maintained with regards to  the 
design/recyclability of packaging as well as innovative technologies, to guarantee a life -cycle 
approach and product innovation along the entire value chain. To realise the Circular Economy, 
virgin-like qualities from biobased or chemically recycled sources  need to complement 
mechanical recycling. Hence, all types of ‘de-fossilized’ plastics should be considered equally. 
Notably, mechanically and chemical ly recycled as well as biobased plastics should be 
evaluated based on  their carbon footprint and circularity potential . To create a broader 
sourcing base for secondary raw materials, a  more strategic allocation of public funds to 
promote innovative technologies  such as chemical recycling  and the building of pilot plants 
powered by green energy is needed. 
• Mass balance accounting can be a key enabler to increase the recycled and bio-based content 
in packaging and products , particularly during the transition phase from a fossil- based to a 
circular (bio)economy. Policy makers should recognize mass balance accounting as a valid 
alternative for direct biobased/recycled feedstocks in the same way than segregated recycled 
materials. 
• The introduction of mandatory recycled content targets  for specific packaging formats does 
not promote high quality recycling  but hinders innovation and the development of new 
recycling streams . As member s of the  Circular Plastics Alliance, Henkel and other industry 
stakeholders have committed to ambitious voluntary targets for recycled content.  
• Tax measures on certain packaging materials undermine innovation and investment in new 
technologies as well as recycling and waste infrastructures. If considered at all, they should be 
earmarked and harmonized to fo ster the Circular Economy while preventing further 
fragmentation of the Single Market. 
3. Promote technological progress to improve recyclability and sortability of packaging 
• To tackle persisting challenges in recycling technologies, substantial technological progress is 
needed. Henkel is continuously working on further improving the recyclability and sortability 
of its consumer goods packaging , by substantially increasing the use of standard packaging 
materials (PET, PE and PP), mono-material solutions and transparent or light colours.  
New design for recycling solutions  such as the introduction of recyclable black plastic 
packaging (“Black Masterbatch”) or a zipper for sleeved plastic packaging are currently  being 
rolled out across the European market.  
Furthermore, Henkel aims to  enable recycling by  improving the compatibility and 
debondability of packaging components with innovative adhesive and coatings solutions (e.g. 
for container labelling applications and in paper and board-based packaging). Two technology 
examples are optimized adhesives for mechanical recycling of mono-material flexible

4. Leverage digital means to facilitate the recyclability of products and materials 
• Digitalisation can facilitate  the transition to a circular economy  and help address the supply 
gap challenge of secondary raw materials . We support the view that  increased digital 
recording and sorting data can facilitate recyclability . The European Commission needs to 
support initiatives aiming at leveraging the digital infrastructure. 
5. Create safe framework conditions for dangerous goods packaging 
• Henkel sells a range of dangerous goods articles that are packed in plastic packaging to ensure 
resistance of the material to the contents over the entire period of use. We are fully aware of 
our responsibility when reusing packaging and/or using recycled packaging for these products. 
A redesign of the PPWD needs to take into account  the specific challenges  in the area of 
dangerous goods products for responsible packers. Therefore, it is important to: 
o Establish EU-wide uniform procedures  on dangerous goods packaging 
approval/inspection procedures, requirements for quality assurance as well as programs 
for production monitoring of manufacturers, remanufacturers and reconditioners.  
o Develop quality standards for dangerous goods packaging containing recycled material 
as well as framework conditions to produce  recycled materials (quality assurance 
program). For example, c urrently there is no standardization (CEN/ISO) of recycled 
materials qualities that considers special filling product requirements. 
o Standardize dangerous goods packaging, e.g. through the  creation of a quality label for 
reusable packaging that defines minimum performances, such as wall thicknesses of steel 
packaging, UV stability of plastic packaging and information on the history of the contents.  
o Invest in research  in the field of reuse/long- term use of plastic packaging , e.g. on the 
resistance of recycled materials to product damage mechanisms such as stress cracking 
resistance and molecular degradation.  An adequate testing infrastructure should be set 
up to ensure the safe use.

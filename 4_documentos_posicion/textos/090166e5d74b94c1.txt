Citeo strongly welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on the initiative undertaken by the 
European Commission to review requirements for packaging and other measures to prevent 
packaging waste. Citeo shares the Commission’s findings on the need for clearer and more 
specific requirements on the composition and the reusable and recoverable, including recyclable, 
nature of packaging.  
 
The review of the Essential Requirements is necessary to ensure they are in line with: 
✓ the Plastics Strategy’s commitment to ensure that plastic packaging is reusable or 
recyclable in an economically viable manner by 2030; 
✓ the new Circular Economy Action Plan and the EU Green  Deal which broadens this 
commitment to all packaging and commits to additional measures;  
✓ the European Plastics Pact’s ambition  to prevent and reduce overpackaging and 
packaging waste;  
✓ the Circular Plastics Alliance  which aims to increase the recycled content of new 
packaging. 
 
Citeo fully endorses the EU’s vision for a packaging circular economy, urging for the material to 
transform from waste to economically, socially and environmentally beneficial resource. As such, 
Citeo endeavors for improving the circularity of all type of  packaging, while also ensuring its net 
environmental improvement over the product’s lifecycle.  
 
This contribution includes several elements we hope will be considered by the Commission:  
✓ First, in order to support waste reduction at source more efficiently, Citeo recommends 
defining the concept of overpackaging with clear criteria.  
✓ Then, a coherent definition of recyclability  – but also compostability and 
biodegradability – enabling producers to produce packaging in a congruent way with the 
industrial technologies available , as well as with the existing collection and sorting 
systems in place in the different Member States , is a pre -requisite for improving the 
recyclable nature of packaging. Moreover, the development of eco -design, through 
specific recommendations developed at the packaging level and investments and 
supports in concrete and innovative solutions, should be su pported. Eco-design can also 
be encouraged by eco-modulation schemes.  
✓ Because authorities are taking a much greater interest in reusable packaging, we need 
to anticipate new framework at the EU level, especially with a clearer definition of reuse 
and the conditions in which any reusable packaging scheme is compliant with the internal 
market.   
✓ Finally, in order to tackle the limited competitiveness of secondary materials from recycled 
packaging relative to virgin stock in view of its quality and availabili ty, Citeo recommends 
several actions to support the promotion of secondary raw materials.

 REDUCTION AT SOURCE 
 
1.1  A clear and transparent definition of “overpackaging” should be set up 
 
The Green Deal identified a need for “new legislation, including targets and measures for tackling over-
packaging and waste generation”1. Moreover, the Plastics Strategy committed the Commission to “look 
into the issue of over -packaging as part of the future review of the essential requirements for 
packaging”2. Thus, “ overpackaging, the use of ‘excessive’ quantities of packaging for goods, is 
recognized as a problem”3 at the EU level.  
 
The current Essential Requirements require “the packaging volume and weight be limited to the 
minimum adequate amount to maintain the necessary level of safety, hygiene and acceptance for the 
packed product and for the consumer”4. While the objective of supporting waste prevention is relatively 
clear, this requirement is too vague to be operational and enforceable. Indeed, “there is little guidance 
for producers, fillers and regulators as to what constitutes the ‘minimum adequate amount’ and the 
evaluation of the Essential Requirements concluded that the inherent subjectivity inhibits compliance 
and enforcement”5. 
 
“The generic term ‘over-packaging’ is used to designate a wide range of packaging systems: envelopes 
within envelopes, multi -packs, packaging that is (or is perceived to be) over -sized (too big, too thick), 
packing (wedging elements), labels, notices, etc.”6. In the absence of a general agreement on what 
does constitute overpackaging, Citeo recommends setting a definition at the EU level to ensure 
that there is no ambiguity for producers, consumers and enforcement bodies.   
To assess whether there is overpackaging, Citeo recommends considering the elements of the 
packaging system from the point of view of the functions they perform or help perform with regard to the 
product and/or users. Indeed, “packaging is first a provider  of a variety of functions with regard to a 
product and its users before being an object or a material” 7. The functions of packaging should be 
analyzed throughout the cycle of life of the packaged product and can include the protection and 
preserving of the product it contains, grouping products together for transport and storage, identifying 
the product and protecting the consumer, being a vehicle for information and contributing to the user -
friendliness of the product. Citeo recommends to clearly define the functions that are considered 
as essential and therefore justify the presence of packaging elements, excluding overpackaging.  
Particularly, reducing packaging at source should not result in underpackaging and the related food 
waste. Indeed, it is important to consider, as recalled in the Farm to Fork Strategy, that “food packaging 
plays a key role in the sustainability of food systems”8. If packaging needs to meet functionalities in order 
to avoid product or food losses, these functionalities should be preserved. Thus, Citeo recommends 
that the impact assessment cons iders food waste/product waste arising from packaging 
reduction but also from transition to reuse models (e.g. implication on food waste of bulk sales). 
For instance, Article 779 of the French anti-waste law for a circular economy requires that, by 2022, any 
retail business selling unprocessed fresh fruits and vegetables should sell them without packaging made

1.2  Clear and transparent requirements to prevent overpackaging should be 
set up, especially on “empty space” 
 
One of the criteria the Essential Requirements could consider when defining overpackaging is the empty 
space. Several countries have already implemented regulations regarding empty space. For instance, 
South Korea11 and China12 have adopted regulations setting  limits on the amount of empty space and 
the number of layers that consumer product packaging can have. These limits depend on the categories 
of products, thus taking into account the specific constraints that exist for each category (e.g. in South 
Korea empty space ratio is 35% for electronic products, 15% for processed food products, 10% for 
beverages as well as for cosmetics and clothes). In some countries, restrictions on the amount of 
headspace or concealed empty space in packaging can also be found in  fair trade regulations. For 
example, both New Zealand 13 and Canada 14 have regulations that forbid deceptive packaging that 
mislead the consumer with respect to the quantity of product 15. 
 
In the EU, there is room for significant improvement in this area, especially in the sector of e-commerce. 
Indeed, “in Europe, the e-commerce sector grew by 11% in 2017 and 13% in 2018, and it is expected 
to continue growing”16. The growth in e-commerce is likely to mean a consequent growth in packaging 
and packaging waste. “Packaging company DS Smith found that 50% of e-commerce packaging is more 
than a quarter empty, with the average empty space in toy packaging at 52%”17. Therefore, e-commerce 
packaging is a specific area that could be suitable for legal limits to reduce the risk of overpackaging.  
 
In this regard, Citeo calls to establish a common EU standard on “unnecessary empty space” of 
e-commerce packaging as well as for all packaging. Based on EU guidelines, companies should 
then define specific ratios for each product category  according to their specificities. This will 
ensure that every packaging is designed to fit the product with minimum empty space, thus preventing 
waste at source while avoiding food/product waste . Furthermore, it should be ensured that void is not 
filled with wedging elements.  
 
Reducing empty space has positive effects not only on the waste prevention. Indeed, “optimizing void 
space in packaging not only reduces the amount of packaging waste to be treated but reduces the 
environmental impact from transporting oversized packages” 18. “Reducing empty space can also 
minimize damage to product and hence reduce the number of returns to retailer and the associated loss 
in product value”19.

 ECO-DESIGN  
 
2.1  Recyclability, compostability & biodegradability  
 2.1.1 Recyclability 
We believe that a harmonized definition of recyclability is a prerequisite to encourage upstream 
eco-design at packaging level by sending a clear signal to the stakeholders of the pack aging value 
chain, while giving a chance for innovative material. 
2.1.1.1 The recyclability definition according to Citeo 
   
Recyclability must be  defined based on existing (in practice and at scale) national collection 
system(s), sorting capacities, recycling streams and design for recycling guidelines.  
 
Furthermore, recycling objectives per material are already set up in the Packaging and Packaging Waste 
Directive, recyclability should thus help to meet the objectives and should not hinder the development 
of good practices.   
 
Today, a packaging can be considered as recyclable if it meets all the following criteria: 
 
Main Material: The packaging is made of at least 50% of a single material (glass, paper/cardboard, 
aluminum, steel or plastics in France). 
 
Collection: At least 90% of the population has the ability to  sort the packaging in a specific 
collection system, or 50% if there is a plan to develop to collection system in order to reach 90% 
within 5 years.  
 
Sorting: Technologies available in the sorting centers (or all other preparation infrastructure) allow 
the sorting of the packaging into the appropriate recycling stream. 
 
Recycling: The packaging is accepted and recycled in an existing and recognized recycling stream. 
There is a full transparency regarding the recycling stream, tonnages recycled and timing of 
recycling (presentation of a recycling certificate).  
 
The recycling process is efficient and allows for a majority of the tonnages sorted to be recycled.  
The quality of the recycled material is sufficient  to guarantee the sustainability of the recycling 
streams over time. This is for example the case when the recycled material can substitute the same 
virgin material without alteration of the quality of the end -product, or when there is no need to pay 
regenerators or recyclers to accept the post-consumption packaging waste.  
 
Optimal recovery of the material without any perturbation : The packaging design follows the 
guidelines of the technical committees representing the value chain (COTREP and CEREC for 
example in France).

2.1.1.2 A recyclability definition that gives a room for innovative materials and new 
solutions on recycling processes   
 
According to Citeo, the Essential Requirements should be thought to allow packaging 
improvement (material or design) and recycling innovations. 
 
The need to prove a 20% recycling rate within 3 years is not adapted to allow innovation packaging or 
materials into the market, with on one side mostly start-ups and small or medium-sized compagnies with 
limited means and on the other side recyclers who need time to invest into new recycling plants. A good 
example is the Polyethylene Furanoate (PEF) , which is supported by the European Commission, and 
will not be able to reach a 20% recycling rate within the 3 years, which proves that those requirements 
will not be coherent with the market signals. MonoPEF packaging could come a substitution for complex 
packaging and we expect it will be recycled within a 5-year time frame. In this perspective, the Essential 
Requirements must be limited to a framework on how to assess and help innovations to emerge 
when they are relevant to the circular economy and more generally to the environment.

Mechanical recycling is much more prevalent than chemical recycling.  However, for certain materials 
which cannot be mechanically recycled, chemical recycling has proved benefits. Indeed, although many 
polymers are in principle recyclable, mechanical recycling does not remove all additives. This impacts 
the quality of recycled feedstock and alters some of its key properties, such as purity and mechanical 
qualities. Other techniques, however , can be used to break down polymer molecules and then extract 
specific components. These chemical recycling technologies can be used to process a wide range of 
plastic waste, including packaging, that are not recyclable with currently known methods. A gro wing 
number of observers see their development as key to overcoming barriers to plastic waste recycling.  
 
Recycling by  depolymerization describes technologies that break down polymer chains into 
different, homogeneous, components, which can then be re -used to make new plastics. Another 
approach is recycling by dissolution, in which polymers can be selected and purified in their liquid 
state. These techniques are of real interest because they produce recycled products with high levels of 
purity, identical t o virgin plastics.  In general,  depolymerization and dissolution would complement 
current mechanical recycling techniques. They will serve to process more plastic waste and significantly 
increase the proportion of recycled material used to make new plastic products and packaging.  
 
“Although innovative chemical recycling techniques are progressing, most technologies have not yet 
been implemented at industrial scale ”23. The overall recyclability of packaging could thus be increased 
by a system change, by making chemical recycling an integral part of waste treatment in Europe.  
 
To this end,  Citeo promotes new technologies and chemical recycling facilities across Europe , 
especially through the Plastic Solution Forum. This international forum aims to promote the development 
of recycling for plastics that stem from research and development in polymers. A first edition, organized 
by Citeo and the EPR organization on packaging from Quebec, Éco Entreprises Québec (ÉEQ), brought 
together 300 participants in Paris in February 2019.  The next edition  organized by Citeo and entitled 
“Exploring the future of new plastics recycling technologies in 2020 ”24 will focus on pyrolysis and 
gasification technologies. It will take place in Paris, the 5 th and 6th of October. 
 
Finally, Citeo promotes R&D and innovation in eco -design through calls for proposals . Citeo's 
latest selection includes 30 projects for a total budget of 7.5 million euros of financial support. Several 
projects are led by consortia and partnerships bringing together marketers, packaging manufacturers 
and recyclers in order to develop solutions that can be rapidly industrialized (within 1 to 3 years). In fact, 
just over 1 million tons of plastic packaging is placed on the French market. Nearly half is perfectly 
recyclable (especially PET bottles and HDPE bottles). However, other types of pack aging require new 
eco-design solutions or the creation of recycling channels.   
Therefore, in order to improve and increase the recyclability of PET packaging, Citeo is supporting 
Sopraloop in the recycling of PET packaging.  This innovation developed by the SOPREMA Group is a 
world first, aimed at recycling all PET plastic packaging. This technique combines mechanical recycling 
with recyclin g using new technologies: first the packaging is transformed into flakes which are then 
cleaned. In a second step, these flakes go through a process that breaks down the plastic molecules 
under the action of glycol ( depolymerization) to return to the origi nal molecule. Once purified, it is 
repolymerized and transformed into polyols, a new material that is the main component of polyurethane 
insulating foams for the building industry. The aim of the Sopraloop process is to be replicated in other 
countries in Europe and North America. 
 
Citeo also works on the  creation of  new recycling schemes/facilities for Polystyrene  (PS). Indeed, 
polystyrene is one of the main resins used in the packaging of fresh dairy products and represents a 
deposit of around 125,000 tons per year (including 50,000 tons of yoghurt pots). In this context, Citeo is 
supporting the development of an eco-designed and recyclable jar with 100% PS meaning: pot, banner 
(without hot-melt paraffin) and heat-sealable seal (without varnish). The projects selected in the context 
of these calls for proposals will contribute to the development of a PS recycling channel in France: 
improving the recyclability of pots, new recycling technologies and integration of recycled material in 
packaging in contact with food.

2.1.1.3 The communication on the recyclability of packaging   
 
In France, sorting instructions are being extended to develop the collection of all packaging on the whole 
territory (instead of “only” glass, metal, cardboard, cartons and plastic bottles currently) and it will be 
fully effective after 2022 (50% of the population will be affected in 2020).  
 
Introducing a harmonized definition of recyclability in the Essential Requireme nts should help 
set up common criteria that then could be used  to communicate and inform consumers on the 
recyclability of packaging, thus preventing misleading claims. Indeed, it is o nly once recyclability 
is defined at European level that rigorous and coherent claims about the recyclability of packaging can 
be put in place.  
 
2.1.2 Compostability & biodegradability 
“The growing number of plastic products and packaging marketed as ‘compostable’ or ‘biodegradable’ 
raises the question of the extent to which biodegradability and compostability of plastic is beneficial in 
the context of the transition towards a circular economy ”25. The revision of the Essential Requirements 
is an opport unity to clarify these concepts and define more precisely to what extent compostable 
and biodegradable packaging should be  regarded a s solutions to tackle the increase in 
packaging waste generation.  
2.1.2.1 A clear distinction between compostability & recyclability should be drawn 
When defining recyclable packaging,  it is important to reaffirm that  in no way should compostability 
be included in the definition of recy clability, as there is no recovery of the material. Indeed, a 
distinction should be done between recyclability, which should be the priority when reuse is either not 
possible or brings no environmental advantage, and compos tability which should be targeted for 
applications in which having a compostable packaging allow to increase the collection of the biowaste 
it encapsulates such as biowaste collection bags, tea bags, coffee pods, fruits and vegetables stickers… 
“In other applications, however, packaging that is designed to be recycled would be preferable in order

to support the circular economy”26, in line with the report of the European Commission on the relevance 
of biodegradable and compostable consumer plastic products and packaging in a circular economy 27. 
Recyclability should be prioritized over compostability, as there is only limited evidence for agronomical 
benefit linked to compostable plastic in compost or digestate.  Only permit th e use of compostable 
packaging for specific  applications where there is some added value is also necessary in order to 
mitigate against the end of life management risks posed by these materials (potential contamination of  
plastic/ biowaste stream with compostable plastics).  
In order to ensure the limited use of compostable packaging, the Essential Requirements should 
reflect criteria for which design for compostability are considered to be  added value when 
compared to reuse or other end of life applications.  
2.1.2.2 A clear distinction between compostability & biodegradability should be 
drawn 
Under Annex II of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive , packaging intended for composting 
should be “of such a biodegradable nature that it does not hinder the separate collection and the 
composting process or activity into which it is introduced ”28, while biodegradable packaging should be 
“such a nature that is capable of undergoing physical, chemical, thermal or biological decomposition  
such that most of the finished compost  ultimately decomposes into carbon dioxide, biomass and 
water”29.  
The Essential Requirements cannot be reviewed in isolation from the harmonized EN standards, 
adherence to which by producers enables a Member  State to presume compliance, among them EN 
13432 defining standards for biodegradation and composting.  
The current Essential Requirements are likely to create confusion – for Member States, suppliers and 
consumers – by not differentiating clearly between compostability and biodegradability. Indeed, the use 
of the terms compostable and biodegradable have introduced tw o separate requirements for the same 
packaging, causing confusion among the concepts of biodegradability and compostability.  
In order to avoid this confusion, the EN standard 13432 should be updated to further specify the 
concepts of compostable and biodegradable packaging.  
2.1.2.3 Clear criteria for labelling packaging as “compostable” or “biodegradable” should 
be set up 
“Compostable and biodegradable packaging is becoming increasingly widespread. At present, labelling 
of such products is typically confusing for consumers, often not providing specific instructions for 
disposal or bearing messaging which is technically incorrect ”30. This lack of information often results in 
plastic litterin g or pollution due to unsuitable environmental conditions or insufficient time for 
degradation. Indeed, the biodegradable nature of a plastic does not mean that it will degrade rapidly 
under natural conditions, such as marine environment , or even under home compositing conditions . 
This is why compostable plastic products are unlikely to be considered a suitable alternative in future 
revisions. 
 In the perspective, as underlined by the Eunomia report for the European Commission on the relevance 
of biodegradable and compostable consumer plastic products and packaging in a circular economy 31, 
the need for a clear labelling of compostable plastics becomes even more apparent as the market shares 
of plastics with biodegradable properties is increasing.  The interest of labelling information on the end 
of life of packaging is both to inform consumers and to help them doing the right sorting gest. It is thus 
necessary that in order to claim “compostable”, there is the adequate system in place to collect the 
packaging so that it can indeed be composted  in the existing facilities . The Essential Requirements

“The essential shortcoming is that EN 13432 assumes certain conditions or practices will be used within

labelling “compostable” should only be possible when the full packaging is “home compostable”. Citeo 
supports for the development of an equivalent European standard.  
Regarding biodegradability, “standard EN 13432 makes clear that it covers biodegradability in industrial 
treatment plants. This means that packaging is tested and certified as compostable in conditions that

✓ Several studies point towards a perception amongst  consumers that “biodegradable” or

development of reuse in France.

4.2  Towards a European framework for reuse 
4.2.1 No new impulse on reuse specifically since 2008  
As far as the Waste Framework Directive , reusable packaging has been identified by the European 
Commission as a primary lever to manage waste: the waste management hierarchy, transposed in each 
Member States, states that waste shall be first prevented, then reused, recycled, recovered or disposed.  
More recently, reuse and reusability are missing from the Directive on the reduction of the impact of 
certain plastic products on the environment, which was designed to prevent and reduce marine litter in 
European Member States. Indeed, while the Directive prohibits to put some products and packaging on 
the market, the objectives to reduce plastic consumption shall be determined by e ach Member States 
and then evaluated by the Commission by 2021. 
In France, the reduction of plastic consumption has been transposed in the anti-waste law for a circular 
economy. The consumption reduction objectives shall be met both via i) reusable packaging targets, ii) 
restrictions to put some products on the French market.   
4.2.2 A context that calls for a signal at the European level  
With a worldwide epidemic challenging our eco nomic, social and environmental status, we have 
identified several factors that could slow down the development of reusable packaging in Europe:  
- Single-Use Packaging is perceived as more cautionary, in the midst of a sanitary crisis (as 
opposed to reusable packaging that has been used, then transported, then washed, then filled 
again before it ends up in your hands); 
- The economic recession we are undergoing will most certainly cut back the investment 
capacities of companies that previously considered launching projects for reusable packaging. 
In the near future, promoting local production and consumption will be part of the solution to developing 
resilient food and beverage systems at the EU level. Reuse systems, when they maximise both the 
environmental and economic benefits, should spread across Europe.   
To that end, we believe the European Commission has a great part to play. More specifically:   
 Clarify under which conditions reuse systems are compatible with the functioning 
of the internal market 
Reuse systems are likely to spread at a local/national level. It is very important, so as to protect the 
overall functioning of the internal market that the Commission watches at the reuse framework 
developed across Europe and monitors any breach to compet ition. All firms will not be able to propose 
reusable packaging, especially if the production centres are international and the products sold 
European-wide (unless those firms transport packaging across Europe, which is likely to produce lots of 
GHG emissions due to transportation). Despite this industrial reality, we want to be sure that there will 
not be any restriction to circulation for those packaging or distortion of competition (ie. national/local 
brands favoured compared to international ones).  
In this perspective, we would be in favour of having recommendations from the Commission 
regarding the compatibility of reuse systems with the functioning of the internal market.  
 Clarify the definition of reuse at a European level  
A clear and shared definition at the European level is essential to monitor the achievement of 
the objective in the European Strategy for Plastics, which is to ensure that by 2030 all plastic 
packaging placed on the Union market is re-usable or easily recycled.

Based on the current texts 43, and given the upcoming revision of this Directive for 2021,  there is an 
opportunity to provide the Commission with a more accurate definition of reusable, in order to better 
capture the different systems that coexist.  
We would propose to include in the definition of reusable the following aspects:  
✓ Maintain reference to EN 13429 (reuse) to define “reusable” 
This definition at a European scale would help define national definitions for the implementation of the 
Essential Requirements at the national scale. Citeo recommends to define “reusable packaging” as 
packaging that has been designed to be reused several times, for which producers are able to 
report the number of trips done by said.  
Using this definition will clarify the difference between “refilled” and “reused”. 
✓ Clarify the usage of reusable packaging 
The sentence “for the same purpose for which it was conceived” could be clarified. The current wording 
could be understood in two ways: 1) the packaging could be used the first time for food or beverage 
products, but then reused a second time for other type of products which is still food or beverage, but of 
a different kind (existing example: a bottle used once for beverage and then to stock peanuts); 2) the 
packaging could be used and reused for the exact same products or type of products (ie. a bottle of beer 
of the brand X is reused and filled with beer from the brand Y). Citeo’s position is to favour option 2.  
 
✓ Clarify that a packaging is considered as reusable when its structural components are 
reusable 
A reusable packaging can be composed of different elements which are not all reusable, such as the 
caps for instance. We propose that the definition of a reusable packaging clearly states that a 
packaging can be considered as reusable if its main/structural component is reusable (and even 
though all its components are not reusable) . This will in turn mean  that structural reusable 
components as well as secondary components should be defined. We recommend secondary 
components to be recyclable.  
This clarification is important in the framework of national objectives towards reused packaging placed 
in the market, to give certainty to producers that, for instance, a reusable beer bottle can be counted as 
a reusable packaging even though the cap is not reusable.  
✓ Specify that the packaging should be recoverable through recycling , unless the barrier 
to recyclability is necessary to make it reusable  and establish clear exemptions when 
necessary 
Regarding the recyclability of reusable packaging, we believe that it is necessary to specify the 
corresponding single -use packaging collection, sorting and recycling system  (household, 
industrial or commercial, etc..).  In this perspective, we propose to constrain the number of 
exemptions to be applied. Exemption could be for individual cases with specific category or packaging; 
such as packaging from the special industrial waste stream; or specific packaging such as a stoneware 
container that wouldn’t be an exception unless it can be proven that only stone ware can be used  for 
several trips (for instance, resisting to several baking). 
✓ Ideally include  in the definition in addition to the number of trips done by a reusable 
packaging, some thresholds on return rate in order to avoid packaging self-declaring as 
“reusable” although there is no industrial system to collect and wash it

economic reconstruction and the switch towards more resilient industries could be favoured, thanks to 
the massive relaunch plans foreseen at the EU level to back EU economies fragilized by the COVID 19. 
As explained above, we conside r that  reuse could contribute to building more resilient food and 
beverage industries, notably because reuse alternatives are often run at a local level, which is one of 
the conditions for its environmental performance.  
Thus, we call for the development of dedicated investm ent plans to favour the spread of 
packaging reuse at a national level . Reuse objectives are already set within national regulatory 
frameworks in most countries (and there is no need to complement them by an additional layer at EU 
level). However, there is a crucial need of financing support for those emerging industries, to back them 
towards profitability.  
Citeo is ready to provide the Commission with specific amounts and industrial priorities to target 
these investments.

 PROMOTION OF SECONDARY RAW MATERIALS (SRM) 
5.1  Including recycled content  requirements under the Essenti al 
Requirements  
The Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive provides that the Commission shall promote, relating to 
the Essential Requirements, “the preparation of Eur opean standards relating to criteria for a minimum 
content of recycled material in packaging for appropriate  types of packaging” 46. The Directive on the 
reduction of the impact of certain plastic products on the environment has introduced such a requirement 
for beverage bottles, setting a 25% target for PET bottles from 2025 and a 30% target for all beverage 
bottles from 203047.   
In order to ensure the circular use of all materials and not on ly plastics , Citeo recommends 
extending recycled content requirements to all materials and introduce such requirements into 
the Essential Requirements. This should be done in collaboration w ith the European Food Safety 
Authority (EFSA) and the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) to ensure the issue of food contact will 
be tackled. Furthermore, in order to ensure the targets  defined for each material will be achieved, it is 
essential to pay attention to th e traceability of these materials  with each marketer and the monitoring 
and reporting system. In these conditio ns, the market uptake of recycled materials will be  effectively 
promoted.  
5.2  Promoting a coherent Secondary Raw Materials (SRM) market in Europe 
in line with existing regulations and legislations on chemicals  
There are many opportunities and benefits for the environment and consumers derived through the 
market for SRMs. However, r isks associated with the presence of substances of concerns in goods 
produced from SRMs are relevant, especially when they are used for food packaging.   
 In this regard, the Commission should work on aligning existing regulations and legislations on 
chemicals (e.g. REACH); waste and products in order to guarantee that products and packaging 
are exempted from substances of concerns , in adequation with the  European Commission’s 
Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability that will be published soon 48. 
5.3  Introducing green procurement criteria and targets for packaging 
Public procurement makes up a significant part of the European economy, accounting for 16% of GDP 
(1,800 billion euros). This economic weight must be used to develop innovations in favor of the circular 
economy and push economic actors to change their mode l. The Directive on public procurement  has 
already opened new possibilities for integrating environmental issues and circular economy into public 
procurement. However, public purchasers still lack simplified operational tools to effectively integrate 
circular economy aspects into their demands.  
The Essential Requirements could introduce green procurement criteria and targets  to support 
public bodies who would like to include sustainability in their packaging procurement strategies.  
5.4  Promoting affordable Secondary Raw Materials (SRM) in Europe with a 
coherent circular fiscal system  
While market forces should be allowed to shape business decisions and as we recognize that regulation 
should be the least burdensome, market forces alone h ave not been sufficient to create incentives for 
decarbonization or foster the circular economy in Europe. A long-term ambitious framework (with a 2030 
and 2050 horizon) is needed to provide legal certainty, pave the way to make Europe’s circular transition 
a success for all stakeholders, and drive investments.

A striking example of this lack of sufficiency of m arket forces in promoting a circular economy is the 
prices of products/packaging made from virgin materials which are in some cases equivalent to those 
of products made from recycled materials . This can be explained by the fact that the price does not 
consider externalities and environmental impacts of products/packaging made from virgin materials.   
In order to answer this challenge of competitivity between Primary and Secondary Raw Materials, we 
believe it is essential to move towards a more circular and ecological tax system within the Member 
states and at the EU level. The Commission should work on the introduction of economic 
incentives, such as a new taxonomy, in favor of recycled materials. This taxonomy should take into 
consideration negative externalities related to the extraction of virgin materials and must provide 
possible ways to offset market losses related to commodity price fluctuations.  
In this regard, the conclusions of the  special meeting of the  European Council  (17-21 July 2020)  
confirmed the introduction of a new own resource based on non-recycled plastic packaging waste as of 
1 January 2021. Citeo recommends applying this tax to the producer of the virgin resin for all products, 
instead of taxing marketers. Such fiscal measures should aim at increasing the use of recycled plastics 
within packaging and reduce the spread between the price of recycled plastic and the price of virgin 
plastic in a context of low oil prices in the aftermath of the Covid-19 crisis.  
Beyond taxation, we need to remove technological and economic barriers to have recycling 
solutions allowing a return to packaging at a low economic and environmental cost and thus a 
strong market for Secondary Raw Materials. Indeed, it is essential to diversify the outlets for recycled 
materials, by promoting outlets with high added value which would allow to have recycled materials less 
depreciated compared to virgin materials and which follow the same economic cycles.

 
   
 
Review of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive 
EUROPEN accompanying document to the Public Consultation questionnaire 
December 2020 
 
 
Striving for an ambitious and coherent legislative review 
 
Packaging of the future will have to be reusable or recyclable in an economically viable way, support the EU 
climate goals and, at the same time, continue to provide safe and sustainable products to consumers.  
The review of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive (PPWD) can represent a real milestone in the process of 
moving towards a sustainable packaging value chain in Europe. The regulatory framework must be fit for purpose to 
effectively prevent packaging from becoming waste, by boosting recyclable and reusable solutions while preserving 
packaging functionalities.   
• Recognising the purpose of packaging 
There are good reasons for products to be packaged: to protect products’ integrity and consumers’ health and 
safety, to increase products’ shelf -life, to red uce product losses , to facilitate transport, efficient handling and 
distribution, to promote the packaged product and to provide information and convenience to consumers.  The 
policy measures to further drive the sustainability of the packaging value chain must ensure these functionalities 
are not undermined. 
• Coherence with the EU environmental and climate objectives  
Pursuing the E U Green Deal’s objectives requires embracing a life -cycle approach to circularity, where climate 
and environmental performance is assessed throughout the entire life-cycle of packaging and product.  A thorough 
impact assessment of unavoidable and potential  trade-offs, resulting in unintended consequences or negative 
impacts (environmental, economic and social impacts) , will be necessary to ensure that these are minimized or 
prevented. 
• Future-proofing of legislation to support innovation  
Future targets, definitions and design requirements need to provide sufficient visibility to drive investments into 
new packaging solutions , reuse schemes and waste management technologies. Rather than i mposing bans or 
restrictions on specific packaging materials or formats , policies should strive to ensure that they can be come 
reusable and recyclable in the immediate future.  
• Single Market for packaging and for secondary raw materials.  
The review must address barriers and bottlenecks resulting from insufficient harmonisation and weak enforcement 
of existing EU provisions at national level. Key provisions must be clearly defined in the legislative text for 
maximum harmonisation across the Member States and enforcement mechanisms must be strengthened to 
oversee their effective implementation.

1. WASTE PREVENTION 
The overarching goal of any future policy measures addressing packaging waste prevention must be threefold: 
1. Optimise the amount of packaging put on the market without undermining packaging functionalities and 
avoiding undesired trade-offs in other life-cycle stages (e.g. climate impact, food waste). 
2. Prevent waste generation and loss of resources due to landfilling, and incineration by maximising packaging 
reuse and recycling; 
3. Reduce the overall EU environmental footprint, with a particular focus on resource use,  GHGs emissions. 
Defining overpackaging and underpackaging  
Both overpackaging and underpackaging need to be clearly defined to effectively address waste prevention by 
eliminating unnecessary (i.e. not functional) packaging whilst preventing negative consequences on product safety and 
integrity.  
Overpackaging should be defined as packaging which is not functional, i.e. it is not essential to fulfil the functionalities 
of packaging foreseen by the Essential Requirements.  
Underpackaging should be defined as packaging which is sub -optimal, i.e.  it does not guarantee the fulfilment of 
packaging functionalities foreseen by the Essential Requirements.  
Reduce the overall EU climate and environmental impacts) 
To effectively tackle Green-House Gas (GHG) emissions and their consequent climate impact, it is essential to consider 
both the GHG emissions linked to the packaging lifecycle  as well as the GHG emissions linked to food and product 
waste and the savings guaranteed through packaging use. The same consideration applies for other environmental 
impacts.  
 
2. REUSE 
Reusable packaging systems have an important role to play in a circular economy. Well -designed systems can 
concretely contribute to resources and material savings while reducing the climat e and overall environmental impact 
of packaging. Upscaling reuse models needs a systemic change of business models and supply chain transformation. 
Reuse targets alone will not drive such systemic change.  
Reuse models’ design and management 
Careful management and design are essential to reap the environmental benefits (including GHG emissions 
reductions) linked to an increase in reusable packaging, while avoiding unintended consequences such as increased 
GHG emissions, energy and water consum ption, complex logistics, cleaning, food and health safety, and others. 
Accurate science -based impact assessments, including from a life -cycle perspective, need to accompany the 
introduction of reuse systems. In this context it is crucial to compare benefi ts and impacts of single -use and multiple 
use packaging for the same product categories. Societal and demographic developments also have implications for 
the effective deployment of reuse systems, which should be part of feasibility assessments. 
A major is sue to assess the environmental impact of business-to-consumer (B2C) reuse systems is data 
availability/quality. Without digital traceability of number of refills and transport distances, this is very hard to evaluate. 
 
   
 
Unique coding/serialization for packa ging that enables complete track and tracing, including refill cycles, would be 
necessary to assess the climate and environmental footprint of reuse systems.  
Reuse must include refill/recharge done both at home and in the shops. This will allow developing refill practices, which 
contributes to the reduction of material used, and ultimately to the reduction of GHG emissions and other  environmental 
impacts.  
Requirements for reuse and refill 
Reuse and refill should comply with the same regulatory requirements that exist for the introduction on the market of 
any packaged product.  The number of rotations of the packaging is not infinite, hence also reusable packaging should 
be recyclable at the end of its life cycle.   As the environmental benefits of reuse are strongly dependent on the number 
of times that the packaging is actually reused , appropriate consideration must be given to the minimum number of 
rotations that reusable packaging can endure. For example , a pragmatic approach could be e nvisaged to test the 
minimum number of rotations per packaging type.  
From niche to large scale reuse models 
To bring significant positive environmental outcomes, B2C reusable packa ging should be designed to be deployable 
at the level of the large distribution. This requires convenient collection infrastructure, including for on -the-go-
consumption, something which is missing largely from today’s Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) and Deposit-
Refund Schemes (DRS) systems. Besides the thorough assessment of overall environmental impact, the financial and 
logistics implications of establishing a take-back infrastructure and storage of empties need to be factored in. 
For beverages in particular, reuse models (through refillable bottles) in a B2C setting must rely on a DRS. When these 
are considered, they should fulfill a number of conditions:  
- Efficient, cost-effective and accountable, 
- Strong environmental outcomes, 
- Shared financial responsibility, 
- Convenient for consumers,  
- Long-term financial sustainability, 
- Allow producers to secure material for closed loop recycling. 
 
 
3. ESSENTIAL REQUIREMENTS 
The Essential Requirements (ER) for packaging must be strengthened, harmonised and properly enforced across the 
EU. They need to be ambitious to enable the transition towards the policy goals of the New Circular Economy Action 
Plan and the 2050 EU climate neutrality objective of the European Green Deal. 
While helping advance packaging sustainability, changes to the ER must preserve packaging’s functionalities, enable 
innovation and prevent restrictions to the free circulation of packaging and packaged goods in the Single Market. 
Defining recyclability  
A clear, harmonised and actionable definition of recyclability is key for the successful revision of the ER and 
implementation of the PPWD. It must be forward looking and not be based only on what is feasible today. This requires 
developing a definition that is technology and material neutral, that allows for  innovation in recycling technologies and 
infrastructure and for new innovative products to meet the definition of recyclability. This definition should foresee that 
 
   
 
a packaging can be recycled at scale into a product, material or substance, encompassing all recycling technologies 
including mechanical, organic and chemical.   
Enabling Innovation 
Strengthened ER can help drive innov ation across the packaging value chain. However, overregulation would have 
the opposite effect by acting as a barrier to the deployment of new and innovative packaging solutions.  
Packaging design is key to improve packaging recyclability. Industry efforts are already well underway to maximise the 
compatibility of packaging design with  the existing waste management infrastructures, protecting the product while 
minimising the use of resources and the climate impacts.  
 
Packaging design alone however will not suffice . To ensure that all packaging  is not just recyclable but effectively 
recycled by 2030 , packaging design must be considered together with the product, the waste management 
infrastructure and the recycling technologies. Setting prescriptive design requirements, with the goal of banning certain 
materials or design, would fail to recognize this.  
 
With the right incentives and investments in recycling infrastructure and technologies, the packaging that today is not 
recycled can be properly collected and sorted, building sufficient volumes to become economically attractive for 
recycling thus helping to create a virtuous  system. A positive example of the great potential of this approach is the 
recent commitment by Fostplus to recycle flexible packaging in Belgium following the example of Germany and The 
Netherlands.  
 
Strengthened EPR schemes, following effective minimum requirements and making the best use of producers’ fees in 
a smart way, will also play a critical role in creating the infrastructure to make all packaging recyclable.  
A shift from ER and harmonised standards towards over prescriptive r equirements would run counter to the goal of 
advancing packaging sustainability, particularly if such requirements fail to take on board innovation and if they only 
reflect the current  situation. 
A strong Single Market to drive circularity 
The ER should continue to constitute the legal minimum requirement that all packaging must meet in order to be 
allowed to enter and freely circulate throughout the EU market. Moving away from harmonised EU standards towards 
prescriptive requirements with different degre e of implementation at national level would open the door to divergent 
national legislation, technical barriers to trade and consequent erosion of the Single Market, thus undermining the legal 
basis of the PPWD. The scale of the Single Market and the certa inty of free movement of goods are essential to 
underpin the major investments in technology and product innovation needed to transition to a circular economy. 
Effective ER enforcement  
 
The process of strengthening the ER must be coupled with their effective enforcement across all Member States.  
Strengthening the ER alone will not suffice. They will remain ineffective in driving sustainable packaging design whilst 
they are not enforced. To address the lack of enforcement of the ER, which is currently left at the discretion of national 
authorities, the application of the CEN standards should become mandatory to prove compliance. To guarantee the 
smart and efficient enforceability of these standards, limiting unnecessary administrative burden, an option would be 
to consider the introduction of a n ex-post EU conformity assessment procedure to be enforced by national market 
surveillance authorities, similarly to what is already done for other regulated products.   
 
 
 
   
 
4. RECYCLED CONTENT 
In order to further drive the uptake of recycled content in packaging, the key policy priority is to ensure availability of 
Secondary Raw Materials  (SRM) of appropriate quality, including meeting the strict requirements for Food Contact 
Materials, at competitive prices. It is also essential to ensure the same level of consumer and product safety as well as 
of packaging performance relative to virgin materials. 
Developing a well-functioning market for Secondary Raw Materials  
The enabling EU policy framework to support the uptake of recycled content in packaging needs to incentivize the 
creation of a well-functioning EU market for SRM. Policy measures to drive the incorporation of recycled content should 
focus on specific applications where the uptake is still limited, effectively addressing the existing regulatory, economic 
and technical obstacles. 
➢ Inadequate sorting, collection and recycling infrastructures, lack of enforcement and uneven implementation 
of existing EU waste legislation are structural issues currently  hindering effective competition between 
recycled and virgin materials.  
➢ Market barriers and other national measures discriminating against SMR sourced from other Member States 
should not be permitted.  
➢ The Waste Shipment Regulation review should strengthen the transboundary movements of waste between 
Member States to support the creation of an EU internal market for SRM.  
➢ The development of effective EU end -of-waste criteria is another prerequisite for a genuine internal market 
for SRM. In parallel, the regulatory framework needs to effectively prevent landfilling and incineration of 
packaging. 
Investments and Infrastructures  
Adequate waste collection and sorting infrastructures in every Member State are essential to prevent the loss of 
valuable resources due to landfilling or incineration as well as to ensure that all packaging waste has access to separate 
collection, can be recycled and then reintegrated in new products. 
 
Improved collection, sorting and recycling technologies can enhance both the quality and quantity of recycled material 
in the EU. This is especially important considering today’s unavoidable losses due to contamination and processing. 
Therefore, any measures to boost the uptake of recycled of content will need to ensure that adequate investments take 
place, both from private and public sectors, in the development and scaling-up of technologies and infrastructures for 
the circular economy. 
 
➢ Businesses need long-term investment visibility to continue to develop innovative packaging and solutions for 
recycling processes and technologies. A concrete example of a breakthrough technology with great potential 
to substantially improve the sorting of packaging waste, hence driv ing the uptake of recycled content, is the 
Holy Grail project on digital watermarking of packaging promoted by several EUROPEN members.  
➢ Any definition of recycling needs to include mechanical, organic and chemical recycling in order to support 
the investments in innovative recycling technologies needed to improve the quality of recyclates.  
➢ If considered, any fiscal measures to incentivise packaging circularity should drive the increased availability 
of high -quality recycled materials across the EU at affordable prices  by encouraging and rewarding their 
uptake.  
 
 
 
   
 
Food Contact Material legislation and REACH 
 
A functioning EU approval process for the use of recycled plastics in Food Contact Materials is needed to ensure 
harmonisation across the EU, remove any legal uncertainty in trading SRM in food contact applications and provide a 
clarity in relations to other EU policies and legislation.  
➢ There is a need for thorough but timely EFSA approvals of processes for recycled materials to be used in 
contact with food or in contact with products that require food grade quality. 
 
Especially relevant will be to ensure coherence between measures to drive recycled content and the new EU Chemical 
Strategy and Food Contact Material legislation.  
➢ REACH and food safety legislation are based on the concept of risk, hence the approach taken for assessing 
packaging safety must also be based on risk vs. hazard: hazardous substances that may be present in 
packaging materials (e.g. as a result of recycling processes) should be subject to adequate risk management 
measures.  
➢ Traceability issues also needs to be adequately addressed: compliance with EU chemical legislation may be 
more difficult for recycled products as the traceability of all their chemical components remains a technical 
challenge. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
   
 
ANNEX:  EUROPEN feedback to the policy options listed in the Public Consultation questionnaire 
 
1. WASTE PREVENTION 
EU wide packaging restrictions/bans for product types 
The choice of packaging formats and materials is based on multiple considerations associated to the packaged product. 
Any such measures should only be considered when they can effectively contribute to reducing climate and 
environmental footprint of packaging, without other life-cycle tradeoffs, and when there are proven alternatives that can 
be readily used. Any ban should otherwise take into account the time necessary to develop alternatives in the way 
products are delivered to consumers.  
National targets/limits on packaging waste generation  
Targets on packaging waste generation, based on the current definition of packaging waste, which equals packaging 
put on the market, would result in an arbitrary target on packaging, completely unrelated from an assessment of either 
product specific packaging functionalities or environmental trade -offs. This would also fail to distinguish between 
residual packaging waste going to landfill or being incinerated and packaging waste which is reused and recycled. 
Bulk sales  
Bulk sales are already envisaged for certain product categories and new bulk sales models are being developed. 
However, these are not suitable for all products, particularly depending on shelf-life. In all cases, sale of loose products 
should not lead to increased product/food waste and should comply with the same requirements of packaged products, 
with regards to product safety, health, traceability and product information.  
E-Commerce 
E-Commerce platforms should own financial waste management responsibility for the packaging they place on the 
market through participation in EPR schemes. Today, this is not the case. Like any other mandatory limits on 
packaging, also limits on e-commerce packaging needs to be based on the same considerations as to the preservation 
of packaging functionalities.  
Mandatory packaging waste prevention plans for companies and organisations 
Waste prevention plans are increasingly being adopted by companies and o rganisations. Rather than imposing 
mandatory obligations, the EU should support awareness raising and best practice sharing.  
Mandatory registry for producers  
The actual efficiency and enforceability of a registry   to report packaging data is  questionable and the quality of the 
collected data risk not to be suitable enough to justify the administrative burden to collect and evaluate them  (e.g. 
numerous changes throughout the year).  
Mandatory threshold ratios of packaging to product  
The establishment of targets for product/packaging ratio are not suitable as they can never suitably reflect the large 
variety of packaging materials and formats. A simplistic ratio approach will overlook  packaging functionalities and 
resource efficiency. As unintended consequence, they could create underpackaging with the negative impact such as 
an increase in GHG Emissions, e.g. resulting from food waste. 
 
 
   
 
2. REUSE 
Requirement on mandatory use reusable packaging for some transport packaging e.g. pallets.  
Transport packaging already has a satisfactory reuse rate, therefore a mandatory requirement is not considered 
necessary. Single-use pallets are in effect re-used several times.  
Guidance on effective reuse systems  
Guidance to develop effective reuse systems through reference to a European standard would be welcome provided 
it takes into account essential aspects, e.g. standardized uniform packaging, pools to share, local distribution, minimum 
refill rates etc. To be effectively enforced, such a standard would require digital tracing and unique coding so that data 
could be centrally managed and verified.  
National advisory body on the benefits of reuse  
The creation of an advisory body helping businesses assess the benefits of reusable products and packagin g should 
be considered a European rather than at national level, to promote a common approach as national solutions would 
result in Single Market distortions. Even a European solution, however, would not provide a system to assess the 
impacts of imports/exports.  
Country level taxes on single use packaging to incentivize using less or reusable packaging 
The Commission should not promote fiscal measures that will distort the EU internal market and could incentivize 
potentially ineffective reuse systems. 
Requirement that all reusable packaging must be recyclable  
Boosting recycling and reuse are complementary solutions to advance packaging sustainability, together with driving 
low-carbon packaging solutions. To ensure that the reusable packaging is returned, rather than be disposed for 
recycling, deposit level/incentives must be right and the number/accessibility of collection points must be sufficient.  
Requirement for all reusable packaging to be clearly labelled as reusable 
Clear harmonised labelling would help to better inform consumers and avoid that reusable packaging gets used and 
disposed for recycling before the end of its life -cycle, to avoid negative environmental trade-offs. In parallel, reusable 
packaging put on the EU market should not be accounted as packaging waste. 
Use of GPP criteria to require the use of reusable packaging within the public sector 
The same considerations on the effectiveness of reuse systems (environmental footprint, logistics, health and safety, 
etc.) should apply also to reusable packaging for public sector procurement. 
 
3. ESSENTIAL REQUIREMENT 
Requirement that all packaging should be reusable or recyclable  
Considerable investments are being made in innovative packaging solutions and technologies  to meet the CEAP 2.0 
goal that all packaging should be reusable or recyclable in an economically viable way by 2030.  This goal must be 
supported by strengthening ER and ensure they are effectively enforced across the EU. Definitions and core criteria 
(low-carbon, reusable, recyclable) must be clearly defined in the body of the Directive t o avoid any ambiguity for 
stakeholders and ensure harmonised and effective implementation across the EU. 
 
   
 
Defining recyclability and Packaging Design 
Currently ‘recyclable’ is defined very loosely in the Essential Requirements and in further detail in CEN Standard. Any 
attempt to define prescriptive lists of negative and positive packaging properties as the criteria for definition of the 
recyclability of packaging will hamper innovation in this space. Lists of packaging properties that could pose a barrier 
to recycling would be based on existing available technologies that allow for certain materials to be recyclable, thereby 
hampering further improvement in product design based on new recycling technologies. It will be important to allow for 
innovation, and for new packaging solutions to demonstrate their recyclability at scale. 
Similarly, setting minimum recycling thresholds for packaging formats would restrict packaging to the formats that have 
been proven to be recyclable . This leads to setting for today status quo, rather than supporting new and innovative 
packaging solutions for a real step change in packaging recycling (e.g. full recyclability of flexible packaging, of 
multimaterial packaging applications, etc.) 
Minimum volume and weight of packaging  
The issue that needs to be addressed first and foremost is the poor enforcement of ER. Amending Standard EN 13428 
will prove ineffective if it will continue to remain unenforced.  
Packaging protect products and preven ts waste. Any measures intended to address oversize or excess packaging 
need to be carefully defined and balanced to ensure the packaging functionality are not undermined. A one-size fits all 
approach will fail to address the fact that different packaging types and formats are necessary to meet different product 
needs. A clear definition of overpackaging (non-functional packaging) and underpackaging (sub-optimal packaging) is 
needed.  
Limiting the information displayed on the packaging to legally required information would be the worst example of 
overregulation. Branding and marketing are essential elements of a market economy.  
Requirement to reduce type of polymers to increase recycling rates 
Packaging design cannot be considered in isolation, as packaging design alone is not the answer to increasing 
recyclability. It must be considered in conjunction with recycling technologies and infrastructure. Setting prescriptive 
design requirements, with the goal of banning certain materials or design specifications, would fail to recognize this. 
An industry-driven process towards the simplification of the polymers used in packaging should be preferred to ensure 
technical feasibility and protection of packaging functionalities.  
Update CEN Standard 13432 to further specify compostable and biodegradable packaging 
The revision of the standard EN 13432 should reflect all available technologies for composting looking at best practices 
across Europe. Biodegradable and compostable packaging should be considered as part of a wider strategy enhancing 
the organic recycling of bio-waste (the biggest waste fraction of municipal solid waste across Europe), which needs to 
deliver high quality compost, vital for the regeneration of degraded soils as important carbon sink useful to fight climate 
change.  
The mandatory separate collection of bio -waste will start in 2024, thus composting infrastructure needs to be further 
developed to treat the inevitable increase of bio-waste, since that today only 34% of bio-waste and 16% of food waste 
are being separately collected in Europe.   
 
 
 
   
 
Requirement mandating compostable packaging when packaging is likely to end up in separately collected 
organic waste 
Organic waste accounts for more than 50% of the total municipal solid waste. Compostable packaging is preferable 
and ideal for food related products, as such packaging can be collected together with food residues and bio-waste as 
they present similar biodegradation properties. Organic waste can be recycled through organic recycling technologies.  
The choice of packaging formats and materials is based on multiple considerations associated to the packaged product. 
A future sustainable and resilient food suppl y system needs a variety of packaging solutions, including single use, 
multi-material, compostable / biodegradable and reusable packaging.  ER should reflect the characteristics of 
biodegradable and compostable materials in order to be placed on the market and not setting criteria restricting the 
types of packaging that can be designed for composting.  
Requirement mandating compostable packaging when it would facilitate the collection of organic waste 
Compostable packaging for food related products, helps to increase a cleaner, separate collection of the organic waste 
stream. The separate collection of organic waste can be recycled organically, avoiding that the waste fraction will end 
up in landfills, where generates methane, which has an environmental impac t regarding emissions worse than CO2. 
The correct management of the organic waste fraction contributes to the low carbon principle 
As of today, this kind of restriction would work for a non -packaging item like the compostable bin liners.  Bio -waste 
bags made of compostable plastics (which are not packaging under the PPWD) increase the capture rate of bio-waste. 
Essential requirements should reflect the characteristics of these materials in order to be placed on the market and not 
setting criteria restricting the types of packaging that can be designed for composting. 
Requirement banning compostable / biodegradable packaging for applications not related to food waste 
capture 
Even though compostable packaging can be assessed as a preferable choice for food rel ated products, which 
consequently allows for the increased food waste capture through organic recycling, setting any type of banning or 
restrictive criteria for the material to be only used in certain applications, sets a discriminatory and not proportiona l 
approach. Such limited approach would also hamper research and innovation. The strategic thinking should involve 
investments to further develop recycling infrastructures for the different waste streams, to be treated through all 
available recycling technologies – mechanically, organically and chemically. 
Requirement for all recyclable packaging to be clearly labelled as recyclable and all non-recyclable packaging 
to be clearly labelled as non-recyclable 
To strengthen ER compliance and help consumers better sort their household waste, we need a harmonised EU 
labelling system and harmonised EU definitions.  This information should be featured on the packaging where 
technically feasible. Consumers should be clearly and transparently informed about the collection availability and the 
type of material they have in their hands. EPR schemes and obliged companies should guarantee the recyclability of 
packaging. 
A common recycling logo might in principle not be useful anymore if all packaging is due to be recyclable by 2030.  
Requirement for all compostable packaging to be clearly labelled as compostable  
To help consumers better sort their household waste, compostable packaging are today already labeled to indicate 
compliance to the EN13432 norm. To strengthen ER compliance, we need a harmonised EU labelling system. A clear 
definition of biodegradable plastics is already available in the SUP Directive and in the current formulation (updated in 
 
   
 
2015) of the Essential Requirements. A further step would be to harmonize the two Requirements, “Packaging 
recoverable in the form of composting” and “Biodegradable packaging” merging them into a clearer one.    
Requirement for all reusable packaging to be clearly labelled as reusable 
It is important to avoid that reusable packaging gets used and disposed of as single -use to avoid negative 
environmental trade-offs. For this purpose a ha rmonised EU label for reusable packaging should be developed to 
clearly inform consumers. 
EU guidance on dimension limits for packaging used for online purchases  
If introduced, dimension limits for packaging used for e-commerce, must be set in the form of enforceable ER. Defining 
this only through non -binding EU guidelines would result in ineffective implementation and divergent national 
measures. Considering that a large part of e -commerce is cross-border, this would have a major negative impact on 
the integrity of the Single Market. Reusable packaging systems should also be considered for e-commerce. 
 
4. RECYCLED CONTENT 
Inclusion in the Essential Requirements of a new CEN Standard setting out a mandatory process to assess 
the potential to include recycled content. 
In order to further drive the uptake of recycled content, it is worth considering the inclusion of a requirement in the 
Essential Requirements for a new CEN Standard setting out a mandatory process to be followed to assess the 
potential to include recycled content in all packaging types. Such process should have a smart and efficient design to 
avoid unnecessary administrative burden and take into product safety and food contact requirements. 
Recycled content targets for specific packaging formats made of plastics or other materials. 
 
The ability of companies to boost the uptake of recycled content in their products will be influenced and/or conditional 
upon several factors, many of which are outside the control of the packaging value chain (i.e., the regulatory and fiscal 
landscape for packaging, the availability of SRM as well as market dynamics). This implies the need for coordinated 
actions on th e part of EU policy -makers, Member States, waste management operators and other relevant 
stakeholders.  
Encouraging the incorporation of recycled content through voluntary commitments (e.g. C ircular Plastic Alliance) can 
support the creation of the right conditions for developing the markets for SRM. The Commission should fully take into 
account the ongoing voluntary initiatives for the uptake of recycled content  in designing policy measures to the same 
end.  
In considering recycling content targets for specific plastic packaging formats where the uptake of recycled content is 
still limited, consideration must be given to possible competitive distortions between polymers. This will be necessary 
to avoid simple substitution of polymers and/or formats with negative overall environmental trade-offs.  
Efficient and reliable methods to prove the percentage of recycled content for all packaging materials and applications 
also need to be in place prior to considering the setting up of mandatory targets to prevent issues with verification and 
enforcement of these requirements.   
Furthermore, for packaging formats already incorporating high levels of recycled content, policy measures should avoid 
unnecessary requirements focusing instead on enabling innovation.

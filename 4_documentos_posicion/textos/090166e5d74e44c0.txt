Public consultation to inform the review of the requirements for 
packaging and other measures to prevent packaging waste 
FEVE position paper 
 
 
FEVE is the association of European manufacturers of glass containers.  The glass packaging industry 
producing a wide range of glass packaging products for food and beverages, perfumery, cosmetics and 
pharmacy for European and global customers. The European Container Glass manufacturing is a 
genuine circular model, which perfectly fits with the EU’s ambition to build a circular economy. FEVE 
is therefore committed to support the European Commission in assessing the options for reinforcing 
the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive  (PPWD)’s Essential Requirements and other meas ures 
to reduce the generation of packaging and packaging waste. FEVE supports the general objective of 
the Directive, which is to ensure a well-functioning internal market through fully harmonised rules on 
packaging, while tackling negative impacts on the environment and health from packaging and 
packaging waste. 
 
In addition to our contribution to the public consultation, we would like to outline in this document 
our position on some of the policy options considered by the European Commission, as well as o n 
additional measures that would help achieve the objectives of the Packaging and Packaging Waste 
Directive. Overall, we regret that the questionnaire of the public consultation only allows stakeholders 
to assess the level of effectiveness and efficiency of the proposed policy measures based on the 
environmental and cost impacts they would have, without providing the opportunity to highlight 
additional important considerations or to make a d istinction between different packaging materials 
or formats when necessary.  
 
Executive summary  
 
I. Waste prevention measures: While we fully share the European Commission’s objective to reduce 
over-packaging and packaging waste, any waste prevention measures should take into account the 
differentiated contribution of various packaging materials to waste generation and their intrinsic 
properties (e.g. ability to be recycled again and again without degrading over time). W aste 
prevention should be achieved through reduction at source  by material and circular packaging 
materials such as glass  should not be discriminated against because of their weight . In addition, 
potential measures to address “overpackaging” should duly take into account packaging 
functionalities, which should be acknowledged and protected. Finally, reuse models in packaging 
for transport and logistics have existed for a very long time , whereas consumer packaging reuse 
models are much more complex and less contemporary. Despite this there are many examples of 
such reusable glass packaging systems in place . Although setting targets for reusable packaging 
sound appealing, FEVE cautions that setting such targets do not reflect the complexities and 
dynamics of the packaging market and may not be effective to promote consumer reuse packaging.  
 
II. Measures to reinforce the essential requirements to improve design for reuse and promote high 
quality recycling and strengthen their enforcement: FEVE fully supports the Commission’s 
intention to require that all packaging placed on the EU market shall be reusable or recyclable in 
an economically viable way by 2030 and to introduce a clear, harmonised and enforceable 
definition of “recyclable packaging”.  The definition of “recyclable packaging” proposed by 
EUNOMIA in the final report on the Effectiveness of the Essential Requirements for Packaging and 
Packaging Waste and Proposals for Reinforcement  is comprehensive and a very good basis for 
further discussion and could be complemented by the use of design for recycling methodologies.

I. Waste prevention measures 
 
The European Commission notes that “despite an overall trend towards light weighting across a wider 
range of packaging formats in the last three decades, there has been an overall increase in packaging 
waste generated” and the generation of  packaging waste reached a record high in 2017. It will 
therefore consider measures to reduce over-packaging and packaging waste.  
 
1. Member State level packaging waste generation reduction targets or limits 
 
While we fully share the European Commission’s objective to reduce over -packaging and packaging 
waste, FEVE does not support Member State level packaging waste generation reduction targets or 
limits relative to population or Gross Domestic Product (GDP).  
 
• Glass packaging waste generation has remained stable: While overall, the quantity of packaging 
waste generated has indeed been increasing (9% growth in tonnage between 2007 and 2017 1), 
the glass packaging waste tonnage has remained stable thanks in a large part to light weighting 
and reuse. Glass bottles and jars have seen a 35% reduction in unit weight since 1990, well above 
the all packaging average of 26% decrease in unit weight.  In addition, by collecting and using 
recycled glass multiple times in a closed loop, the glass industry significantly reduces the use of 
other virgin raw materials. Glass recycling has increased by more than 139% in the last 20 years 
and 76% of glass is collected to be recycled into a bottle closed loop in Europe today. Despite the 
growth in glass packaging, normalised comparison of GDP versus waste generation between 2010 
and 2017 (normalised to 201 0) shows that for  glass packaging  the rate of increase in waste 
generation was below that of the rate of increase in GDP, inferring, unlike other materials, that a 
level of decoupling occurred  (see Annex for more details) . The normalised comparison of 
population versus waste generation between 2010 and 2017 (normalised to 2010) highlights that

• Switching to a lighter alternative material does not constitute waste prevention:  In the 
Background Paper on Packaging Waste Prevention, Eunomia notes that Permanent Materials – 
glass and metals – which can be infinitely recycled without losing their properties have been to 
some extent substituted by other materials and “difficult to recycle p ackaging”2. There must be 
an approach to ensure that all packaging reduce their environmental impact for their own 
material. Switching to lighter materials from heavier materials will not necessarily reduce 
environmental impacts but could result in fact in increased adverse consequences and volumes of 
units of unrecyclable/unreusable packaging. Achieving waste prevention through reduction at 
source by material should follow the CEN standard and the revised PPWD should highlight that 
“the substitution of one packaging material by another is not a basis for source reduction”.  
 
2. Efficient use of packaging to tackle “overpackaging” 
 
• Packaging serves multiple functions, which must not be overlooked . Packaging is designed to 
protect a specific product (e.g. from air, humidity, light, external pressures during transport, 
bacteria etc.), convey product information, prolong shelf-life, preserve quality, make it safe, easy-
to-use and attractive for consumers and create brand differentiation . The design of packagi ng 
consists of finding the right compromise between packaging’s ability to fulfil all these functions 
and environmental considerations. Potential measures to address “overpackaging”, including the 
revision of the critical areas  that limit further reduction s in the volume or weight of packaging , 
should duly tak e into account packaging functionalities, which should be acknowledged and 
protected.  
 
• The introduction of packaging to product ratios is currently not feasible . The European 
Commission is considering the introduction of reporting requirements if the packaging exceeds a 
specific packaging to product ratio or the setting of mandatory thresholds for the packaging to 
product ratio. We question the feasibility of introducing such measures in the absence of data on 
packaging and products parameters (i.e. weight, volume, area) available to calculate the ratios 
and set the thresholds at a meaningful and granular enough level. A considerable amount of data 
on specific produ ct and packaging types would need to be collected before considering such 
ratios. The final report on the Effectiveness of the Essential Requirements for Packaging and 
Packaging Waste and Proposals for Reinforcement  concluded that “it does not currently seem 
feasible to either set one generic ratio or a number of different ratios for specific product or 
packaging types, mainly because the evidence is not currently available to indicate whether a ratio 
is justified (i.e. it would have a clear beneficial impact) and at what level any ratios would be set, 
or for which types of packaging”.  
 
• Weight is only one of the parameters of packaging design . The container glass industry is 
committed to reducing the weight of its products. Light-weighting is an area of substantial interest 
to most customers of glass packaging as sustainability and carbon footprint have become key 
priorities. However, weight is only one aspect of packaging design and not the most important 
factor in terms of environmental footprint for gla ss. The graph below shows that increasing 
recycled content in glass bottles has a much bigger impact than lightweighting in terms of CO2 
emissions reduction. Packaging should be considered together with the packaged product to 
reduce its environmental impact over its whole life-cycle. Extending shelf life, avoiding food waste 
and littering, preserving taste, preventing food contamination and recyclability are critical aspects

3. Reuse measures and targets 
 
a. Reusable glass containers in a nutshell 
 
• Reuse models in packaging  for transport and logistics  have existed for a very long time while 
consumer retail reuse models are less contemporary. Despite this there are many examples of 
consumer reusable glass packaging systems in place. Germany, Croatia and Czech Republic are 
the European leaders in terms of market share of refillable beverage containers. Glass dominates 
the refillable beverage containers market and  the beer, soft drinks and water categories are the 
key segments for refillable beverage containers. We therefore regret and question that glass is 
not listed in the reusable packaging item options in question 6.1.1, contrary to plastic, metal and 
bamboo, which represent a minor share of the refillable water bottle market. This question also 
disregards the fact that refillable containers are not only used for water bottles but also in the 
beer and soft drinks categories.  
 
• While being both sustainable from an environmental, economic and social perspective, reusable 
glass bottles have distinct characteristics compared to one-way glass containers:  
- Design: Durability is the key factor, with more material used per bottle. The shape, weight and 
size of company-branded or standard bottles are designed for reuse.  
- Investment: Putting re -usable packaging on the consumer market requires a considerable 
upfront investment (i.e. empty glass bottles, transport crates, filling infrastructure and set up 
of return schemes). 
- Collection: It depends on private agreements between competing brands on the market and 
retailers to set up and manage deposit schemes, as well as collect and stock empty bottles.  
- Resource efficiency: Glass bottles can be reused up to 50 times, most effectively when used 
locally, in short -distance markets. Reuse bottles can also be recycled  at end of life, saving 
virgin raw materials, and reducing energy consumption and CO2 emissions.

b. Policy considerations moving forward 
 
• Although setting targets for reusable packaging sound appealing, FEVE cautions that setting 
such targets do not reflect the complexities and dynamics of the consumer packaging market 
and may not be effective to promote reuse packaging as has shown to be the case in Germany. 
Introducing reuse systems would represent a systemic change of business models and supply 
chains and FEVE considers that it is premature to consider the introduction of targets on reuse. 
We would however welcome a thorough assessment of and discussion on the environmental, 
socio-economic, consumer safety impacts of consumer reuse systems, materials compatible with 
reuse systems, with a deep-dive into the benefits and drawbacks of reuse systems in comparison 
with single-use systems. The establishment of a national advisory body could help in that regard, 
provided that the participation of the industry is ensured and that the assessment of the benefits 
of reusable products is science-based.  
 
• Reuse should clearly be defined. If all packaging are required to be recyclable or reusable by 2030, 
it is critical that a clear definition of “reuse”, and conversely of “single-use”, is set in the PPWD, to 
complement the current definition3 based on the below considerations: 
- The definition of reuse of packaging should only cover products specifically conceived and 
designed to accomplish within their life cycle a minimum number of trips or rotations in 
order to avoid any risks linked to the use of products unsuited for reuse. T he minimum 
number of trips or rotations to be accomplished should be specified.  
- Reusable packaging is that which can effectively and efficiently be reused at scale and in 
practice in order to ensure that a well -functioning industrial system (organisationa l, 
technical or financial arrangements) for reuse is in place so that reusable packaging performs 
multiple rotations.  
- Packaging is considered reusable when its main or structural components are reusable. This 
means that a reusable beer bottle is considered a reusable packaging even though the cap 
is not reusable.  
- Reusable packaging should be recyclable.  
- Reusable packaging should maintain product and food safety even after multiple trips. 
- The Essential Requirements should acknowledge that reusable packaging needs to be 
heavier than one-way packaging in order to be durable and accommodate multiple rotations.  
 
• A clear distinction should be made between consumer packaging and packaging that is used in 
a business-to-business or transit context.  The collection of consumer packaging requires a lot 
more investment and partnership than business-to-business transit packaging, which is based on 
products that are designed to make several rotations within a business -to-business context.  
Consumer packaging is a far greater challenge for resource efficiency and to reduce littering than 
business-to-business transit packaging. A target on reuse of all packaging without distinction 
would create a distortion of statistics, artificially boosting rates for some materials that are present 
on the business-to-business transit market over those that are not. 
 
• Any policy measures on reuse should consider the impacts on the food and beverage packaging 
market, and the relative impact on some materials over others to contribute towards the 
attainment of reuse objectives. A target on reuse of consumer packaging for food and beverages 
would affect a limited number of materials, due to their inherent properties, including food safety

• Some reuse markets for glass packaging exist but they are typically local . Reusable glass 
packaging is used by food and beverage customers (brands, fillers) for fast -moving consumer 
goods, and operate on local deposi t-refund schemes for reuse (e.g. Germany). Such reusable

• Reuse systems should respect the functioning of the Internal market. Setting a reuse target may 
create distortions and barriers to the free movement of goods in the internal market as reuse 
systems are local. It is important that the Euro pean Commission clarif ies the conditions under

measures do not lead to distortions of competition and fragmentation of the internal market.  
 
• Fillers decide to use one-way or reusable products based on thorough consideration of logistics, 
environmental, technical, industrial, marketing and consumer aspects of reuse compared to 
one-way packaging. Refillables are not suited for all markets and brands should have the flexibility 
to offer a choice to their consumers. 
 
• Reuse should be recognised as top of the Waste hierarchy. Reusable food and beverage 
packaging is a non-waste and should be considered as a waste prevention measure and top of the 
waste hierarchy. Glass packaging does not undergo any preparation for reuse,  process or repair 
other than cleaning. If EN 13428 is revised to refine the critical areas that limit further reductions 
in the volume or weight of packaging, it should recognise that the minimum packaging weight is 
likely to be heavier if it is reusable.

2. Promoting infinite recycling and the role of permanent materials  
 
• Glass is a Permanent material that can be infinitely recycled in a closed loop or bottle to bottle 
recycling. Permanent materials have the potential to be recycled over and over again without 
losing their inherent properties. Once they are produced for the first time, and properly collected 
and processed at its end of life, they become raw materials for new and endless production loops, 
hence contributing to maintaining circular material loops. Such materials are and will remain at 
the heart of any proven and well-functioning Circular Economy. 
 
• Permanent materials must be incentivised as they reduce dependence on the use of virgin 
materials for packaging and are safe for human health and the environment. The review of the 
PPWD is an opportunity to encourage more circular packaging. The Directive ref ers to “high -
quality recycling” but does not define this concept. As “high -quality recycling” is mentioned in 
relation to recycling rate targets 5, we support a definition that would introduce a distinction 
between multi and non-multi recyclable packaging materials:  
• Multi recyclable”: potential to be recycled with no loss of the main intrinsic properties. The 
recycling process for these materials can be repeated over and over again with no loss of the 
main intrinsic material properties and thus maintain a circular material loop. 
• “Non-multi recyclable”: potential to be recycled, but with significant loss of the intrinsic 
properties and this already a fter a limited number of recycling loops (1 -7). The recycling 
process for these materials leads to a gradual degradation of the original material. In other 
cases, the recycled material cannot substitute the primary material on a like-for-like basis. This 
qualifies it as a non-circular material loop and downcycling.

III. Measures related to increasing recycled content in packaging to ensure a well -
functioning market for secondary raw materials 
 
• Introducing requirements on recycled content has been conceived as a market driver for 
materials which today are not effectively recycled due to technical and market limitations 
directly linked to their inherent properties . This approach is not effective for fully recyclable 
materials such as glass for which the increase of the average recycled content is directly linked to 
the availability of more, better-quality recycled glass.  
 
• On average, market demand for quality recycled glass exceeds the supply in Europe. Technically, 
bottles and jars can be produced with up to 100% of recycled glass, and so endlessly. Glass is a 
very simple material that can be endlessly and easily recycled into new glass applications, without 
any degradation of its quality. It always re mains safe even when recycled. Glass packaging 
manufacturers are keen to use recycled glass as it brings major environmental benefits:  
- On average, a 10% increase of cullet in the furnace decreases its energy use by 3% and CO2 
emissions by 5%.  
- Cullet use also reduces reliance on newly extracted raw materials and the energy, and 
emissions associated with this. One ton of cullet replaces 1.2 tons of virgin raw materials.   
All good quality recycled glass goes back in a closed loop to a production facility to  produce new 
packaging. Glass which is not recycled in a bottle for market reasons can be recycled into other 
applications and thus avoid being lost.

Nevertheless, in countries where there is colour-sorted separate collection, the industry is capable

• Setting a recycled content target is not a solution to actually increase recycled content in glass 
packaging. Optimised separate collection and sorting are the prerequisite to guaranteeing high-
quality recycling processes, and ultimately higher recycled content: 
- Member States having difficulties to meet the new ambitious EU recycling targets should be 
helped by targeted EU funding to install an innovative collection and sorting infrastructure 
and to invest in high quality recycling facilities. In the case of glass such investments have an 
immediate beneficial environmental and economic impact since this material is easy to 
recycle and has a positive scrap value.  
- Sharing of best practices on separate collection among Member States should be promoted 
at EU level to make collection simple for the consumer and optimal for the recycling value 
chain. For instance, the bottle bank system for glass packaging has achieved over 90% 
collection in several countries already.  
- Member States should improve and harmonise existi ng separate collection systems at 
national level, taking account of regional and local circumstances. As a first step, for example, 
a Member State should have at least a consistent colour scheme for the different types of 
waste they collect through either curbside collection or a bring-system. 
- Consumers play a key role in waste collection and sorting and it is critical for Member States 
to continue initiatives to further promote accessible and easy to understand guidance for 
the consumers to sort and dispose their waste. 
- Multi-stakeholder partnerships aiming to increase the collection and recycling of packaging 
materials, such as Close the Glass Loop , should be supported. This multi-stakeholder 
Partnership brings together glass manufacturers, glass recyclers, food & beverage producers, 
extended producer responsibility schemes, and local and regional authorities to achieve a 
90% average EU collection rate of used glass packaging by 2030 and improve the quality of 
recycled glass.  
 
IV. Additional measures for consideration to e nsure the safe recycling of packaging 
materials into food contact applications 
 
• FEVE supports policy initiatives that set rules to ensure the safe recycling of packaging materials 
into food contact applications. The success of the circular economy will depend on a market for 
secondary raw materials that competes in terms of cost, qual ity and safety with virgin raw 
materials. However, only recycled materials in food contact applications that do not pose a risk to 
human health ( re-entering waste materials into the production process increases the risk of 
contamination of the final products placed on the market) and the environment (risks caused by 
migration and release of hazardous chemicals in material cycles) should be placed on the market.  
 
• Against this backdrop, e mphasis should be made on closed loop recycling for food contact 
materials, to increase the safety of the recycled materials that enter into contact with food. Closed 
loop recycling limits the risk of contamination at the recycling stage, but it is also a guarantee that 
the packaging placed on the market has the highest possible recycled content – rather than being 
produced from virgin raw materials. 
 
• We regret that the policy options relating to the use of hazardous substances in packaging 
considered in the final report on the Effectiveness of the Essential Requirements for Packaging 
and Packaging Waste and Proposals for Reinforcement are not part of the public consultation. 
In light of the EU’s focus on moving towards a circular economy and as recently highlighted in the

About FEVE  
 
FEVE is the association of European manufacturers of glass containers.  The glass packaging industry 
generates 125 000 direct and indirect jobs thanks to 160 Container glass production sites in Europe 
producing a wide range of glass packaging products for food and beverages, perfumery, cosmetics and 
pharmacy for European and global customers. FEVE members have plants in 23 European Member 
States. Container glass is one of Europe’s best recycled products. See more on www.feve.org.

REVISION OF THE PPWD’ ESSENTIAL REQUIREMENTS:   
A FOCUS ON BIODEGRADABLE & COMPOSTABLE PLASTICS PACKAGING 
 
Introduction 
The review of the Packaging and Packaging Waste D irective (PPWD) in 2018 required  the European 
Commission to examine the feasibility of reinforcing the Essential Requirements (ERs) for packaging with a 
view to “ improving design for reuse and promoting high quality recycling, as well as strengthening thei r 
enforcement”. In this context and, moreover, in the context of the EU’s New Circular Economy Package and 
Plastics Strategy , the European Commission will  work on the revision of the Essential Requirements for 
placing packaging (Directive 1994/64).  
The European Commiss ion assigned to th e consultants, Eunomia Research & Consulting Ltd, a study 
assessing possible options and feasibility for the review of the PPWD’s Essential Requirements  (ER). The 
study was recently published 1 and this document represent a first analysis with a particular focus on 
biodegradable and compostable plastics packaging.    
 
Executive Summary 
The study on the legal review of the ER focuses on the need to provide a definition of “recyclable”  
packaging. Within the several options prov ided by Eunomia, it seems that the definition does not include 
the organic recycling/reprocessing of packaging.  
Not including the reprocessing of compostable packaging (both paper and cellulose) within the definition of 
recyclable packaging would not resp ect EU legislation (Waste Framework Directive and Packaging and 
Packaging Waste Directive) creating legal uncertainty and would lead to different legislative frameworks 
across Europe. It is, therefore, paramount that the definition of “recyclable” packagin g includes 
expressively organic recycling.   
For the revision of the Essential Requirements for biodegradable and compostable packaging  Eunomia 
outlines three different options. The preferred option – in line with the other study carried out by Eunomia 
“Relevance of Biodegradable and Compostable Consumer Plastic Products and Packaging in a Circular 
Economy”2 – would introduce a requirement for an assessment at the packaging specification/design stage 
of whether or not compostable packaging is suitable for the intended application  and “ choices for 
products and packaging should prioritise recyclability over compostability”.This option would:  
• fix by law what could be done with compostable plastics – which per se  is a serious 
discrimination for these materials; 
• neglect the organic recycling/reprocessing of packaging  creating a hierarchy among recycling 
technologies  that represents a breach of EU legislation (WFD and PPWD).

1. Definition of “recyclable” packaging 
The study on the legal review of the ER focuses on the need to provide a definition of “recyclable” , which 
should help to define which packaging is effectively and efficie ntly recycled at European level and 
consequently admitted on the market. The study gives three options:  
• Option 1 : Recyclable defined by qualitative statements : it would include general conditions 
regarding collection and reprocessing of packaging.  
“Recyclable packaging is that which can be effectively and efficiently separated from the waste 
stream, collected, sorted and aggregated into defined streams for recycling processes, and recycled 
at scale through relevant industrial processes such that it is turned into a secondary raw material, in 
line with Article 6a of the PPWD for calculating recycling targets, and of a sufficient quality that it 
can find end markets to replace the use of primary raw material. Innovative packaging placed on 
the market that re quires new infrastructure to be developed shall be recycled at scale within a 
certain period of time. At least 95% of the functional unit of packaging shall be recyclable according 
to this definition, with the remaining minor components compatible with the  relevant recycling 
process and not hindering the recyclability of the main components, through reference to CR 
13688.” 
As admitted by  Eunomia a practical in terpretation will vary between Member S tates, due to 
differences in recycling capacities and technologies.  
• Option 2: Attach to the above reported definition of recyclable a combination of both DfR (design 
for recycling/positive-negative lists) and recycling rate approach. At its essence, the DfR approach  
would require the adoption and regular updating (every 3 years) of lists of design features that are 
compatible with or disrupt recycling processes . The recycling rate approach would add that 
producers would have to prove that they are recyclable and recycled above a thresh old level  
(20% is suggested). 
The approach to design for recycling criteria has evolved into a complex series of permutations 
aiming to address the shortfalls highlighted by stakeholders of such a system.  The procedure to 
establish the lists would involve a technical committee and the final deliverable would take the 
form of a legally -binding act, probably adopted by comitology . Eunomia suggests that all 
stakeholders would be represented on the technical committee, nevertheless it is not clear what 
would be the legal basis for civil society stakeholders’ participation and for an appeal process. Only 
EU institutional stakeholders have the legal authority under the Treaties to adopt legally -binding 
acts via a technical committee 
• Option 3: Attach to the above r eported definition of recyclable a recycling rate threshold  would 
be added. Producers would have to prove that the packaging is recyclable and recycled above a 
threshold level (20% is suggested) at European level.  
Eunomia states that this would be a more objective approach and one that would define the ‘what’ 
without prescribing the ‘how’, thus offering more flexibility in packaging design. However, a 
significant effort would be required to make it technically feasible.  
CONSIDERATION ON “RECYCLABLE” DEFINITION’S OPTIONS  
Concerning biodegradable and compostable plastic packaging  it should be recognized that the current 
definition of “recyclable” (option 1) might neglect the organic recycling/reprocessing of packaging , 
even though the definition proposed recalls article 6a of the PPWD which includes organic recycling in 
line with the current Essential Requirements. If this recycling technology – thus compostable materials – 
3 
 
would be excluded from this definition it would represent a serious violation of EU legislation  and it 
would discriminate  a packaging  that ha s been on the market for over 25 years complaint to EU’s 
legislation and relative harmonized standards (EN 13432) . The definition of “recyclable” packaging 
needs to explicitly comprise not only mechanic but also organic recycling as is the case in WFD Art 3 point 
17, which states: 
‘recycling’ means any recovery operation by which waste materials are reprocessed into products, 
materials or substances whether for the original or other purposes. It includes the reprocessing of 
organic material but does not include energy recovery and the reprocessing into materials that are 
to be used as fuels or for backfilling operations 
Organic recycling  is relevant for both paper and compostable plastic items,  especially in the light of  
Member States that allow for certified compostable packaging to enter the biowaste collection and 
treatment (article 22 of the WFD) . Consequen tly, it cannot be discriminated, t hus the definition of 
recyclable should be revised in order that it explicitly covers also organic recycling in line with the WFD 
and calculation method of the recycling of compostable plastic packaging provided by the PPWD 
(article 6a.4).  
The option providing a combination of b oth DfR  (design for recycling/positive -negative lists) and 
recycling rate approach (option 2), is quite challenging. The level of complexity of the DfR approach does 
not reflect all recycling technolo gies: the study provided by Eunomia is clearly based onl y on examples 
coming from mechanical recyclers of plastics, neglecting – once again – the organic recycling of plastics, 
thus composters. This said, it is clear that the DfR approach does not fit the purpose of a necessary 
harmonization of the Essential Requirements  due to the different recycling infrastructure at European 
level, which might lead to have some packaging formats being classified as recyclable in some Member 
States and non-recyclable in others.  
Finally, the approaches regarding a recycling rate threshold approach  (option 3) , including the 
corresponding data generation, w ere presented to be in line with calculation rules established through 
the Implementing Decis ion 1004/2019 of PPWD Art 6(a) .4, which apply also to compostable plastic 
packaging. A critical point to urgently adapt when considering this option is that the relative data of the 
organic recycling of compostable at European level will be available only after that the mandatory 
separate collection of bio-waste will come into force in 2024.  
 
2. Requirements Specific for Compostable and/or Biodegradable Nature of Packaging 
The study provides 3 option s for the review of the essential requirements regarding  biodegradable and 
compostable packaging.  
Option 1: 
• Merge the current requirements on composting and biodegradable packaging 
• CEN Standard 13432 is updated to further specify the concepts of compostable and biodegradable 
packaging 
• Label packaging only as compostable  
The aim of this measure is to prevent packaging being labelled as biodegradable if it cannot be composted. 
Accepted composting conditions would be set out in an updated version of standard EN 13432 on 
packaging recoverable through composting and biodegradation. 
4 
 
Option 2:  
• Commission to mandate CEN to update EN 13432 to ensure actual composting conditions are 
taken into account and label packaging only as compostable 
• Label packaging only as compostable  
This option is sim ilar to the one provided in option 1 apart from the actual review of the Essential 
Requirements, which is missing in option 2. 
Option 3:  
• Amend Annex II [of the PPWD, i.e. the ERs] on the basis of  criteria to determine applications for which 
design for compostability can be considered to be of added value 
• Commission to mandate CEN to update EN 13432 to ensure actual composting conditions are taken 
into account 
• Label packaging only as compostable 
This would introduce a requirement for an assessment at the packaging specification/design stage of 
whether or not compostable packaging is suitable for the intended application. To illustrate the point, 
Eunomia makes reference to the  study “ Relevance of Bio degradable and Compostable Consumer Plastic 
Products and Packaging in a Circular Economy” where criteria have been identified. 
CONSIDERATION ON BIODEGRADABLE & COMPOSTABLE PLASTICS’ OPTIONS  
While we can agree on the fact that two separate requirements for the same packaging have created, over 
the years, confusion among the concepts of biodegradability and compostability, we believe that the 
concept of biodegradable should not be eliminated, b ut “merged” with composting. As provided by CEN 
standard EN 13432, biodegradability and compostability are conceived as one because:  
• Biodegradability, refers to the property of the material;  
• Compostability, refers to the end-of-life of that material.  
Eliminating the word biodegradability could open a regulatory gap on the term, which is not in line with 
the Single Use Plastics Directive that provides a definit ion of “biodegradable plastics”, and that could lead 
to an unregulated approach to the labelling  of biodegradable packaging. This would not also be in line 
with the standard EN 13432: when the European Commission mandated CEN to draw up the standard, 
intended to give presumption of conformity with essential requirements in line with “Annex II § 3, (c ) and 
(d) of the Directive”.  
On the revision of the standard EN 13432 , we welcome the idea of a “more detailed consultation with 
industry”, bearing in mind the entry into force of the mandatory separate collection of bio -waste by 
2023.The EU’s composting infrastructure shall be enabled to treat an increased amount of bio -waste 
aimed at the production of high -quality compost vital for the regeneration of degraded soils and to fight 
climate change. Therefore, we fail to grasp  the sense  generic statements such as the standard “needs to 
meet real life conditions” and that the standard  is based on “optimal conditions that do not necessarily 
match reality”. We think that after 20 years and being confirmed in three systematic reviews  (2005,2010, 
and 2015) the standards needs to be revised in order to take into consideration the possible evolutions of 
the sector of organic recycling.   
Option number 3 would limit by law the application of compostable plastics  in packaging . This 
represents a discrimination towards a specific packaging material . This is a potential trade distortion

because it affects a material whereas the legislator should focus on the desired (environmental) 
performance. This option, despite the development of a robust Europe an industry thanks also to a clear 
regulatory framework, would represent a U -turn of the European Commission’s policies regarding 
compostable plastics. 
 
3. Compostable Packaging: ensuring a right and balanced review of the Essential Requirements   
The general assessment of the study is that the overall conclusions on both the points analyzed in the this 
document (definition of recyclable and the review of the ER for compostable packaging) often do not 
adequately reflect what it is written in the study and offer option s which resemble a serious breach of 
European legislation.  
When it comes to organic recycling and the specific Essential Requirements for compostable plastics , the 
study suggests to completely rewrite not only the Essential Requirements but the basics set up by 2018’s  
Waste Package:  
• the Waste Framework Directive considers organic recycling as the way to recycle bio -waste and 
gives the options to Member States to include compostable plastics within this waste stream . 
interpretations within Member States; 
• the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive  recognizes the contribution given by compostable 
plastics allowing that the organic recycling of this material is calculated for the recycling targets set 
by the PPWD.  
Not including the reprocessing of compostable packaging (both paper and cellulose) within the definition 
of recyclable packaging would create legal uncertainty and would lead to different legislative frameworks 
across Europe.  
It should , also,  be highlighted th at Eunomia confirms its biased assumption on compostable plastics  
already envisaged in the study “ Relevance of Biodegradable and Compostable Consumer Plastic Products 
and Packaging in a Circular Economy ”. Within this study – which we understand it is also relevant for the 
review of the Essential Requirements – Eunomia makes the following wrong assumptions:  
• “compostable packaging does not bring any agronomic or ecological benefits ”: t his statement 
constitutes a relevant claim from a legal point of view , as the agronomic benefits are a 
requirement for considering the composting of packaging as a real form of recycling (organic 
recycling). Packaging compliant with the European harmonized standard EN 13432  is: (a) 
biodegradable under composting conditions; (b) it does not contain heavy metals and dangerous 
metals above thresholds defined on the basis of the requirements imposed on agronomic soil 
improvers at European level; (c) it does not produce phytotoxic e ffects in final compost. All these 
characteristics assure the production of a final compost of high quality, suitable for agronomic 
application. Thus the output of the organic recycling , compost, will be of “benefits to agriculture 
or ecological improvemen t” only if of good quality, proper mat uration, free of contaminations 
and compostable packaging contributes to produce such good quality compost 
• “half of compostable plastic is ‘lost’ to CO2 air emissions as it biodegrades in composting ”: the 
Carbon missing in the final balance is not “lost” , but rather oxidised to heat the composting pile 
and make the composting process, including pasteurisation, happen without any external energy 
source. Even more, this reasoning can also be applied to cellulosic food pac kaging items coated 
with compostable plastics.   
6 
 
• “Material compliance with EN 13432 does not automatically lead to effective treatment of 
compostable plastics”: the main reference of the study is the German example, while the best -case 
scenario, represented by It aly, is neglected. The study does not consider  the findings of the 2019  
German Environment Agency’s (UBA) publication  titled “Determination of criteria for high -quality 
recycling of bio -waste and requirements for the existing asset of (Ge rman) facilities to comply” 3, 
which found that German “fresh compost” should be aerobically treated in order to obtain mature 
compost. The assumptions made by Eunomia are  not, also, considering the recent study published 
by Wageningen University & Researc h (WUR) 4, which show s that the tested EN 13432 certified 
products break down within a maximum of 22 days in Dutch composting facilities.  
• Within the paragraph “ The Risk of Littering Biodegradable Plastics ” contradicting conclusions are 
made. While it is r eported that there is not any “empirical evidence that clearly correlates the 
marketing of plastics packaging or products as biodegradable/compostable with an increase in the 
tendency to litter”, the authors anyhow draw the conclusion that there might be t his risk, citing 
studies which mainly refer to other products (e.g. cigarettes buds)5.   
Last but not least, Eunomia’s preferred option for the review of the Essential Requirements of 
compostable plastics (Amend Annex II [of the PPWD, i.e. the ERs] on the basis of criteria to determine 
applications for which design for compostability can be considered to be of added value) would be based on 
the criteria laid out in the study6 “Relevance of Biodegradable and Compostable Consumer Plastic Products 
and Packaging in a Circular Economy” (see table below)

The identification of environmental benefits ove r alternative materials should be applied for all

addresses the issues of compostable entering the current recycling s ystem and not the opportunities, for 
example, of the scenario in case specific product categories would be completely shifted to biodegradable

It is worth to mention that i n the UK, WRAP used the same approach and came to different and more 
positive conclusion, considering the use of compostable plastics beneficial for the following applications:  
1. Food caddy liners  
2. Fruit and veg stickers  
3. Tea bags  
4. Coffee pods  
5. Ready meal trays  
6. Closed loop situations like at festivals or within buildings like coffee shops7 
In conclusion, the option that foresees the inclusion of the above reported  criteria in the Essential 
Requirement  for compostables does  not, first of all, reflect the real contribution that compostable 
plastics could give in the production of good quality compost and, more important, would fix by law what 
could be done with compostable plastics – which per se is a serious discrimination f or this materials – 
and will have a very negative impact on both innovation and employment in Europe. This is why we 
consider the option 1 (merge the current requirements on composting and biodegradable packaging and 
an updated of the CEN standard 13432) a more suitable solution which would allow to have a more clear 
and stable legislative framework for compostable plastics.

Dow’s response to review of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive (PPWD) consultation 
accompanying document to the PPWD questionnaire.  
 
The European Commission’s ‘Green Deal’ sets a clear direction of travel: Europe will become carbon neutral by 2050 
and will accelerate the circular economy for plastics packaging. 
 
At Dow, we understand the gravity and the urgency and recognize the cost of inaction, so we will continue to accelerate 
our efforts to contribute meaningfully to these goals. This is why our mission is to be the leading producer of 
circular plastics with t he lowest CO2 footprint , reinforced by challenging public commitments, supported by 
significant investments and partnerships across plastics manufacture, new low C02 feedstocks and chemical recycling. 
 
The review of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Direct ive (PPWD) presents a critical milestone in achieving our 
shared ambitions for packaging to be reusable or recyclable in a cost -effective manner by 2030. This will require an 
enabling, harmonized and enforceable regulatory framework, to further accelerate innovation in reusable 1 and 
recyclable packaging solutions, packaging waste reduction, boost the uptake of recycled content in packaging and the 
development of necessary sorting and recycling infrastructure to achieve recycling targets – all, whilst maintaining 
packaging functionality, consumer safety, product integrity and enabling the proper functioning of the Single Market. 
 
At Dow we s hare the EC and stakeholder urgency and bias to act. We call for a revision of the PPWD that is future 
proofed to support innovation across packaging design  (mono-materials), sorting (including digital watermarking), 
recycling (physical and chemical) and provides a stable and predictable regulatory framework for years to come, without 
the need for another early revision that would further reduce industry’s ability to build the solid investment cases needed 
to implement the projects needed to deliver the desired outcomes. 
 
Policy that imposes restrictions on specific packaging formats, material choices and that curtails specific recycling 
technologies based on what is scalable today will risk stifling innovation, partnerships and investments in the EU, 
creating a competitive disadvantage at a time when the Circular Economy Action Plan bears the promise of positioning 
EU industry as a frontrunner in developing, testing and demonstrating circular solutions. 
 
We stress that the PPWD plays an important role in achieving the EU Green Deal’s objectives not only in the area of 
the Circular Economy but also in achieving climate neutrality by 2050. It is vital that the PPWD pays due consideration 
to the significant GHG savings that are demonstrably being achieved via light weight, resource efficient films and flexible 
packaging. Recent, very significant value chain investments, innovation as well as alignment on design for recyclability 
guidelines supported by verifying recycling trial results, has enabled these formats to be recycled in practice.  Sufficient 
time should be given to allow market actors to transition to these recyclable formats, to reflect additional innovation and 
investments in waste infrastructure so the overall GHG savings the resource efficiency and functional benefits of films 
and flexibles can be further optimized. 
 
 
1. Packaging Waste Prevention 
 
We are concerned that essential packaging for fresh fruit and vegetables, meat, ready m eals, dried foodstuffs etc., is 
potentially being considered as “unnecessary” packaging in the context of efforts to prevent packaging waste.  
Packaging provides essential functions – protecting consumer health and  ensuring food safety, protecting product 
integrity and increasing product shelf-life to avoid food waste (and associated GHG emissions), enabling legally required

of goods across the retail supply chain. These critical functionalities must not be comprised. Additionally, any proposed 
packaging waste reduction targets mus t be clearly defined and linked to relevant metrics in Member States/ brand

To address packaging waste reduction and in the context of the Green Deal’s objectives on carbon neutrality, a ‘whole

What may appear to be “non-essential” packaging or unnecessary plastic wrapping around food products is in fact often

recyclable. It is critically important that a scientific, LCA evidence -based impact assessment is undertaken prior to 
restricting so called “non -essential” packaging, especially food packaging to avoid unaccounted for  environmental 
impacts. These assessments must consider the pack and product and must include, consumer health and safety, 
product integrity, and the full environmental burdens associated with  product losses in transport, retail and  crucially 
within the home, due to damage and spoilage. This is of critical importance for carbon intensive products such as meat 
and dairy products.  
 
We remain committed to continue to optimize the amount of packaging placed on the market that is the most resource 
efficient and is recyclable, without undermining packaging functionalities , which do not lead to undesirable 
consequences. Within the framework of an assessment on reducing packaging waste, it is also essential to develop 
clear definitions of “over-packaging” and “under-packaging” to help prevent negative consequences on product safety, 
integrity and food losses.  
 
We ask to p revent packaging waste from being landfilled by significantly accelerating the landfill ban and to severely 
restrict the incineration of plastics packaging. We have pledged through investments and partnerships to accelerate 
chemical recycling, as a complementary technology to mechanical recycling. We believe chemical recycling can 
accelerate plastics circularity, help Member States achieve recycling rates, and to supply recycled content into sensitive 
applications such as food contact applications. We have already undertaken scenario LCAs and we remain committed 
to answer any questions from stakeholders on the environmental, social, and economic impacts of this recycling 
technology.

Reusable packaging clearly has a role in the circular economy. Due to its versatility and durability, reusable plastic 
packaging is already widespread in industrial, consumer and commercial applications 3. The upscaling of existing 
reuse/refill pilots will require a systemic change in business models, consumer behavior, supply chain distribution and

It is particularly important that scientific, LCA evidence -based impact assessments are undertaken prior to the 
introduction of re-use and refillable packaging systems to avoid unexpected environmental and social impacts. These 
assessments must consider the pack and product and the full environmental burdens associated with the use of 
additional materials/ heavier materials, GHG emissions, energy, water, and detergent usage as well as more complex

Reusable packaging must also be recyclable. If a reusable option is not recyclable, then it should follow that it would 
have to achieve a greater number of rotations to “break even .”  There will be a need for tracking and traceability to 
prove, heavier more durable packaging does in fact deliver the number of rotations it has been designed to achieve and 
accomplishes overall net positive environmental impacts.  
 
The number of rotations of reusable applications is not infinite and must be pre-determined to avoid no loss in quality 
or packaging functionality. The enforcement of reusable / refillable options is necessary, as it conceivable that packaging 
could be ‘designed to achieve a minimum number of rotations’ that realistically and practically may never be achieved 
due to packaging losses, drop -off in consumer engagement etc. resulting in  additional and unaccounted for 
environmental burdens. It is also important to recognize that reusable/refillable may not be appropriate for all areas, 
especially rural areas of the EU where logistic may make its cost and resource  use prohibitive to realize any benefits. 
Reuse options should be egalitarian and not limited to ultra -urban society: societal, demographic, and geographical 
considerations should also be assessed.  
 
Transport packaging such as pallets is well advanced in our sector and therefore setting mandatory reuse targets is 
unnecessary. Imposing requirements to return pallets linked to exports outside of the EU makes little environmental or 
economic sense.   
 
Dow does not support the promotion of country level taxes on SUP to incentivize reusable packaging.  The promotion 
of fiscal measures that distort the EU Single Market should be avoided and which could have the unintended impact of 
incentivizing less sustainable and ineffective reuse systems.   
 
3. Recyclable 
A definition of recyclability must be future -proofed and should not be based on what is achievable today.  It 
should be technology and material neutral and it must allow for innovation in packaging, sorting and recycling 
technologies. A definition should provide sufficient time to allow market actors to transition to these recyclable formats, 
to reflect additional innovation and investments in waste infrastructure 
 
The definition must foresee that plastics packaging can be recycled at scale into a valued -adding products, materials 
or substance – from physical and chemical recycling processes. This requires the development of a recyclability 
definition that has the support of all impacted actors across the value chain

4. Recycled content  
In addition to the activities of the CPA to increase the uptake of recycled content in food packaging.  The supporting 
policy framework should ensure that plastics recyclate is of the appropriate quality in sufficient volumes and must deliver 
on the stringent requirements required for Food Contact Ma terials. There must be no comprising on consumer and 
product safety. This means that plastic recycled content in food contact applications whether originating from physical 
or chemical recycling processes – must be required to meet equivalent standards on performance and safety as 
achieved by virgin plastic.  
It is of critical importance the regulatory framework be strengthened to deliver a well-functioning Single Market not just 
for packaging but also for Post-Consumer Recyclate (PCR). The PPWD should address the regulatory barriers arising

Chemical recycling has an important role to play in making plastics packaging recyclable and accelerating the circular 
economy for plastics packaging as a complementary technology to mechanical recycling. Advanced r ecycling should 
be treated as a useful technology that can handle contaminated plastics waste that is not mechanically recyclable and 
therefore currently has a low value (e.g., contaminated plastics waste, including much packaging waste). Advanced 
Recycling has the potential to increase the value of this plastic waste by converting it into a valuable raw material from 
which to make virgin- equivalent plastic, for use in sensitive and highly regulated applications such as food contact and 
pharma applications. Thanks to its ability to deal with “legacy chemicals”, advanced recycling has the ability to bring 
mixed plastics waste into a non -toxic circular economy and prevent it from being landfilled or incinerated.  The 
application of advanced recycling can play  a crucial role to advancing the circular economy of plastics and plastics 
packaging and will help boost the ability of Member States to achieve EU recycling targets. This will require regulatory 
clarity on the ability to count advanced recycling towards the achievement of the EU recycling targets.  
 
 
5. Labelling requirements for recyclable/ non-recyclable packaging  
 
We believe labelling could  enhance separate collection, but an assessment as foreseen in the CEAP and impact 
assessment is necessary. An EU wide approach will not reflect local conditions and could drive even more consumer 
confusion.  A binary approach should be avoided. We recommend that all plastics packaging is collected for sorting and 
recycling with the requisite investments in these technologies . The role of digital water marking could be substantive 
and achieve higher sorting and recycling rates rather than rely on inconsistent consumer behaviour to sort packaging.

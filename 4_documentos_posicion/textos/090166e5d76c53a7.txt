 Metal Packaging Europe submission to the public consultation to inform the review of the requirements for packaging and other measures to prevent packaging waste	 21 December 2020, Brussels Introduction Metal Packaging Europe (MPE), the association bringing together European producers of rigid metal packaging, welcomes the possibility to express its opinion on the revision of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive (PPWD) in the public consultation. MPE believes that most of the proposed measures would facilitate that the goals, as laid down in the EU Green Deal and the new Circular Economy Action Plan (CEAP) are reached in the most effective way. Specific arguments on the different measures can be found below.   EU Wide overall packaging waste reduction target or waste generation limit/ Consumption reduction targets or limits targeting specific packaging types or applications/ Bans on specific packaging formats (for example, some single-use packaging items (not only plastic packaging))  MPE supports the intentions behind the proposals related to waste reduction targets, consumption reduction targets and bans on specific packaging formats, which were outlined in the public consultation. However, packaging put on market should not be considered as waste, as based on the subjective/objective definition of waste, until the point when it is actually discarded.   MPE is in addition concerned, since based on the trend of growing population and consumption as presented during the workshops organized by the Commission (or its consultants) any packaging waste reduction target would go hand in hand with reduction in placement of the product on the market, which would seem contradictory to guaranteeing sufficient supply of products to all consumers (be that food, beverage, etc.). Limits relative to the GDP could be considered, however these would need to be properly impact assessed. Similarly, packaging reduction may also lead to an increase in food/product wastage and it can also lead to incentivizing the uptake of solutions which have worse environmental impacts.

MPE, therefore, does not support generic packaging waste reduction targets. Given the excellent performance of metal packaging with respect to recycling rates (latest being 76.1% for aluminium beverage packaging and 82.5% for all steel packaging), recycling yields and permanent material quality we think that generic waste reduction targets would be too prescriptive and unnecessarily punish a material, which has an excellent performance when it comes to circularity. We believe that waste prevention measures should focus on those packaging materials and formats that are not recycled at large and don’t perform well from a circularity perspective as well as at those that represent overpackaging.  When it comes to bans, MPE does not support this measure. The focus should be put on EPR fee modulation with significant enough fee differentials that incentivise well-recyclable packaging and/or disincentivise badly recyclable packaging, or packaging with disruptive elements to the recycling process.   MPE believes that if some of the above-mentioned measures would be put forward, they should be set on the EU level. We understand that there is a need to differentiate to a certain degree due to the specific contexts of Member States, but the risks of these prescriptive measures on market fragmentation are far too great.   Measures and targets on reusable packaging e.g. refill quotas/ Information campaigns, including on environmental benefits of reuse and how to reduce packaging consumption/ Including incentives for refillable/ reuse targets for transport packaging/European Guidance on effective reuse systems MPE understands the focus on reuse, since optimized reuse packaging systems along with optimized single use packaging systems are needed to achieve the ambitions of the Circular Economy Action Plan and the EU Green Deal. In addition, MPE represents companies that produce reusable packaging such as reusable drums that are made of steel or reusable IBCs. MPE acknowledges that reuse is higher in the waste hierarchy than recycling, but there is a concern with the perception of reuse as the ultimate solution to reduce environmental impacts of products.   As we know, based on LCA methodology, in some cases re-useable alternatives do have equal or greater environmental impacts compared to well recyclable single-use products with high recycling rates. MPE emphasizes that there are certain settings where reuse delivers better overall performance and desired environmental impacts and different settings where this is achieved through single-use systems. Imposing strict reuse provisions such as mandatory quotas and levies for single use systems without defining system boundaries and performance evidence methods for reuse can lead to inefficiency and distortions that are neither ecologically nor economically sensible.   For that reason, MPE would suggest a thorough impact assessment including additional research looking into both systems. This research should take into account different indicators such as trippage rates and their influencing factors such as logistics for bottle exchange and individually designed packaging formats that have to go back to the original filler, before strictly imposing reuse provisions to make sure that any future measures, if opted for, have indeed better overall environmental impacts. Furthermore, as a part of the broader assessment of reuse, there should be some considerations with respect to the logistical and hygiene limitations, e.g., food safety issues related to the cleaning and refilling, water consumption issues and challenges related to ensuring the optimum number of trips being reached before the reusable packaging is discarded. Consequently, before communicating the environmental benefits of reuse, the above-mentioned assessment needs to be carried out for the most common existing packaging categories and based on actual verifiable data, not estimations, which would provide evidence for such statements.  While an additional impact assessment is necessary, it is a static and costly method of evaluating environmental performance of reuse systems. Going forward, we need a more continual method of providing performance evidence. Given the number of potentially positively disrupting technologies for effective packaging recovery we think serialization of packaging items (based on unique coding of products) within the various packaging systems embedded in a larger digitalization strategy could play and important strategic role going forward with understanding the system boundaries and conditions when assessing which system works the best (tracking performance). This could be set up in a way that would not only enable resource tracking but also dynamic tracking of product changes e.g., to track how often reusable systems are actually reused including associated logistics and transport. This should be in line with the objectives of the Green Deal and the CEAP and could also help with the future incorporation of reusable formats into EPR schemes. When it comes to the European guidance on effective reuse systems, MPE agrees that this option could deliver potential reduction in environmental impacts but only if the assessment outlined above is carried out prior to the development of the guidance and its conclusions are reflected therein. MPE has certain reservations with respect to mandatory transport reuse targets, that follow the arguments of the above proposed assessment. For certain types of packaging such as shrink wraps it might be difficult to implement, but this packaging format is only used in some situations where necessary due to travel distances or for hygiene and product safety. Use of Green Public Procurement to require the use of reusable options for specified purposes within the public sector This measure needs to be looked at and analysed in more detail. Reusable formats could be an option in situations where reuse has been proven to perform better and to deliver the desired environmental outcomes (see other comments on reuse), e.g. the options are part of a pool system with standardized and shared packaging and the distribution of the product is local/regional with short transport distances to the consumer and back to a pool filler, etc. and where the general aim of decreasing carbon footprint of the products is guaranteed. The criteria for product selection under GPP could therefore include provisions such as local sourcing of products, pool packaging or proximity provisions when it comes to reuse. In addition to the overall focus on resource-efficiency, reuse should consider also material reuse opportunities. Moreover, the green claims that companies make need to be evaluated to confirm that the products deliver the presented environmental performance to avoid situations when public authorities opt for products that eventually have worse impacts than those that they have substituted. Promoting points of sale for unpackaged products and products packaged in bulk MPE understands the potential of this measure in terms of limiting packaging waste, however its scalability needs to be considered. Additionally, it may lead to unintended consequences in the areas of food safety, hygiene, shelf life and others, which need to be firstly properly assessed. For that reason, further assessment is recommended. Obligatory corporate packaging prevention plans More and more companies are adopting voluntary packaging prevention plans. MPE is of the opinion that before making any such plans mandatory, the scope and key terms such as “waste prevention” need to be specified, since it can be unclear whether it relates to material usage or something else. Levies on packaging, either generally applied, or for specific formats (for example, single-use) First, punitive measures such as levies on single-use packaging should not be devised, if there isn’t a clear scientifically verifiable basis that the alternatives have overall better environmental outcomes (see our statement above on reuse measures). In addition, MPE believes that there are social impacts to consider with this measure, since for it to be effective single-use products would have to be made more expensive than the refillable alternatives, which would result in the price increase of all available packed products. This would affect some consumers negatively, and mostly those with less disposable income. MPE therefore questions whether this measure would be appropriate since many other consumers would still buy products based on convenience.  Basic requirements related to packaging  MPE supports the proposal for all packaging to be reusable or recyclable by 2030 as similarly laid out in the new CEAP. MPE understands that this will mostly depend on the definition of what is to be considered reusable or recyclable. Arguments for those provisions can be found in the relevant sections.  Efficient use of packaging/packaging reduction/minimization (addressing “overpackaging”)  MPE supports the proposal for packaging not to exceed minimum volume and weight necessary for functionality; mandatory assessment method based on revised list of critical areas in EN 13428. MPE fully acknowledges the need to tackle overpackaging or excessive packaging and welcomes the initiative. A first step should, however, be for the Commission to define what should be considered as ‘overpackaging’ and how it will be measured. Overpackaging should focus on products being packed in an unnecessary amount of packaging (often empty space or sealed air) which ends up misleading the consumers as to the amount of product actually contained within the packaging. Overpackaging should be defined as all excessive packaging which is not required for the purpose of making sure that the product it contains arrives in its intended form to the consumer, taking into account all mandatory labelling. making all packaging recyclable or counteract other sustainability and policy goals such as the reduction of food waste or obesity.  When it comes to the provision for producers to report to central registry on the volume, weight and planar area ratios of packaging to product if, for either one of these three measures, the packaging exceeds a specified threshold percentage of the product, MPE is also supportive, however, asks for the thresholds to be material specific to avoid any discrimination or incentivizing switching from one material to other. Given the material specifics some packaging materials are heavier than others, however this does not constitute overpackaging, which should also be explicitly stated. Additionally, these thresholds could be more granular within the materials and set per product categories (food cans, beverage cans, etc.) since this would enable further reduction of packaging materials without risking the recyclability and also allow to address the volume and weight including specific technical constraints that the industry is facing because of transports or other reasons.  Recyclable nature of packaging/definition of recyclable  MPE welcomes the proposal of qualitative definition of “recyclable” but has reservations whether it would deliver the desired outcome since the overall definition would need to be stringent enough when it comes to packaging being actually recycled at scale. MPE wants to avoid that a packaging format is deemed as recyclable anywhere in the EU if it has just a limited infrastructure for recycling and therefore gets de facto recycled only in few locations. That, combined with the new harmonized “recyclable“ label would then mislead consumers who would assume that the packaging is recycled in any given member state.   MPE is supportive of the definitions that incorporate the elements of design for recycling (positive/negative lists) and the definition focusing on recycling rate thresholds. MPE is of the opinion that a proposal combining the two would be the most feasible out of the proposed ones, even though we understand that it can be difficult to implement in practice. The design for recycling criteria would balance out the need for high granularity that the thresholds require on their own (if introduced separately as in recycling rate thresholds option). Additionally, given the initial lack of data on thresholds for formats the option for design for recycling would provide a substantial basis while the data would be gathered making it a more feasible starting point than the last option. Over time as data would be collected there could be a slow phasing out of design for recycling criteria and solely rely on recycling rate thresholds.

Labelling  MPE conditionally supports the proposal for packaging to be labelled as reusable or recyclable or compostable, since this will be dependent on which proposals will be accepted for the definitions of those terms. The criteria need to be clearly defined to avoid any market fragmentation. Since labels will depend on the definition of the terms, claims relating to degree of recyclability should not be permitted anymore to avoid consumer confusion. Additionally, since in the EU there is currently no standard which would evaluate the degree of recyclability, just different consultants that conduct assessments with different results.

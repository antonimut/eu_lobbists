 
 
PEPSICO’S SUPPORTING PAPER TO THE EUROPEAN COMMISSION PUBLIC 
CONSULTATION ON THE REVIEW OF THE REQUIREMENTS FOR PACKAGING AND 
PACKAGING WASTE IN THE EUROPEAN UNION 
22 December 2020 
PepsiCo welcomes the opportunity to input into the important work the European Commission is doing to 
further the sustainable packaging agenda with the revision of the essential requirements for packaging 
and packaging waste in the European Union.   
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY  
• PepsiCo supports the Commission’s objective to reduce packaging waste and strongly believes in the 
need for coherent and supporting policies for waste prevention that can be well translated into 
deliverables. Waste prevention measures should encourage recycling and uptake of recycled content 
incorporation even beyond packaging , anticipate the ban on landfill  and discourage incineration to 
ensure that packaging needs never become waste.  
• Mandatory Recycled Content targets should apply to all types of packaging and go beyond packaging 
while taking regulatory and technical barriers into account, to accelerate the shift towards a stronger 
market for secondary raw materials.  
• An efficient  EPR schemes’ ecosystem based on strong and harmonized minimum requirements should 
be the channel to ensure packaging formats currently not recycled are collected and sorted . We are 
happy to work with regulators, recyclers, PROs and industry to unlock technical, infrastructural and 
regulatory solutions and improvements in existing and emerging recycling technologies.  
• We support a s cience based approach to reuse, facilitating the uptake of reusable models when it 
makes both environmental and economic sense.  
• Industry needs one clear definition of recyclability that stays technology-neutral and forward-looking 
to allow for innovation in packaging design.  
 
This note includes supportive facts that explain the answers provided by PepsiCo to the questionnaire. 
 
1. WASTE PREVENTION MEASURES 
Packaging functionalities include hygiene but also food safety, food preservation, consumer 
information and regulatory compliance. 
 
PepsiCo supports the objective of reducing packaging waste. We appreciate some of the proposed waste 
prevention measures con sider the important function packaging plays for hygiene  which indeed is key , 
particularly for food packaging . However, we believe any measures should also consider food safety, 
consumer information and the role that packaging plays in ensuring regulatory compliance with any legal 
requirements, as well as carrying information relating to transparency of nutritional values , mandatory 
ingredient and allergen declarations.

Similarly, any consideration to increase the portion of products  sold in bulk should consider the need to 
constantly ensure health and safety of the product  - particularly where the consumer would be 
encouraged to provide an article to be filled . Furthermore, delivering products of top quality without 
individual packaging is in some cases impossible (e.g. potato chips would be exposed to oxygen and light 
and become soft, unappealing) as well as challenging for some type of formats to ensure also the hygiene 
and the recommended portion in line with European dietary recommendations. Finally, another issue that 
would need addressing is the liability for any potential cross contamination of food products. 
 
PepsiCo does agree , however, that when the conditions above  are met, sales in bulk should be 
incentivised. As a matter of fact, our acquisition of Soda Stream was a step in that direction that has 
allowed us to explore packaging -free solutions where consumers can make their favourite product at 
home, and a safe and convenient alternative to meet waste prevention objectives.    
 
Complementary policies to ensure packaging waste reduction  
PepsiCo believes that p ackaging is one element of waste, and the overall costs of municipal waste 
management should not  be borne d isproportionately by producers. Complementary policies will  
encourage packaging waste reduction, trigger a stronger market for secondary raw materials, enable EPR 
and drive greater recovery of packaging materials. These include:  
• government mandates for (and enforcement of) the separation of recyclable materials from waste;  
• landfill bans for recyclable materials;  
• mandatory recycled content targets for different packaging types starting with all those that are mono-
materials and less complex, and 
• targeted measures such as deposit return systems, which can achieve high rates of collection and 
recycling for specific packaging types. 
 
Favourable market conditions for investments in recycling infrastructures and better collection, 
sorting and recycling to create a strong market for secondary raw materials  
 
Higher availability of food grade recycled content can only result from an efficient collection and recycling 
system for all types of food packaging and favourable market condition s for investments in recycling 
infrastructures. PepsiCo believes that waste management should achieve the same, high level of recycling 
rates across a ll EU Member States, which will mean making adequate investments in some countries 
where recycling rates are poor and EPR schemes (intended as both Packaging Recovery Organisations – 
PRO- and Deposit Return Schemes – DRS) need strengthening.  
 
EPR schemes should be industry-led and comply with harmonized minimum requirements across the EU. 
Strong minimum requirements will include:  
• clear demarcation of costs for the obliged industry;  
• a granular fee structure for fee modulation that allows setting fees base d on the actual cost of 
recycling, without cross-subsidization between materials and polymers;  
• transparent and accurate reporting, monitoring and independent auditing;  
 
 
• strong government oversight of the transparency of the PRO in particular when in pres ence of a 
competing PRO’s environment;  
• separate household collection that will make unnecessary and disproportionate the treatment of 
residual waste. 
Avoid duplication of costs through taxation in favour of circularity  
 
As a brand owner, PepsiCo recognises the importance of financially supporting a system that effectively 
recycles our packaging. However, we don’t support a generic tax that would feed into a general budget 
rather than being earmarked towards better recycli ng infrastructure, improved collection, increased 
awareness against littering and better sorting technologies.  
 
 
2. REUSABLE MODELS  
 
Reusable packaging models will need a deep rethinking of supply chains, increased consumer 
education and will unavoidably be a longer term solution to packaging waste.  
PepsiCo is exploring a number of ideas to reinvent the ways consumers can enjoy our products, which 
include reusable packaging. Reusable models can be an effective model to be prioritised over recyclability 
of packaging, but they will require a rethinking in the organisation of supply chains , both in business-to-
business and in business -to-consumer relations. When products are delivered in reusable packaging, 
consumers will need to change behaviour in the way they interact with products and packaging, and it may 
require some time for the adapt ation to take place. Moreover, there is a need to create an enabling 
environment for reuse in the market: supportive and coherent legislation will be crucial (i.e. food safety 
regulations, incentives for local authorities to help create a reuse system) as well as a collaborative 
approach with the full value chain to work in partnerships that include food manufacturers, retailers, 3 rd 
party washing business and brand owners. 
 
Supporting the most environmentally viable packaging format while also allowing for  a free 
choice of packaging formats for producers to select and consumers to choose.  
While supporting the need for packaging design to move towards more reusable or recyclable options, we 
strongly believe there has to be a free choice of specific packaging formats and materials for producers to 
select the most appropriate based on the product, its consumption and its distribution system.  
It is important to consider the overall carbon footprint of reusable models, which may require heavier 
packaging and lo nger trips for returning the reusable packaging. PepsiCo is already investigating these 
aspects through the acquisition of SodaStream. While reuse is set to have a promising role in the future of 
packaging, these models should be encouraged but not mandated and especially not at the cost of overall 
carbon footprint of the packaging model. 
 
 
For example, setting mandatory reusable formats for transport packaging may fail to consider the 
environmental footprint of empty containers completing the journey after goods have been delivered, as 
well as the weight reusable containers could have compared to recyclable materials such as paper 
cardboard.  
PepsiCo supports the requirement for all packaging to be reusable or recyclable by 2030. PepsiCo’s goal is 
to design 100% of our packaging to be recyclable, compostable or biodegradable by 2025 and to use 100% 
of recycled plastics (rPET) in our Pepsi brand beverage bottles in 9 European countries by 2022.   
 
Prescriptive definitions for a strong level playing field 
PepsiCo supports clear definitions of reusability to be built into the mandate for reusable packaging. For 
instance, different packaging types would be reusable 3 times, 5 times, 10 times and perhaps within a 
certain timeframe, this should all be clearly defined. Similarly, PepsiCo believes what qualifies as ‘transport 
packaging’ should be clearly spelled out to differentiate between the examples listed in the questionnaire 
(i.e. pallets) and any other secondary or tertiary packaging used to serve other typ es of functionalities 
while in transport.  
 
3. ENFORCEMENT 
Transparent and harmonised methodology for compliance 
Measures to account for compliance and enforcement of packaging requirements should be based on clear 
methodologies, descriptive calculation methods, sector specific considerations, inclusive of all packaging 
and harmonised across all Member States.  
 
Better regulation, less administration to avoid discouraging packaging innovation 
PepsiCo believes any enforcement methods to be introduced should ease the administrative burdens 
companies and administrations have to currently bear , in line with better regulation principles.  The 
possibility to have to report to a central registry for packaging ratios overlooks the incredible burden this 
would pose on the registrants and also underestimates the risk of hindering innovation that would follow. 
The timely registration of any packaging type,  size, and material will encourage the choice of the same 
existing packaging type to avoid the lengthy registration, hence discouraging any innovation in packaging 
design. 
 
Securing confidential business information  
The disclosure of packaging information for a European public packaging registry should ensure that any 
confidential business information is well preserved and kept confidential to ensure a competitive market 
also for packaging types and packaging design.  
 
 
 
One clear definition of overpackaging that would consider packaging functionalities  
PepsiCo believes there is a need to clearly define overpackaging, taking into account the functionality that 
packaging performs, according to the essential requirements. It is important that any action targeting the 
reduction of overpackaging does not automatically include multipacks , but considers whether the 
multipack format has been designed to meet the essential requirements, and/or if it follows ambitious 
environmental sustainability criteria including prevention of food waste   
Multipacks serve a large portion of European consumers and are instrumental in portion control. The last 
years have brought significant changes in societal behaviour with the increase in 1 -person and 2-people 
households. Multipacks of single p ortions provide important choice for those households and offer a 
balanced and healthy alternative to family-size portions that may not be in line with dietary requirements 
and would increase the risk of food waste.  
Similarly, when looking at potential packaging ratios, any approach that would define the maximum ratio 
a packaging could be compared to the packaged good may lead to favouring bigger sizes for packaged food 
such as savoury snacks.  
 
4. RECYCLED CONTENT 
Mandatory recycled content targets should apply to all packaging and go beyond packaging.  
The market for recycled materials is not currently well functioning  and this is the main obstacle to 
incorporating recycled content in packaging, besides safety and technological barriers. The low feedstock 
volumes for certain materials (such as flexible snacks packaging) do not make it economical viable to 
recycle them even if they are in theory recyclable. Starting from collecting more of those materials as part 
of household separate collection would represent the right trigger to build up volumes and incentivize 
investments in sorting and recycling technologies, bringin g to life an efficient market for secondary raw 
materials.  
Mandatory Recycled Content targets will lead demand to rise and help to increase collection, thereby 
supporting the market for recyclates. Therefore, mandatory targets should apply to all type of packaging 
and go beyond packaging while taking into account regulatory and technical barriers. A stronger EPR 
system should be the channel to ensure packaging formats currently not recycled are collected and sorted 
and we are happy to work with regulators, recyclers, PROs and industry to unlock technical, infrastructural 
and regulatory solutions. We acknowledge existing barriers to a broader market for secondary raw 
materials that include technical and regulatory obstacles and we want to play an active role working with 
regulators to identify those barriers and facilitate potential unlocks. 
As a user of food -grade recycled plastic (rPET), we understand how important recycled content is to 
improving our sustainability record. Earlier this year we were proud to announce that PepsiCo will move 
to use more 100% rPET bottles for our Pepsi brand in  nine European countries over the next two years. 
This important commitment emphasises PepsiCo’s true belief in a circular economy for Europe while also 
 
 
outlining some important  challenge that need to be overcome in collaboration with policy makers, 
customers, and consumers to make the use of recycled content the new normal. 
 
One harmonised definition of recycled content that is technology neutral and supports 
packaging innovation  
The revision of the essential requirements for packaging also presents a good opportunity to propose a 
harmonised and prescriptive definition of recycled content that would stay technology -neutral while 
accounting for latest innovation in recycling technologies and that would be intended the same way 
uniformly across Member States.  
 
5. RECYCLABLE PACKAGING 
 
One clear and forward-looking definition of recyclable packaging, without negative and positive 
lists as they would hamper innovation in packaging design.  
PepsiCo strongly supports the need to define ‘recyclable packaging’. One clear and harmonised definition 
will be key for the successful revision  of the essential requ irements and implementation of the PPWD, 
together with a strong incentive to increase Europe’s recycling capabilities and technological uptake in the 
space of recycling of packaging waste.  However, any attempt to define prescriptive lists of negative and 
positive packaging properties as the criteria for definition of the recyclability of packaging will hamper 
innovation in this space. Lists of packaging properties that could pose a barrier to recycling would be based 
on existing available technologies that allow for certain materials to be recyclable , thereby hampering 
further improvement in product design based on new recycling technologies.  
PepsiCo believes the door should be left open for innovation and that the definition of recyclable packaging 
should be a concept that changes and evolves in time and that is regularly reviewed to allow for innovation 
in packaging design as well as recycling technologies to be taken into account. What is not yet recyclable 
today could be recyclable in a few years; any po licy developments should allow for a transition time and 
should ensure EPR schemes are the channels used to collect, sort and potentially recycle by scaling up 
solutions that are currently niche. The example of the recycling of flexible packaging in German y, the 
Netherlands and Belgium through EPR schemes is a success story that should be reproduced in other 
countries and to do so we will need time and a stronger collaboration of industry, recyclers, and PROs.  
Harmonisation of the way recyclable packaging is defined will allow for a coordinated approach to 
packaging design and packaging waste collection and recycling across EU Member States, and potentially 
even beyond EU borders. The definition of recyclable should also include a definition of ‘at scale,’ whereby 
innovation in recycling technologies is incentivised, tested and included in scope once in place at a 
 
 
sufficient scale. In this respect, PepsiCo is a signatory of the Ellen Macarthur Foundation’s New Plastics 
Economy and supports their definition of ‘recyclable’ and ‘at scale’1.   
Stricter essential requirements will drive innovation in the packaging value chain but 
overregulation should be avoided. Packaging design should be assessed together with waste 
management infrastructures, the product and recycling technologies.  
Stricter essential requirements can help drive innovation across the packaging value chain. However, 
overregulation would have the opposite effect, i.e. acting as a barrier to the deployment of new and 
innovative packaging solutions. Packaging design alone is not the answer to increasing recyclability, but it 
must be considered together with the product, with the waste management infrastructure and with 
recycling technologies. Setting prescriptive design requirements, with the goal of banning certain materials 
or design, would fail to recognize this. 
 
Our goal is for all packaging to be recycled in practice and not only theoretically. We are working on 
packaging design to make it as simple and in line with the current waste management as possible while 
ensuring it min imizes the use of resources and climate change impacts , and  protects the product it 
contains. In some cases, the packaging that ticks all those boxes is still not in line with the recycling 
infrastructures that are in place at scale today. This doesn’t mean that it cannot be recycled. With the right 
incentives and investments, that packaging can be collected as part of the separate waste streams in  
households, be sorted properly and constitute enough volume to become economically attractive for 
recycling.

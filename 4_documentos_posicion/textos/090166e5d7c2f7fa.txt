1. BACKGROUND 
The troublesome concerns the difficulties that producers of containers made of ceramic  have 
found in the implementation of the European Directive of P ackaging and Packaging waste1 
which forces ceramic food containers to be adhered to a “Return, Collection and Recovery  
System”,2 although they cannot be associated with the concept of  packaging waste. That 
obligation is a consequence of the lack of E uropean Union (EU) harmonisation disregard the 
fact that ceramic is a non-polluting material and that it would not be economically reasonable 
to implement an individual collection and treatment channel; therefore, an important fee is 
being paid in some E uropean Union countries for a non-effective service that , additionally,  
cannot be put in place. 
Ceramic food containers have some characteristic features that differentiate them from the 
rest of food packaging. As it is an inert material, it implies no risk for natural sources like wat er 
and soil, neither for human health, animals and plants. Another of the reasons why ceramic is 
so friendly with the environment is its endurance, which makes it a long -lasting product that 
consumers are willing to keep  (between 75% and 95% of the cases)  and not get rid of: as a 
result, it helps avoiding packaging waste. 
Ceramic food containers are designed and manufactured to be genuinely marketed as 
packaging, kitchenware or tableware. In this sense , ceramic food containers can easily 
integrate our houseware given the fact: they generate  no unwanted  smell and can resist 
relevant temperature changes  (they can be used in the oven, freezer and microwave) ; for 
these reasons, ceramic food containers’ usefulness finds no threshold since they can be reused 
as kitchenware or tableware  - one of the original purposes for which they are designed and 
manufactured - multiple times by way of a simple washing and drying , being the reason why 
they do not end in landfill. That feature fits exactly with the concept of reusable packaging3 set 
by the Directive of  Packaging and Packaging Waste, as those which have been  conceived, 
designed and placed on the market to accomplish within its lifecycle multiple trips or rotations 
by being reused for the same purpose for which they were conceived. 
Such attributes meet The European Green Deal’s purposes by which the European Commission 
(EC) has committed to lead a global change and leave a better Planet to the next generations 
by turn ing Europe into a more competitive economy through a more efficient use of the 
Planet’s resources.4 Throughout said strategy , the EC reflects the necessity of reducing waste 
and over-packaging so as to achieve a sustainable development. This aim also can be found in

Under the Directive 94/62 on Packaging and Packaging waste, packaging can be managed by 
alternative systems. Two of them are : Packaging Integrated Waste Management System and 
Deposit and Return System. In the first, the management system is  in charge of their pick -up,

packaging fillers who pay an amount based on the quantity of packaging waste they put in the 
market, in compliance with the “polluter-pays principle”. By means of the Deposit and Return 
System, a deposit fee is refunded to consumer once the item has been properly returned. 
Although E uropean Union (EU)  has been fully committed with the environmental impact of 
packaging waste since the Directive 94/62 was adopted, there is still a lack of harmonisation 
among national regulations, particularly with an inefficient management of ceramic food 
containers waste since there is no specific container where they  can be placed and no

European Directives are not being addressed. Such a lack of harmonisation is also against the 
common goals and schemes proposed by the E C Circular Economy Action Plan ,6 that will 
attempt to h omogenise treatment and recycling channels  once consumers get rid of 
packaging. In compliance with this, the Circular Economy Action Plan encourages to 
standardise and set up separate recycling systems.

2. CERAMIC FOOD PACKAGING WASTE’S LEGISLATIVE CONTEXT 
With the aim  of harmonising national measures within the E U regarding the management of 
packaging and packaging waste , so as  to prevent their generation or at least  significantly 
reduce it, the European Parliament and the Council adopted the Directive 94/62. This text lays 
down measures with the purpose  of preventing the production of packaging waste . It takes 
particular account  at reusing packaging, recycling and other forms of recovering packaging 
waste; all of them with the main goal of avoiding waste generation, reducing its final disposal. 
Even though the Directive was elaborated before the  European Green Deal, t he objectives of 
the latter were already reflected in the first in terms of waste prevention mainly. 
Bearing in mind the relevance that prevention has in the European packaging regulation, as 
one of its cornerstones, it must be highlighted that the Directive states that Member States 
shall ensure the  implementation of  preventive measures to decrease packaging waste 
generation and to minimise its environmental impact. In this regard, it prom otes incentives 
through extended producer responsibility schemes to minimise  the environmental impact of 
packaging. 
On the other hand, i t must be borne in mind that this text was also adopted with the aim of 
ensuring the functioning of the European Single Market by avoiding obstacles to trade and the 
likely competition restriction, by virtue of article 1 of such Directive. Furthermore, the said text 
compels Member States do n ot impede the placing of packaging on their terr itory which 
accomplish such European regulation.7 
At this point,  a reference to ceramic food packaging waste discussion regarding 94/62 
Directive’s impact has to be  made. The core of the conflict ar ose from the fact that ceramic 
food packaging were not excluded from the Directive’s scope , as it should be mostly given its 
features explained before. Additionally, the lack of a specific reference in the European text to 
this packaging category  has led to a legal uncertainty for  all those companies invo lved in this 
sector. This situation ends in a different approach in each Member State, which consequently 
distorts competition in the food packaging market at the same time it impedes to achieve the 
Single Market. 
At this respect, in the context of the legislative reformation of 94/62 Directive  (2001-2004), a 
discussion at the European Parliament about ceramic food packaging waste took place. Within 
the debate several members urged a legislative amendment by excluding ceramic food 
packaging from the application of the European regulation, based on the  very negligible 
quantity they represent in comparison to all packaging waste produced in the EU, do not cause

food packaging waste. EC clearly stated that 94/62  Directive did not oblige Member States to 
set up recycling systems for the ceramic packaging  (regardless the Directive does compel 
Member States to adopt a Return, Collection and Recovery System). EC also said that such text 
provides Member States the freedom to take any measure on ceramics packaging, as long as it 
complies with the framework of the Treaty of Functioning of the European Union.  
After all these discussions article 20 of Directive 94/62 was replaced8 by the following text: 
“The Commission, in accordance with the procedure laid down in Article 21, shall determine 
the technical measures necessary to deal with any difficulties encountered in applying the 
provisions of this Directive in particular to inert packaging materials, the quantities of which 
do not exceed 0.1% of all packaging put on the market in the European Union, prima ry 
packaging for medical devices and pharmaceutical products, small  packaging and luxury 
packaging”. 
The relevance of this amendment finds its basis  in the evolution of this article up to this date; 
in particular, i t was  amended in 2018  by 2018/852 Directive,9 which empowered the E C to 
adopt delegated acts to deal with those difficulties regarding the inert packaging materials 
placed on the EU market in neglig ible quantities. This is particularly the case of ceramic food 
containers, since it is an inert material which does not harm environment and that is placed in

3. CERAMIC FOOD PACKAGING WASTE CURRENT CONTEXT 
A. Current situation for ceramic food containers waste management 
As it was stressed before, the Packaging Integrated Waste Management System controls the 
packaging waste recycling process. 
The ceramic food containers, in spite of their environmentally -friendly nature, are subject 
either to a  Packaging Integrated Waste  Management System or to a De posit and Return 
System given that  their treatment is still governed by the 94/62 Directive as no regula tory 
amendment was finally adopted. However, n o Ceramic Food Packaging Integrated Waste 
Management System is currently feasible since no distinct container has been installed in the 
streets (it would also not be reasonable in economic terms) , which means that no selective 
pick-up is considered for  ceramic food containers; hence, even they are placed into packaging 
containers, they are finally  treated as regular  (domestic) waste, which ends to be also a 
problem for Packaging Integrated Waste Management Systems. As far as we are concerned, no 
a Deposit and Return System has been set up in any Member State. 
Several more issues arise due to the absence of a specific regulation since, the fees structure is 
not harmonised within EU. This gives rise to an extraordinarily fees increasing in some Member

B. A long history of conflicts in France 
In France the “Solidary and Ecologic Transition Ministry ” is  devoted to accomplish the EU 
environmental goals by preparing and implementing government policy in the areas of 
sustainable development, environment and green technologies, e nergy transition, industrial 
safety, transport and infrastructure, equipment and sea.  
CITEO is the Packaging Integrated Waste Management System in charge of ceramic  food 
containers waste management in France and the one wh ich receives payments from  
containers fillers for the total amount of ceramic food packaging they place in French territory. 
Although the conflict in France started long time ago , we can focus on  2012, when said 
Ministry introduced a penalty in the fees structure (known as the “malus”), which represented 
a 100% increase  over the total amount  to be disbursed by users of those materials which did 
not have a specific recycling channel (ceramic among them).13

euros/kg in 2020 , which represents a 108% increase).  Previsions for 2021 do not s uggest a 
hopeful future either. In CITEO’s fees structure for 2021 , the tariff per weight of ceramic

C. Case-629/19, CJEU’s Judgment, 14th October 2020 
For the purpose of this paper, a reference must be made to  the recent Judgment of the Court 
of Justice of the European Union issued  on the 14 th of October, 2020 , Case C -629/19, in 
relation to Directive 2008/98, which regulates waste in general terms. The relevance of this 
case is that article 3 from the 94/62 Directive, when defining the key-word “packaging waste”, 
alludes to the definition of waste contained in 2008/98 Directive. 
This definition states the following: “ ‘waste’ means any substance or object which the holder 
discards or intends or is required to discard ”. The Court’s analysis mostly reflects on the 
concept of “discarding” and can be really useful for this paper’s purpose. In this sense, it is 
remarked that the Directive intends to cover all items discarded by cons umers regardless its 
economic value (without prejudice to the considerations that will be later explained and that 
are linked to this  same circumstance). Hence, any substance or object falling in the Directive’s 
scope must be recovered or disposed with the least damage possible for nature. 
So as to figure out whether an object is regarded as waste, the Court of Justice proceeds to 
review some features that such items shall gather;14 an important one is the fact that it cannot

Bearing in mind what has been described  throughout this report, we call for an urgent action 
at E U level through either , a 94/62 Directive amendment which excludes ceramic food

position in certain EU’s territories based only in  the material they use to manufacture  their

 The EFPIA view of the Public Consultation ‘Reducing packaging waste – review of rules’   Author: Environment, Health and Safety Expert Group  Date: 1/2021  Version: Final  
Introduction The European Federation of Pharmaceutical Industries and Associations (EFPIA) represents the biopharmaceutical industry operating in Europe. Through its direct membership of 36 national associations, 39 leading pharmaceutical companies and a growing number of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), EFPIA’s mission is to create a collaborative environment that enables our members to innovate, discover, develop and deliver new therapies and vaccines for people across Europe, as well as contribute to the European economy.  EFPIA, welcomes the opportunity to participate in the public consultation on the review of the requirements for packaging and other measures to prevent packaging waste: ‘Reducing packaging waste – review of rules.’  The pharmaceutical industry recognizes the need to shift towards a more environmentally sustainable future, including the European Commission’s goal to review and update the essential requirements of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive and by these means prevent packaging waste and set measurable targets and requirements to reduce the environmental impact of packaging.  This statement aims to advance the key view of EFPIA, on the public consultation initiated by the European Commission and includes the following recommendations from EFPIA:  • An exemption for the requirement to use recycled materials for primary packaging for medicinal products and medical devices (both primary packaging and device materials in direct contact with the medicinal product) • The scope of the updated Directive should provide clear guidelines for the pharmaceutical sector • Hurdles caused by conflicting requirements between the EU pharma law and the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive should be addressed in the updated version of the Directive • EFPIA would be willing to work with Commission and relevant partners to identify the quality and supply chain drivers that would enable uptake of any resulting new recycled materials while guaranteeing patient safety • The Commission should consider investing public R&D funds in seeking solutions to the safety issues around primary packaging in medicines and other highly regulated sectors. This could support relevant initiatives under the European Green Deal. EFPIA and its members would support with such a workstream

Background  The purpose of the open public consultation ‘Reducing packaging waste – review of rules’ is to gather views on possible measures to review Directive [Directive 94/62/EC] on Packaging and Packaging Waste aiming to improve packaging design to facilitate its cost-effective recycling and reuse and reduce the generation of packaging waste.  The current essential requirements are at the core of the review. They specify standards and demands for: • the manufacturing and composition of packaging • the reusable nature of packaging • the recoverable nature of packaging  The review conducted by the European Commission will include: • improving packaging design to promote reuse and recycling • increasing recycled content in packaging • tackling excessive packaging • reducing packaging waste.   The EFPIA positioning on the Open Consultation   Key points of view The pharmaceutical sector is one of the most regulated in Europe and the world, operating effectively within an evidence-based framework to maintain the highest standards, ensuring packaging never compromises patient safety or product effectiveness. The packaging material is intended to protect and safely deliver the product to the patient and health care professionals. Packaging includes primary, secondary1 and tertiary2 units which are interdependent on each other.   The purpose of packaging is to contain, protect, transport and identify drug product until patient administration and/or use. Through regulations, it is required that the selected packaging proves to support and maintain product quality, safety, identity, purity and potency. EFPIA member companies are also contributing to the reduction of waste by transitioning to a Circular Economy and ensure sustainable, safe and cost-effective recycling solutions for packaging materials originating from our sector.  1. The definitions on primary and secondary packaging differ between the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive and in the Pharma6 and Medical Device Regulations7.  Deciding whether a drug container with a functionality, e.g. a cartridge with a moving plunger, should be considered a device (drug container) or a packaging material requires very specific and technical expertise. The current EU regulatory framework doesn’t provide for enough clarity and certainty to define what falls within the scope of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive and EFPIA suggests including more examples on pharmaceuticals and medical devices in Annex 1 to support clarification of scope and harmonization across the EU. Reference8 gives an example of how this is done on national level in Belgium.  The complexity of packaging has increased even more due to the requirements on serialization9 and validated sealing on medical device and pharmaceutical packaging. Since the tamper evident seals (or equivalent) cannot be broken by the dispensing pharmacy, the outer packaging will - in some EU member states – carry an instruction label with sensitive personal data (e.g. name and personal identification number) that should not be exposed during the recycling process.    Due to the high quality and purity requirements for medicines10 the use of recycled plastic content is not currently achievable for direct contact materials under present pharmaceutical regulatory requirements, as the manufacture of plastic containers for pharmaceuticals is required to preclude the contamination with other plastic materials or their ingredients. Product-contact packaging plays a critical role in the overall safety and efficacy of a healthcare (drug) product and the potential for unknown substances (contaminants) in the recycled plastic to interact with our products is of significant concern.  This concern is also addressed by the biological evaluation requirements (ISO10993) for primary containers with direct contact to the human body or the pharmaceutical product.  To our knowledge, there has been no testing of the effect of primary packaging based on recycled plastics on medicine safety and there are very few recycled plastics available that meet the necessary quality grade for such testing.   Our initial estimates – outlined below in Annex 1 – suggest that, once a suitable material is available for use, then the necessary development of packaging and testing would take substantial time and investment to meet the high standards, incl. the requirements of stability and safety,  that regulators and industry deem essential.

For primary packaging made of glass, strict requirements also apply11, that ensures control of the extractables and leachable profiles and safeguards patient safety. The Commission Decision of 19 Feb 200112 allows for a derogation for glass packaging in relation to the heavy metal concentration. Accordingly, some recycled glass materials can be added but in strictly limited amounts if quality should not be jeopardized.   EFPIA and its members recommend that: • an Article 20 exemption be given to primary packaging for pharmaceutical products, until such a time when the pharma sector packaging rules are amended that supports the use of recycled materials in primary packaging and a commercial market for such packaging exists in the EU. • the updated Directive on Packaging and Packaging Waste provide more guidance on the scope.  2.  Recycling and reuse Recycling and reuse are important steps to decoupling plastic packaging use from the consumption of fossil-based feedstock and to reduce waste generation in general for packaging materials. Several hurdles still must be overcome in order to make the Circular Economy principles work well for secondary pharmaceutical packaging:   • Requirements of recyclability marking of material type on packaging material is impacted by regulatory approval of labelling on e.g. sales cartons. As any marking on a pharmaceutical labelling must be approved by health authorities before placing on the market, any recyclability requirements must be aligned with the European Medicines Agency (EMA). • The risk of abuse of discarded primary or secondary packaging for falsified medicine/counterfeit medicine must be addressed and shredding or destruction of components at point of dispense/collection is imperative to the prevention of such elicit potential. • Additionally, even the use of secondary packaging containing recycled content (e.g. PCR13 containing paperboard) is limited in some non-EU markets due to dust particle contamination in packaging lines. Introducing a mandatory recycled content target would therefore result in different packaging material configurations for different markets, hampering standardization and particularly causing challenges for low volume products. • Moreover, chemical consistency can be impacted, when introducing recycled content in paperboard for pharma packaging, leading to unknown migration of substances: there has been recorded instances and recalls of migration of inks and heavy metals from paper board cartons into the product through primary packaging specifically for liquid and gels14; 15. Consequently, patient safety could be jeopardized, and the material would have to be rejected. • For secondary packaging, partnerships with academia, regulators and specialist within packaging industry could pave the way for safe, innovative solutions for the use of recycled materials, considering the specific approval requirements that also apply for secondary pharmaceutical packaging. The EFPIA members would be supportive of such collaborations. EFPIA recommends considering innovative partnership projects to focus on identifying solutions on the use of recyclable materials for secondary packaging materials for pharmaceuticals while still ensuring patient safety.  3.  Recycling Waste Today most pharmaceutical packaging used in hospitals are incinerated with energy recovery. Turning waste into valuable secondary raw materials to be used by relevant industrial partners, is an opportunity for the pharmaceutical industry to minimize the environmental impact of its activities across the entire value chain. This approach is already widely adopted by the pharmaceutical sector, also for packaging materials, as is the circular economy principle of designing out waste of processes and products whenever possible and enabling the transformation of discarded materials into new resources.  Innovation is needed in the EU to permit the recycling of discarded primary packaging for pharmaceuticals, which is today in most cases classified as hazardous waste and thus considered difficult to recycle due to the possible contamination with residues of medicine (e.g. cartridges).    This calls for a dialogue between the pharma industry, the competent  authorities for waste and waste handlers to provide for safe, innovative and compliant ways to facilitate the recycling of primary packaging of pharmaceutical products and keep the material in use for consumption in other sectors while making sure that any hazard is eliminated resulting in no community health impact.  Controlled and highly regulated mechanisms of collecting and recycling these components must be outlined in order to mitigate risk to unauthorized distribution of “recycled” components through elicit means as seen in cases of counterfeit, diverted and/or adulterated product.  EFPIA would support initiatives to develop such technical and logistic processes in collaboration with waste handlers and partners from other relevant sectors aiming at turning hazardous waste into secondary raw materials.  EFPIA proposes to work with all stakeholders to reduce the barriers for recycling of used primary packaging.  4.  Electronic leaflets Another option for the pharmaceutical industry to support the principles of the EU Circular Economy, would be a transition to the use of product information in electronic format (ePI16) as addressed in the newly published EU Pharmaceutical Strategy17. This would on the one hand facilitate the delivery of safe and up-to-date information on the medicine to healthcare professionals and patients in the EU’s multilingual environment and support wider availability of medicines across Member States while significantly reducing the spend of valuable materials in terms of paper.           Further to the European Commission Pharmaceutical Strategy, the value of ePI has been clearly recognised in the draft European Medicines Regulatory Network Strategy18 where the multiple benefits for patients’ empowerment, operational excellence and sustainability of the Regulatory Network have been envisaged for the all healthcare and pharmaceutical environment.

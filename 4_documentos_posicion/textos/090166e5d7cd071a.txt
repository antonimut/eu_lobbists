AIMA and ACC Response to AIFMD Review Inception Impact Assessment 
The Alternative Investment Management Association L imited (AIMA)1 and the Alternative Credit 
Council (ACC)2 appreciate the opportunity to comment on  the European Commission’s  
(Commission) inception impact assessment on the AIFMD review. 
Our members note that the AIFMD review happens in a particular context with important 
economic challenges ahead. The Commission has confirmed the importance  of developing and 
supporting the growth of EU capital markets in its recently adopted Capital Markets Union (CMU) 
action plan to help address these economic challenges .3 Fund managers, and notably AIFMs, are 
an essential component of the CMU project and, if the objective is to deepen and enlarge EU capital 
markets, a clear decision should be taken to consider how and whether the review of the AIFMD 
can support the development and activities of fund managers in EU capital markets.

Indeed, a lthough we understand that the AIFMD review was foreseen in the AIFMD itself, our 
members’ widely shared concern is that any attempt to change, clarify or “improve” the framework 
would create more uncertainties in the legal framework,  generate more operational costs and 
ultimately reduce the bandwidth for our members to focus on their activities and contribute to 
the economic recovery.   
Furthermore, a Proposed Directive, as foreseen in the inception impact assessment, is a legislative 
proposal that is discussed at a political level, due  to the nature of the  EU legislative process. We 
are concerned that highly technical discussions could become subject to  political compromises 
which often result in decisions that have little to do with the reality of the market. Although difficult 
to quan tify, our view is that such “level 1” process conducted in the current climate has the 
potential to create long-lasting damage for an entire industry , hindering the investment needed 
to support the economic recovery and negatively impacting the Commission’s ultimate objective 
to develop liquid and attractive capital markets. Our key recommendation is therefore to avoid a 
general overhaul of the current framework and focus any proposals for potential “improvements” 
or clarifications of the AIFMD on amendment s to existing  delegated acts or using supervisory 
guidelines. 
Maintaining the original purpose of this directive should also be a priority: the AIFMD should 
remain a “manager -focused” directive dedicated to the wholesale market.  It should not include 
financial products-specific requirements (for example on retail AIFs or loan originating funds) and 
it should also avoid conflating retail market issues with the wholesale market.  
As regards macro -prudential tools, AIMA  and ACC  note the resilience  of the EU AIFM industry 
during the COVID-19 crisis. This real life stress test demonstrates the robust framework provided 
by the AIFMD to ensure fund managers  set adequate liquidity management policies and efficiently 
use their liquidity risk management tools. 
As regards the use of leverage, we support the International Organi zation of Securities 
Commissions’ recommendations to improve leverage measurement and assessment.  We support 
these recommendations being embedded in the technical provisions of the AIFMD reporting 
framework. 
Finally, we feel that most of the concepts addressed in the Level 1 text of the AIFMD and further 
clarified in the delegated regulation 231/2013 (the ‘AIFMR’)  or by the various level 3 guidance 
issued by ESMA , such as  the concepts of investing on own account or the  delegation of some 
functions to third parties, are well defined and do not require any further clarification. We would 
recommend that NCAs use the supervisory convergence tools that are already available to them 
to support the common application of the European rules. We are concerned that reviewing these 
concepts in the AIFMD Level 1 text or even in AIFMR  would result in profound, and unnecessary, 
disruption of the actual regime. 
We would also like to precise that these  comments on the  general topics mentioned by the 
Commission in its inception impact assessment paper are to be considered as preliminary 
feedback on which we are still consulting our members. Our final position and comments will be 
detailed in our response to the public consultation ending on 29 January 2021.

1- Sub-threshold AIFMs passports 
We feel that the current AIFMD framework strikes the right balance between the breadth of 
regulatory requirements and the rights conferred to firms meeting those regulatory requirements, 
i.e., the ability to passport their EU AIFs across the EU . Changing this balance to enable sub -
threshold AIFMs to market their AIFs in other Member States  would probably lead to raising the 
level of regulatory requirements sub-threshold AIFMs are subject to today  for all sub -threshold 
AIFMs and not just those wanting to market outside their home Member State, raising costs and 
perhaps being detrimental to their overall development  where costs are fixed but revenue is 
potentially variable due to volatile markets. Rather than impairing all sub-threshold AIFMs, we feel 
this desire for further marketing options  should be dealt with at a national level or within 
regulatory vehicles that offer the passport for smaller funds (EuVECA, EuSEF and ELTIF).  
2- Depositary passport 
We agree with the Commission staff’s conclusions from its Staff working document accompanying 
the report assessing the application and the scope of the AIFMD (SWD(2020)110 final)  that 
the “AIFMD regime for depositories is judged to remain relevant, effective and efficient”. Although 
we are in favour of a passport, we do not think this should come at the cost of a general re-opening 
of the AIFMD Level 1. 
3- Loan originating AIFs 
The establishment of a specific framework for loan originati ng funds within the AIFMD would 
represent a significant departure from  the existing AIFMD framework  which, in order to 
accommodate a heterogeneity of investment strategies, avoids the regulation of the structure or 
the composition of the portfolio of AIFs (See Recital 10 of the AIFMD). 
We agree with this approach which ensures there is a consistent framework for different types of 
alternative investment strategies (e.g. , hedge funds, private credit, private equity, real estate , 
infrastructure). This has proven  to be the most  effective way of regulating the sector at the 
European level. Any introduction of ‘product’ level requirements within the AIFMD risks 
undermining the effectiveness of the directive by distorting the framework .  We note that the 
AIFMD specifically provides for Member States to introduce product-specific requirements where 
they feel there is a supervisory need.  Therefore , we do not feel it is necessary for this matter to 
be considered within the context of the AIFMD review. 
Loan originating AIFs do not pose any unique  supervisory risks that cannot be addressed using 
tools currently available to supervisors  within the AIFMD.  These include  rules and requirements 
relating to  the authori sation of AIFMs, liquidity management, investment risk management , 
portfolio diversification, investor protection as well as the use of leverage.  The AIFMD framework 
therefore provides NCAs with the necessary tools to authorise and supervise loan originating AIFs. 
Our white paper on non-bank lending in Europe  provides a detailed analysis of how loan 
origination funds can be supervised under the existing AIFMD framework . 
We also understand that the growth of non -bank lending in Europe is seen as a potential risk to 
financial stability.  We would highlight that, while the sector has grown in recent years, the impact

of loan originating funds on systemic risk and financial stability is unlikely to be significant.  Most 
loan originating funds operate in private markets, use closed-ended structures, are unlevered or 
employ modest levels of leverage  and almost exclusively serve  professional investors who are 
themselves prudentially supervised. Although non-bank lending is increasing in the EU, it is still a 
relatively small industry. For example, while the total market volume of loan originated by funds 
is approximately €200bn, corporate lending by Eurozone banks currently totals more than €5tn.   
Any assessment of loan  originating funds should also consider the positive role they play in 
financing the economy and provi ding much needed capital to SMEs and mid-market businesses.  
Enhancing the CMU will require the non-bank lending market to develop further in Europe.  This 
is particularly true at the current time with greater finance and investment needed to support the 
economic recovery .  Our members are concerned that any addition of further regulatory 
requirements within the AIFMD will hinder the development of the European non -bank lending 
market and have a negative  impact  on the European economy .  Instead, we would u rge the 
Commission to focus on the ELTIF Regulation and enhance the effectiveness of this framework .  
The ELTIF Regulation provides a more appropriate policy instrument to support the growth of loan 
origination by investment funds  and is an  area where policy reform is needed to realise the  
substantial unrealised benefits of the ELTIF regime. 
4- Retail investor access 
We do not have strong views on retail investor access to AIFs other than recalling that the AIFMD 
is a framework that was designed for the professional investor universe. We feel it is important to 
recognise and confirm that the primary role of AIFMs is to serve EU professional investors .  
Layering retail investor protections over the AIFMD requirements runs a very large risk of imposing 
requirements on relationships with professional investors that are neither needed nor desired in 
the name of investor protection . The market experienced with the MiFID regime what over-
protection of wholesale sectors can cause  in terms of administrative burdens and business 
deterrence, and we would recommend avoiding this  outcome in the AIFMD review. We believe, 
however, that the ongoing ELTIF review is a good opportunity to improve retail access to AIFs while 
not endangering the overall AIFMD framework. 
5- Uneven playing field between AIFMD, MiFID firms and UCITS management 
companies 
We believe the current framework works reasonably well and has been crafted primarily of 
principles-based requirements . Members have not reported any  significant difficulties in 
navigating the current frameworks and the controls and processes to put into place if they are 
subject to two or three of the regimes. 
6- Macroprudential rules 
Although we still do not think the current macro -prudential rules warrant a political discussion, 
AIMA and ACC support an improvement of the tool-kit available for managing liquidity risks in all 
Member States. It is important to recall , however, that, where the fund is a separate legal entity 
from the AIFM, liquidity risk management tools are to be decided upon and implemented by the 
fund, and not by the manager, because the tools are established through the fund’s constitutional 
  
 
5 
documents. Therefore, any regulatory changes on this matter should be mainly done via local fund 
structuring rules, although the AIFMD could establish an obligation for Member States to act  to 
make those fund structuring options available. 
From the inception impact assessment paper , we understand that  the Commission is assessing 
whether ESMA’s powers over third country firms ‘from a macro-prudential perspective’ should be 
increased. Although it is not clear which powers or tools this sentence refers to, we note that the 
AIFMD framework already allows NCAs and ESMA to have a good view of macroprudential risks 
thanks to (i) the systemic risk reporting that any fund marketed in the EU must provide to the local 
NCA and (ii) the supervisory cooperation arrangement between the home supervi sory authority 
of both the non-EU AIFM and the non-EU AIFs, with the EU host NCA that is required under Article 
42 of the AIFMD. We fail to see any particular reason to increase ESMA’s powers over third country 
firms but would urge the Commission to conduc t a thorough impact assessment and broad 
consultation on this matter should it indeed be considering such option.  
As regards leverage, our members would like to reiterate their support  of IOSCO’s 
recommendations for improving the  AIFMD’s existing leverage calculation methodologies which 
we have previously described to the Commission  as being inadequate.   Indeed, our view is that 
both the gross and the commitment methodologies are over -inclusive and do not provide any 
indications as to the actual risk related to the use of leverage. 
When looking at leverage from a macroprudential perspective in the EU, we would like to 
encourage supervisors and policymakers to run comprehensive, granular and data -driven 
exercises on the EU  investment management industry and its use of leverage to improve the 
general understanding of market mechanisms and how to best mitigate systemic failures.  We feel 
such conceptual and comprehensive evidence is currently missing at the EU level. 
It also has to be noted that during the COVID-19 crisis, very few suspensions were observed among 
EU funds (0.2% according to ESMA) which is an indication of the robustness of the sector. We 
therefore question the need to fundamentally review the AIFMD rules on the management of 
liquidity or leverage risks given these have proven to be efficient and sound even during the most 
intense real-life stress test we have experienced earlier in 2020. 
When it comes to reporting, AIMA is supportive of some targeted changes and additional fields to 
the AIFMR reporting template on the condition that  the frequency of reporting is being reduced 
and some of the current less useful data points are being deleted, so as to keep the reporting 
workload reasonable. AIMA position paper “ Improving Reporting under the AIFMD ” details our 
recommendations on this matter.  As regards overlaps with ECB reporting, our understanding is 
that this exercise is currently undertaken by fund administrators and applies to EU funds only (EU 
AIFs and UCITS) , when many EU AIFMs manage non -EU AIFs. Adding such a requirement in the 
AIFMD would therefore represent an operational burden, that could easily be avoided with a better 
coordination among banking and market supervisors. Furthermore, no additional data fields 
should be added to the Annex IV template to better capture AIFs’  exposures to leveraged loans 
and the CLO market.  An AIF’s principal exposures and portfolio concentration levels are already 
reported under existing requirements. It is unclear why any exposure to leveraged loans and CLO

market would merit additional disclosure through new data fields beyond what is  already 
required. 
7- Definitions clarification 
Although we understand the need for clarity for some EU concept s, we feel that such 
“improvements” do not warrant a political discussion but can be dealt with in supervisory 
guidelines or delegated acts.  
As regards the definition of “delegation”, we find Article 20 of the AIFMD to be clear, especially with 
the various elaborations adopted via delegated acts (Article 82 of the AIFMR) as well as  via 
supervisory guidelines and opinions. We feel there is no need to propose specific amendments to 
the directive itself and would rather  encourage NCAs and ESMA to make use of the powers 
available to them that have been recently increased with the entry into application of the amended 
Regulation on the powers and governance of the ESAs.  
Our members frequently utilise the ability to delega te some functions , such as 
portfolio management, to centres of expertise located anywhere in the world to efficiently serve 
their clients. They view the delegation of functions under the AIFMD as strictly  regulated in order 
to avoid any “letter -box entity” as set out in  Article 20 of the AIFMD and related portions of the 
AIFMR which have recently been reinforced with detailed and prescriptive guidance from ESMA.  It 
is natural for  supervisory procedures to evolve over time as new requirements are applied an d 
NCAs gain experience of how market practice has been impacted. This supervisory evolution has 
also been guided by the 2017 ESMA opinions and our members have recently seen the effects of 
this supervisory evolution through further specific guidance from v arious NCAs in the EU . 
However, the fact that supervisory processes have evolved forcing AIFM practices to evolve as well 
should not be taken as an indication that the regulatory framework for delegation in the AIFMD 
also needs to change.  
Our view is that delegation allows fund managers to benefit from economies of scale, 
specialisation and access to an adequate pool of service providers. This is ultimately beneficial to 
the CMU project and to the EU investors. Using restrictions on delegation to artif icially increase 
fund management activities in various EU capitals risks resulting in further  frictions and costs in 
fund managers operations, which would be detrimental for the end investor, as well as in further 
fragmentation of the market, ultimately defeating the purpose of the CMU.

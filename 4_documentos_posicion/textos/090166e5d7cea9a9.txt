 
Review of the Packaging 
and Packaging Waste 
Directive  
Position Paper 
To fulfill our purpose of making food safe and 
available, everywhere 1, Tetra Pak welcomes the 
European Commission’s review of the Packaging 
and Packaging Waste Directive (PPWD) to align 
it with the European Green Deal ’s objectives of 
achieving climate neutrality while preserving 
natural resources and ensuring resilient and 
sustainable food systems . Working towards the 
exact same goal we strive to deliver the world’s 
most sustainable food package: carbon-neutral, 
fully recyclable and made only from renewable 
or recycled materials. 
Packaging plays a critical role in a sustainable 
food system. But food packaging waste has no 
place in our environment. More can be done to 
strengthen collection systems and keep 
packaging materials in circulation longer. 
We firmly believe to achieve these 
objectives, the revised Packaging 
and Packaging Waste Directive 
should mandate that by 2030, all 
packaging is low-carbon, recyclable 
or reusable, and that raw materials 
are responsibly sourced.  
In the case of beverage cartons, we need a 
product specific collection for recycling target 
because what is not collected cannot be 
recycled.  
A future -proof PPWD that mandates 
packaging to be low-carbon and recyclable 
or reusable, while fostering innovation and 
food safety

Drawing on our  experience, Tetra Pak calls on 
the European Commission to  consider the 
following recommendations:   
Climate first 
In line with the European Commission’s 
commitment to review all policies based on their 
contribution to climate neutrality 2, the revised 
PPWD should require all packaging placed on 
the EU market to be low carbon , reusable or 
recyclable, and that raw materials are 
responsibly sourced, by 2030.  
This could be achieved by mandating that 
packaging marketed in the EU after 2030 must 
comply with standards on carbon footprinting, 
recyclability and sustainable sourcing of raw 
materials. Furthermore, the revised PPWD 
should mandate  a maximum carbon  threshold 
for packaging3. This is especially critical because 
total greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from 
materials used for packaging are already larger 
than those from global aviation and are expected 
to increase significantly, jeopardising the targets 
agreed under the Paris Agreement4.

Innovation rather than restriction  
The PPWD should be outcome-based and  set 
clear, long -term targets and realistic timelines 
for all stakeholders in the packaging value chain 
to achieve the desired objectives. If packaging is 
both low carbon and recyclable, there should be 
no further requirements or restrictions.  
Economic operators should then be responsible 
to determine the most effective measures to 
meet targets and to report on progress made; 
which competent authorities should monitor 
closely.  
Restrictions on the use of specific packaging 
materials or formats deter innovat ion and risk 
being counterproductive. For instance, single use 
packaging should not be regulated as one 
category: recyclable, low -carbon single use 
packaging made from responsibly sourced 
renewable materials with low littering potential,  
such as beverage c artons, contribute to the 
ambitions of the Green Deal and should thus be 
incentivised5..

About Tetra Pak’s Innovation Roadmap 
Tetra Pak is the global leader in providing packaging and processing solutions to the food 
and beverage industry in over 160 countries. Together with our customers and business 
partners, our vision is to make food safe and available, everywhere. Looking to the future, we 
will continue to innovate, using our expertise to meet changing customer and consumer 
expectations, in ways that protect food, people and the planet. 
Our current innovation workstream is committed to delivering the world’s most sustainable 
food package. We will invest approximately €100 million per year over the next ten years to 
develop more sustainable packaging solutions: 
 In 2020, we started offering customers our first aseptic package without an aluminum 
foil barrier which delivers an important positive impact on the carbon footprint while 
still protecting food from exposure to oxygen and light. 
 By 2023, we will launch our first fully renewable aseptic package. 
 By 2025, we aim to trial our first packages with significantly increased fibre content 
where the paperboard and all barriers will be derived from fibres and plastic is 
minimised. 
 Before 2030, we aim to launch a package that is almost fully fibre based. 
 
 
Clear definition of recyclable packaging 
A clear, harmonised and actionable definition of 
recyclability is key for the successful 
implementation of the essential requirements 
and will also contribute to eliminating waste 
which is not reused or recycled. Such a definition 
allows for innovation in recycling technologies 
and infrastructure and for new innovative 
products to meet the definition of recyclability. 
This definition should foresee that packaging can 
be recycled at scale into a product, material or 
substance, encompa ssing all recycling 
technologies including mechanical, organic and 
chemical.  
Beverage cartons are recyclable and recycled at 
scale in the EU and we are committed to radically 
increase the recycling rate in collaboration with 
the recycling chain 7. But without a legal target, 
our investments and partnerships are at risk. We 
call for a mandatory collection  for recycling 
target for beverage cartons of 90% by 2030 , like 
the target established for plastic bottles 8. Where 
such targets have been put in pl ace, recycling 
rates for beverage cartons are highest, such as in 
Germany with a 77% recycling rate for beverage 
cartons in 2019 . Increased collection and 
recycling of packaging waste starts with raising 
consumers’ awareness of packaging recyclability; 
we hence support clear and harmonised 
labelling of recyclable packaging.

play in a more resource efficient and climate

Low on plastic 
Our packages are already low -plastic because 
they contain on average more than 70% 
paperboard and thus are predominantly made 
from renewable plant-based raw materials. We 
will reduce the plastic content in our cartons 
even further; by 2030, we aim to have a package 
that is almost fully fibre based. 
Renewable 
Over the last decades, we collaborated wit h our 
supply chains, civil society groups and 
certification schemes to ensure that the 
materials in our packages are sustainably 
sourced. All our plant -based materials are fully 
traceable to their origins and certified under 
internationally recognised sust ainability 
standards11. 
Renewable materials should be equally 
incentivised as recycled content, particularly in 
food packaging. If managed sustainably, the use 
of renewable materials is advantageous for 
several reasons: they h ave the lowest carbon 
footprint12, are regenerative by nature  and 
capture, or sequestrate, carbon dioxide in 
growing trees13.  
By replacing fossil -based materials with 
renewable materials, the European Commission 
can also reduce dependency on finite resources 
whilst reducing carbon emissions.

The market for the paperboard used in beverage 
cartons is well-established: in 2019, the recycling 
rate for paper reached 71% and over 54% of the 
paper and board produced were based  on 
recycled fibres 16. In Europe, one out of two 
cartons placed on the market was collected for 
recycling and transformed into a wide range of 
new products17. And we aim for more.  
On the other hand, the market for recycled 
plastics is very limited today, mechanical 
recycling technologies are not available for all 
types of polymers – particularly polyolefines used 
to protect perishable foods like milk. 
Food safety re mains our highest priority, 
therefore all packaging materials (whether 
primary or recycled) need to be fully assessed for 
their suitability for food contact applications. 
Currently, there are no existing EFSA approvals 
on the suitability of recycled conten t of LDPE / 
HDPE for food contact materials.  
To help boost demand and expand the market 
for recycled plastics, we have voluntarily 
committed to use a minimum of 10% recycled 
plastics content across our beverage cartons sold 
in Europe by 2025.  
Low carbon 
Our cartons have a significantly lower carbon 
footprint ( GHG emissions or climate impact) 
compared to alternative packaging used for the 
same product categories because: the materials 
used are renewable, lightweight and transport 
efficient, and are produce d using mostly 
renewable energy. 
In the dairy and juice categories, a beverage 
carton typically has a carbon footprint 2 -4 times 
lower than equivalent plastic bottles and 5 -8 
times lower than equivalent glass bottles 18. In a 
recent study, beverage cartons also showed 
lower emissions when compared to reusable 
packaging (i.e. reusable glass bottles), even when 
the transport distance was only a 100km19. A truck 
can be loaded with 25% to 41% more milk using 
beverage cartons compared to glass bottles 20. 
This significant lower carbon footprint should be 
reflected in policy.  
At the same time, our carton packages help 
reduce food waste as they protect the nutritional 
value and flavour of perishable food and 
beverages without preservatives for longer than 
six months without the need for energy use in 
refrigeration and cold distribution chains 21. 
Indeed, the beverage carton is the only pa ckage 
available that is both recyclable at scale and has 
the material layers necessary to protect 
perishable foods such as long-life milk22.

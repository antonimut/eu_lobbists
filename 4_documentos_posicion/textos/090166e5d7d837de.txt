European Women’s Lobby recommendations towards an 
 EU legal comprehensive framework to disrupt the continuum of 
violence against women in Europe 
 
 
The EU should adopt  a comprehensive EU -wide strategy and an Action plan  on disrupting the 
continuum of violence against women, aiming at putting an end to all forms of violence against women 
and providing peace and security for all women and girls in Europe.  
 
The European Women’s Lobby calls on the European Commission to persistently work on the adoption 
of a clear legal basis for the EU to act on violence against women. Violence against women is a 
widespread human rights violation in Europe which calls on European level action and prioritsation. 
Only by securing the EU’s acc ession to the Istanbul Convention and the addition of violence against 
women to the list of EU crimes, will it be possible to finally give to European women’s the protection 
against violence they urgently deserve.  
 
To ensure the design of a comprehensive legal framework, the European Commission should make a 
legal assessment on the existing legislation on violence against women and identify all gaps which allow 
further victimisation of women in Europe. This assessment should lead to the adoption of a Direc tive 
on violence against women applying to all forms of violence against women in all spheres of life, whether 
online or offline, ensuring sexual exploitation and prostitution are included in European legislation. This 
should also set the ground for the Eu ropean Commission to review existing legislation to ensure 
coherence and consistency throughout the European legislation. 
 
The recommended process to develop specific legislation is detailed further below.  
 
Reinforcing legal basis for EU action to end violence against women 
 
First of all, the European Commission should aim at reinforcing the legal basis for EU action to end 
violence against women.    
 
Under the leadership of the European Commission, the EU should accede to the Council of Europe 
Convention on preventing and combating violence against women and domestic violence, the 
Istanbul Convention. This should be done within the broadest scope of competence as possible as 
per the proposal of the European Commission in 2016. 
 
The European Commission should also propose the addition of “violence against women” as a 
Eurocrime in the Treaty of Functioning of the EU (Article 83.1) 1; in order to activate the passerelle 
clause, taking into account the special need to combat this crime on a common basis.

Assessing the EU legal framework: existing and needed 
 
Simultaneously, the European Commission should develop an assessment of the legal framework 
existing and needed, to ensure all women in Europe are protected from all forms of viol ence, including 
sexual exploitation.  
 
The European Commission should develop an assessment of its legal framework, aiming at:  
- Identifying areas where current EU legislation needs enhancement to comply and be in 
alignment with international standards; namely the Council of Europe Convention on 
preventing and combating violence against women and domestic violence; the provisions of 
the ILO Convention on violence and harassment in the world of work; and the CEDAW 
general recomendation 35 on gender-based violence against women. 
- Mapping remaining gaps when it comes to ensuring all women in Europe are protected from 
all forms of violence. Special attention should be given to identify current gaps in providing a 
comprehensive legislative response to sexual exploitation of women, which is recognised as a 
eurocrime in Article 83 of the TFEU. EU legislation to address this matter should be in 
allingment with the UN 1949 Convention for the Suppression of the Traffic in Persons and the 
Exploitation of the Prostitution of Others and the 1979 CEDAW Convention, Article 6. 
 
By analysing the existing responses, mapping remaining gaps, the assessment should seek to create 
a comprehensive legal framework to tackle the continuum of violence against all women.  
 
Adopting a Directive on preventing and combating violence against women 
 
The European Commission must propose an EU Directive establishing minimum rules concerning the 
definition of criminal offences and sanctions in the area of violence against women, in order to 
ensure direct effect in the legal order.  
 
The Directive should introduce common provisions, taking into account the gendered perspective, to 
strengthen the prevention of this crime and the protection of the victims thereof.  
 
The Directive should be coherent and complementary to the Istanbul Convention. The EU 
Directive should mention the Istanbul Convention as a source for its rules and principles 2. 
 
This Directive will need to work in alignment with: 
● Directive 2011/36/EU on preventing and combating trafficking in human beings and protecting 
its victims;

Encompassing all forms of violence against women 
 
It should address all forms of violence against women, encompassing the forms of violence against 
women defined in the Istanbul Convention, and other forms of violence including sexual exploitation 
and prostitution, and violence against women online as detailed below. All these forms of violence 
should be considered in the framework of family/domestic violence committed by an intimate partner 
or ex-partner; in the works related sphere and the public sphere, committed by acquaintances or 
offenders unknown to the victim, whether online or offline.  
 
As per article 41 of the Istanbul Convention, aiding or abetting the commission offences above or the 
attempts to commit these offences should be criminalised. 
  
As per the articles 33 to 40 of the Istanbul Convention, the EU Directive should include minimum  
definitions of the following criminal offenses: psychological violence, stalking, physical violence, 
sexual violence including rape, forced marriage, female genital mutilation, forced abortion and forced 
sterilisation and sexual harassment. Based on the existing Directives on the principle of equal 
treatment for women and men3, this Directive should also include the definition of harassment and 
sexual harassment as a form of violence against women. Directive 2011/36/EU should be referenced 
with regards to the definition of trafficking. 
 
Including sexual exploitation and prostitution 
 
 As per the Article 83.1 of TFEU and in alignment with the European Parliament Resolution of 14 
February 2014 on “sexual exploitation and prostitution and its impact on gender equality” 4, the 
Directive should include a definition of sexual exploitation and prostitution, acknowledging the role 
which the European sex trade plays in driving demand to the global sexual exploitation of women and 
girls; and the newer forms of sexual exploitation which are causing significant violence and preventing 
equality, including pornography.  
 
As per the Equality model, this Directive should decriminalise all of those directly exploited in the 
prostitution system, and criminalise all forms of coercive control and exploitation including pimping 
and trafficking; and the prohibition of the purchase of a sexual act, while calling on Member States to 
ensure all necessary protections and supports are provided to those directly affected as provided for 
in the EU legislation.

Tackling the online dimension of violence against women 
 
In line with the recommendations of the European Parliament Resolution of 12 September 2017 on 
“EU accession to the Council of Europe Convention on preventing and combating violence against 
women and domestic violence”5 , the Directive should recognise that the online dimension 
exacerbates and maintains the perpetration of violence against women in all its defined forms.  
 
The Directive should adopt a comprehensive and holistic approach to eradicating violence against 
women, by ensuring that legislative measures recognise the online space as a facilitator of 
violence against women as well criminalising acts of violence explictly perpetrated in the online 
space.   
 
Enhancing data collection and protection of victims 
 
The Directive should include common standards for data collection to ensure a coherent system for 
collecting statistical data on violence against women and girls in the EU. In order to ensure a coherent 
system for collecting statistical data, definitions for statistical purposes might be considered such as 
femicide. 
 
The Directive should refer to the Directive on common minimum standards on the rights, support 
and protection of victims; and complement with minimum specific provisions as per the standards of 
the Istanbul Convention with regards to protection, access to justice, compensation and reparation 6.  
 
As per articles 59-61, the EU Directive should be complementary to parallel reviews of European 
migration policies that the rights of migrant women and girls are protected, specifically in relation to 
their residency status. Provisions relating to asylum claims should ensure that: gendered assessment 
for protection is considered, that reception procedures are appropriate, and that the principle of non -
refoulement is applied. 
 
The Directive should call Member States to work closely with civil society organisations, including 
recognised and specialised non-governmental organisations in this field working with survivors of 
violence against women with a gender sensitive approach, in particular in policy-making initiatives, 
information and awareness-raising campaigns, research and education programmes and in training, 
as well as in monitoring and evaluating the impact of measures to prevent violence against women.  
 
 
Reviewing existing legislation incidently addresing violence against women  
 
In addition to the adoption of a specific Directive on violence against women, the EU should review 
its existing legislation  to ensure compliance with the standards of the Istanbul Convention and 
alignment with the EU Directive on preventing and combating violence against women.

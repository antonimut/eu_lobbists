Feedback to the Roadmap on Inception Impact Assessment on Gender-based 
and domestic violence initiative 
 
Summary of recommendations 
 
In order to ensure that women and girls in their diversity enjoy full and equal 
protection throughout EU Member States, it is imperative that the Commission 
develops a holistic legislative initiative that provides minimum standards on gender -
based violence and domestic violence as proposed under option III.  
 
The proposed initiative must be worded and designed so that resulting policies and 
actions will address the full variety of intersectional experiences of women and girls, 
including LBTI women and girls in accordance with international and regional human 
rights framework. The initiative should harmonise terminology as much as possible 
where it is mentioned in the new Gender Equality Strategy, by framing the initiative in 
reference to women and girls in a ll their diversity, which the Gender Equality 
Strategy defines as “where women or men are mentioned, these are a 
heterogeneous categories including in relation to their sex, gender identity, gender 
expression or sex characteristics 1. It affirms the commitment to leave no one behind 
and achieve a gender equal Europe for everyone, regardless of their sex, racial or 
ethnic origin, religion or belief, disability, age or sexual orientation”.1  
 
In addition, the initiative should ensure inclusion of experiences of LGBTI people and 
children in the context of domestic violence by using broad definition of domestic 
violence to include specific forms of violence against LGBTI children, intimate 
partner violence among LGBTI couples, intergenerational violence an d violence in 
the domestic unit as it is reflected in the Istanbul Convention. 
 
In cohesion with the Gender Equality Strategy and common European values, this 
initiative should reflect the reality of intersecting gender -based oppressions. 
Therefore the EU should consider including indicators related to SOGIESC, and 
collect disaggregated data on the bases of sexual orientation, gender identity and 
expression, and sex characteristics.  
 
In its continued efforts the EU must improve the quality of partnership b y ensuring 
continued EU coordination and dialogue at the international level with the UN and 
Council of Europe, 1 as well as coordination at the national and local levels with 
women’s and LBTI grassroots organisations, among other actors. LGBTI 
organisations directly working with violence against LBTI women and girls and 
LGBTI victims of domestic violence and victims support and crime reporting, coming 
together in the ILGA-Europe network should be included in consultation processes.

Introduction 
 
The recent Gender Equality Strategy clearly sets out to address inequality for  
“women and men, girls and boys, in all their diversity”. Going further, the Strategy 
explains that “the expression ‘in all their diversity’ is used in this strategy to express 
that, where women or men are mentioned, these are a heterogeneous categories 
including in relation to their sex, gender identity, gender expression or sex 
characteristics. It affirms the commitment to leave no one behind and achieve a 
gender equal Europe for e veryone, regardless of their sex, racial or ethnic origin, 
religion or belief, disability, age or sexual orientation”. 2 In keeping with t his 
understanding, this feedback will highlight opportunities for policy cohesion between 
the Gender Equality Strategy and the Gender-based and domestic violence initiative 
in terms of LGBTI inclusion and intersectionality, to ensure that the EU’s policies, 
programmes, and positions are comprehensive, cohesive, and modern, address the 
gender-based needs of all women and gi rls in Europe and beyond and acknowledge 
the existence of non-binary and third gender European Union citizens. 
 
ILGA-Europe, a regional network fighting for the rights of LGBTI people across 
Europe and Central Asia with over 600 member organisations in 54 countries, 
welcome the commitments made by the previous initiatives such as the  EU Gender 
Equality Strategy 2020-2025, the EU LGBTIQ Equality Strategy, the Action Plan on 
Gender Equality and Women's Empowerment in External Action 2021 –2025 (GAP 
III)  and Victims’ Rights Strategy  to assess the intersection of gender with other 
inequalities, and the recognition that gender stereotypes put all people at risk, 
without negating the unequal power relation that women and girls are forced into. 
With this feedback ILGA-Europe wants to highlight the specific needs and vulnerable 
situation LGBTI people and specifically LBTI women face at risk of and as victims of 
gender-based violence and domestic violence and puts forward recommendations 
on how the impact assessment will be made fully inclusive, and thus live up the 
commitments made. The following will point to specific components of those 
commitments, which require more attention in this initiative, to ensure the work set 
out through the impact assessment will be comprehensive, concrete and coherent.

2. Domestic violence 
 
Intimate partner violence is an issue  that affects people regardless of their 
SOGIGESC status. Even though there are a few studies on partner violence among 
LGBTI people, they highlight that the phenomenon occurs at a rate that is 
comparable or even higher than heterosexual intimate partner violence8.  
 
Due to absence of protection of LGBTI people from household and intimate partner 
violence, non -recognition of rainbow families in some Member States and LGBTI -
phobia in Member States, household and intimate partner violence among LGBTI 
people r emains invisible and unaddressed by protection and prevention actions.  
Moreover, to avoid exposing their family, their relationship, and themselves to 
unwanted negative attention and LGBTI-phobic reactions, LGBTI victims/survivors of 
household and partner  violence tend to avoid seeking help from law enforcement 
and victim support services9.

In addition, many LGBTI children and young people also experience violence on the 
hands of family members or co -habiting person because of their SOGIGESC, 
including exposure of LGBT children to so -called “conversion therapy” 10 by their 
parents or legal guardians.  
 
The 2019 Eurobarometer indicated that only 55% of Europeans would be 
comfortable if their child was in a relationship with an LGB person, dropping to 44% 
for an intersex person and 43% for a trans person 11. Experiencing family rejection 
significantly impacts on mental health and stress level of LGBTI people and can lead 
to harassment and, in worst case scenarios, to violence from family members.  
 
A recent glo bal survey on “conversion therapy” suggests that young people are 
disproportionately subjected to these practices. 4 out of 5 persons subjected to these 
practices are people 24 years of age or younger at the time of exposure to the 
“conversion therapy” practice, of those, about half were under 1812.   
 
Conversion therapy is seen as form of torture or other cruel, inhuman, or degrading 
treatment or punishment by various experts and against the best interest of the 
child13. Thus, to protect LGBTI children from all forms of violence, the initiative 
should recognise specific forms of violence against LGBTI children in the household 
by legal guardians and parents, including conversion therapy.  
 
Moreover, to ensure protection of LGBTI people from domestic and intimate partner 
violence, the initiative should ensure that legal definition of the domestic violence is 
similar to the definition provided in the Istanbul Convention and recognises inter -
generational and same-sex intimate partner violence among others.

4. Incorporation of violations against intersex people into the scope of 
gender-based violence alongside female genital mutilation, femicide 
 
Related to on e’s right to maintain their psychological and physical integrity, one 
additional important component of work under the gender equality stream of 
prevention of violence against women and girls in all their diversity is combatting 
female genital mutilation (FGM). In order to also address the human rights violations 
that intersex infants and children face across the world, any EU future initiative 
should include intersex genital mutilation (IGM) as a key focus, under the same 
auspices as FGM. 
 
Intersex genital mutilation15 (IGM) functions in a very similar way to FGM, but has yet 
to receive the same broad attention and clear condemnation. These practices are 
similar in that they (1) are both framed in terms of the need for social acceptance, (2) 
are both motivat ed by beliefs about what is considered acceptable sexual 
behaviours, (3) are both motivated by the notion that body parts that are not 
considered female (or male) enough should be removed or altered, and (4) are both 
impactful on the person’s life and health.16

5. Gender stereotypes  
 
The previous Gender Action Plan (II)  stated that “ gender stereotypes that 
disadvantage girls and women are a serious obstacle to gender equality. Also, 
certain conceptions of masculin ity put boys and men at risk of living a life of 
violence”.  
 
The Action Plan notes that gender stereotypes have a detrimental effect on women 
and men, but that ultimately “social norms lock girls and women into unequal power 
relations”. It also commits to  taking a rights based approach to gender equality, 
including assessing the intersection of gender with other social inequalities.  
 
Gender stereotypes have a detrimental effect on women and girls, which is 
exacerbated where gender intersects with sexual orientation, gender identity and 
expression, and sex characteristics (SOGIESC). In a context where binary gender 
stereotypes are the n orm - and most societies in the modern world are patriarchal 
and heteronormative - women are exposed to increased violence due to their sexual 
orientation, gender identity or sex characteristics. For example, research has shown 
that patterns of experienced  violence for lesbian women vary from heterosexual 
women in that, amongst others, there are a higher share of perpetrators that are 
known (or even a household member) and that LBTI women experience higher 
levels of domestic and/or intimate partner violence  than cisgender, endosex, 17 
heterosexual women. 18  In some cases of female genital mutilation (FGM), this is 
performed “as a cure for lesbian practices or suspected inclinations”. 19 In addition, 
anyone challenging the prescribed binary gender stereotypes is exposed to 
heightened physical, verbal and online violence (including effeminate cisgender men 
or masculine cisgender women, regardless of sexual orientation, or trans, gender

In addition to the intersection of gender with SOGIESC, various other intersections 
exacerbate the situation of women and girls, and must be actively incl uded in the 
Roadmap when talking about women, to ensure “women in all their diversity” are 
represented and included. These intersections include but are not limited to the 
intersections with race, age, disability status, ethnicity, religion, socioeconomic 
status, and stateho od. The inclusion and protection of LGBTI people is an 
entrenched European value, 21,22,23,24 and therefore, when talking about gender 
stereotypes, it is imperative to understand that LGBTI people are seen as 
transgressing societal norms of gender positions, ex pression and relations in a 
context where binary gender stereotypes are regarded as the norm and not to be 
transgressed. This heightens the marginali sation of LBTI women in a context where 
women are assumed to be cisgender and heterosexual.  In other words,  coherent 
efforts to address gender stereotypes must include a focus on the intersection of 
gender and SOGIESC to be able to address all implications and impacts of 
stereotypes and to draw out their root causes. 
 
6. Non-binary, gender diverse and third gender people exist  
 
In Europe and globally 25, there is an ever -growing number of jurisdictions which 
include legal non -binary or third gender markers . Also within the EU, an increasing 
number of citizens identify and are legally recognised as non -binary. International 
travel documents such as passports, as governed by the International Civil Aviation 
Organisation, have allowed non -binary gender markers for decades.  For gender 
equality policies and actions to be modern and comprehensive, the legal and lived  
reality of non -binary, gender diverse, and third gender people must be taken into 
account.

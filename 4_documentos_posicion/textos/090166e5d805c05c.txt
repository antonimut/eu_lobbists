Lumos’ Recommendations to the proposal for a Directive on 
Combating gender-based and domestic violence  
– protecting victims and punishing offenders 
 
1. Introduction 
 
Gender-based and domestic violence are serious crime s that can cause long term  physical, 
psychological and social harm to the victim.  Domestic violence is one of the most prevalent yet 
relatively hidden and ignored forms of violence against women and girls , not only in the EU, but  
globally. It is a health, legal, economic, educational, developmental and, above all, a human rights 
issue.1 Emerging data show s that interpersonal violence, particularly domestic violence, increased 
since the outbreak of COVID -19 in the context of the confinement measures. 2 The World Health 
Organisation estimates that 1 in 3 children experience some form of interpersonal violence  
worldwide.3 Lumos therefore welcomes the European Commission’s initiative to address the issue of 
gender-based and domestic violence and to protect victims and punish offenders , thereby 
contributing to the implementation of the Istanbul Convention and to achieving its objectives. In our 
view, this should be laid down in the form of a proposal for a Directive.  
 
In order to have a proposal for a Directive which is as effective as possible, it should address all forms 
of gender-based and domestic violence in different settings and promote the different ways in which 
violence and abuse  can be prevented.  Domestic violence includes violence that occurs within a 
domestic unit4, and therefore also within residential institutions for children. R esearch suggests that 
children, especially girls, that live in residential institutions, including orphanages, residential special 
schools and reception centres that accommodate unaccompanied children on the move , are at high 
risk of sexual abuse and other forms of gender-based and domestic violence.5 It is therefore of utmost 
important that the Directive recognises the risks for this vulnerable group of children , and proposes

2. The increased risk of violence and abuse in institutional care facilities 
 
2.1 The risk of domestic violence in institutional care facilities for children 
 
In residential care settings, where children are not protected by a safe and loving family, there is a 
higher risk that they will be victims of sexual abuse and other forms of domestic violence. For example, 
a study based on both sentinel report and self-report revealed higher prevalence rates of child sexual 
abuse in out-of-home care than in the general population, with the highest prevalence in residential 
care.6 Many residential institutions have frequently changing and inadequate numbers of  qualified 
staff to ensure permanent supervision which c ould prevent abuse  and other forms of violence . 
Domestic and g ender-based violence such as sexual abuse  can be committed by staff, volunteers, 
people from outside the institution or other residents. In institutions where adults and children live 
together, children are at greater risk of being abused by older residents. However, violence and sexual 
abuse occur as well among residents within children’s institutions. Sometimes the circumstances 
make the chances of abuse even higher: for example, in the Czech Republic, children who have been 
abused can be placed in residential schools together with children who have committed criminal 
offences or who display challenging behaviour.7 When abuse does occur in institutional care settings, 
it can be particularly difficult to uncover due to their closed nature and lack of complaint mechanisms.8 
When leaving the institution, young people are often still at risk  of being abused or exploited . Care 
leavers, especially adolescent girls, are at risk of suicide, drug addiction, sex trafficking, and criminal 
activity.9  
 
Trafficking is another risk which is connected to violence that children in institutional care face.  Aware 
of the extreme vulnerabilities of children living in institutions, human traffickers across Europe are 
known to approach potential victims directly inside or in the close  vicinity of the institution, often

2.2 The gender-based dimension of violence against institutionalised children 
 
Gender-based violence includes violence that affects women and girls disproportionately. 13 Apart 
from the risks of institutionalisation that account for all children, girls in institutions often face a higher 
risk of sexual abuse. Research on child sexual abuse in both foster care settings and residential 
institutions in the Netherlands shows t hat in 95% of the reported cases the victims were girls. 14 
Moreover, girls with disabilities face a greater risk of being victims of forced sterilisation when living 
in institutions.15   
 
In migration transit sites and reception centres the risk of sexual violence and abuse is also particularly 
high for girls, due to a lack of clear information and ability to access (female) interpreters, a lack of sex 
segregated facilities such as restrooms, a lack of designated spaces for children within shelters, and 
overcrowding.16 However, boys are also at risk. Reports of sexual exploitation of unaccompanied 
refugee boys in Greece, linked to a lack of suitable care and accommodation facilities, 17 highlight the 
breadth and gravity of this issue.   
 
Gender-based discrimination and violence can also lead to children being placed in institutions.  As we 
can tell from our professional experience with children with a history of institutional care and their 
families, there are cases where single mothers ab andon their children at birth because of 
discriminatory attitudes within the community or society towards them  and their circumstances . 
Violence against women within families can be another reason why children eventually end up in 
institutions, as well as unwanted teenage pregnancies. Moreover, women that experience domestic

3. Orphanage volunteering and the risk of sexual violence, exploitation and trafficking  
 
Sexual violence against children, especially against girls,  is a risk associated with  orphanage tourism 
and orphanage volunteering.  Orphanage volunteering is a term used to define a spectrum of activities 
related to the support of orphanages and children’s homes by individuals who are primarily, or were 
initially, tourists on holidays.19 In recent years, there has been a growing trend for citizens of wealthier 
nations to volunteer in and donate to residential institutions in the global South. Many institutions are 
set up simply to receive donations, in some cases through volunteers, rather than to help children who 
do not have families. 20 Traffickers may actively recruit children to fill ‘orphanages’ by deceiving or 
coercing vulnerable parents into giving up their children. This is being increasingly recognised as a 
form of trafficking and modern slavery known as ‘orphanage trafficking’.  
Recent research h as mapped the way in which European countries contribute to supporting 
institutions overseas and the harmful impact of short -term volunteering, and how this increases the 
risk of sexual abuse of children.21 An analysis of criminal investigations in the United Kingdom and the 
Netherlands shows that 15 to 20 percent of all suspected cases of sexual exploitation of children 
abroad occur in the context of volunteer programmes.22   
It was also pointed out by self -advocate Dieudonne, that unfortunately, volunteers do not always 
have the best intentions: “I remember seeing tourists, women and men, sleeping with children in 
rooms. In the morning, the children share their experience. They are obliged to be kind and 
courteous to the abusive tourists. As they are poor, they pay with their dignity for the right to have 
a little food and to survive.”23   
 
This is not the only harm caused by volunteering in orphanages: the regular turnover of volunteers 
who offer affection and care for a few days or weeks, also means that children only receive pockets 
of affection, without consistent and stable support. This  harms children’s ability to form secure 
attachments, essential to healthy development.24  
There is a growing movement of governments, businesses, charities, and individuals shifting away 
from supporting institutions. For example, the UK and Dutch governme nts have issued strongly 
worded official travel advice for their citizens to warn of the harms. The UK Foreign Office warns

4. Lumos’ Recommendations to the proposal for a Directive on combating gender -based and 
domestic violence – protecting victims and punishing offenders 
 
In order for the Commission’s initiative to be as effective as possible, Lumos recommends that it takes 
the form of a Directive, aimed at preventing violence and strengthening the protection of victims and 
the punishment of offenders.  
 
The below recommendations highlight the link between gender-based and domestic violence and 
institutionalisation as described above. Following the EU’s commitment to support the transition from 
institutional to family- and community-based care, as well as its declared priority to fight gender-based 
and domestic violence, Lumos recommends that the proposal for a Directive on combating gender-
based and domestic violence:  
 
1. Explicitly recognises that children in institutions and those who are not in a safe and loving 
family or family-based environment are at increased risk of violence such as sexual abuse. 
The Directive should therefore call  on EU Member States to support the transition from 
institutional to family - and community-based care and  avoid institutions as a response to 
domestic abuse in family environments.  
 
2. Encourages Member States to make sure  that any commercial act of  buying, selling or 
exchanging control over  a child in order to  transfer or place that child in an orphanage or 
other residential children’s institution is a criminal offence  to commit, in order to prevent 
child trafficking into orphanages. They should also be encouraged to make sure that the act 
of recruiting, transferring, transporting, harbouring or receiving a child into an orphanage or 
other residential child institution for the purpose of financial exploitation  shall be a criminal 
offence to commit.   
3. Focuses on preventive evidence-based interventions for the protection of children against 
gender-based and domestic violence, including the prevention of family separation, to 
ensure that children are protected by a safe and caring family environment. The causes of 
domestic abuse should be addressed, and intervention mechanisms put in place to make sure 
violence is prevented and families receive adequate support whenever needed.  
4. Calls on Member States to invest in targeted services supporting young people leaving the 
care system, as they are often at high risk of being sexually abused or exploited. 
5. Calls on Member States to ensure unaccompanied migrant, refugee and asylum -seeking 
children have equal access to the mainstream child protection system, so  they are better 
protected agai nst all forms of  violence including gender -based and domestic violence . 
Moreover, unaccompanied migrant and refugee children are overrepresented in residential

Institutionalisation of children 
 
Millions of children worldwide live in institutional care facilities, including so -called orphanages, 
residential special schools and migrant reception centres 26 that deny their human rights and cannot 
meet their needs.27   
There are numerous definitions of what the term ‘institution’ 28 means when referring to children’s 
residential care. A group of experts working on this issue for the European Commission determined 
that an institution is any residential setting where an ‘institutional culture’ prevails. Children living in 
an ‘institutional cul ture’ are isolated from the broader community and are compelled to live with 
children to whom they are not related. These children and their families do not have control over their 
lives, or decisions that affect them. Crucially, the requirements of the or ganisation tend to take 
precedence over the children’s individual needs.29 
Research consistently demonstrates that on average more than 80 per cent of children in institutions 
are not 'orphans',30 but are placed there due to reasons such as poverty, disabi lity, discrimination, a 
lack of family support services in the community and as a result of migration and trafficking.31 Over 80 
years of research from across the world has demonstrated the significant harm caused to children in 
institutions who are depriv ed of loving parental care and who may consequently suffer life -long 
physical and psychological harm. 32 The harm institutionalisation may cause to children include s  
delayed physical growth (height and weight for age) and delayed brain and cognitive development. 33

International and EU policy and legal framework 
A number of international and EU policy and legal instruments declare that institutional settings are a 
breach of human rights. The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) , ratified by all EU 
Member States, affirms that as far as poss ible, all children have a right to live with their families and 
that parents or other legal guardians have the primary responsibility to protect and care for the child 
(Art. 18).   The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD) and the UN 
Guidelines for the Alternative Care of Children35 also call on States to ensure that families have access 
to services which support them in their caregiving role. 
Furthermore, the UN Resolution on the Rights of the Child, adopted in December 2019 and co-drafted 
by the EU, expresses a concern that millions of children continue to grow up deprived of parental care, 
states that family- and community-based care should be promoted over placement in institutions and 
urges States to take effect ive action to provide support to families and to prevent the unnecessary 
separation of children from their parents, including through investment in social protection services 
and social services (para 34). 
The EU has recognised the harm caused by institutionalisation by introducing an ex-ante conditionality 
on social inclusion in the European Structural and Investment Funds (ESIF) Regulations in the 2014-
2020 programming period with a dedicated investment priority on the transition from institutional to 
community-based care. By doing this, the EU has played a leading role in supporting vulnerable 
children and driving the transition from institutional to family- and community-based systems of care 
in a number of countries across the EU . This commitment has be en further reaffirmed with the 
introduction of enabling condition 4.3 in the draft Cohesion Policy Regulations for the 2021 -2027 
programming period, and by identifying in the 2019 country reports deinstitutionalisation among the 
priorities for investments in Cohesion Policy Funding 2021–2027 (annex D). Moreover, the European 
Commission showed high political commitment for deinstitutionalisation globally by introducing a 
reference to the transition from institutional to community-based care for children in its proposal for 
the Neighbourhood, Development and International Cooperation Instrument (NDICI) .36 This 
proposal is supported by the European Parliament and the Council and has been included in the 
provisional agreement on the Regulation.37 
Additionally, the European Parliament Resolution on the Rights of the Child , adopted in November 
2019, also highlights the importance of strengthening family- and community-based services to allow 
all children to grow up not in institutions but in families and communitie s and of using EU funds to

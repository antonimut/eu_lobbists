Gender-based and domestic violence initiative 
Feedback to the European Commission inception impact assessment  
Submitted by Transgender Europe (TGEU) 
13 January 2021 
 
Transgender Europe (TGEU) is a member -based organisation created in 2005. Since then, 
TGEU has kept growing and established itself as a legitimate voice for the trans community in 
Europe and Central Asia with 157 member organisations in 47 different countries. 
 
Introduction 
TGEU welcomes the European Commission’s initiative to provide uniform standards for 
preventing gender-based and domestic violence and thereby work towards the EU accession 
to the Istanbul Convention. With regards to the options presented in the inception i mpact 
assessment, TGEU strongly supports option 3 with its holistic approach to end gender -based 
violence and domestic violence. The analytic lense used should be ‘gender’ in the sense of the 
Istanbul Convention and not merely focus on violence against women. 
 
EU policy framework 
TGEU welcomes the initiative of the European Commission that will follow the objectives set 
out in the EU Gender Equality Strategy 2020-20251, which affirms the equality of “women and 
men, girls and boys in all their diversity”, including with regard to their gender identity and 
expression (GIE). 
The European Commission’s initiative clearly points to the origin of gender-based violence; 
“it originates from stereotypes on the roles and capabilities of women and men and is 
inherently based on existing inequalities”. This situation results in structural imbalances that 
may translate to violence towards women but also targets those who do not fit such 
stereotypes and gender norms, including trans people.

In relation to gender, trans people face violence and discrimination with regards to their 
gender identity and expression (GIE) and are also affected by misogyny. Trans women and 
transfeminine non-binary people are particularly affected by gender-based violence. Other 
exclusion axes such as race, migration status, sex work status, age and/or disabilities also 
impact in particular ways the violence and discrimination experienced by trans people. 
The Court of Justice of the EU has held that the scope of EU protection against sex 
discrimination “cannot be confined simply to discrimination based on the fact that a person is 
of one or other sex.” It found EU law also applies to discrimination on grounds of the gender 
reassignment of a person, since it is essentially if not exclusively, [based] on the sex of the 
person concerned2. Hence, EU law covering sex discrimination need always to be interpreted 
to extend to trans persons. Newer EU legislation has replaced the outdated language 
("gender reassignment") with "gender identity" and "gender expression" as a better 
framework to cover all trans and gender diverse persons, independent of the willingness, 
financial ability or the existence of a regulatory framework to undergo gender reassignment 
treatment. 
The Victims’ Rights Directive (recital 17) refers to gender-based violence as “violence that is 
directed against a person because of that person’s gender, gender identity or gender 
expression or that affects a person of a particular gender disproportionately”3. The EU 
Strategy on Victims’ Rights4 (2020-2025) is also inclusive of the experiences and needs of 
trans victims. This strategy will hold a dialogue and coordinate with other strategies, 
including the LGBTIQ Equality Strategy. 
The LGBTIQ Equality Strategy 2020-20255 shows the Commission’s awareness and 
commitment to support the rights of LGBTIQ people in order to achieve full equality in the 
EU. This strategy emphasises the intention to mainstream LGBTIQ concerns into all EU 
policies, legislation and funding programmes.The European Commission has committed to 
protect trans people from violence, including their exposure to harmful practices such as 
forced sterilisation through the Recommendation on harmful practices against women and 
girls announced in the Gender equality strategy 2020-2025, in line with the Istanbul 
Convention (Article 39) that outlaws the forced sterilisation of women. 
Finally, TGEU welcomes the European Commission’s initiative as it will support the 
implementation of other relevant policy initiatives on preventing and combating gender-
based and domestic violence, such as the EU Strategy on victims’ rights (2020-2025), the 
upcoming Strategy on the Rights of the Child, and presumably the EU LGBTIQ Equality 
Strategy (2020-2025).

Data 
The EU LGBTI Survey II (FRA 2020)6 found that trans people are exposed to high rates of 
violence and harassment. Trans people were more likely to experience physical or sexual 
violence (17%) or harassment (48%) in the past five years than LGBTI people in general (11% 
and 38%). These numbers reach 28% and 61% respectively in the case of trans women. 
Young people (aged 15 to 24), and particularly trans and intersex youth, experienced 
violence at higher rates than those older. While most violent incidents take place in public 
places, trans and gender diverse children and youth also face neglect and violence in their 
own families, including through the denial of their gender identity by legal guardians. These 
experiences can drive trans young people into homelessness and render them to a cycle of 
poverty, exclusion and violence. 
Aggregated data from TGEU’s global project Transrespect versus Transphobia project, 
indicates that Black and migrant trans women of colour are more vulnerable and frequently 
targeted.Gathered data shows an alarming trend of a gradual increase in murders of trans 
and gender-diverse people per year between 2008 and 2020. However, these figures are not 
complete. Due to data not being systematically collected in most countries, added to the 
constant misgendering of victims by families, authorities, and media, it is not possible to 
estimate the number of unreported cases. Data from TGEU’s global project “Trans Murder 
Monitoring” (update from 2020) shows that: 
 
- 350 trans and gender-diverse people were murdered globally, 6% more than in the 
TMM update 2019; 
- 98% of those murdered globally were trans women or trans feminine people; 
- 62% of murdered trans people whose occupation is known were sex workers; 
- 11 trans people murdered in Europe; 50% were migrants; 
- 38% of the murders took place on the street and 22% in their own residence; 
- The average age of those murdered is 31 years old; the youngest being 15 years old. 
 
At the same time, those groups are repeatedly silenced and underrepresented within our 
communities and societies. Although COVID-19 affects us all, social differences and 
inequalities are deepened by the pandemic, emphasising gaps in lack of legislation and 
systemic protection of trans and gender-diverse people7. Data collected from TGEU’s 
community survey with members show that trans communities have been disproportionately 
affected by the virus, especially trans and non-binary people forced to shelter with 
unaccepting and abuse family, trans sex workers who have lost their income and face 
enhanced police control and brutality and many more8.  
Many trans people in Europe engage in sex work. Trans sex workers face a very specific form 
of discrimination. Social stigma and criminalisation of sex work expose trans sex workers to 
abuse, exploitation, and violence. At the same time, sex workers are often regarded as 
victims of gender-based violence by actors who define all forms of sex work as sexual

in commercial sex and have consented to do so (that is, are choosing voluntarily to do so), 
making it distinct from trafficking. Conflating consensual sex work and gender-based 
violence is dangerous for sex workers, since it makes actual gender-based violence within sex 
work invisible. TGEU therefore supports the full decriminalisation of sex work9, as also 
recommended various international institutions such as UNAIDS10. Any legislative initiative to 
combat gender-based and domestic violence should clearly distinguish between human 
trafficking and sex work and be implemented in a way that improves the rights and lives of 
sex workers instead of stigmatising and criminalising them.  
 
Recommendations 
These pieces of evidence indicate that many Member States have so far failed to effectively 
protect trans and gender diverse people from violence. Trans and gender diverse people 
need protection from gender-based and domestic violence through a holistic legislative 
initiative on preventing and combating gender-based violence and domestic violence by the 
EU. 
Therefore, TGEU recommends the European Commission to: 
1. draft a holistic legislative initiative on preventing and combating gender-based 
violence and domestic violence, which extends the prevention and support 
mechanisms currently in place for certain forms of gender-based and domestic 
violence to all forms; 
2. use a definition for gender based-violence which is inclusive of gender identity and 
gender expression (GIE) such as in use in the EU Victims Rights Directive, which 
defines gender-based violence as “violence that is directed against a person because 
of that person’s gender, gender identity or gender expression or that affects a person 
of a particular gender disproportionately”; 
a. Clarify that these definition is inclusive of non-binary people who do not 
identify as either “women” or “men”, “girls” or “boy”, but encounter gender-
based and domestic violence nonetheless. 
3. clarify that attempts to change a minor’s sexual orientation or gender identity 
against their will by parents or legal guardians constitutes a form of domestic 
violence and that the denial, neglect, rejection, discrimination or violence exercised 
by legal guardians towards children in relation to their gender identity or expression 
is considered as a form of abuse;  
4. include an intersectional perspective in the Recommendation on harmful 
practices against women and girls announced in the Gender equality strategy 2020-

Top Lines 
• ClientEarth welcomes the start of the initiative to revise and strengthen the Ambient Air Quality 
Directives 
• Legally enforceable air quality standards (“limit values”) fully aligned with the WHO 
recommendations should be the backbone of the revised Ambient Air Quality Directives 
• In order to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of the Ambient Air Quality Directives, the 
Commission should introduce provisions on access to justice, sanctions, penalties and damages 
• In parallel with working on the revision of the Ambient Air Quality Directives, the Commission 
should immediately use its implementing powers under Article 28 of Directive 2008/50/EC to 
strengthen the requirements for monitoring, modelling and air quality plans

Background 
ClientEarth is a non-profit European environmental law organisation with offices in Brussels, London, 
Madrid, Berlin, Warsaw and Luxembourg (as well as Beijing and Los Angeles). In total, ClientEarth 
currently has over 200 staff working on projects in more than 50 countries. Using the power of the law, 
we develop legal strategies and tools to address major environmental issues, we provide legal support 
and information to most of the environmental NGOs in Brussels (and beyond) and use the courts where 
necessary to enforce environmental law. The organisation is composed of programmes on Climate, 
Energy, Fossil Fuels Infrastructure, Trade, Oceans, Harmful chemicals, Plastics, Clean air, Wildlife, 
Forest, Agriculture and Environmental Democracy.  
ClientEarth welcomes the start of the initiative to revise and strengthen the Ambient Air Quality 
Directives. Air pollution is the top environmental risk to human health in the EU, responsible for around 
400,000 premature deaths every year in Europe. The rules laid down in Directive 2008/50 on ambient air 
quality put into concrete terms the EU’s obligations concerning environmental protection and the 
protection of public health. The revision of the Ambient Air Quality Directives is an essential step to 
ensure cleaner air, protect the health of EU citizens and implement the EU’s obligations following from 
the Charter of Fundamental Rights and the treaties. 
ClientEarth welcomes a range of elements in the initiative, including: 
1. the alignment of the EU air quality standards with scientific knowledge including the latest 
recommendations of the World Health Organisation (WHO); 
2. the focus on implementation and enforcement, including the introduction of provisions on 
sanctions and penalties to be established in national systems for non-fulfilment of relevant 
obligations deriving from the Directives; 
3. the stronger harmonisation of public information requirements in relation to air pollution; 
4. the strengthening of air quality monitoring, modelling and plans; 
5. the focus on impacts on public health, social equity, environment and fundamental rights. 
The importance of each of the above depend on how they are articulated, interpreted and implemented. 
We therefore recommend the following to be considered in the inception impact assessment: 
1 Alignment of the EU air quality standards with scientific 
knowledge 
1.1 High level of protection 
As found in the Fitness check, “the current air quality standards are not as ambitious as established 
scientific advice suggests for several pollutants, especially fine particulate matter (PM2.5)”. 
The European Green Deal announced that the Commission would align EU air quality standards closely 
with WHO recommendations. The WHO is expected to publish updated global air quality 
recommendations in the first half of 2021.  
We expect the Commission to show global leadership on this topic and enshrine the new WHO 
recommendations into EU law. Even if many parts of Europe are not in line with the current 2005 WHO

guideline values, various modelling studies show that compliance with WHO guideline recommendations 
is achievable in Europe, if supported by strong political will and ambitious policies.  
According to the technical report of IIASA accompanying the Second Clean Air Outlook, the baseline 
scenario will already bring levels of PM2.5 in the vast majority of Europe (90%) below 10 μg/m3 by 2030. 
In many areas, levels of PM2.5 would even be lower than 5 μg/m3. The same technical report shows, 
however, that without further legislative/policy change, the baseline will not change significantly between 
2030 and 2050. New ambitious air quality standards are, therefore, essential to keep driving 
concentrations of air pollutants as low as possible in the framework of the Zero Pollution Ambition. 
Another study from IIASA shows that it is technically feasible to achieve very low concentrations of fine 
particles in the EU. According to IIASA’s analysis, ambitious policies and measures can “deliver 
significant cuts in PM2.5 levels throughout the world. In Europe and the Americas, population-weighted 
concentrations could be brought down to about 5 µg m³” by 2040 (see Markus Amann et al., Reducing 
global air pollution: the scope for further policy interventions, https://doi.org/10.1098/rsta.2019.0331). 
The revised Ambient Air Quality Directives should fully align EU air quality standards with the upcoming 
WHO recommendations. When assessing feasibility of compliance with the WHO recommendations, the 
baseline scenario should include not only the current Ambient Air Quality Directives, but the whole EU 
acquis that will contribute to lower emissions of pollutants. That includes the NEC Directive 
2016/2284/EU, various relevant source regulations (including the upcoming Euro 7 standards and new 
CO2 targets for vehicles) and other such sector policies, such as for energy, mobility and agriculture. 
The impact assessment should also consider the overlaps and co-benefits between air pollution and 
climate change policies and should consider the increased climate ambition towards reducing 
greenhouse gases by 55% by 2030 presented by the Commission in 2020.  
1.2 Legally enforceable standards 
ClientEarth underlines that the backbone of the revised Directives should be a system of legally 
enforceable limit values. The Fitness check found that “limit values have been more effective in 
facilitating downward trends than other types of air quality standards, such as target values”. The 
revision of the Ambient Air Quality Directives should be based on the non-regression principle. Given the 
limited impact that target values have had in the last 10 years, it would be a mistake to amend (and 
weaken) the existing limit values into target values. Conversely, the Commission should consider setting 
more effective air quality standards for the pollutants currently regulated as target values and transform 
them into limit values. 
Target values can be useful as a temporary step for pollutants that are currently non-regulated (Ultrafine 
Particles and Black Carbon). Standards for these pollutants could be a target value during a preliminary 
stage, but should eventually become a limit value. 
Moreover, the revision of the AAQ Directives should consider establishing legally binding exposure 
reduction obligations. However, there are still many uncertainties about how to estimate exposure of 
people to pollutants. Limit values are an essential instrument to protect the life of every individual. Efforts 
to achieve compliance with air quality levels only with respect to population-level average exposure 
would have important negative impacts on inequality and the most vulnerable segments of the 
population. Therefore, the setting of binding exposure reduction obligations should not lead to the 
weakening of the system of limit values. Moreover, the revised AAQD should introduce a mechanism to

1.3 Mechanism for automatic revision of air quality standards in light 
of scientific progress 
ClientEarth considers that the inclusion into the Directives of an explicit mechanism for automatically 
revising the air quality standards in light of technical and scientific progress would be an extremely 
important feature. Article 32 of Directive 2008/50/EC expressly requested the Commission to review the 
standard for PM2.5 and, as appropriate, other pollutants by 2013, and present a proposal to the European 
Parliament and the Council. Even if in 2013 it was already clear that the annual limit value for PM2.5 was 
too weak to effectively protect human health, the need to go through ordinary legislative procedure has 
prevented the adaptation of the air quality standards to scientific knowledge.  
Rather than making the revision of the air quality standards subject to the ordinary legislative procedure, 
the new AAQ Directives should allow the automatic revision of the air quality standards in light of new 
scientific evidence. This mechanism can also be important for pollutants that are currently non-regulated 
(Ultrafine Particles and Black Carbon). 
The new AAQD should recognise the importance of expert advice and ensure it plays an independent, 
transparent and meaningful role in the setting and reviewing of air quality standards. The WHO 
recommendations are the fundamental reference. However, the WHO global guidelines usually are not 
updated before at least ten years. It will be essential for the EU to be able to receive more timely advice 
on new scientific developments. New EU air quality laws could create a mechanism to systematise 
previous experiences and allow the EU institutions to receive periodic updates on scientific 
developments. The REVIHAAP and HRAPIE projects provide a good model. 
2 Better rules to address non-fulfilment of relevant 
obligations deriving from the Directives 
The implementation gap relating to the current Ambient Air Quality Directives highlights why it will be 
essential to improve the legal framework to address non-fulfilment of relevant obligations deriving from 
the Directives. 
ClientEarth welcomes the focus on implementation and enforcement in policy option 2. In particular, the 
revised Directives should include provisions on three different aspects. 
2.1 Access to justice 
The Fitness Check found that enforcement action by civil society actors in front of national courts has 
proven to be important to accelerate downward trends for air pollution. The Roadmap correctly notes that 
“the effectiveness of legal enforcement action by civil society is linked to the functioning of access to 
justice at national level”. 
However, there are no explicit access to justice provisions in the current AAQ Directives. A horizontal 
access to justice directive was proposed in 2003, but was blocked by Member States and so was never 
adopted and has now been withdrawn. The result is that national access to justice rules vary widely

between different EU Member States. Numerous studies, including the 2012/2013 access to justice 
report (the ‘Darpö report’)1 and the 2019 Milieu Study on the implementation of the Aarhus Convention in 
the area of access to justice in environmental matters,2 both carried out on behalf of the Commission, 
demonstrate that there are still significant hurdles to effective access to justice at national level. The 
Milieu study concluded inter alia that “broad legal standing is granted by law and in practice in less than 
half of the Member States (13 out of then 28)” and “the issue of legal standing is an enduring one, as 
demonstrated both by the legal settings in the EU-28 and the experiences of potential claimants (NGOs) 
and national judges.”3 In some jurisdictions, there are limitations regarding the type of cases that can be 
brought. Overly restrictive national legislation means that changes in the legal framework will be 
required. 
While the CJEU case law provides a solid basis for pursuing clean air cases before national courts, 
access to justice problems persist in certain jurisdictions - particularly in Central and Eastern Europe, 
where national courts routinely ignore the case law of the CJEU, denying individuals and NGOs standing 
to challenge air quality plans. In particular, ClientEarth has already identified and faced serious access to 
justice barriers in Bulgaria and Poland (in May 2020, the Commission started infringement proceedings 
in this regard).  
Even in those countries where individuals and NGOs have standing, there may be other access to 
justice problems (including length of proceedings, costs and lack of effective remedies). For instance, in 
the Czech Republic, there are no effective remedies: in 2018, national courts quashed the air quality 
plans in Prague, Brno, Ostrava and Ustecky region – but the courts did not have the power to issue an 
order to the government to adopt a new plan. More than two years later, the government has not yet 
adopted new plans.  
The most efficient way of ensuring adequate and timely access to justice and achieving a level playing 
field across the EU would be the introduction of explicit access to justice provisions in the revised 
Ambient Air Quality Directives. 
On 14 October 2020, the Commission published a Communication on improving access to justice in 
environmental matters in the EU and its Member States (COM(2020) 643). In this Communication the 
Commission identifies as a priority area that the co-legislators “include provisions on access to justice in 
EU legislative proposals made by the Commission for new or revised EU law concerning environmental 
matters“ (para. 33). The revision of the Air Quality Directive is a procedure where this commitment 
should be immediately implemented. 
The Commission further states that “access to justice provisions will be drafted in light of CJEU case law 
as summarised in the [Commission Notice on access to justice in environmental matters].”4 This 
Commission Notice makes extensive reference to CJEU case law on the Air Quality Directive,5 giving the 
Commission an extensive basis for its drafting.

2.2 Sanctions and penalties 
As clearly demonstrated by the ruling of the Court of Justice of the European Union in case Deutsche 
Umwelthilfe (C-752/18), individuals and NGOs are facing hurdles in certain national systems in relation 
to the enforcement of the provisions of the Ambient Air Quality Directives. For instance, in Germany, 
authorities that refuse to comply with their obligations under the Directives only face the threat of 
financial penalties of low amount that do not result in a significant persuasive effect, for the reason that 
the payment of penalties does not involve actual losses for the competent authority. 
Conversely, in France and Belgium, court cases targeting air pollution resulted in the imposition of 
significant financial sanctions (up to Euro 10 million for each semester) that keep growing for any further 
continued period of breach (so-called “astreintes”). 
Clearly, there is wide discrepancy in the national systems, resulting in lack of effective remedies in 
several countries. The current situation highlights the need to revise the provisions on penalties in the 
Ambient Air Quality Directives and introduce a more detailed provision, compared to the current vague 
wording of Article 30 of Directive 2008/50/EC. 
In Deutsche Umwelthilfe, the CJEU stated that national legal systems must provide for “effective 
coercive measures in order to ensure that the public authorities comply with a judgment that has become 
final, such as, in particular, high financial penalties that are repeated after a short time and the payment 
of which does not ultimately benefit the budget from which they are funded.”6 In the sense of the 
Communication referred to above, the Commission should draft provisions based on this case law of the 
CJEU on access to justice. 
More specifically, in ClientEarth’s view, the most effective way to ensure compliance is to threaten time-
based penalties for further periods of non-fulfilment of the obligations. The payment of the penalties 
could contribute to the establishment of a “clean air fund” to support the implementation of pollution 
abatement measures and/or compensate victims of air pollution. The revised Directives could also 
include a list of criteria to be taken into account for the imposition of penalties and provide guidance on 
the level of sanctions. In this regard, the Commission could refer as an example to the systems of 
sanctions already existing in other fields of EU law. For instance: 
• Title VIII Enforcement (Articles 89-93) of Council Regulation (EC) No 1224/2009 of 20 November 
2009 establishing a Community control system for ensuring compliance with the rules of the 
common fisheries policy 
• Article 13 of Directive 2005/29/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 11 May 2005 
concerning unfair business-to-consumer commercial practices in the internal market  
• Articles 83 and 84 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation). 
2.3 Compensation of damages 
The Roadmap states that the Commission will take “into account the latest case law of the Court of 
Justice of the European Union (CJEU), including on the entitlement of citizens to bring claims for 
damages, within the conditions set by the CJEU case law on State Liability.” 
ClientEarth welcomes this policy option, as damages claims can be an important tool to ensure fulfilment 
of the obligations deriving from the Ambient Air Quality Directives. The biggest legal hurdle for

individuals who have suffered negative health impacts from exposure to air pollution is to establish the 
causal link. There is overwhelming epidemiologic evidence on the negative health impacts of air pollution 
on the population. However, given that air pollution is usually just one of several concurrent causal 
factors, it is difficult to prove the link at individual level. A recent ground-breaking decision issued by the 
inner south London coroner found that air pollution was a cause of the death of a nine-year-old girl, Ella 
Kissi-Debrah, in February 2013. However, it is still too difficult for victims of infringements of the Air 
Quality Directives to prove that they have actually suffered harm as a result of air pollution and to obtain 
compensation. For instance, several French citizens have started legal actions (including in Montreuil, 
Lyon, Grenoble) seeking damages for health impacts related to air pollution. While in all the cases the 
courts of first instance found that the French government has failed to comply with the obligations set 
under Directive 2008/50/EC, no compensation has been awarded, because the claimants have not been 
able to prove that they have actually suffered harm as a result of the infringement. 
Another example comes from a series of cases in Poland. Citizens deprived of access to justice to 
challenge the illegality of air quality plans turned to civil law and argued that air pollution is breaching 
their personal right to a clean and healthy environment. They have claimed damages for non-pecuniary 
damage. In some cases, the national courts accepted that “a right to live in an environment which is at 
least equivalent to the standards and values set out in EU legislation” is a personal right protected by the 
civil law and awarded symbolic damages. In other cases, such claims were dismissed. The discrepancy 
between lower courts’ rulings led to the referral of a preliminary question to the Polish Supreme Court, 
where the matter is currently pending. 
The revision of the Ambient Air Quality Directives should include provisions to harmonise rules on 
compensation. It would be important to regulate the following aspects: 
• Clarify that any person who has suffered material or non-material damage as a result of an 
infringement of the relevant provisions in the air quality Directive shall have the right to receive 
compensation. 
• Facilitate claims of victims of air pollution in line with precautionary principle and principle of 
prevention. Neither the burden nor the standard of proof required for the establishment of the 
causal link should render the exercise of the right to damages practically impossible or 
excessively difficult. When an individual can provide prima facie proof that they suffer health 
impacts from air pollution (for instance, by making reference to reliable epidemiological studies), 
there should be a rebuttable presumption that they suffered harm as a result of the infringement 
of the Directives. This rebuttable presumption should apply unless the infringer can credibly 
demonstrate to the satisfaction of the court that air pollution had no material contribution to 
causing the actual harm. 
• Establish clear limitation period so that victims have sufficient time to bring an action. In 
particular, victims should have at least 5 years to bring damages claims, starting from the 
moment when they had the possibility to discover that they suffered harm from an infringement. 
In this regard, the Commission could refer as an example to the systems of compensation for damages 
already existing in other fields of EU law. For instance: 
• Directive 2014/104/EU on antitrust damages actions 
• Article 82 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation).

3 Harmonisation of public information requirements in 
relation to air pollution 
Timely information about high pollution episodes is an essential tool to protect human health against 
short-term impacts of polluted air.  
The revision of the AAQ Directives should introduce harmonised information and alert thresholds for all 
pollutants (filling the current gap concerning alerts for particulate matter). Information should also be 
provided for pollutants currently non-regulated (Black Carbon and Ultra-fine Particles). 
The alert thresholds should consider the vulnerability of certain subgroups of the population e.g. asthma 
patients, who require alerts ahead of notifications to the general population. The thresholds should be 
set at the same level of the WHO recommendation for short-term exposure (hourly/daily).  
Authorities should be required to issue information before the pollution events occur, relying on 
forecasting methods and models. The alerts should contain information on the likely health impacts and 
provide recommendations on how to reduce exposure.  
Finally, the revised AAQ Directives should reintroduce the obligation to adopt short-term action plans to 
prevent and/or address high pollution events. 
4 Strengthening air quality monitoring, modelling and 
plans 
The revision should consider several improvements to the legal framework, including the following 
aspects: 
• Increase the minimum number of PM2.5 stations and set clearer requirements for the proportion 
between different types of monitoring stations 
• Introduce definitions of different types of monitoring stations 
• Require the installation of monitoring stations for Ultra-fine Particles  
• Ensure continuity of measurements for all pollutants 
• Strengthen the siting criteria 
• Provide clearer requirements for the content, publication and review of the documentation on 
network design and site locations 
• Require a more regular use of models and indicative measurements to support information from 
fixed sampling points and introduce reference methods for modelling and indicative 
measurements 
• Strengthen and clarify the requirements in Annex XV concerning minimum content of air quality 
plans and provide a checklist of pollution abatement measures to be considered during the 
preparation of air quality plans 
• Set out clearer requirements concerning the process for the adoption and revision of air quality 
plans, including requirements on timeframes, public participation and provision of information on 
technical assessment and forecasting. 
ClientEarth underlines that, while it is important to include the suggested changes in the revision of the 
AAQ Directives, the Commission should provide additional guidance on air quality monitoring, modelling

5 Impacts on public health, social equity, environment 
and fundamental rights 
Clean air is essential for a thriving society and economy. The first and most important aspect to be 
considered in the impact assessment should be the health benefits linked to stronger air quality laws.  
ClientEarth welcomes the intention to assess the impacts of the initiative on fundamental rights. It must 
be underlined, however, that improving air quality would have impacts not only on supporting a high level 
of protection of health and environment (Article 35, second sentence, and Article 37 of the EU Charter of 
Fundamental Rights), but also on protecting the fundamental right to life (Article 2 of the Charter) and 
right to private life (Article 7 of the Charter). As noted by AG Kokott in her opinion in Craeynest (Case C-
723/17), “[t]he rules on ambient air quality […] put in concrete terms the Union’s obligations to provide 
protection following from the fundamental right to life under Article 2(1) of the Charter”. Cleaner air is 
also essential to implement the equality and non-discrimination obligations under Article 21 of the 
Charter. 
Finally, ClientEarth would like to draw the Commission’s attention on another potential impact that is not 
currently mentioned by the Roadmap. When assessing cost-effectiveness of monitoring, reporting and 
assessment regimes in Member States, it is essential to take into account a benefit that often goes 
overlooked. Availability of greater information about concentrations of pollutants in the air and population 
exposure has allowed significant developments in science about health impacts of air pollution. It is 
difficult to put a monetary value on this benefit, but it is clearly essential. Greater scientific knowledge of 
air pollution is essential to support decision making and policies. This aspect would be particularly 
important with regard to supporting scientific research on different components of particulate matter 
(Black Carbon/Elemental Carbon) and on Ultrafine Particles.

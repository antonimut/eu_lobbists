PICUM Input 
EU Consultation on Combating Gender-based Violence 
 
January 2021 
PICUM is a network of 167 organisations that has worked for nearly twenty years to advance the 
rights and improve the situation of people who are undocumented across a variety of areas, 
including access to health care, access to justice, the rights of undo cumented workers, labour 
migration, the rights of children, families and youth, as well as fundamental rights in the context of 
immigration detention and return.  
We appreciate the opportunity to provide input on the EU’s Gender-based and domestic violence 
initiative.  
 
Background 
PICUM welcomes the European Commission’s commitment to addressing gender-based violence 
and domestic violence, and urges a forward-thinking and inclusive approach that strives to achieve 
real change in the lives of all women,1 without discrimination. 
Addressing violence requires acknowledging it. In the case of migrant women, their experience of 
violence is often made invisible or normalised. Europe’s migration system entrenches structural 
asymmetries of power and opportunity, based on socio -economic status, race and gender . For 
instance, immigration rules profoundly limit who can visit, work and live in  Europe. Visa programs 
and work permit schemes are based heavily on a person’s nationality, economic means and formal 
qualifications. While family reunification is one of the key avenues for regular migration to the EU, 
with m any women migrating as dependents of their spouse , there are nonetheless few 
opportunities for women, transgender and binary people to migrate on their own to do so safely, to 
find decent work and qualify for permits that would foster their stability, autonomy – and their 
safety. These restrictions exist despite European societies continu ing to benefit from migrants’ 
labour, social and cultural contributions, while failing to ensure equal rights, recognition and 
protection.  
Violence experienced by undocumented women is often closely tied to the economic and social 
precarity they experience because of measures aiming to reduce irregular migration, as well as 
persistent discrimination against migrants and ethnic minorities living in Europe. The reinforcing of 
Europe’s external borders to discourage people from arriving irregularly  itself has profound 
consequences for migrants’ safety, with women and girls exposed to violence 
Too often, when violence against  migrant women is acknowledged, it is through the lens of  a 
“vulnerable group” . This label mistakenly implies that migrant women are inherently weak or

susceptible to abuse, denying the many systemic factors that put them at risk of harm in a wide

The European Commission’s initiative on gender -based violence and domestic violence 
contemplates three possible pathways forward: 
(1) The Commission continues with the status quo, addressing gender-based violence through 
existing EU legal instruments that, while not directly regulating it, cover some types of 
violence against women;  
(2) The Commission proposes targeted legislative measures in specific areas, namely on the 
prevention of harmful practices; or  
(3) The Commission proposes comprehensive legislation on preventing and combatting 
gender-based violence and domestic viol ence through a directive focused on preventing 
such violence, strengthening the protection of victims and witnesses, and punishing 
offenders. 
We propose in the following how, under each option, the Commission can meet its commitments 
to tackling gender -based violence in a way that addresses, acknowledges and empowers all 
women, regardless of migration status.   
If the European Commission keeps the status quo (option 1): 
• There is scope to improve the situation of undocumented women  who are at risk of or have 
experienced violence through improved implementation of existing legislation and under 
existing policy channels, such as the EU Strategy on victims’ rights and the Anti-Racism Action 
Plan.  
Important strides could be made in protecting undocumented women from violence and supporting 
survivors by improving the  implementation of existing legislation, such as , among others,  the 
Victims’ Directive and provisions of the Employers’ Sanctions Directi ve that give undocumented 
workers the right to effective complaints mechanisms for violations of their labour rights. The EU 
Victims’ rights strategy  also contains relevant  action points for the Commission , including the 
gathering and exchange of good practices facilitating the safe reporting for migrants, regardless of 
status, among member states ; and possible legislative proposals by 2022, which could – and 
should – address the situation undocumented victims of gender-based violence. 
According to Article 1 of the Victims’ Rights Directive: “The rights set out in this directive shall apply 
to victi ms in a non -discriminatory manner, including with respect to their residence status ” 
(emphasis added). In its elucidation of Article 1, t he official guidance note that accompanies the 
Directive emphasises member states’ obligation to ensure that the “rights set out in this Directive 
are not made conditional on the victim having legal residence status on their territory or on the 
victim’s citizenship or nation ality,” highlighting the particular importance of equal application of 
these rights in the context of racist and xenophobic hate crime, and acts of gender-based violence 
committed against undocumented people. 
Beyond this clear statement of its non -discriminatory application, the Directive includes several 
provisions relevant to undocumented victims, including: 
• Articles 8 and 9 , which provide for robust rights to free and confidential support services 
that extends to all victims of crime and their family members, including emotional and 
psychological support, advice on financial and practical issues arising from the crime and 
on the risk and prevention of repeat victimisation. These rights do not depend on the victim 
reporting the crime to the polic e, or on their participation in the criminal proceedings or 
cooperating with any criminal investigation. And the Directive makes clear that a person’s 
status as a victim does not depend on whether a perpetrator is identified, apprehended, 
prosecuted or convicted (Recital 19).

• Specific recognition that victims who are not nationals of the country where they were 
victimised are “ particularly vulnerable ” or at “ particularly high risk of harm ” and might 
therefore need specialist support and legal protection, which “ could include providing 
shelter and safe accommodation, immediate medical support, referral to medical and 
forensic examination in the case of rape or sexual assault, short and long -term 
psychological counselling, trauma care, legal advi ce, advocacy and specific services for 
children as direct or indirect victims” (Recital 38).  
 
• Provisions that tackle language barriers by requiring that all victims be informed of their 
rights and of their case in a way they understand, that interpretatio n be provided during 
criminal proceedings, and that translations of essential documents be made available upon 
request (Articles 3-7). Recognising the information gap that often limits victims’ access to 
assistance and support, the Directive puts the onus on authorities to ensure that victims 
are properly and promptly informed of their right to services, and alerted of specific 
available services they may need, including legal aid, medical support and shelter. 
Special Adviser on compensation for victims of crime Joëlle Milquet has called  for the 
implementation of “firewalls” that ensure access to justice, protection and compensati on for 
undocumented victims , given the “particular vulnerability of migrants” , by ensuring that their 
interactions with criminal justice actors  do not mingle support with immigration enforcement 
actions. Indeed, this dilemma has been explicitly taken up in the EU’s Strategy on Victims’ Rights, 
among the EU’s priorities in the area of victims’ rights 2021-2025.  
Recent studies underscore that the protections of the Victims’ Rights Directive have not been 
adequately implemented by member states to the benefit of victims with insecure status. This 
means there remains great scope to promote improved safety, protection and rights through 
improved implementation. This may require additional guidance from the European Commission 
about what constitutes adequate implementation, as well as exchange and promotion of promising 
practices, based on insights from academics, practitioners and advocates working directly with 
undocumented people, and affected individuals.  
Protections for migrant women can also be found under other EU directives, such as the Anti-
Trafficking Directive 2004 (in the case of victims of trafficking in human beings) , the Employer 
Sanctions’ Directive (for undocumented people who have had their labour rights violated), as well 
as the Citizens’ Rights Directive and Family Reunification Directive (both of which make available 
residence permits for survivors on spouse-dependent visas).  
The EU’s Racial Equality Directive (2000/43/EC)  also bears on the safety of undocumented 
women. It prohibits discrimination on the grounds of racial or ethnic origin in the areas of 
employment and occupation, education, social protection including healthcare, social advantage, 
and access to and the supply of good and ser vices available to the public, including housing. 
Notably, the Racial Equality Directive does not address discrimination by law enforcement  and 
includes an exception under Article 3(2) 2 that allows differential treatment based on nationalit y. 
This excepti on has been criticised as a “significant gap in protection” that can obscure  
discrimination against people who “look foreign to the police”. 3 A report on the directive’s 
implementation will be published this year, with possible legislative proposals by 2022 to address 
gaps.

The EU  Anti-Racism Action Plan 4 recognises concerns about the “relationship between law 
enforcement bodies and minorities” and devotes specific attention to the problem of unlawful and 
discriminatory profiling.5 It addresses structural and historical racism, acknowledging the need to 
adopt an intersectional approach to combat it effectively; and commits to mainstreaming issues of 
racial equality across all areas of EU policymaking – including migration – through the work of the 
EU’s Equality Task Force, launched in 2019. Deepened engagement is foreseen with different 
stakeholders, including civil society organisations active in tackling racism, and a coordinator on 
anti-racism will be appointed to support the Action Plan’s implementation. As part of the plan, the 
Commission will also organise a summit on racism in 2021 and present legislation to address 
shortcomings in EU law on racial equality by 2022. 
 
If the European Commission  proposes targeted legislation  on harm ful 
practices (option 2): 
• Any targeted legislation on honour crimes must be crafted and implemented in a way that does 
not promote prejudice.  
If the European Commission prepares legislative proposals on the prevention of harmful practices, 
care must be taken to ensure that matters such as “honour” crimes, female genital mutilation 
(FGM) and child marriage are addressed in a way that ensures accountability for violence, but does 
not foster racism, xenophobia or islamophobia . Any forthcoming legislation should recognise that 
violence against women is endemic is virtually all societies. The design and implementation of such 
legislation must directly involve affected individuals and those organisations that work with them 
to promote their rights.   
 
If the European Commission proposes comprehensive legislation on gender-
based violence and domestic violence (option 3): 
If the European Commission opts to propose comprehensive legislation on gender-based violence, 
the following should be considered if it is to truly benefit all women, including women in Europe 
who are undocumented who have insecure residence status. 
• Any new legislation should f ocus on prevention of violence and strengthened support and 
recognition for victims.  
It may be tempting to tackle gender -based violence by strengthening criminal penalties against 
those who commit violence. But putting more resources into the apparatus of the criminal justice 
system does not necessarily achieve more protection or justice from a victim’s perspective, and 
indeed often harms women who experience criminalisation.  
 
Undocumented women confront violence in various settings – including in close relationships or in 
the workplace, from strangers or by state actors. The policy agenda is often limited, however, to a 
narrow focus on migrant women as victims of trafficking in human beings, and in particular  as 
victims of sexual exploitation. And yet trafficking itself provides an example of the complex factors 
behind women’s abuse and exploitation, which remain unaddressed (and may even be worsened) 
by the criminal justice system ’s response . Trafficking is a function of  global inequality, gender

discrimination, exclusion and poverty. Trafficking occurs within a context of globalisation, de -
regulation of labour and the normalisation of poor working conditions in some sectors, especially, 
but not only, for migrants. Strengthening criminal penalties for traffickers is unlikely to make women 
safer – particularly if the women they exploit are themselves criminalised for being undocumented, 
and face profoundly limited social and economic opportunities due to gender inequality, structural 
poverty and restrictive immigration policies. A broader social agenda and intersectional approach 
is needed to address the various causes and risks  for exploitation people face at different stages 
of their migration experience, and to realise the fulfilment of every person’s human rights. 
 
Any proposed legislation tackling gender -based violence should, therefore, follow the Istanbul 
Convention’s comprehensive approach in having a strong focus on prevention, and addressing as 
far as possible those factors that drive gender-based discrimination and violence. 
 
• Combating violence against women and domestic violence requires more investment in holistic 
services that reach all women. 
Related to the above, any proposed new legislation should prioritise the provision of holistic 
services for survivors that reach all women who need them, without discrimination on any basis, 
consistent with Article 9 of the Istanbul Convention and Article 8 of the Victims’ Directive. It should 
foresee tailored support delivered through trusted providers that are adequately and sustainably 
funded, including community-based and specialist organisations. For undocumented women, this 
is often women migrant-led and -focused organisations, examples of which exist across PICUM’s 
network.  
The lack of funding for women’s services means that shelters and other support organisations may 
feel the need to ration their services, and be in adequately supported to receive women with 
complex needs – including women who may have limited local social support structures and limited 
fluency in the national language , who in a time o f crisis need support in accessing benefits and 
long term affordable housing, and legal advice on how to regularise their status or resist 
deportation.   
There is a n urgent need for sustained political attention and investment of resources in quality 
support services for all women  – including support to obtain emergency shelter, housing, and 
financial stability following violence, as well as to address matters dealing with child custody and 
protection. Consistent with the Victims’ Directive, this support should be available to every survivor 
based on need, without discrimination and regardless of her status, the nature of the violence 
experienced or whether she chooses to file a complaint or cooperate with authorities.   
• Where women do engage with authoritie s and with the criminal justice system, this must be 
from a foundation of respect and professionalism, and of safety from secondary victimisation. 
Women who have suffered violence, including women with insecure status, often avoid the criminal 
justice system because they feel disbelieved and unsafe.  
For undocumented survivors of gender-based violence, authorities often respond to them through 
the lens of immigration law  and not based on their needs. This tends also to be  the case in 
workplace i nspections: anyone who cannot provide a valid work authorisation is at risk of  
immigration enforcement, and possibilities to exercise their rights as workers or potential victims 
of labour exploitation are limited. This perpetuates cycles of abuse and discrimination. In a situation 
where police are often the gateway to the criminal justice system, this cuts off access to protection 
measures and opportunities for reparation through the criminal justice system.   
 
For undocumented people who have experienced or witnessed violence, there must be assurances 
that engaging with authorities will not lead to  secondary victimisation. Legislative proposals on 
gender-based violence should  reinforce the Victims’ Directive’s guarantees of respectful and 
professional treatment by all relevant actors in the criminal justice system, and eliminate the risk 
that undocumented women will face possible deportation because of coming forward by putting in 
place “firewalls” that shield them from immigration control.

• Any new legislation addressing violence against women should explicitly reference an inclusive, 
non-discriminatory approach including about a victim’s or witness’s residence or migration 
status. 
 
Legislative proposals on gender-based violence must show coherence with the Victims’ Directive’s 
Article 1 (also reflected in Article 4 of the Istanbul Convention), which explicitly requires the non-
discriminatory implementation of rights, without regard to migration or residence status.  
 
• Any new legislation addressing violence against women should, as far as possible, contemplate 
residence permits for survivors of violence. 
For a wom an, being undocumented or having dependent  residence status creates a  power 
asymmetry that can increase the possibility of violence or exploitation, and make exiting situations 
or violence even more difficult, especially when coupled with extremely limited access to suppo rt 
services. 
Recognising that a women’s status creates this vulnerability, the EU Citizens’ Rights Directive and 
Family Reunification Directive, respectively, make it possible for women on spouse-dependent visas 
to qualify for autonomous residence status if their relationship breaks down because of violence 
by their partner. While imperfect in their implementation, these provisions are intended to empower 
survivors by removing the risk of becoming undocumented, which itself can  be used as tool of 
coercion and manipulation by an abusive spouse. Several countries (including France, Greece, Italy, 
the Netherlands and Spain) go further than these directives require, including undocumented 
survivors of violence among those who can qualify for a residence permit.  
Residence permits are also available, under EU law, under certain conditions, for victims of human 
trafficking and of severe labour exploitation; and some member states extend these protections to 
undocumented victims of various other crimes.6   
Article 59 of the Istanbul Convention and Article 14 of the Council of Europe’s Anti -Trafficking 
Convention make residence permits available for survivors either for cooperation with the 
authorities or based on personal reasons , which is more consistent with a protection -centred 
approach. New legislation on gender-based violence should, building on this foundation of EU and 
international law, as well as legislation in many member states , include provisions for residence 
permits for survivors of violence who are undocumented or who have dependent status.  
• A comprehensive approach to violence should address violence in the workplace.  
An EU directive addressing gender-based violence should take a holistic approach towards violence 
in settings that extend beyond violence committed by a spouse to include violence and harassment 
in the workplace.  
In January 2020, the European Commission adopted a proposal  for a Council Decision allowing 
member states to take forward a process of ratifying ILO Convention 190 on the elimination of 
violence and harassment in the world of work, recognising that half of women have reported 
experiencing sexual violence by age 15, with one third of incidents occurring in the context of work. 
New legislation addressing violence should ensure that all women can safe ly access redress for 
violence and harassment they experience in the workplace , as well as violations of their labour 
rights through effective complaints mechanisms, as defined for example in PICUM’s guidelines and 
consistent with Article 13 of the Employers’ Sanctions Directive . It should also reinforce the 
application of labour standards for all workers, regardless of sector  and in nonstandard forms of 
employment (e.g., sex workers, domestic workers, platform workers, etc.). 
• A comprehensive approach to violence should address violence in immigration detention.

Evidence shows that women in immigration detention centres are particularly vulnerable to gender-
based violence.7 In interviews conducted by JRS on immigration detention in the European Union, 
women aged between 18 and 24 reported they were exposed to physical and verbal abuse by staff 
and other detainees.8 
Transgender women in particular are among the most at risk of  physical and sexual abuse, 
especially when they are detained with men.9  
An EU directive addressing gender-based violence should take a holistic approach towards violence 
in state settings and ensure that effective preventative measures, protection syste ms and 
accessible complaint mechanisms are in place. 
 
• Any new legislation addressing and codifying violence against women should not lead to further 
criminalisation of sex work, and rather safeguard the dignity and human rights of sex workers. 
Criminalising the purchase and facilitation of sex work undermines the safety of  sex workers, 
putting them at greater risk of violence, discrimination, and other rights violations. Harms are 
exacerbated if a sex worker is also undocumented. Any EU initiative  addressing violence against 
women must not, directly or indirectly, promote criminal penalties for the purchase or organisation 
of sex work. Cis and trans women engaged in sex work are disproportionately exposed to violence, 
harassment and murder because of criminalisation and gender-based discrimination. They should 
therefore be among the affected communities meaningfully and directly consulted in the design 
and development of any initiative to tackle gender-based violence.  
• Adequate monitoring and evaluation must be foreseen, in a way that allows input from affected 
individuals and organisations that advocate on their behalf.  
New legislation on gender-based violence must include provisions for a dequate monitoring that 
allow input from a range of stakeholders, including civil society and affected people themselves, as 
well as support to those stakeholders to facilitate and acknowledge the value of their engagement.  
• Awareness-raising campaigns should accompany any new legislation, and they should be 
empowering,  
Any EU action, under existing or new legislation, should be accompanied by  public awareness-
raising campaigns that involve directly affected communities in their development and design; and 
integrating an intersectional approach to gender equality. They should go beyond social  media 
campaigns to support national and local efforts focused on raising awareness and understanding 
of historical and systemic forms of discrimination and racism against women; and on empowering 
women and girls through positive, inclusive messages affirming their rights.

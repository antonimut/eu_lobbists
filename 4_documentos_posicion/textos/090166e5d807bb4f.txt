I. Main thesis 
• Article 82(2) and Article 83(1) TFEU cannot be legal basis for taking legislative 
action by the EU in order to unify substantive criminal law of Member States . 
Furthermore, in practice, the planned imposition of unified definitions of crimes on 
Member States would be a violation of the treaty rule provided for in Arti cle 67(1) 
TFEU. Article 84 TFEU is also not sufficient legal basis for the Commission’s 
actions regarding the Gender-Based and Domestic Violence Initiative . In fact, it 
authorises EU institutions only to adopt measures promoting and supporting crime 
prevention actions of Member States. The provision also explicitly excludes any EU 
actions resulting in any harmonisation (even minimal) of national legislative and 
regulatory regulations. 
• The roadmap 1 presented as the basis for the legislative initiative propo sed by the 
European Commission is established in its entirety on the controversial gender -based 
violence concept. This concept assumes that femininity and masculinity are social 
constructs and that violence against women is a structural phenomenon whose ca use 
lies in the different perception of women and men.  
• This document indicates that the conclusion of the Istanbul Convention by the European 
Union is a political priority. Furthermore, it assumes that in the case of failure to adopt 
the Istanbul Conventi on by EU, it will be necessary to enforce a Union -level legally 
binding document based on the same controversial premises on combating violence as 
the Convention. Meanwhile, it should be emphasised that the Istanbul Convention is

are basically the same provisions of TFEU as listed in the request for an opinion submitted by 
the European Parliament to the European Court of Justice regarding the legal basis for the 
conclusion of the Istanbul Convention on behalf of EU 6. Therefore, also in reference to the 
planned legislative initiative of the Commission, the concerns raised in Chapter 10 of the Report 
remain valid.  
In the first place, it must be emphasised that the scope of the European Commission’s initiative 
is to be the same as the regulations provided for in the Istanbul Convention. Hence, the same 
provisions, i.e. in particular Article 82(2), Article 83(1), Article 84 TFEU, are considered to be 
the legal basis. It must also be stressed that the correctness of selecting such legal basis for the 
adoption of the Istanbul Convention by the EU on the one hand is still being veri fied by ECJ, 
and on the other has already been reasonably refuted in the Report. An analysis of the 
aforementioned treaty provisions shows beyond doubt that none of them is adequate legal basis 
for such EU action.

The communication announcing the Gender-Based and Domestic Violence Initiative  
(“Communication”) states that this action “falls under the scope of EU law on the definition of 
criminal offences and sanctions”, which contradicts a number of treaty provisions. The implied 
EU competences in this scope would include the possibility to harmonise substantive criminal 
law of Member States, for which there is no legal basis in the TFEU provisions referred to in 
the Communication or mentioned in the request of the European Parliament to ECJ regarding 
the ratification of the Istanbul Convention. Pursuant to Article 82(2) TFEU, the European 
Parliament and the Council may, by means of directives, establish minimum  standards of 
criminal procedures in Member States. To this end, minimal rules are established (referred to 
in Article 83 TFEU) applying only to matters with a cross -border dimension. The scope of 
Article 82 definitely does not include the implementation of minimal norms to substantive 
criminal law. It must be borne in mind that according to the principle of conferral, EU may 
only act within the limits of the competences conferred upon it by EU countries in the Treaties 
to attain the objectives provided the rein. Competences not conferred upon EU in the Treaties 
remain with the EU countries (Article 5(2) of the Treaty on European Union – TEU). Hence, 
in terms of defining crimes, the only competences conferred upon EU – referred to in Article 
83 TFEU – involve cross-border crimes. Article 82(2) TFEU also clearly indicates that based 
on this provision, only minimum rules on the following can be established:  
a) mutual admissibility of evidence between Member States;  
b) the rights of individuals in criminal procedure;  
c) the rights of victims of crime; 
d) any other specific aspects of criminal procedure which the Council has identified in 
advance by a decision.  
In this context, we must not ignore Article 67(1) TFEU, which states that an area of freedom, 
security and justice is constituted by EU institutions with respect for the different legal systems 
and traditions of the Member States.  
Therefore, it is clear that like in the case of the conclusion of the Istanbul Convention, Article 
82(2) and Article 83(1) TFEU cannot be legal basis for taking legislative action by the EU 
in order to unify substantive criminal law of Member States . Furthermore, in practice, the 
planned imposition of unified definitions of crimes on Member States would be a violation of 
the treaty rule provided for in Article 67(1) TFEU.

the Gender-Based and Domestic Violence Initiative. In fact, it authorises EU institutions only

2. The contradiction between the gender-based legislative initiative and EU primary 
law in the context of treaty terminology. 
Another legal basis for the Commission’s legislative initiative on counteracting and fighting 
gender-based violence referred to in the Communicat ion is Article 157(3) TFEU. Pursuant to 
this provision, EU institutions are entitled to adopt measures to ensure the application of the 
principle of equal opportunities and equal treatment of men and women in matters of 
employment and occupation, including  the principle of equal pay for equal work or work of 
equal value. Considering the aforementioned regulation, it must be emphasised that although it 
contains a laudable idea of levelling the chances for male and female employees, it is not basis 
enough for the European Commission to initiate legislation that redefines treaty terms. Article 
157(4) and other TFEU provisions use the term ‘sex’; therefore, the Commission has no ground 
to use the term ‘gender’. The term ‘gender’ is not used in the Treaty on the Functioning of 
the European Union, just like in any other legal act of EU primary law.  There is no doubt 
that the terms ‘gender’ and ‘sex’ must not be used interchangeably, because they have 
completely different meanings. The meaning of ‘sex’ is based on objective biological grounds, 
whereas the term ‘gender’ is rooted in a subjective feeling of an individual, including one that 
contradicts the natural conditions. Hence, ‘sex’ and ‘gender’ are not and cannot be treated as 
synonyms that could be used alternatively in EU secondary legislation.  
Considering the above, it must be stressed that the adoption by the European Commission 
of legal solutions based on the gender concept and ideology would require TFEU 
amendments, as its current version does not authoris e EU bodies to take such actions . 
Furthermore, the procedure of treaty amendments ultimately requires unanimous ratification by 
all Member States according to their respective constitutional requirements, which is provided

3. The legislative initiative of the European Commission will violate the principles of 
proportionality and subsidiarity.  
The principles of proportionality and subsidiarity are among the fundamental rules governing 
the functioning of the European Union. Pursuant to Article 5(3) TEU, the Union shall act only 
if and in so far as the objectives of the proposed action cannot be sufficiently achieved by the 
Member States, either at central level or at regional and local level, but can rather, by reason of 
the scal e or effects of the proposed action, be better achieved at Union level. Moreover, 
according to Article 5(4) TEU, under the principle of proportionality, the content and form of 
Union action shall not exceed what is necessary to achieve the objectives of th e Treaties. 
Importantly enough, in line with Annex 2 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European 
Union (Protocol on the application of the principles of subsidiarity and proportionality), each 
EU institution shall ensure constant respect for the principles of subsidiarity and proportionality 
(Article 1) in the context of EU legislative procedure. Pursuant to Article 5 of the said Protocol, 
draft legislative acts shall be justified with regard to the principles of subsidiarity and 
proportionality. 
The principles of subsidiarity and proportionality have been also frequently referred to in case 
law of the European Court of Justice. According to ECJ case law, every act of EU law should

IV. Problems with the conception of gender and gender-based violence 
 
“Gender” is a relatively new term, the origins of which lie in some psychological and 
sociological theories. It has its special place in the second and third wave of feminist concepts. 
It should be emphasised that this term carries a strong ideological cha rge. Despite numerous 
doubts and objections against the idea of sex understood as gender in literature, this concept 
was included in the official current of discussions in international institutions. It is particularly 
strongly present in documents of non-binding nature, and this is despite the lack of widespread 
support of the countries for this concept. Lack of clear definition and application limitations of 
this category, as well as progressive substitution of the classic concept of "sex” with “gender”, 
raise significant doubts.  
 
In the literature it is indicated that „gender” is a socially constructed definition of women and 
men. It is the social design of a biological sex, determined by the conception of tasks, functions 
and roles attributed to women a nd men in society and in public and private life.  It is also 
commonly emphasised that the idea of “gender” includes unequal power relations. For the 
proponents of this concept, the existing gender roles and differences between women and men 
result from oppression, the victims of which women have become throughout history. As we 
can read in a report of a group of experts of the Council of Europe on gender mainstreaming, „ 
Gender is not only a socially constructed definition of women and men, it is a socially  
constructed definition of the relationship between the sexes. This construction contains an

2. States’ opposition to the Istanbul Convention and the gender-based perspective in 
combating violence.  
 
Thirteen Member States of the Council of Europe refuse to conclude the Istanbul Convention, 
i.e.: Hungary , Armenia, Azerbaijan, Bulgaria, Czechia, Latvia, Liechtenstein, Lithuania, 
Moldova, Russia, Slovakia, Ukraine and the United Kingdom. Some of  them openly say that 
the reason to reject the Convention is its ideological character.  
 
The EU Member States that have voiced the most outright rejection of the Istanbul Convention 
are Hungary, Slovakia and Bulgaria. Slovak 18 MPs argued that by changing the traditional 
definition of marriage, the Istanbul Convention undermines the constitutional meaning of this 
term as a relationship between a woman and a man only. A similar resolution was passes by the

femininity and masculinity, it violates the principle of the rule of law. Moreover, in Poland, 
citizens started a legislative initiative aimed at withdrawing from the Istanbul Convention and 
adopting an ideology-free, science-based alternative called the Convention on the Rights of the 
Family. It is noteworthy that despite the difficult pandemic -related situation (signatures were 
collected during the peak of the second wave of COVID-19), the initiative was signed by over 
150,000 individuals. The increasing opposition to the Istanbul Convention and its ideological 
perspective of combating violence in Europe is also reflected in the growi ng number of

III. Ineffectiveness of the gender-based violence paradigm in combating violence against 
women and domestic violence.  
 
As reiterated in the roadmap, which is the basis for further action of the Commission, the EU 
legislative initiative on combating domestic violence and violence against women is to be based 
on the gender perspective. The starting point for the initiative i s supposed to be a 2014 study 
conducted by the Fundamental Rights Agency, which showed that 33% of EU women declared 
they experienced physical and/or sexual abuse, 22% reported intimate violence, and 55% 
reported sexual harassment26.  
 
The authors of the roadmap, though, did not draw the right conclusions from the studies 
referred to in the roadmap and presented them incorrectly as the basis for introducing a 
Union-level binding legal document on combating violence rooted in the perspective of the 
gender ideology.  
 
Both the Fundamental Rights Agency research referred to in the roadmap and more recent 
studies conducted by OECD distinctly show that a gender -based policy of fighting violence is 
ineffective. The highest rates of violence against women are recorded in countries that adopted 
the gender-based violence perspective long ago (Denmark – 52%, Finland – 47%, Sweden –

EL*C feedback to the European Commission’s public consultation on gender based and domestic violence     ABOUT EL*C   EL*C – EuroCentralAsian Lesbian* Community (EL*C) is a feminist intersectional network of lesbian organisations and lesbian, bisexual and trans women, and non-binary and intersex persons from over 50 countries of Europe and Central Asia. EL*C was founded in 2017 with the aim to improve the lives of lesbian women and (re)build the lesbian movement. As such, EL*C strives to be a representative, powerful and visible voice of the lesbian movements in Europe and Central Asia, and has implemented numerous activities in the areas of movement building, networking, advocacy, public campaigning and research.    The EL*C uses the word lesbian as a broad inclusive term, including lesbians who identify as trans, non-binary, as well as intersex lesbians, and bisexual or queer women. For the purpose of the following feedback, unless otherwise specified, the term “lesbian” encompasses all LBTIQ women experiencing violence because they are non-heterosexual women or are perceived as non-heterosexual women.   SUMMARY  In the past years, throughout Europe, EL*C has noticed an increase in violence against lesbians, that takes all forms, from cyberharassment to “corrective rapes” and femicides. The situation is further complicated by the rise of the nationalist, far-right and anti-gender movements as well as by the Covid-19 pandemic, enhancing the risk of attacks and backlash against women and LGBTI people as well as the exposure to domestic violence. This exposure to violence makes it crucial that lesbian needs and interests are adequately and explicitly addressed risking other ways to "fall through the cracks" of different policies and measures.  In order to completely tackle this phenomena, and in line with the intersectional approach chosen by the European Commission in its Strategies on Gender Equality and LGBTIQ Equality, it is particularly important to take into consideration the specific positions of non-heterosexual women, be they cisgender, trans or intersex. Data and cases, mentioned further in this document, show that lesbians are victims of gender based and domestic violence because they are exposed to lesbophobia, a powerful conglomerate of sexism, misogyny and homophobia.

COMMITMENT TO AN INTERSECTIONAL APPROACH   Lesbians are extremely marginalized socially, economically and politically, due to high levels of lesbophobia, misogyny and sexism, and to the fact that their invisibility exposes them to heightened risks of discrimination and violence within and outside their families. Violence, discrimination and social stigma experienced by lesbians are linked to the interrelationship between their gender and their sexual orientation in overlapping and mutually reinforcing ways. Lesbians, being women or being perceived as women, experience violence, discrimination and harassment because of their gender, however the overlap and intersection with their sexual orientation is a specific phenomenon that needs to be fully understood and analysed. Furthermore, the levels of stigma multiply and intensify depending on other perceived or claimed social identities that a lesbian might embody, such as ‘gender identity’, ‘race,’ ‘class,’ or ‘ableness.’  The risk, when building and implementing public policies aimed to tackle gender based violence and domestic violence, is failing to understand these interrelationships, presuming that all women will experience in the same way these phenomena, and result in being blind to the impact of the stigma associated with being a non-heterosexual woman in a lesbophobic society. This failure is often the case and this trend leads to a situation in which the needs of lesbians are increasingly underrepresented, marginalized and overlooked also when dealing with lesbophobic gender based violence towards lesbians in institutional efforts to eliminate all forms of violence towards women, both at the EU level and at the level of member states.   In this respect, EL*C strongly welcomes and encourages the EC’s commitment to an intersectional approach, as announced notably in recently adopted EC Strategies on Gender Equality and on LGBTIQ Equality, which are to take into consideration “the combination of gender with other personal characteristics or identities, and how these intersections contribute to unique experiences of discrimination – as a cross-cutting principle.” This approach will ensure that the work of the Commission will take into account the specific needs of lesbians in building future measures, policies and activities. This commitment was further reinforced by Commissioner Dalli stating that “All persons are unique, and have different elements in their personality, which is why intersectionality will be used as a cross-cutting principle throughout the implementation of the strategy. These include sex, racial or ethnic origin, religion or belief, disability, age, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression and sex characteristics.” As such, a truly intersectional approach requires the inclusion of the needs and interests of the lesbian community, a population which is affected both by the persisting gender-based discrimination and violence in our societies, as well as by social stigma and hateful violence on the ground of sexual orientation. 2020, “to extend the list of EU crimes to all forms of hate crime and hate speech – whether because of race, religion, gender or sexuality.” In addition to sexist and misogynist violence, lesbians are indeed facing specific forms of hate crime, such as so-called “corrective rape” and “honour crimes”, which require to be recognised as lesbophobic hate crime, investigated, prosecuted and tried as such, as it is not merely forms of gender-based violence, nor of homophobia. An intersectional perspective should in fact entail the inclusion of those especially heinous crimes affecting lesbians in the upcoming Recommendation on harmful practices against women and girls.  An intersectional approach to combating gender based discrimination and domestic violence is also aligned with the standards of the Istanbul Convention. SPECIFICITIES OF GENDER-BASED VIOLENCE AND DOMESTIC VIOLENCE AGAINST LESBIANS  ● VIOLENCE AGAINST LESBIANS   Lesbians still face high levels of hate crime, gender-based and domestic violence, sexual violence and harassment, and hate speech. Recently, many examples of lesbophobic violence in EU member states and accession countries have been reported and have attracted media attention, including verbal and physical harassment and violence in the streets and in public places as well as during and after public demonstrations such as Pride Marches and Dyke Marches. This worrying trend has seen also some particularly obnoxious hate crimes in 2019 such as the recent femicide of an Italian lesbian woman by a man that she had refused, and the rape of a young lesbian woman in France with the explicit motive of “correcting” and punishing her for her sexual orientation. These are all forms of violence that are experienced by lesbians and are rooted in the deep social stigma linked with being a woman with a non-conforming sexual orientation, as well as with the sexualisation of lesbians and the dehumanising stereotypes linked to the lesbian identity.

According to several studies, including the Violence Against Women Survey conducted by FRA in 2014, lesbian and bisexual women report higher rates of violence than heterosexual women. This data is confirmed by the 2020 FRA LGBTI Survey, in which lesbian and bisexual women that were victims of harassment indicated that they were harassed because of their gender, in addition to or as part of the harassment linked to their sexual orientation. The data included in those surveys is especially important because there is very limited research on lesbian experiences and not enough reliable data on lesbians is collected. There is therefore a need to improve the designing of surveys on gender-based and domestic violence as well as a general need to make sure that data collected is properly disaggregated and visibilizes the experience of non-heterosexual women, as different from the experience of heterosexual women. This trend is particularly dangerous in case of data concerning violence, where the limited understanding of phenomena such as lesbophobic violence or “corrective” rape leads to gaps and shortcomings in the building of public policies responding to these forms of violence, and very low rates of reporting for those crimes. In the 2020 FRA LGBTI Survey, compared with all the other LGBTI groups, lesbian and bisexual women have the lowest rates of reporting of episodes of harassment and even of sexual and physical violence to any institutions. Numbers are particularly low when considering the reporting rate to the police.  Regarding the issue of asylum, a high number of countries in the  world still criminalise same-sex conduct. As a result, many lesbians flee their countries of origins to seek international protection in the European Union. This, also in light of the measures and policies in place (see e.g. the Common European Asylum System). However, once in the Member States, lesbians often face obstacles to their safety and good living conditions. These obstacles can emerge during the asylum procedure - stereotypes and prejudices compromising credibility assessments and the decisions on the asylum claim, and applicants may be returned to their country of origin on the misconception that they can conceal their sexual orientation. Reception facilities and integration also require due consideration to the particular needs of individuals that are subject to three stigmas, due to their gender, sexual orientation and as asylum seekers-refugees.   ● DOMESTIC VIOLENCE   Throughout EU member states, the family unit is still identified as a powerful site of violence against lesbian women. Because of social expectations of women’s traditional roles and the society's failure to provide equality for women permitting full economic, social and legal independence, lesbians may be more likely to depend on their biological families and/or to find themselves obliged and socially conditioned to marry a man. The consequence of these circumstances implies that lesbians are more likely to become subjects of domestic violence.  Across the EU, the so called ‘honour crimes’ are taking place and they typically take the form of violence against women, including murder, motivated and justified because the woman has

‘dishonored’ the male members of her family. Standards of ‘honour’ almost always include norms of sexual purity: women who have sex, or are believed to have sex, with men before or outside of marriage violate them. There are other ways, however, in which women can endanger the status and reputation of parents, brothers or husbands. Dressing or walking the wrong way, having non-heterosexual love relationships, can infringe gendered expectations for how women should behave. Although EU countries stand proud with their protection of women rights by constitutional law, such crimes still happen. In Italy, in September 2020, a young woman was killed in a road accident caused by her brother. Just after the accident, the partner of the victim (present during the accident and who is a trans man) was assaulted by the brother. The latter declared that he did not want to kill his sister but needed to teach her a lesson because she had been infected by her trans partner.  Beside the most violent cases described above, transgressing the boundaries of gender and/or sexual orientation and bringing “shame” onto families and communities, often implies destructive and painful relationships with biological family members (parents, siblings, the grandparents, aunts and uncles) resulting in different forms of violence and harrasment towards lesbians, especially towards younger ones, and also towards children within lesbian ranbow families.   Additionally, lesbian women may be less likely to report intimate partner violence because of societal stigma and discrimination portraying same sex relationships to be “dysfunctional” and “immoral” or because they fear the consequences of coming out in an environment perceived as non-lesbian friendly.  Domestic violence towards lesbians was particularly increased during the last 10 months in every single EU country, as the Covid-19 pandemic has forced countless lesbians to go back to their biological families and endure prolonged exposure to unaccepting family members, which exacerbated rates of domestic violence and physical and emotional abuse, as well as damage to mental health.  ● ONLINE LESBOPHOBIC HARRASSEMENT AND VIOLENCE  In the fast-growing digital age, online life is so enmeshed in day-to-day life that increasingly the online and offline world are not separate, while digital technologies provide additional avenues for hate speech and harassment. Patriarchal and misogynistic behaviour has been transferred online, appearing as lesbophobic and sexist comments, posts or tweets, combined also to the fact that the word “lesbian”, in several EU languages, is often disadvantaged in search engines and social media because allegedly linked to pornographic content, impacting severely the visibility of organisations, activities and events that mention specifically the word “lesbian”. National data from Austria shows that lesbian and bisexual women are more often exposed to online hate speech than heterosexual women (28% of LB women against 10% of straight women).

This online hate speech against lesbians has serious consequences in real life, and sometimes online hate speech is part of a wider pattern of harassment and abuse that is happening in other areas of the lives of lesbians (ie: a neighbour that is targeting a lesbian in her home and online). Many lesbians who are active and visible online (especially those lesbians who are public figures, such as activists, journalists, politicians, etc.) report shocking stories of death or rape threats received via Twitter, Facebook, Instagram or email, and describe intricate strategies they have developed for shielding themselves from cyber violence, which is a particularly damaging and dangerous form of violence, that has negative impacts on the mental and physical health and wellbeing, and on the visibility of lesbians in online spaces.   RECOMMENDATIONS  In light of the above and considering in particular the exposure to intersectional violence and harassment based on gender and sexual orientation, it is key that the lived realities of lesbians are taken into account in the designing of all actions concerning gender based violence and domestic violence. In particular,  lesbian civil society organisations at national and European level should be consulted in the evaluation of the current EU legislation on violence against women and domestic violence, as well as in the development and drafting of future legislative and non-legislative actions.  EL*C welcomes the Commission’s legislative initiative proposal under policy option 3 of the Inception Impact Assessment, which would most effectively provide the necessary legal tools with binding minimum standards to combat the phenomena of gender-based violence and domestic violence across the EU, and ensure the protection of the rights of victims of hate crimes based on gender and sexual orientation.   In this respect, we recommend that the comprehensive sectoral directive adopt an intersectional approach and:  - Recognise that the gender-based violence against lesbians has its own roots, patterns, modes and consequences, uniquely formed by intersections between gender and sexual orientation;   - Include hate biases on grounds of sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression as aggravating factors, in particular when considering offences related to sexual violence, sexual harassment and physical violence aimed at punishing or “correcting” lesbians and women perceived as non-heterosexual;  - Ensure that the hate bias of sexual orientation in cases of violence against lesbians is recorded as aggravating circumstances, whether they are perpetrated in the public or private sphere, in particular in cases of domestic violence concerning young lesbians whose vulnerability is

 
 
European Respiratory Society input to the European Commission consultation on the roadmap for 
the revision of EU Air Quality Rules 
 
The European Respiratory Society (ERS) is an international organisation that brings together 
physicians, healthcare professionals, epidemiologists, and other scientists and experts working in 
respiratory medicine. It is one of the leading medical organisations in the respiratory field, with a 
growing membership representing over 160 countries worldwide. Its mission is to promote lung health 
in order to alleviate suffering from disease and drive standards for respiratory medicine globally. We 
appreciate the opportunity to input to the inception impact assessment on the revision of the ambient 
air quality directives which need updating.  We support the most ambitious proposals available based 
on the latest cutting edge science on the health effects of air pollution.  
The case for the revision of the EU legislation to tackle air pollution is unequivocal.  According to the 
European Environment Agency, air pollution is the biggest en vironmental risk to health. It not only 
causes serious disease such as lung cancer and asthma , but it exacerbates existing respiratory 
conditions in patients. We witness the impact of this daily in our patients and support revision based 
on what we discover in our scientific research.  
Air pollution knows no borders. Air pollutants released in one Members State are often transported 
in the atmosphere, contributing to or resulting in poor air quality elsewhere. Despite progress in 
reducing emissions due to E U legislation, in most countries the quality of life in certain hotspots 
remains impaired, as air quality standards are still not met. The situation is especially severe in urban 
areas, where a majority of Europeans live. Too many citizens are still exposed to concentrations of air 
pollutants above the limit or target values set in the Ambient Air Quality Directives, and even more of 
them are exposed to levels above those recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) Air 
Quality Guidelines. Yet more are exposed to low level concentrations not picked up by the target 
values and levels.  
Policy area 1: a closer alignment of the EU air quality standards with scientific knowledge including 
the latest recommendations of the World Health Organisation (WHO) 
We fully support the suggestion that the impact assessment “will consider including into the Directives 
an explicit mechanism for adjusting air quality standards to technical and scientific progress, including 
for air pollutants that are presently not covered.” 
Scientific progress is constant and dynamic. Environmental epidemiologists and others are continually 
updating the evidence on the health effects of air pollution. An explicit mechanism in the legislation 
to take this into account would radically improve the effectiveness of the legislation. However, it must 
be ensured that the baseline maximum levels are fully aligned with WHO’s guidelines from the start. 
For instance, recent studies have reported strong health effects at air pollution levels well below 
current standards, with no observable thresholds. New studies also strengthen the evidence on the

Policy area 2: improving the air quality legislative framework, including provisions on penalties and 
public information, in order to enhance effectiveness, efficiency and coherence. 
Penalties a re an essential part of enforcement and we are pleased to see the impact assessment 
consider the options here. We would also propose that the assessment consider also incentives as 
well as penalties. While it is important to have the stick, sometimes the carrot is also needed.  
On the public information side, all options to make this as accessible and as straightforward as possible 
are to be encouraged.  
Policy area 3: strengthening of air quality monitoring, modelling and plans. 
What gets measured, gets done, and considering solutions in this area are key to making the legislation 
future proof . Expanding the monitoring requirements to include more harmful air pollutants and 
relevant health information currently not covered (e.g. for black carbon or ultraf ine particles) is 
essential. Could it be considered that monitoring requirements could also be updated in line with the 
latest scientific evidence similar to the ‘explicit mechanism’ mentioned in option 1?  
We would especially recommend measurement (and po tential future regulation) of ambient 
particulate sulfur and elemental carbon, which are both easily measurable on a continuous or semi -
continuous basis, which, at minimum, serve as useful indices of combustion PM. Chemical 
characterisation, oxidative properties and complementary measures may enhance the understanding 
of sources, which is the really important issue for regulations. Biofuels, such as biodiesel is important 
in this respect, as we see in -vitro and in human experimental studies very similar lun g and 
cardiovascular effects as diesel, despite quite significant chemical differences. 
The assessment must take into consideration that the estimated health benefits outweigh by far the 
implementation costs of air quality actions.  As stated in the second Clean Air Ou tlook, if Member 
States fully implement clean air measures, premature deaths due to air pollution could be reduced by 
55% in 2030.  Furthermore, there are significant key co -benefits between air quality and climate 
actions.

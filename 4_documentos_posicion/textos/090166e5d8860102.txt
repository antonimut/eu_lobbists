Feedback on the new rules to prevent methane leakage in the energy sector: „Proposal for 
a legislative act to reduce methane emissions in the oil, gas and coal sectors“ 
 
 
General Feedback 
The Austrian gas industry has been making a significant contribution to methane  emission 
reduction for decades. We, the Austrian gas industry, therefore welcomes the initiative of the 
European Commission to reduc e methane emission s. However, it  must be conside red, that 
according to the Austrian Climate Protection Report 2020, a total of 72% of methane 
emissions in Austria in 2018 were caused by agriculture, 18% by waste management and only 
9% by the energy sector. According to the Austrian Greenhouse Gas Invent ory, methane 
emissions from gas extraction, storage and distribut ion were 0.24 Mt CO2eq in 2018 
representing 0.3% of the total Austrian greenhouse gas emissions. This shows the progress 
the Austrian gas industry has already made and the importance of focus ing also on other 
sections such as agriculture and waste.  
 
Compulsory measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) 
A uniform "monitoring and reporting" system is positively recognized by the Austrian gas 
sector. However, it must be ensured  that no blanket measures are taken, e.g. a general 
reduction of methane emissions by a fixed percentage. It is important that the MRV is focusing 
on „big emitters" or "super emitters" where relevant savings can be achieved.  
In order to be able to pursue a uniform measurement system and reporting of methane 
emissions internationally, a uniform understanding or categorization of the infrastructure 
components with corresponding definitions must be established in advance. There are very 
large differences and discrepancies between the respective member states. An international 
system must be clearly comparable. However, e xcessive measurements and bureaucratic 
effort should be avoided. 
Methane mitigation instruments should, above all, be flexible so that technologies can be used 
that allow the highest emission reduction at the lowest cost and in the shortest time.  The 
focus must be on clear definitions, the improvement of collection and reporting.  
The instruments should also be in a reasonable cost-benefit ratio. As already described above, 
the emission share in the gas sector is only marginal. A cost-benefit analysis therefore must 
be applied across all sectors.

Obligation to improve leak detection and repair (LDAR) 
The European gas infrastructure is a highly complex construct with many different 
components. In Austria, strong effort was made to improve this infrastructure both in terms 
of safety and emission reduction. In addition to extensive renewals, such as  replacing old 
pipelines with modern insulated steel or plastic pipes, work has also been and continues to be 
carried out on the further development of working procedures. An Austrian legislative act 
(GWG 2011, BGBl. I No. 107/2011-§ 7 (1) 53) determines the periodical inspections of pipelines 
and systems to detect even the smallest leaks in the parts-per-million range. Further, technical 
rules (continuously developed) by the Austrian Association for Gas and Water provide detailed 
specifications for the detection and repair of leaks in this area for more than two decades. As 
a result, the Austrian gas infrastructure is now one of the most modern and densest in the 
world. This shows that  the respective initial situation (condition of the infrastructure, 
maintenance program and working procedures used) of individual member states must be 
taken into account in each case. 
 
Further Feedback on the proposal 
The Commission will also consider a legislation on eliminating routine venting and flaring in 
the energy sector covering the full supply chain, up to the point of production. Unfortunately, 
a clear definition of “routine” venting and flaring is still missing at this point.  
In Austria, companies in the gas industry have already implemented mandatory as well as 
voluntary measures and projects to stop or reduce routine blowdown or flaring. These include 
pressure reduction, recompression, smart plugs, or the use of modern bladde r setters in the 
course of maintenance and repair work and replacement of gas -operated valves. The use of 
mobile gas flares has also established itself as a very successful method of reducing methane 
emissions and is difficult to replace with other techniques. Especially in the low-pressure range 
and correspondingly low quantities, there are often no technical alternatives. In addition, the 
aspect of supply security and the energetic expenditure in comparison to the emission 
reduction achieved must also be taken into account in these considerations.  
Furthermore, exceptions should be created for safety-related trainings. Regular practice on a 
training gas flare for fire brigades enables the correct reaction in the event of an incident. In 
any case, such prac tical safety -related trainings dealing with natural gas must remain and 
cannot be replaced by theoretical trainings. The safety aspect of these trainings is more 
valuable than the limited emission of CO2 in these trainings.

Therefore, a blanket ban on rout ine venting or flaring across all infrastructure components 
and activities is seen critically. Instead, provided that all reasonable costs are recovered by the 
system operators, positive incentives might be introduced in the regulatory framework by the 
competent authorities according to the specific MS need. 
 
The International Energy Agency estimates that roughly 40% of methane emissions in the oil 
and gas sector could be saved at no-net cost. It must be considered, that some countries have 
performed many activities to reduce methane emissions . Therefore, reduction costs are 
higher in these countries but less effective.

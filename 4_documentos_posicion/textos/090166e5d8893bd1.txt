Honeywell invents and commercializes technologies that address some of the world’s most 
critical challenges including climate change. As a software -industrial company, we are 
uniquely positioned to blend physical products with software to provide sustainab le 
alternatives. Honeywell is focused on accelerating the development of critical new

Honeywell is a premier manufacturer of gas leak detection systems, including gas cloud 
imaging systems, which detect and measure the volume and concentration of methane 
leaks for the ene rgy sector . Thus, we believe that Honeywell is uniquely positioned to 
provide the European Commission with input and guidance on how to approach legislating 
on methane emission reductions in the energy sector.  
 
Key Asks 
 
▪ Legislation is future -proof and encourages best in practice: the Commission should 
consider including a non-exhaustive list of the types of technology that are approved 
for use. The Commission should also identify technological attributes that a given 
technology requires to be approved for use, such as the ability to identify and quantify 
hydrocarbons such as methane in real -time video. By making the list of existing 
qualified technologies non -exhaustive, this approach maintains flexibility, while also 
providing operators and manufacturers with additional certainty as to whether a given 
technology is approved or likely to be approved for use. 
▪ Legislation requires the deployment of Leak Detection and Repair  (LDAR) 
continuously and across the entire operation: if this is not obliged by law, the likely 
outcome of legislation will be unambitious targets and an ability for operators to hide 
behind false alarm rates. The technology exists (as explained further below) to cover 
the entire energy operation – upstream, midstream and downstream and now is the 
opportunity to set the right standards, to make a real impact. 
▪ Legislation should incentivize early adopters of the best-in-class technology: tackling 
environmental matters has to date co me second in the energy sector to safety 
standards, and so investing in the infrastructure needed to tackle methane emissions 
now, will incur costs by industry . The right incentives however will go a long way in 
allowing European energy suppliers to adapt and invest quickly in the best technology. 
▪ Legislation should oblige  LDAR technology to have the capability to  continuously 
measure and quantify emissions : only by requiring the use of technology which can 
discover, isolate, quantify and report leaks of natural gas, will we know the full extent 
to which past methane emissions have been escaping from the energy sector and the 
impact this will have on the future.

Introduction 
 
As countries around the world strive to address global warming, the European Green Deal is 
calling for zero net emissions of greenhouse gases by 2050. Honeywell fully supports this aim. 
When it comes to tackling methane emissions, Honeywell is also fully s upportive of the 
European Commission ’s methane strategy , as according to the  Yale School for the 
Environment, methane traps 86 times as much heat as carbon dioxide over a 20 -year period 
and accounts for a quarter of total atmospheric warming to date1. The reduction of methane 
emissions is clearly an effective way to slow down global warming. 
The need for legislation to be future-proof and encourages best in practice 
Methane is a potent greenhouse gas and emission . New technology already exists to 
significantly help the energy sector to reduce their methane emissions, and legislation must 
be adopted to both oblige this continuously across the entire operations – and incentivize its 
use, especially for early adopters. 
Legislation is considered as being future -proof if it is proactive, provides legal clarity and 
certainty, and if citizens see it as legitimate. The EU has ambitious targets to meet under the 
Green Deal, and many across the EU (and beyond) are fully supportive of the critical aims it is 
trying to achieve. In the areas of methane emissions in the energy sector, Honeywell believes 
that as there is new and emerging technology already on the market, this must be reflected 
in the Commission’s upcoming proposal. By being future-proof and ensuring best in practice 
technology is obliged, industry across the entire energy chain can play a vital role in reducing 
the EU’s methane emissions. 
The need for l egislation to require the deployment of LDAR continuously and across the 
entire operation 
A critical challenge — and a key consideration in the EU Methane Strategy — is how to detect 
and repair  methane emissions across the energy sector . Today, o ne commonly accepted 
approach is to deploy workers to periodically inspect a site, using handheld thermal cameras 
to identify the sources of leaks and arrange for a repair. However, this protocol has multiple 
drawbacks, including:  
▪ It is time- and people-intensive 
▪ It often discovers inconsequential pinhole leaks versus major releases and is prone 
to human error; and 
▪ The leak detection happens only some of the time versus all of the time – there is no 
continuous monitoring of methane emissions resulting in more time will elapse 
between detection and remediation 
LDAR technology needs to be in use continuously in order to give a clear, concise and accurate 
picture of where main leaks are occurring. Honeywell Rebellion’s Gas Cloud Imaging (GCI) 
system is one such new technology. It improves on earlier generations of imaging technology

through the use of proprietary hyperspectral imaging and machine learning analytics which 
can identify the source of gas leaks and measure the volume and concentration of leaks on a 
continuous, 24/7 basis.  
Whereas earlier modes of action for imaging systems require the use of handheld cameras by 
human operators, the Honeywell Rebellion GCI can be deployed using fixed sites without 
active operators, providing for continuous, real-time, cost-effective monitoring while limiting 
the risk of human error. The continuous , real-time nature of the Honeywell Rebellion GCI 
deployment can identify leaks that would otherwise go undetected for extended periods of 
time (e.g., until they were detected by periodic monitoring) and notify operators remotely as 
soon as a leak occurs, helping the user to act quickly and support shorter durations for gas 
leaks. We estimate that GCI systems have the potential to reduce fugitive emissions by 30-45 
times relative to handheld optical gas imaging. 
In addition, using technology such as GCI allows for: 
1) Monitoring the “fat tail” 
When it comes to the improved detection and measurement of methane emissions, the best 
approaches are based on meaningful outcomes  for regulators, communities and the 
environment — not on prescriptive, tick-the-box processes that identify small leaks that may 
not pose a material threat. In some cases, the repair of a pinhole leak will release more 
methane into the air than the leak itself.  
Technologies such as GCI, on the other hand, provide continuous quantification monitoring, 
enabling a focus on the significant methane leaks — the “fat tail” — that are creating the 
biggest problem. Indeed, almost all energy  management operations generate methane, but 
studies have shown that 10 % of the methane sources in an area are often responsible for 
more than 50 % of the total emissions 2. With real -time GCI analytics, companies can more 
quickly quantify the gravity of a methane leak and prioritize the repair.  
2) Reducing labor cost through always-on monitoring 
Since GCI cameras operate around the clock, supported by automated analytics, companies 
can manage detection, repairs and reporting based on real -time data — not on an arbitrary 
schedule of manual site inspections that may not identify significant methane releases.  
This reduction in required manpower can significantly reduce labor costs, which is one of the 
most expensive parts of current methane detection protocols. And that  is especially critical 
as the EU Methane Strategy calls for cost -effective reduction of greenhouse gases – and has 
been identified in the Inception Impact Assessment as a potential area of concern for the 
energy sector. Companies are more likely to comply with a strategy if they can do it while 
minimizing expense building in best-in-class standards is the only way to achieve this.

3) Identifying the precise source and making repairs 
Historically, determining the relative methane emissions from different sources has been very 
difficult, as leaks can be temporary or prolonged, with emission rates changing over time, and 
stemming from various pieces of processing equipment that may be in close prose proximity. 
As a result, some methodologies lack precise data on the size and loca tion and duration of 
leaks – something that the European Commission wants to tackle – as often, traditional 
methodologies rely on statistical probabilities to measure gas characteristics.  
Conversely, GCI technology detects and measures a methane leak in m inutes and with 
precision including: 
▪ Location 
▪ Size 
▪ Quantification of the plume (total mass)  
Building into legislation that obliges the use of technologies that can provide continuous 
feedback on the a bove points is vital if the Commission wants the energy sector to be 
successful in reducing their methane emissions footprint. 
Some companies in the energy sector (and beyond) are already using technology such as GCI 
monitoring software to definitively see the source of a leak, such as the tubing connector on 
a certain pipe, so they can diagnose the problem, quantify it and repair it. This is very different 
from canvassing a site on foot to look for leaks based on probabilities.  
To reach ambitious climate targets, the European Union and the global  energy community 
need an effective and efficient approach to methane LDAR. Legislation must ensure that LDAR 
is deployed continuously, across the entire operation (upstream, midstream, downstream). 
 
The need to incentivize early adopters of the best-in-class technology 
 
There is no doubt that investment will be needed to ensure that energy suppliers are able to 
purchase new LDAR that is best in practice and class. Despite evidence that energy suppliers 
will ultimately make a net gain from investing in technologies such as Honeywell’s, there will 
still be an upfront cost which the Commission should consider. 
 
Time is ticking for the environment.  Reducing the EU’s methane emissions sooner rather than 
later is essential. The uptake of the right technology needs to be incentivized – especially as 
there is scientific consensus that continuous monitoring is beneficial. 
 
The need to oblige LDAR technology to have the capability to measure and quantify 
emissions 
 
As a minimum, the rollout of legislation in relation to methane emissions in the energy sector 
needs to mandate that LDAR technology has the capability of measuring and quantifying 
emissions. Without doing so, there will be no way of knowing whether the control technology 
 
works and whether the legislation works /is working over a period of time – especially in the 
run up to 2050 and the Green Deal’s aim of making the continent carbon neutral. 
 
Conclusion 
Honeywell appreciates the Commission’s work i n identifying strategies to reduce methane 
emissions from the energy sector. Honeywell makes the above recommendations to feed into 
the Commission’s work in this area, which needs to ultimately provide clarity to both energy 
operators who will be most directl y affected, and the manufacturers of leak detection 
technologies who provide technologies that support efficient and safe production. We 
believe, based on our prior assessments of continuous monitoring systems, that increasing 
incentives for the deployment  of best-in-class LDAR systems will yield real and measurable 
benefits in the reduction of methane emissions.  
Honeywell is committed to working with the Commission in a constructive way to further 
these goals and remains ready to answer any questions arising from this paper.

rules to prevent methane leakage in the energy sector 
25 January 2021 
Executive Summary 
● KAYRROS, a leading geospatial platform providing asset based economic and 
environmental data analytics and key contributor to the Methane Tracker of the 
International Energy Agency (IEA), supports the implementation of option 3  of the 
Commission’s Ince ption Impact Assessment : Translating the OGMP framework into 
E.U. legislation applicable to the full supply chain. 
● Data provided to the IEA Methane Tracker shows that the decrease of approximately 10% 
of global methane emissions recorded in 2020 from the oil and gas sector remains fragile. 
Some regions like Russia increased their visible methane emissions. 
● KAYRROS detections show that most large methane emission events occur during the 
pre-production, extraction, processing, liquefaction, and transportation processes, before 
the fuels reach their end-users in the European Union (E.U.).  
● Different operational practices among operators result in wide disparities in their ‘visible 
methane’ intensity (ratio of observed methane emissions to producti on volumes). For 
example, only one leak of visible methane w as detected in Saudi Arabia, the world’s 
largest oil exporter, in 2019  and 2020 . In contrast, t he ‘visible methane’ intensity  of 
Turkmenistan was 1,000 times greater. Midstream players also run the gamut from zero 
release, as in the case of pipelines  within the E.U., to multiple emission events detected 
from pipelines in both the U.S. and Russia.  Current regulations do not adequately 
incentivize high-intensity suppliers to reduce their emissions. 
● Thanks to the Sentinel-5P and Sentinel-2 satellites operated by the Copernicus program, 
KAYRROS helps operators identify super-emitters above 1t/h in near real-time. Stripping 
large methane emissions from the oil and gas supply chain would remov e 1 Gt CO2e, 
which is equivalent to cutting E.U. carbon emissions by 25% on a 20-year global warming 
power basis.  
● Monitoring satellites covering the full earth with daily or near-daily revisit frequency  
such as Sentinel -5P and Sentinel -2, combined with  Artificial Intelligence , are the 
cornerstone of any comprehensive monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV) system. 
Other technologies such as aerial campaigns and mobile sensors bring added value for an 
OGMP 2.0 Onshore Upstream methane monitoring system. 
● Based on the OGMP 2.0 framework, the E.U . should establish a mandatory monitoring 
and reporting framework for all operators in the energy supply chain. To be fully efficient, 
operators should implement real -time measurement systems with a real -time alert 
function, a key factor to eliminate super-emitters. 
● Current self -reporting mechanisms should be replaced with formalized, independent 
certification mechanisms  that can transparently guarantee whether the production of 
hydrocarbon energy has been deemed free of major leaks of visible methane or routine 
flaring. Third-party audit can contribute with objective, unbiased, and auditable 
measurements to the future International Methane Emissions Observatory and the 
Methane Supply Index. 
● Other environmental policies can also help achieve the E.U. Methane Strategy, by setting 
targets to limit CO2 emissions-equivalent instead of CO2 emissions.

KAYRROS is a leading geospatial platform providing asset based economic and environmental 
data ana lytics for the energy market. KAYRROS is headquartered in Paris , with offices in 
Houston, New York, London and Singapore and operates worldwide with clients in more than 
30 countries. Its clients include most of the largest energy companies as well as fina nce 
institutions and asset managers.  Its highly technical team of 150 people partners with world-
class research institutions such as the Laboratoire des Sciences du Climat et de l'Environnement 
(LSCE), Centre Borelli, The French National Centre for Scientific Research, and the University 
of California, Berkeley, among others.  
KAYRROS is a major contributor to the International Energy Agency (IEA) Methane Tracker 
and provides maps and near -real time analytics  to several IEA publications , including the 
flagship World Energy Outlook. KAYRROS uses extensively the Copernicus data from the full 
spectrum of the Sentinel constellation of satellites operated by the European Space Agency and 
the European Commission  to detect, quantify and attribute large anthropogenic methane 
emissions across the world. The artificial intelligence (AI) technology using Sentinel satellites 
was developed through the E.U. program for research and development Horizon 2020. 
KAYRROS welcomes the European Commission’s goal to take concrete actio ns to reduce 
methane emissions, one of the most pressing issues on the pathway to achieve the Paris 
Agreement goals and the European Green Deal. Methane is a greenhouse gas (GHG) which is 
84-87 times more potent than carbon dioxide in the first twenty year s after release and is 
responsible for at least 25% of global warming. 
According to the IEA, “the concentration of methane in the atmosphere is currently around two-
and-half times greater than pre -industrial levels and is increasing steadily. This rise has  
important implications for climate change, particularly in the near term. Methane has a much 
shorter atmospheric lifetime than CO2 (around 12 years compared with centuries for CO2), but 
it is a much more potent greenhouse gas, absorbing much more energy while it exists in the 
atmosphere.” 
Even though some regions recorded a decrease in GHG emissions in 2020, the move was not 
just one-way and remains too fragile to be considered encouraging. Lower economic activity 
due to the COVID -19 pandemic led t he d ownturn, making it subject to rev ersal once the 
economy recovers. In the United States, the oil-market impact of the pandemic, notably a price-
driven decrease in energy production, combined with the expansion of take-away capacity for 
natural gas, substantially reduced emissions. Elsewhere, on the other hand, even COVID -19 
could not stop the growth in GHG emissions from accelerating. Russia, one of the main 
suppliers in energy of the European Union (E.U.), is a c ase in point. Its visible methane 
emissions increased by +32%. 
The risks entailed by doing nothing to limit methane emissions could be catastrophic. It is of 
critical importance for the E.U. to not only curb emissions at home but to exercise world 
leadership and eliminate methane emissions from its entire supply chain. Doing so would also 
help level the playing field between E.U. companies and non-E.U. market participants. 
The European Commission’s Inception Impact Assessment provides several policy options to 
improve the availability and accuracy of methane emissions information in the energy sector. 
Of these options, KAYRROS supports the implementation of option 3: Translating the 
OGMP framework into EU legislation applicable to the full supply chain. Please find below 
an overview of the status of global methane leaks in the oil and gas sector observed by 
KAYRROS (I) and a set of proposed concrete actions to mitigate methane leakage, venting, 
and flaring that the E.U. could implement in the short to medium term (II).

I. Status of global methane leaks in the oil and gas sector 
Established by the European Space Agency and the 
European Commission, the Copernicus program   
for multi -spectral imaging instruments for land, 
ocean, and atmospheric monitoring  has provided 
unprecedented transparency on global methane 
emissions for severa l years . Processed with 
advanced algorithms such as those developed by 
KAYRROS, Copernicus imagery combined with  
various other verifiable data sets is now enabling 
accurate measurement and reporting of methane 
emissions at the  country, operator, and facility 
level.  
Self-assessments often appear inconsistent with the 
estimate of global methane emissions  provided by 
world experts and multilateral organizations such 
as the IEA , however. Reporting operators often 
base their calculations on standard emission factors 
and lack the tools to monitor leaks (see Figure 1). 
But the  data generated by Copernicus and processed by KAYRROS allow to  accurately 
measure the methane footprint of the full oil and gas supply chain. The following conclusions 
can be taken out of the existing data: 
1) The concentration of most large methane leaks outside the E.U. calls for the global 
monitoring of the full supply chain 
Most large methane leaks attributed to the oil and gas sector are geographically concentrated 
outside of the European Union. The main methane emission events caused by hydrocarbon fuels 
occur during the pre -production, extraction, processing, liquefaction, and transmission 
processes, way before the fuels reach their end-users. There are very few detections within the 
E.U. (Figure 2). Rather, most of the leaks happen before fossil fuels enter the E.U. (Figure 3). 
Addressing emissions only in the Union will thus have only a marginal effect on climate.

Far from being a necessary cost of energy production, these emissions can easily be avoided . 
Measurements done by KAYRROS show a great deal of variance in methane intensity across 
operators, a clear sign that emissions are not proportional to the volume of oil and gas produced 
or transported. Rather, the differences between operators reflect differences in their operational 
practices and in the regulatory environment of the regions where they operate (Figures 4 & 5). 
The differences in methane leaks events are therefore quite wide between operators, but most 
of them are supplying energy to the E.U. market, without any disincentive placed on large 
emitters to change their practices. 
2) Most leak-generating operational practices can be addressed at no or low additional 
cost 
Leaks can be caused by equipment failure and/or physical damage, with many of the methane 
hotspots identified by KAYRROS to date being associated with oil extraction and the release 
of natural gas during pipeline maintenance. The operat ors chose not to flare or capture the 
associated gas, resulting in avoidable releases of methane.

2020, even though this is one of the world’s largest oil and gas producers.

At one end of the curve, Saudi Arabia, the world’s largest oil exporter, caused only two leaks 
of visible methane in 2019  and 2020 . At the other end, the ‘visible methane’ intensity of 
Turkmenistan was 1,000 times greater. 
This variation in methane emissions and intensity affects both the upstream (oil and gas field 
production) and midstream (transportation and storage) operations. The E.U. has rel atively 
limited oil and gas production, but it is home to an extensive oil and gas distribution network. 
However, while the pipeline operations in the E.U. show virtually zero visible methane release, 
other pipelines, notably in the U.S. and Russia, reveal frequent releases of methane. KAYRROS 
reached out to pipeline operators on whose network in detected such  incidents. In 90% of the 
cases, the operators confirmed the timing and volume of the releases. Most of the releases were 
done for safety reasons before maintenance. Methane releases during pipeline maintenance can 
be avoided with the use of portable flaring equipment that can be easily rented. A simple 
regulation banning venting and mandating flaring on pipelines bringing gas to the E .U. would 
be a very efficient climate action, verifiable by an E.U. satellite. 
The IEA has also argued that most methane leakage s can be addressed at no operating costs. 
Leak avoidance often saves valuable fuel and can be revenue generating.  
3) Systematic h otspot detection  can deliver steep, short -term reduction s in GHG 
emissions 
KAYRROS welcomes the “rules to prevent methane leakage in the energy sector” as a 
regulating factor to include “binding rules on monitoring, reporting, verification, leak detection 
and repair in the energy sector,” as these rules will raise operational standards for oil and gas 
operators. 
As technology evolves rapidly, E.U. certified monitoring, reporting and verification ( MRV) 
systems ought to capture the full benefit of the latest technological ad vances. KAYRROS 
believes that current real-time detection technologies based on artificial intelligence (AI) and 
Copernicus data can eliminate methane super-emitters within two to three years.  
 
6/10 
 
The super-emitters detected by KAYRROS in the oil and gas sector using Sentinel 5-P satellite 
imagery, which also includes clusters of smaller sources, release on average an estimated 
26,000 tons of methane globally on any given day. Stripping large methane emissions from the 
oil and gas suppl y chain would remove 1 Gt CO2, equivalent to cutting the Union’s carbon 
emissions by 25%. It is an actionable route for climate action and would be one of the quickest 
and biggest wins since the signature of the Paris Agreement.  
Satellite monitoring is a key instrument to accurately mapping emissions globally. 
The Sentinel-5P satellite operated by Copernicus helps identify super -emitters. Sentinel -5P 
provides near daily readings of methane concentration across major oil and gas basins with a 
resolution of 5.5 km by 7 km. Its minimum detection threshold is 5 tons of methane per hour. 
The Copernicus Sentinel-2 satellite has also good methane detection capacity and a finer spatial 
resolution that Sentinel-5P, of 20 meters by 20 meters. Its minimum detection threshold is also 
considerably lower, at 0.5 tons of methane per hour. 
Other satellites tested by KAYRROS  provide additional detection, quantification, and 
attribution capacity . The Landsat-8 satellite operated by NASA  complements Sentinel -2, 
having similar technical capabilities, 16 days revisit time and global coverage.  
As opposed to these high frequency and global coverage monitoring satellites, there is another 
class of satellites that need to know where to acquire information from, called tasking satellites. 
They can have a lower detection threshold than existing monitoring satellites but can only 
provide information on specific locations on which they have been targeted. They can acquire 
a limited number of sites per day. Due to the intermittency of most methane emissions and the 
huge number of potential sources, these tasking satellites cannot be used for monitoring but are 
a good complement to a methane detection system. 
Overall, monitoring satellites such as those in the Copernicus constellation can detect millions 
of potential methane sources on any given day. Indeed, the largest and most deleterious methane 
leaks are intermittent in nature and thus require continuous observation. 
The combination of monitoring satellites covering the full earth daily (or every 2 or 3 days) and 
Artificial Intelligence is creating the breakthrough technology needed to reduce methane now.

II. Concrete actions to mitigate methane leakage, venting, and flaring 
Concrete and handy actions start with robust MRV systems, measuring the methane intensity 
of the energy reaching the end -users in the European Union. We recommend pursuing the 
following policy action steps in that regard: 
1) Mandatory monitoring and reporting modelled on the OGMP 2.0 methodology 
framework 
As a general principle, existing self -reporting models do not allow for the gathering of 
comprehensive data on global methane emissions, data which are key to adopting appropriate 
and effective policy actions. 
The Oil and Gas Methane Partnership (OGMP) brings together major oil and gas producers 
operating at the global level . Accurate reporting of OGMP members’ emissions can 
significantly improve the scientific and technical understanding of methane emissions. 
Based on the OGMP 2.0 framework, the E.U . should establish a mandatory monitoring and 
reporting framework for all operators in its supply chain. Th is framework should enable the 
tracking and aggregating of data on emissions stemming from at least the production of oil and 
natural gas, gas associated with oil production and handling, gas transmission, distribution, and 
consumption, as well as active and decommissioned coal mines. 
The E.U . should encourage operators to implement real-time measurement methane 
emissions systems with a real -time alert function. Such a system would let asset operators 
address any potentially large emission release as soon as it occurs in their facilities.

Experience gathered by KAYRROS and its partners on MRV can be summarized as follows:  
● Monitoring satellites are the cornerstone of any comprehensive methane monitoring 
system for large leak detection, full inversion and wind-corrected averages. 
● Tasking satellites  provide limited and at best  marginal value added within the 
operational envelope of existing monitoring satellites. Subject to further assessment, the 
tasking satellites could bring some added value in tropical or snowy regions. 
● Aerial campaigns are important to create a precise prior for the full inversion. 
● Drones can be a useful asset for monitoring offshore and large, complex, hard-to-access 
infrastructures. 
● Low-cost sensors  which are being developed should  be further assessed, as f ixed 
sensors are an expensive solution. 
● Mobile sensors ( car- or truck -mounted) are a noteworthy solution to lower the 
detection threshold to ~10kg/hour and precisely attribute emissions. 
KAYRROS teams up with other methane-detection companies that leverage various kinds of 
sensors to provide comprehensive methane monitoring and abatement packages. KAYRROS 
and its partners recommend  such combinations of monitoring satellites, aerial campaigns and 
mobile sensors for an OGMP 2.0 Onshore Upstream methane monitoring system. 
KAYRROS’ Methane Watch tool has been modelled on the OGMP2.0 Key Principles. The 
OGMP 2.0 methodology provides a precise and comprehensive reporting mechanism which: 
• Works on all operated and non-operated assets. 
● Applies to all sources of metha ne emissions including fugitives. KAYRROS Methane 
Watch has a daily revisit and can detect fugitive emissions. 
● Gives priority to increasing coverage and direct measurement. KAYRROS Methane 
Watch meets the same principles as it uses monitoring satellite s with wide coverage 
instead of precise sensors with extremely limited bandwidth. 
● Includes reporting at individual source level, which is today possible by combining 
satellites with mobile ground sensors. 
● Applies materiality rules to focus OGMP members' efforts on the biggest opportunities. 
Asks companies to announce either an upstream methane intensity target or an emission 
reduction target. This is made possible by the capacity of satellite imagery to go back in time 
(2019 for Sentinel 5, 2013 for Landsat 8).      
2) Establishment of an  independent certification system to foster  transparency and 
accountability 
To build momentum and lead by example, the E.U. should set as a priority to replace current 
self-reporting mechanisms for methane emissions with formalized, independent certification 
mechanisms subject to third-party audit. A large part of the underlying data is already publicly 
available thanks to public satellite sources, though the raw data  may require advanced 
technological skills and expertise to process and analyze.  
 
9/10 
 
These a uditable, third -party verifiable  data could serve as the basis for the issuance of 
certificates of low methane-intensity recognizing hydrocarbon energy whose production has 
been deemed free o f major leaks of visible methane (VM) or routine flaring (RF) . Such 
certificates would include the following information:  
o Whether the fossil fuel w as produced in a sustainable manner, free from VM (defined 
as greater than 1 ton per hour) and RF.  
o The period of time during which the fossil fuel has been produced. 
As molecules of oil and gas cannot be differentiated and get mixed in the global complex energy 
system, KAYRROS recommends applying the concept of Guarantee of Origin  used in the 
electricity market to the fossil fuel supply chains . This would entail establishing a scheme for 
tradable certificates of cleanly produced energy. 
Using objective, unbiased, and auditable measurements would be a real asset to exploration and 
production (E&P) companies. The majority of E&P companies need to document their progress 
on environmental, social and governance criteria in a trusted and unassailable way to maintain 
the trust of investors, especially in the current economic context. Companies such as BP, Total 
and Repsol have already set a net zero emissions goal by 2050, committing in the case of BP to 
implement methane measurement at all its oil and gas processing sites by 2023 and reduce 
methane intensity of operations by 50% 1. Transparent and indepen dent performance 
benchmarks can provide accurate data to com panies to adjust their strategies to new risks and 
achieve such efforts. 
Verifiable data from verifiable third parties will be a key support to the future instruments 
proposed by the European Commission’s Methane Strategy - the International Methane  
Emissions Observatory and the Methane Supply Index – which will have to fill the data gaps 
in the supply chain and gather accurate data regarding the methane intensity of the E.U. fossil 
fuel supply.  
3) Creation of economic incentives for the production of low methane-intensive energy 
Implementing a MRV system modelled on the OGMP 2.0 framework is  part of the process to 
cut emissions. As the world’s largest buyer of internationally traded gas 2, the E.U. has a key 
lever to make operators beyond its borders  accountable for their methane emissions and 
encourage them to adopt the best practices in the industry. 
In its E.U. Methane Strategy, the Commission has proposed to use a default value for volumes 
that do not have adequate MRV systems in place . KAYRROS recommends instead relying on 
verifiable, independent  measurements to assess the methane footprint of energy supplies , 
whether within the framework of an MRV or in the absence of it, as long as these volumes can 
be detected and measured with available third-party technology. Using default values could be 
a pertinent solution only for volumes of energy that fall beyond the current range of satellite.  
Additionally, the E.U. is preparing several other policy tools and policies, all of which should 
be aligned with the objective of the E.U. Methane Strategy:

o The carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM): The CBAM could effectively 
reduce methane emissions if it is based on CO2-equivalent emissions rather than just 
CO2. According to the IEA’s Methane Tracker 3, oil and gas operations worldwide 
emitted more than 70 million tons of methane into the atmosphere in 2020, which is 
equivalent to the total energy-related CO2 emissions in the Union. The CBAM will only 
be efficient and fair if it is addressing the full scope of GHG emissions. 
o Revision of the emission trading system directive (ETS) and the Effort Sharing 
Regulation (ESR):  The ETS is covering carbon dioxide emissions, nitrous oxide 
emissions from all nitric, adipic, glyoxal and glyoxylic acid production and 
perfluorocarbons (PFC) emissions from aluminium production in several energy -
intensive sectors. The ESR is covering CO2 emissions from road transport, heating of 
buildings, small-scale industry and so-called non-CO2 emissions from agriculture and 
waste. Th eir scope is too limited today and does not reflect the targets set in the 
upcoming European Climate Law. Both the ETS and the ESR should see their scope 
expanded in order to cover CO2-equivalent of methane emissions, as a clear sign of the 
Union’s commitment to reach its own climate targets. 
o Zero Pollution Action Plan : Carbon dioxide and methane are the primary causes of 
carbon pollution. To improve air quality, the Union needs to step up pollution 
prevention measures and air quality monitoring. The Zero Pollution Action Plan should 
prepare the implementation of GHG performance standards calculated in terms of CO2-
equivalent, especially in the most polluting sectors such road transport and industrial 
facilities. 
o COP26: The upcoming COP26 climate talks should set clear targets for states and 
industry to reduce methane emissions as part of their drive to slow down climate change. 
In its report Driving Down Methane Leaks from the Oil and Gas Industry, the IEA shows 
that the oil and gas sector emitted around 70 Mt of methane (approximately 2.1 Gt CO2-
eq based on 100-year GWP, or 6 Gt CO2-eq based on 20-year GWP) in 2020.4 To stay 
in line with the Paris Agreement, the oil and gas industry will need to reduce its 
emissions by 20 Mt per year by 2030, a drop of more than 70% from 2020 levels.

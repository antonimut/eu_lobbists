RED CROSS EU Comments on the European Commission’s proposal for a Regulation introducing a 
screening of third country nationals at the external borders 
 
The Red Cross EU Office (RCEU) represents the 27 National Red Cross Societies in the European Union, 
the British Red Cross, the  Norwegian Red Cross , the Icelandic Red Cross and the International 
Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies (IFRC). Based on the Red Cross Red Crescent’s 
expertise in working with and fo r people in need both inside and outside the EU, the key concerns 
identified below reflect our principled approach to preventing and alleviating human suffering. Our 
aim is to promote EU policies and programmes in the areas of migration and asylum, that ef fectively 
contribute to supporting the dignity and well -being of the most vulnerable people in society. While 
the EU is at a turning point in defining the future direction of EU asylum and migration policies, we 
believe it is urgent to take decisive measures to ensure that no one is left behind.  
 We welcome the European Commission’s initiative to consult civil society organisations on the 
Proposal for a Screening Regulation and hereby wish to highlight the following associated 
humanitarian concerns: 1) detention could become the norm; 2) vulnerabilities risk falling between 
the cracks; 3) Procedural safeguards are at stake; 4) Swift and effective access to asylum will be 
postponed.  
The proposed pre-entry screening system as per the Proposal for a Screening Regulation is one of the 
major elements introduced by the Pact on Migration and Asylum.  The overall objective of such a 
system is to speed up asylum processing and to accelerate returns of third country nationals by quickly 
determining the type of procedure to apply, i.e., return - border procedures – normal asylum 
procedures – relocation (when applicable). This was the original intention behind the setting up of the 
hotspot system.1 A key additional humanitarian concern of the proposal is thus in our view that such 
a system would likely lead to a replication and further normalization of human rights violations 
including inadequate reception conditions which characterise the current implementation of the 
hotspot approach2. As illustrated by the situation on the Greek islands, this may lead to migrants being 
trapped at the borders in undignified reception conditions, for long periods of time without proper 
assessment of their vulnerabilities or their protection claim3. 
1. Detention becomes the norm.  
Pending the completion of the screening process, which could take up to a maximum of 10 days (5 
days normally), migrants are obliged to remain in ‘’designated facilities’’. Although preamble 20 of 
Screening Regulation encourages Member States’ authorities to set up screening locations at or in 
proximity to the external borders, the reception conditions for those arriving at the borders and those 
apprehended within territory during the screening process remain unclear.

The Regulation d oes not preclude the use of detention during the screening process, leaving it to 
national law to determine in which situations the screening requires detention.  
Based on the above, since the screening proposal explicitly foresees movement restrictions, it would 
as such result in migrants undergoing periods of detention-like conditions almost systematically. Read 
in conjunction with the proposal for an Amended Asylum Procedures Regulation, this would mean 
that during mandatory asylum and return procedures, Member States can apply detention in line with 
the upper limits introduced by the Return Directive, namely 6 months, which can be prolonged up to 
12 months. To this period, detention -like conditions during screening would be further added. As 
alternatives to detention are largely overlooked, this would mean t hat detention upon entry, the 
conditions of which are scarcely regulated, also becomes the norm. This goes against EU law according 
to which detention should only be determined on a case-by-case basis but also conclusive research 
showing that even the shortest periods of detention can have a detrimental impact on the well-being 
and integration prospects of migrants. 4 
We call upon co-legislators to ensure that detention remains a measure of last resort, throughout the 
entire screening, asylum and return procedures, be it at the borders or within the territory of Member 
States. Children and other vulnerable groups should never be detained. Community -based 
alternatives to detention should be considered whenever liberty is deemed not to be appropriate, 
including in cases of returnees. The necessary safeguards set out in the EU law linked to maximum 
detention time limits and detention conditions must be upheld.  
2. Vulnerabilities risk falling between the cracks.   
If adopted, all migrants arriving irregularly at EU borders either through land or sea including following 
disembarkation would be subject to a mandatory screening. Screening would also become mandatory 
for those persons apprehended in a Member State’s territory that presumably evaded border controls 
upon entry into the EU. The screening process is designed in such a way that specific vulnerabilities , 
such as victims of human trafficking, torture survivors or migrants with mental health issues, risk being 
overlooked. According to the proposal, such checks will be considered necessary only if there are 
relevant indications and/or circumstances concerning the general state of the third-country nationals, 
contrary to the Member States’ existing obligation for a thoro ugh assessment of vulnerabilities (see 
Art. 22 Reception Conditions Directive and Art. 24 Asylum Procedures Directive). Limited attention is 
however paid to the competence, training and profile of the staff conducting the screening and the 
medical and vuln erability checks, even though sensitised and adequately trained officials is key to 
properly identifying, assessing, and referring vulnerabilities.  
In addition, Recital 27 of the Proposal for a Screening Regulation provides that special attention should 
be paid inter alia to persons “visibly having suffered psychological or physical trauma”. Such a narrow 
consideration of vulnerability may lead to omissions with regard to the timely identification of 
individuals such as migrants with mental health  issues, victims of torture or trafficking, whose 
vulnerabilities may not be visible at first sight. Moreover, the requirements laid down in the Proposal 
for a Screening Regulation, i.e., that only obvious special needs are noted down, the lack of sufficient

Effectively responding to vulnerabilities requires the design of early identification and referral systems 
operated by skilled staff. Such systems would subsequently help providing the most vulnerable 
migrants, including unaccom panied or separated children, with adequate support and reception 
conditions. Factors such as gender, age, and disability, as well as torture, rape, and other forms of 
psychological and physical violence linked to or aggregated by the migratory journey mus t be 
considered when assessing the protection needs of asylum seekers. This requires limiting the use of 
border procedures to ensure that all migrants benefit from a humane, individualised treatment which 
allows for a comprehensive assessment of their vulnerabilities and protection needs, as soon as they 
reach the EU.  
3. Procedural safeguards are at stake. 
Applicable safeguards during the screening process are unclear. According to the proposed screening 
provisions, the authorities’ obligation to infor m migrants about their rights as asylum seekers and 
respectively register their application does not necessarily apply (see instead type of information 
provided in Article 8 and reference in the proposal that Articles 26 and 27 of the future Asylum 
Procedures Regulation will not apply).  
In addition, the proposal suggests that EU asylum acquis, such as the Reception Conditions Directive, 
should apply only after the screening has ended. The above, however, are not in line with existing EU 
asylum law obligati ons (see respectively (see Art. 2 (c) Asylum Procedures Directive and Art. 2 (b) 
Reception Conditions Directive) that require that migrants be considered applicants for international 
protection from the moment they express their wish to apply for asylum, regardless of whether they 
were apprehended in the territory or at the borders. This means that the set of rights linked to 
reception, health care, freedom of movement and safeguards, such as maximum detention time limits 
and detention conditions, which asylum seekers should enjoy, cannot be derogated from. Otherwise, 
the screening proposal would wrongfully result in a new phase during which the rights and safeguards 
accompanying the asylum seekers status do not apply.  
The outcome of the screening process will be captured in a ‘’debriefing form’’. The form aims to 
support the authorities in channeling migrants either to immediate return procedures in line with the 
new Return recast Directive, in case no asylum claim was made, or to the asylum procedure, and would 
also contain an indication of whether the claim should be examined under mandatory border 
procedures or not, as per the new amended 2016 Asylum Procedures Regulation proposal. While the 
content of this form will be decisive regarding the future of the migrants and hence cannot be

The above becomes highly problematic given the current assumption upon which the proposal is 
based according to which migrants undergoing screening and border procedures, including during the 
return mandatory procedures, would not be considered to have formally entered the territory of the 
concerned Member State (Art. 4 Proposal for a Screening Regulation, Article 41 and 41a Amended 
Proposal for an Asylum Procedures Regulation). This would imply that for a protracted period, 
migrants will be stuck in limbo with curtailed safeguards. This contradicts the case law of the European 
Courts of Human Rights 6 according to which Member States’ competence covers their borders, and 
that fundamental rights guarantees, including the prohibition of non -refoulement, ap ply to all 
migrants arriving to their territories, including at borders.  
We, therefore, call upon co legislators to ensure that access to essential services as well as the 
provision of legal assistance and the necessary information about rights must  be upheld for all 
migrants irrespective of legal status and at all stages of the procedure, including during screening and 
border procedures. The right to an appeal and to an effective remedy must be accessible at all stages 
of the screening process. Poss ibilities for Member States to derogate from EU asylum acquis should 
be carefully circumscribed as they risk further downgrading the rights and guarantees available to 
migrants and asylum seekers. Humanitarian organizations should also be given access to all migrants 
in need, throughout the envisaged border management measures, including in detention and facilities 
at the borders.  
The proposed establishment of national border monitoring mechanisms (Article 7) can be a positive 
step towards addressing any w rongdoings occurred during the screening. However, for the 
mechanism to be effective, it should encompass the entire border management measures beyond the 
screening. to support the independence of the mechanism other international and non-governmental 
organizations should be involved alongside the Fundamental Rights Agency (FRA).  
4. Swift and effective access to asylum is postponed. 
The compulsory screening process for irregular arrivals refers to usual practices by border authorities, 
namely security checks, fingerprinting and health checks which have recently been generalized as a 
result of the COVID -19 pandemic. The novelty of  the new screening procedure resides in that it 
formalises a so-called ‘’triage’’ system by immediately channeling migrants to different procedures: 
return - border procedures – normal asylum procedures – relocation (when applicable). As a result, 
screening will be used to distinguish between those applicants that are granted access to a complete

The Alternative Credit Council (ACC) 2 and the Alternative Investment Management Association 
(AIMA)3 welcome the  opportunity to comment on the  European Commission’s review of the 
European Long-Term Investment Fund (ELTIF) regulatory framework. 
 
The need for deeper and more liquid capital markets in Eu rope is well understood.  Despite the 
progress that has been made strengthening the banking sector during the past decade, European 
businesses often lack access to the capital they need to invest, grow and support job creation .  
These same businesses  also require significant long -term investment as they transition to more 
sustainable models.  This situation has been exacerbated by the COVID -19 pandemic.  Improving

We agree with the conclusions of the CMU High Level Forum report4 that the ELTIF structure holds 
great promise as a pan-European vehicle.  Targeted reform of the ELTIF Regulation can encourage 
the growth of market-based lending across the European Union and provide a European version 
of U.S. Business Development Companies (BDC).  BDCs currently provide over $100bn worth of 
finance to an estimated 12,500 businesses.5  It is realistic to expect that ELTIFs could reach a similar 
scale and become a substantial source of finance for European SMEs.   
 
Despite the substantial growth of capital allocated to European -based lending strategies by asset 
managers and their investors during the last decade, ELTIFs have not been the vehicle of choice to 
invest this capital.  There are currently very few active ELTIFs providing less than €2bn equity and 
debt finance to SMEs across Europe.  By comparison, the broader European direct lending market 
is estimated to have grown from €39bn to €242bn during the last decade.  While the ELTIF vehicle 
has some positive features, such as the ability to originate loans on a cross-border basis, there are 
also deficiencies which make ELTIF unattractive to investors and asset managers seeking to 
establish European-focused lending strategies.   
 
It is essential that the ELTIF Regulation is reformed to better align with the needs of investors if it 
is to reach its potential.  We have provided detailed comments in response to the questions posed 
by the review, but we would encourage policymakers to focus on the following aspects of the 
Regulation: 
 
- Fund structuring requirements; 
- Operating requirements; 
- Conflict of interest and co-investment rules; 
- Borrowing rules; 
- Retail marketing and distribution requirements; 
- Local facilities requirements; and 
- Withholding tax considerations. 
When considering how to enhance the ELTIF framework we would also urge policymakers to 
refrain from using the ELTIF Regulation to target specific assets or operating models.  Such 
decisions are better taken by investors rather than policymakers.  An overly prescriptive 
framework is one of the key reasons why ELTIF in its current form is unsuita ble for investors.  
Continuing this approach risks preventing ELTIF from reach its potential and further hindering the 
development of stronger European capital markets.

1.1 Please explain your position on your responses to question 1, providing key arguments 
to support your answers. 
A reformed and a well -functioning ELTIF regime, aligned with the needs of investors, has the 
potential to help European businesses access the capital needed to fund innovation and support 
their long-term growth.  This will ensure that SMEs have the finance needed to recover from COVID 
and support the transition to a more sustainable economy. It is essential that  the ELTIF’s role and 
effectiveness in supporting the flow of finance to the real economy and, in particular, the  parts of 
the economy that are not well served by banks, is enhanced to ensure it can meet these objectives. 
There has been a low take-up of the ELTIF fund vehicle since its establishment, despite the growing 
demand for private capital, including lending -based investment strategies to non -rated or non -
listed corporates and projects in Europe. Although assets under management and the volume of 
originated deals grew at a rapid pace during the past five years, ELTIF has not been the vehicle of 
choice despite some unique and potentially favourable characteristics. The cross -border 
marketing and investment passport is one of the most attractive features of the ELTIF regime for 
investment managers, yet the benefits it provides are diminished by provisions elsewhere in the 
legislation. We would therefore recommend that policymakers focus on the following areas  to 
enhance the effectiveness of ELTIF: 
- Fund structuring requirements; 
- Operating requirements; 
- Conflict of interest and co-investment rules; 
- Borrowing rules; 
- Retail marketing and distribution requirements; 
- Local facilities requirements; and 
- Withholding tax considerations. 
We have enclosed a copy of our position paper  on ELTIF alongside our response to the 
questionnaire.  This position paper, and our response to later questions in this consultation, 
provide further detail on how each of these elements can be reformed to enhance the ELTIF 
framework.  We believe that these reforms are necessary for ELTIF to reach its potential as a pan-
European lending vehicle.

3.1 Please explain your position on your answer to question 3, providing your arguments, 
and where appropriate, concrete examples and data to support your answers: 
The primary challenge limiting the greater take-up of the ELTIF by asset managers and investors 
lies in the restrictive operating requirements that the ELTIF Regulation prescribes. These include 
the fund maturity, eligible assets, liquidity profile and leverage of the fund. In addition, safeguards 
that accompany the distribution of ELTIFs to retail investors – for example, suitability and 
marketing requirements – have diminished the attractiveness of ELTIF to its target market which 
will be professional or sophisticated investors rather than the broader retail/consumer market.  
 
The other factor limiting the take up of ELTIF is how the vehicle attracts Withholding Tax (WHT). 
This makes i t harder for an asset manager to achieve ‘tax neutr ality’ for their investors. Tax 
neutrality simply means that the investors would pay no more tax than they would if they were to 
invest directly, rather than through a fund or other investment entity. These limiting factors make 
ELTIFs inefficient for investors seeking exposure to longer-term or illiquid assets such as SME loans 
or infrastructure investments. 
 
We have provided further comments on these factors in our response to subsequent questions 
within the questionnaire.

Question 4. Is the scope of the ELTIF authorisation and operating conditions appropriate? 
(5000 character limit) 
 
ELTIFs managers are already subject to an authorisation process under the Alternative Investment 
Fund Managers Dire ctive (AIFMD).   The AIFMD was designed to focus on Alternative Investment 
Fund Managers (AIFMs) because the universe of Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs) is so diverse 
and broad. 
 
The ELTIF regime is therefore a specific regulatory investment product framework which is linked 
to the AIFMD as only duly authorised AIFMs are entitled to manage ELTIFs.  The regulatory and 
supervisory framework provided by the AIFMD means that managers of ELTIFs are already subject 
to an authorisation process and ongoing supervision before they seek authorisation for an ELTIF. 
 
While we would encourage supervisors to look at streamlining the process and introducing more 
efficiency where possible, the current authorisation process is broadly appropriate.  We do not 
believe it is necessary to introduce any further requirements to obtain authorisation beyond those 
which currently exist.

6.1 Support statement, focus on providing further details on the possible definitions and 
scope of eligible assets which should be encouraged (5000 characters)  
 
We would strongly encourage an expansion of the eligible assets within ELTIF.  The scope of the 
assets deemed eligible is currently too narrowly defined for ELTIF managers to pursue an effective 
investment strategy.  This means that ELTIF compares unfavourably to other fund structures that 
can be used to finance SMEs or investment in infrastructure.  This is a substantial disincentive to 
investors looking to invest in European credit -based investment strategies.  Therefore, the scope 
of eligible assets should be significantly expanded to allow greater freedom for investors and ELTIF 
managers to pursue more flexible investment strategies. 
 
We would however strongly urge policymakers to refrain from trying to target assets or activities 
too specifically when amending the scope of the eligible assets.  Where we have responded that 
certain investments should be ‘strongly encouraged’ this should be understood as saying these 
assets should be permissible rather than listed as a specific eligible asset.  
 
Furthermore, the current Regulation already allows the investment in the activities listed above if 
the financial instrument related to the underlying activity fits with the definition of eligible assets. 
We would therefore recommend that the Commission does not focus on enlarging specific types 
of activities and prescribing what activities an asset manager should invest in, but rather on the 
  
 
15 
expanding the type of instruments or corporate structures in which the fund manager can invest, 
or the levels of concentration/diversification of investments.  
 
Imposing further prescriptive requirements and restrictions upon the eligible assets under the 
framework, will only serve to decrease the competitiveness and attractiveness of the ELTIF vehicle 
for market participants.   
 
If it is deemed desirable to incentivise investment in the specific assets or industries mentioned in 
the preceding question, we do not believe that this should be done via asset management 
regulations. Incentives should be introduced through other tools available to policymakers (e.g. 
fiscal policy or improving connectivity between these sectors and finance providers) as this will 
support this objective without negatively impacting ELTIF.  
 
It should also be recalled that specific vehicles such as EUVECA and EUSEF have already been set 
up by EU policymakers with the ultimate objective of channeling more investments toward specific 
industries (namely start -ups and social entrepreneurship SMEs) . The limited adoption of such 
vehicles is evidence that prescribing sectoral investments into fund regulation is perhaps not the 
most effective way to support the deployment of private capital in the EU. 6 
 
Due to the format of this questionnaire, we are unable to comment fully on how the scope of 
eligible assets should be reformed. We have attached a supplementary document which list 
specific areas where we would recommend expanding the scope of eligible assets  and why this 
would support the further take up of the ELTIF vehicle. 
 
Specific areas where we would recommend expanding the scope of eligible assets would include:  
 
• Removing the requirement for qualifying portfolio undertakings to  have a market 
capitalisation of no more than €500m. Such a requirement currently restricts the ability of 
ELTIFs to build sectoral portfolios that mix SMEs, lower and upper -mid market corporates. 
Removing this restriction would help an ELTIF achieve greate r scale, allow faster deployment 
of capital and reduce overall costs. Improving the efficiency of the ELTIF in this manner would 
benefit both other borrowers and the ELTIF’s investors. 
 
Introducing greater flexibility for the ELTIF to invest in non -EU assets would widen the appeal 
of ELTIFs and support the ability of ELTIF managers to develop balanced and diversified 
portfolios. The requirement for a qualified portfolio undertaking to be established in a 
Member State (notwithstanding the enabling conditions  in Article 11 (1)c) positions ELTIF as a 
solely European investment product and creates uncertainty regarding how ELTIFs might 
invest in third countries, including investments in third country businesses that may wish to 
grow their footprint in Europe. Th is provision limits the ability of the ELTIF to offer investors

exposure to businesses that are geographically diversified. This is a significant appeal of

of which is cur rently invested in European businesses. Introducing greater flexibility for the 
ELTIF to invest in non -EU assets would therefore widen the appeal of ELTIFs and support the

• ELTIFs are unable to invest more than 10% of their capital in units or shares of any single ELTIF, 
EuVECA or EuSEF. This restriction prevents the existence of ELTIF fund of funds. Investing 
through a  fund of fund s can provide investors with  higher levels of diversification, lower 
volatility and an additional layer of screening, as the managers of the underlying funds have 
already conducted research on the initial investments. These features of fund of funds can be 
particularly beneficial for  retail investors. Reforming the ELTIF Regulation to allow for a fund

Question 8.1 Please explain your answer to question 8. In particular, please describe in 
detail any necessary adjustments to enhance legal certainty, for instance, with respect to 
the proportion invested in EU Member States with a view to benefit the ELTIF market, their 
managers and the broader European economy. (5000 characters)  
 
While the ELTIF regime has a permissive approach to investment in third countries the drafting of 
the legislation, particularly Article 11(1)c, creates practical problems when seeking to invest in non-
EU assets. The need to assess any potential third count ry investments against the requirements 
of Article 11(1)c adds cost and complexity to the operation of the ELTIF  and the ELTIF manager.  
This disincentivises the development of strategies which invest in both EU and non -EU assets – 
something that investors  value to diversify their exposure and  potentially increase the potential 
return on their investment. 
 
As well as being less attractive to investors , this provision also creates practical challenges for 
prospective ELTIF managers by limiting  the pool of po tential assets the ELTIF can invest in.   This 
limits the speed at which an ELTIF can be launched or scaled up.   This is a significant appeal of 
BDCs, a US vehic le with similar objectives and a comparable investor base to ELTIFs, where the 
rules regarding investment in non-US assets have supported the ability of BDC managers to deploy 
capital more efficiently and achieve scale. 
 
Introducing greater certainty on the ability of ELTIFs to invest in non-EU assets would therefore be 
consistent with comparable investment  products, widen the appeal of ELTIFs  to investors  and 
support the ability of ELTIF managers to develop balanced and diversified portfolios.  
 
We believe that greater certainty could be provided through the following means:  
 
• The ELTIF Regulation should clarify that it is permissible for the ELTIF to invest in non-EU assets 
and that ELTIFs can invest in EU assets indirectly through non -EU Special Purpose Vehicles 
(SPVs). 
• Article 11 (1)c (ii)  requires portfolio undertakings in third countries to have signed an 
agreement on exchange of information with the home Member State of the ELTIF manager 
and any other Member State in which the ELTIF is marketed creates an ongoing compliance 
challenge for the ELTIF manager . Its removal would reduce the compliance and operational 
burden on prospective ELTIF managers and provide grea ter certainty regarding investments 
by ELTIFs in third countries.  An alternative approach would be to only maintain the 
requirement that the manager should confirm that the third country fully complies with the

standards laid down in Article 26 of the OECD Model Tax Convention on Income and on Capital.

16.1 Support statement 
 
Borrowing by ELTIFs is currently limited to 30% of the net cap ital of the ELTIF that is available for 
investment. Any use of leverage is, in practice, much lower as the asset managers will not run the 
risk of breaching the leverage limit, meaning that ELTIFs are effectively unlevered. This contrasts 
unfavourably with both other EU fund structures which permit leverage to be employed within 
specified constraints, and US BDCs which allow borrowing of 100% of Net Asset Value (NAV) or, 
under certain conditions, up to 200% of NAV (if BDC managers elect to do so and this is  endorsed 
by their investors). 
 
Borrowing by ELTIFs to support the financing of assets will enhance their ability to finance SMEs.  
Increasing borrowing limits will also improve the return profile of ELTIFs and make them more 
appealing as an investment product. 
 
A further restriction on borrowing by ELTIFs can be found in Article 16, which requires the ELTIF to 
borrow funds in the same currency as the assets which will be acquired with the borrowed cash.  
This may not always be the most efficient approach for borrowing as, depending on the totality of 
circumstances, it may be more efficient to borrow in another currency.  For example, many assets 
may be denominated in one currency, or located in a country with one currency, but traded in 
another.  Similarly, assets denominated in one currency may have important revenue streams in 
another.  Requiring ELTIFs to only borrow in the same currency prevents the ELTIF manager from 
using other borrowing methods  that are potentially more efficient  and allow them to man age 
potential risks more effectively.  A further significant drawback of Article 16 is that is restricts 
investment in other jurisdictions.  Most EU-focused ELTIFs are likely to be denominated in Euro 
which restricts the ability of ELTIFs to invest in Memb er States using other currencies.  Removing 
this requirement would be a significant boost to ELTIF’s intended role as a pan-European financing 
vehicle. 
 
Borrowing limits also create an operational challenge at the start of the ELTIFs life (the ramp -up 
period).  Disapplying borrower limits during this period would also help provide ELTIF managers 
with greater flexibility in the early stages of the ELTIF’s life as they establish its investment strategy.  
  
 
33 
 
Greater flexibility of using leverage within an ELTIF, subject to proportionate safeguards around 
the way borrowing is facilitated, would make it easier for ELTIFs to leverage their capital, provide 
more finance to SMEs and deliver better returns for their investors. 
 
Question 17. What should be the optimal maximum allowed net leverage allowed for ELTIF 
funds? 
 
We would propose that there are no quantitative limitations on how leverage is used, whether as 
subscription lines, liquidity support or asset purchases  within the ELTIF regulation .  Instead, any 
potential risks arising from the use of leverage should be addressed with a high-level requirement 
for ELTIF manager s to manage leverage appropriately with respect to the investment and any 
potential maturity or currency mismatches.  
 
It should be noted that in the absence of any quantitative  leverage limits there are also other 
factors which would have a substantial impact on leverage employed by ELTIF .   Our data on the 
broader private credit market indicates that there is  little investor appetite for lending -based 
investment strategies which employ substantial leverage with almost all direct lenders employing 
less than 200% leverage 9.  Furthermore, an y ELTIF manager/AIFM employing more than 300% 
leverage is subject to additional supervisory and investor disclosure s under the existing AIFMD 
framework.  Therefore, we believe there are existing market and supervisory mechanisms which 
place natural limits on the use of leverage by ELTIF managers  and that quantitative limits are 
unnecessary to mitigate any perceived risk of ELTIFs as a source of financial instability or  a fund 
vehicle which poses additional investor protection risks. 
 
At a minimum borrowing limits for ELTIF should be brought in line with UCITS (i.e. assets plus 100% 
exposure).  This would provide a consistent approach to wards leverage across two pieces of 
legislation, place ELTIF managers and UCITS managers on a level playing field and provide a 
consistency of treatment towards retail clients with respect to leverage.   We would also 
recommend that higher leverage limits are allowed in certain conditions (for example, where the 
ELTIF manager elects to do so and this is endorsed by their investors) and supported by 
appropriate governance. This would be consistent with the a pproach of US  BDCs which allow 
borrowing of 100% of NAV or, under certain conditions, up to 200% of NAV (where BDC managers 
elect to do so and this is endorsed by their investors).   
 
As well as increasing the maximum allowable leverage for ELTIF funds, co nsideration should be 
given to the following: 
 
• There should be no restriction on how the borrowed funds are used and the Regulation should 
explicitly allow for the use of borrowing for the purposes of managing liquidity, subscriptions 
and the financing of assets in the investment portfolio.

Redemption rules and life of ELTIFs 
 
Question 23. Please provide a critical assessment of the impacts of the ELTIF Regulation 
rules on redemption policy and the life-cycle of ELTIFs, including the appropriateness of the 
ELTIF Regulation for the structuring of the ELTIF funds, taking into account the legitimate 
interests of the investors and achieving the stated investment objective of ELTIFs. (5000 
characters) 
 
The ELTIF Regulation requires an ELTIF to be established as a closed -ended structure and for it to 
have a fixed maturity.  A practical consequence of these requirements is that they restrict the 
assets an ELTIF will consider for investment to those with a sufficiently short duration to ensure 
there is a suitable buffer between the life of the loan and the life of the ELTIF.  This is to safeguard 
the ELTIF manager from inadvertently breaching the fixed maturity requirement, however it also 
entails a significant drawback.  This requirement increases the proportion of the fund’s life during 
which investor capital is not fully invested.  This reduces the capital efficiency of the ELTIF for the 
investor and makes ELTIFs less attractive when compared to other vehicles.    
 
Policymakers should consider allowing for the ELTIF structure to e xist as a permanent capital or 
‘evergreen’ vehicle with no fixed maturity.   This could be modelled on the successful example of 
BDCs with both public and private evergreen ELTIFs.  For private ELTIFs, consideration would need 
to be given to ensuring that appropriate liquidity triggers are available to investors (for example, 
an annual continuation vote made by a majority of investors or the board of the ELTIF). 
Furthermore, some debt investments may be acquired with a view to a secondary exit prior to its 
natural life, which is a strategy that may be compromised by this restriction.   
 
Allowing an ELTIF to be set up as a permanent capital vehicle (i.e. with no fixed maturity) would 
overcome this issue.  Without an end date and with the ability to raise more capital, managers can 
truly focus on long-term capital appreciation for investors.  This would fit with the policy objectives 
behind ELTIF while also accommodating the preference of some investors and asset managers to 
establish these structures for their investments. 
 
We would however caution against the development of a prescribed ‘evergreen structure’ within 
the ELTIF regulation.  While allowing ELTIFs to adopt the features of evergreen structures will 
support the take of ELTIF by investors and asset managers we would not support a prescribed 
approach towards this within the Regulation.  Asset managers and investors require the flexibility 
to adopt ELTIF structures that are appropriate to their needs and the investment strategy.  
Therefore, these features should be permissible rather than prescribed in the Regulation.

We would suggest that the ELTIF Regulation is reformed to allow the use of the following interim 
liquidity measures: 
 
• Side pockets – managers could set aside a certain amount of subscription monies to meet

• A portion of investments in liquid/transferable securities – e.g. a portion of the ELTIF could be 
reserved at any one time for investment in money market instruments.  Consideration should 
also be given to liquid assets in similar asset classes such as private and public utilities.

Marketing strategy for ELTIFs 
 
Question 32. What are the key limitations stemming from the ELTIF framework that you 
consider reduce the attractiveness of the E LTIF fund structure or the cross -border 
marketing and distribution of ELTIFs across the Union? (5000 characters)  
 
The ACC urges the Commission to consider the significant administrative burden imposed upon 
ELTIF managers as a result of requirements associa ted with the distribution of ELTIFs to retail 
investors.  Satisfying multiple cross -jurisdictional marketing registration and notification 
procedures substantially increases time to market, costs for investors and burden on the fund 
sponsor. 
 
When distributing an ELTIF to retail investors, ELTIF managers must obtain the necessary 
information to make a suitability assessment. This includes (i) the retail investor's knowledge and 
experience in the investment field; (ii) the retail investor's fina ncial situation, including that 
investor's ability to bear losses; and (iii) the retail investor's investment objectives, including that 
investor's time horizon. Many ELTIF managers do not have the capacity to undertake this due 
diligence on retail investo rs, as this creates significant complexity for ELTIF managers whose 
operating infrastructure is more aligned to meeting the needs of institutional investors. This is 
often the most critical issue when deciding whether to set up an ELTIF.  The practical consequence 
of this is that any asset manager looking to establish an ELTIF must do so in partnership with a 
wealth manager or private bank of scale.  
 
One way of overcoming this complexity would be to reform the ELTIF Regulation to permit the 
establishment of publicly listed and private ELTIFs. As noted above, this would be consistent with 
other retail-focused lending vehicles such as BDCs and Investment trusts. Although Article 19(1) of 
the ELTIF Regulation already allows for the admission of units or shares of an ELTIF to be admitted 
to trading on a regulated market, there has been little take-up of this option in the market.   
 
Publicly listed ELTIFs would provide a consistency of approach for retail investors across the EU 
  
 
42 
whilst also providing greater cer tainty for asset managers. This approach would allow retail 
restrictions to be eased for publicly listed ELTIFs as they would be compliant with a host of other 
requirements that are similar to ordinary bonds and shares.  
 
A further issue for ELTIF managers relates to the impact of MiFID distribution rules. These do not 
align with the ELTIF’s intended market, and the existing cross -border marketing process inhibits 
the ability of investment managers to scale products. The detrimental impact of these rules on 
retail investors is not confined to ELTIF, and this has been recognised by policymakers who are 
now consulting on whether the rules can be updated to address this issue.  
 
Updating the MiFID investor definitions and target market rules , specificall y the need to have 
carried out an average of 10 or more transactions per quarter over 4 quarters, would support the 
ELTIF in realising its potential as a retail investment vehicle.  This requirement is inconsistent with 
investment in longer term-assets and acts as a barrier to knowledgeable retail investors accessing 
private markets.   As noted in our earlier comments any reform to investor definitions or 
requirements should be on an ‘opt -in’ basis to ensure that asset managers and distributors are 
not required to engage in a significant repapering exercise to determine investor categorisation.

Question 35. Is the effectiveness of the ELTIF framework impaired by national legislation or 
existing market practices? Please provide any examples you may have of “g oldplating” or 
wrong application of the EU acquis. (5000 characters) 
 
ELTIF managers are required to set up local facilities in each Member State where it intends to 
market units or shares of the ELTIF. This requirement feels anachronistic at a time when investors 
expect these facilities to be provided via online channels that do not require a local presence in 
each individual Member State. 
 
In practice, this requirement has also been interpreted and applied in different ways by different 
Member States. Thi s creates an additional operational and compliance burden on the ELTIF 
manager as well as an unlevel playing field.   Furthermore, the local facility requirement has been 
recently removed for UCITS funds (be they distributed to retail investors or professional investors) 
in the Cross-Border Distribution of Funds directive adopted in June 2019. Removing this obligation 
from the ELTIF Regulation would therefore be consistent with broader policy towards retail funds 
across Europe.

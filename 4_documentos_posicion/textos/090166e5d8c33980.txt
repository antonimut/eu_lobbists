Response to European Commission consultation on the review of the Alternative 
Investment Fund Managers Directive (AIFMD) – Additional information

General comments: 
While it is certainly necessary to take into account more recent political, economic and social 
developments, ALFI thinks that one should not deviate from the idea of a targeted review on the 
application and scope of the AIFMD as provided in Article 69(1) AIFMD. The latter refers to an 
analysis of the experience acquired in applying this directive, its impact on investors, AIFs and AIFMs 
in the EU and third countries, and the degree to which the objectives of the AIFMD have been 
achieved. In our view, this makes it clear that the general concept of a manager directive should 
not be put into question, e.g. by introducing product specific rules on loan originating funds. Any 
additional layer of regulation at the level of the funds could impair existing flexibility. The AIFMD has 
played a major role in helping to create an internal market for AIFs and a harmonised and stringent 
regulatory and supervisory framework for AIFMs. If the AIFMD standard is still meant to allow the EU 
fund industry to be competitive and to be exported to countries worldwide, it would in our view be 
detrimental to change the basic principle of manager regulation. One of the arguments for the AIFMD 
was to become competitive to the much larger US funds, which has not yet been achieved. 
Overall, we concur with the European Commission’s conclusion that the impact of the AIFMD on 
AIFs and AIFMs has been largely positive, with the AIFMD playing an important role in creating an 
internal market for AIFs and reinforcing the regulatory and supervisory framework for AIFMs in the 
EU. The industry got used to the new manager related requirements, which also provided room for 
new product developments at national level.  
The AIFMD is already known in many parts of the world, even though we would say that it is not yet at 
the same level as the UCITS brand. To ensure the development of a brand and the EU’s 
competitiveness, there is primarily a need for stability. Improvements should at this stage focus on 
areas where clear issues have been identified. Given the conclusions drawn from the consultancy 
survey in 2019 and the Commission’s report in 2020, ALFI is of the view that changes should mainly 
be of a technical nature, and they could be achieved at level 2 and/or level 3 of the Lamfalussy 
process. Another option is to address specific aspects via separate legal instruments (e.g. MiFID as 
regards for the opening of the AIF market to semi-professional investors including, for example, well 
informed investors and high net worth investors). 
From this perspective, ALFI thinks that the UCITS framework and AIFMD should not be merged, 
and harmonised rules could only be appropriate in very few selected areas. 
There are a number of reasons why both directives were calibrated differently (product regulation 
versus manager regulation, different investor types, different asset classes, different risk profiles and 
different processes and requirements for risk management, valuation and portfolio management). A 
single rulebook could have been envisaged when fund rules were introduced for the first time. Now

general terms and sub-categories. The EU must be aware that the choice of investors worldwide for

The following detailed explanations, market analyses and statistics relate to our answer to question 
3.1. They explain the choices we made:  
Ad a) Creating internal market for AIFs – somewhat agree 
ALFI agrees that the AIFMD has been largely successful in creating an internal market for AIFs.  
Indeed, the passport for marketing of EU AIFs in Member States other than in the home Member 
State of the AIFM is widely used and reflective of the approach adopted by many asset managers to 
set up their AIFM and AIFs in one Member State to prevail of the marketing passport under the 
AIFMD for the distribution of AIFs across the EU. 
As of 25 January 2021 (source CSSF) 206 Luxembourg AIFMs (1561 funds and 2138 sub-funds) use 
the passport according to Article 32 of the AIFMD (marketing of units or shares of EU AIFs in Member 
States other than in the home Member State of the AIFM). 
The countries of distribution vary and many AIFs are distributed in several host Member States as 
shown in table I) in the Appendix. 
However, at the same time, the notifications to the CSSF according to Article 42 of the AIFMD 
(conditions for the marketing in Member States without a passport of AIFs managed by a non-EU 
AIFM) are numerous. 375 third country AIFMs have notified the CSSF under the NPPR rules, and 864 
AIFs and 1007 sub-funds are concerned by this constellation (source CSSF). This triggers the 
question of whether there is a level/unlevel playing field as further discussed under question 49. 
Furthermore, notifications to the CSSF according to Article 36 of the AIFMD (conditions for the 
marketing in Member States without a passport of non-EU AIFs managed by an EU AIFM) are less 
frequent. Only 12 AIFMs of EU Member States have notified the CSSF and 25 AIFs are concerned by 
this constellation (source CSSF). These figures are an indication for a lack of attractiveness of the EU 
AIFM regime if it does not grant access to the marketing passport. 
With respect to investors, the definition of professional investors is excessively narrow and certain 
institutional and/or sophisticates investors naturally interested in investing in AIFs do neither fulfil the 
criteria to qualify as professional investors nor do they fulfil two of the three criteria needed for the opt-
in status under MiFID. 
Ad b) Enabling monitoring risks to the financial stability – somewhat agree 
Pursuant to Article 24 of the AIFMD, various far reaching reporting obligations have been imposed on 
AIFMs allowing regulators to monitor risks that stem from the activities of AIFMs. KPMG in its report 
of 2018 notes that “The collection of information through the AIFM reports to NCAs makes it easier for 
regulators to detect and respond to risks in the relevant markets on a harmonised level in the EU. 
This strengthens the monitoring of cross-border activities of AIFMs and enables the NCAs to identify 
systemic risks to the financial system, thereby contributing to financial market stability”. 
In addition, Article 24.5 provides that “where necessary for the effective monitoring of systemic risk, 
the competent authorities of the home Member State may require information in addition to that 
described in this Article, on a periodic as well as on an ad-hoc basis. [..]”. 
The volume of data provided to regulators should enable them to monitor financial stability risks. Prior 
to making any amendment to the AIFMD’s reporting obligations, it should first be verified and 
explained that specific information cannot be extracted and used by regulators to monitor systemic

risk with the amount of data collected. Efficient monitoring also presupposes that NCAs adequately 
follow up and verify in detail the data received from AIFMs. 
The Covid-19 crisis has been a major stress test to the alternative investment fund industry and no 
unknown systemic risks were detected, comforting ALFI in its perception that the rules as currently set 
out in the AIFMD are efficient. 
Ad c) Providing high-level investor protection – fully agree 
The AIFMD is the outcome of a G20 consensus for closer regulatory oversight of actors and activities 
in the alternative investment funds sector. In particular, it aims at setting up robust risk and liquidity 
management systems and enhancing transparency towards investors. Indeed, investor protection has 
been a key driver of the AIFMD since its adoption in 2011. Investor protection has been implemented 
through the introduction of numerous requirements to be complied with by AIFMs, in particular the 
various policies and procedures within the context of risk management, liquidity management, 
compliance and conflicts of interest. In addition, the AIFMD has imposed on the AIFM by virtue of 
Article 23, the obligation to disclose adequate information to investors thus enabling them to make an 
informed judgement about the proposed investments and risks related thereto.   
As from 2 August 2021, these requirements will be further completed, in particular in respect of 
marketing communications by Regulation (EU) 2019/1156 of the European Parliament and of the 
Council of 20 June 2019 on cross-border distribution of collective investment undertakings and 
amending Regulations (EU) No 345/2013, (EU) No 346/2013 and (EU) No 1286/2014; please see the 
following extracts of Article 4 of Regulation 2019/1156: 
Article 4.1.  “AIFMs, EuVECA managers, EuSEF managers and UCITS management companies shall 
ensure that all marketing communications addressed to investors are identifiable as such and 
describe the risks and rewards of purchasing units or shares of an AIF or units of a UCITS in an 
equally prominent manner, and that all information included in marketing communications is fair, 
clear and not misleading.” 
Article 4.4. “AIFMs, EuVECA managers and EuSEF managers shall ensure that marketing 
communications comprising an invitation to purchase units or shares of an AIF that contain specific 
information about an AIF do not contradict the information which is to be disclosed to the 
investors in accordance with Article 23 of Directive 2011/61/EU, with Article 13 of Regulation (EU) 
No 345/2013 or with Article 14 of Regulation (EU) No 346/2013, or diminish its significance.” 
ALFI considers that the AIFMD achieves the objective that investors understand the risks related to 
their investment. Transparency and disclosures are indeed of essence. Importantly, since the AIFMD, 
unlike UCITS, does not offer any passport for distribution to retail investors, it is of essence not to 
impose any restrictions on the risks which professional investors are willing to take in order to 
generate return. 
Ad d) The scope of the AIFM license is clear and appropriate – somewhat agree  
A perceived lack of consistency in the interpretation of the AIFMD between NCAs has not been 
favourable to a more extensive use of branches. Branches of AIFMs in host Member States have 
mainly been established in order to market AIFs managed by AIFMs in such host Member State. 
Furthermore NCAs appear to have adopted different interpretations with respect to the performance 
of services by an AIFM to AIFs managed by another AIFM. Indeed it is unclear whether an authorised 
AIFM is also authorised to provide the services referred to in Annex I to the AIFMD as delegate to 
another AIFM, or whether it is only permitted to provide such services for the benefit of AIFs for whom 
it is the designated AIFM. The internal market would be strengthened if the former AIFM would be 
permitted to provide administration and marketing services to the AIFs or to act as delegate for certain 
services to the latter AIFM, subject obviously always to compliance with Article 82 of the AIFMR 
substance requirements.

As described in question 4 below, further clarifications concerning the additional activities that an 
AIFM may perform, i.e. discretionary portfolio management on an individual basis, investment advice 
etc. would be helpful. Such clarifications can however be provided without changing the AIFMD level 
1 provisions. 
Furthermore there proves to be a lack of consistency as regards the concept of marketing amongst 
the various NCAs. To remedy this situation, it would be adequate to clarify the concept by expressly 
confirming that marketing activities neither fall within the scope of investment advice nor within the 
scope of reception and transmission of orders. This goal could be achieved through the issuance of 
an FAQ and would therefore not require any amendment to the AIFMD. 
Ad e) The AIFMD costs and benefits are balanced (in particular regarding the regulatory 
and administrative burden) – neutral  
 Authorisation of AIFMs 
The documents, policies, procedures and information to be provided to NCAs at the time of 
submitting an application to be authorised as an AIFM are clear and fair, and they enable 
NCAs to have a full view of the AIFM’s internal organisation and operating model. Should 
any further clarifications be necessary, this could be done through an FAQ or a regulation.  
Concerning AIFMs, barriers to entry are however high, both in relation to obtaining an EEA 
license, but also for accessing several national markets within the EU. This is in particular 
problematic for smaller fund companies and it is the reason for an increase in white-labelling 
servicing solutions (which are favoured by ALFI as elaborated in our answer to question 51). 
The consequence is that the EU market is dominated by large fund companies, which has 
overall a negative impact on competition from an investment product point of view. 
 Reporting 
One of the important aspects of the AIFMD is the imposition of reporting obligations on 
AIFMs. The detail of data to be provided to NCAs has significantly increased since the 
adoption of the AIFMD. According to the report issued by KPMG in December 2018, “the 
AIFMD reporting regime has directly caused a significant improvement in increased 
transparency at the EU level. The collection of information through the AIFM reports to NCAs 
makes it easier for regulators to detect and respond to risks in the relevant markets on a 
harmonised level in the EU”. While this enables NCAs to monitor financial stability risks, the 
significant costs triggered by the high volume of data to be reported should not be 
underestimated from an investor’s point of view. 
 Registrations for marketing 
ALFI considers the information and documents to be provided to the home Member State 
within the context of a notification procedure for the marketing of an AIF in another Member 
State to be clear. Nevertheless, differences have been observed in certain jurisdictions 
setting additional requirements to be complied with prior to being authorised to market the 
AIF in the relevant Member State.  
The issues identified should however be solved to a large extent as a result of Directive (EU) 
2019/1160 and Regulation (EU) 2019/1156 as regards cross-border distribution of collective 
investment undertakings. 
Ad f) The different components of the AIFMD legal framework operate well together to 
achieve the AIFMD objectives - somewhat agree  
ALFI considers that the AIFMD legal framework largely operates well to meet the AIFMD’s objectives. 
Those objectives have been financial stability, avoidance of systemic risk, functioning of the passport, 
transparency towards investors, enhancing investments in the alternative asset classes in the context

of the CMU. According to Recital 4 of the AIFMD “The Directive aims to provide for an internal market 
for AIFMs and a harmonized and stringent regulatory and supervisory framework for the activities 
within the Union of all AIFMs [...]”. From the KPMG report issued in December 2018 one may note 
that, “most areas of the provisions are assessed as having contributed to achievement of the specific 
and operational objectives, to have done so effectively, efficiently and coherently, to remain relevant 
and to have EU added value”. ALFI shares this view. 
The managers’ regulation approach, as opposed to fund regulation, is indeed working well. As a 
managers’ directive, the AIFMD should continue to regulate managers. This was, and should remain, 
the scope of the AIFMD as mentioned in Recital 10: “this Directive does not regulate AIFs. AIFs 
should therefore be able to continue to be regulated and supervised at national level”. Any additional 
layer of regulation at the level of the funds could impair existing flexibility.  
The AIFMD has played a major role in helping to create an internal market for AIFs and a harmonised 
and stringent regulatory and supervisory framework for AIFMs. However, it is not yet recognised as a 
global brand comparable to UCITS. From an EU competitiveness perspective, if the AIFMD standard 
is still meant to be exported worldwide, it would be detrimental to change the principle of managers’ 
regulation. One of the arguments for the AIFMD was to become competitive to the much larger US 
funds, which has clearly not yet been achieved. 
However, the passport regime should be available to authorised EEA AIFMs marketing non-EEA AIFs 
to professional investors in the EEA. The timetable initially announced foresaw that upon issuance of 
a positive advice from ESMA on the application of the marketing passport, the European Commission 
could issue as early as 2015 a delegated act allowing non-EEA AIFs to be marketed in the EEA on 
the basis of the marketing passport regime (pursuant to Article 35 of the AIFMD). ALFI considers that 
any review of the AIFMD should foremost focus on the provisions concerning the third country 
passport and NPPRs. ALFI does not support the idea of changing AIFMD level 1 provisions without 
tackling this issue. In the absence of any changes to the marketing passport for third countries, 
changes could be limited to level 2 changes. 
Ad g) The AIFMD objectives correspond to the needs and problems in EU asset 
management and financial markets – somewhat agree  
The objectives of the AIFMD as described above largely correspond to the needs and problems in the 
EU asset management and financial markets. One important objective not to forget is the creation of a 
competitive legal framework allowing EU asset managers to compete on a global basis.  
Concerning one of the objectives of the CMU i.e. to raise capital in the EU and to attract new 
investors, one should reconsider the definition of professional investors as currently stated in MiFID, 
because at present it does not capture all types of institutional and/or sophisticated investors investing 
in private equity. Therefore, it is important that the alleviations that are currently proposed to be 
brought to the “opt-in” regime for investors to be treated as professional investors are taken into 
account through an update of the MiFID framework.  
As indicated under point f) above, the AIFMD has been adopted to regulate AIFMs; each of these 
managers may follow and implement a different strategy depending on the target investment of the 
investment fund. Hence, envisaging to introduce product rules in the AIFMD does not make sense as 
these would have to cover all possible investment strategies.  
Furthermore, ALFI is not in favour of a regulation for loan funds. In particular, taking into consideration 
a post Covid-19 environment and financing needs of the economy, product regulation for loan funds 
could unnecessarily restrict non-bank financing.

ALFI considers that the AIFMD has contributed to the growth of the alternative investment fund

In relation to question 4, we would like to comment in more detail on other functions to be added or 
not to the AIFM licence: 
The AIFM licence as set out in the AIFMD is appropriate. There are however instances where better 
use of existing regulatory convergence tools, preventing in particular goldplating, could improve the 
functioning of the AIFM licence. Mechanisms for regulatory convergence could be used in order to 
clarify that:  
- AIFMs can by delegation from an AIFM, provide selected services as set out in Annex 1 to the 
AIFMD also to AIFs for which they are not the designated AIFM; 
- The origination of loans is an authorised AIFM activity. The need to keep capital flowing within the 
commercial markets should, in our opinion, provide comfort that such investments are appropriate 
for AIF structures. Whether a loan is held on a bank balance sheet or within an AIF does not 
 
ALFI response to European Commission consultation on the AIFMD review – additional information       Page | 8  
change the risk to the borrower. In addition, the nature of fund investors in AIFs is different from 
bank depositors, and AIFMs, unlike banks, have no balance sheet risk. 
We would encourage regulatory convergence to ensure that such lending activities are possible on a 
commercial basis within the entire EU. For clarity we would not support retail lending be permitted i.e. 
AIFs granting consumer loans. 
 
We would like to explain our answer to question 7 as follows: 
ALFI believes that the AIFMD is sufficiently clear that it “should not apply to the management of 
pension funds and employee participation or savings schemes”, as outlined in Recital 8 of the AIFMD. 
ALFI also agrees with the rationale that pension funds should not fall under the AIFMD due to the fact 
that pension funds are subject to specific regulation. 
As regards the intention of the AIFMD with respect to managers that manage AIFs wherein only 
pension funds invest and the question of whether these are in or out of scope of the AIFMD, ALFI 
believes that guidance provided by the European Commission questions and answers is also 
sufficiently clear. According to the wording of Article 3(3) AIFMD the exclusion depends on whether 
an AIFM in addition to managing pension funds also manages AIFs. Thus "in so far as they do not 
manage AIFs" should be interpreted to mean that a pension fund manager would fall within the scope 
of the AIFMD if, apart from a pension fund, it also manages at least an AIF.  
An AIFM can be exempted from the AIFMD only if it manages exclusively pension funds. An AIF does 
not become a pension fund merely because pension funds invest into it. So managers managing such 
AIFs are covered by the AIFMD. Under the AIFMD the individual portfolio management of a pension 
fund by an AIFM can be done only under the conditions laid down in Article 6 of the AIFMD. 
 
We would like to explain our answer to question 8 as follows: 
We are not aware of any issues concerning AIFM capital requirements which would warrant any 
changes. The current regime under the AIFMD is appropriate.  
 
We would like to explain our answer to question 9 as follows: 
The current regime under the AIFMD is appropriate. The existing initial capital limit of EUR 10 million

We would like to explain our answer to question 11 as follows:

In relation to question 16, we would like to comment in more detail on the assets under management 
thresholds laid down in Article 3 of the AIFMD: 
The thresholds for small AIFMs are adapted as they allow small managers to start managing private 
equity funds without incurring all the costs of compliance of the AIFMD.

The threshold for leveraged funds or open ended funds appear to be too low, as imposing AIFMD

However, given the existence of white label solutions, we do not believe that any change should be

Please find hereafter the last two paragraphs of our answer to question 24: 
A further request in regards to look-through would be the clarification of the ownership verification 
requirements, and the addition of the ability to rely on a signed list of assets by the AIFM, to replace 
the need to collect an ownership verification document for each individual asset. Requiring annual 
individual ownership evidence documents such as land registers, title insurance deeds etc. can be a 
contentious issue for the depositary and the fund. In some cases where a look through is required, the 
AIFM may not in fact have access to some documentation. The collection and review of these 
documents on an annual basis can also be expensive, requires significant resource, and is a heavy 
administrative burden for both the AIF/AIFM and the depositary. In all instances where there is only 
one relevant proof of ownership (e.g. a loan agreement), it is even unnecessary to recollect always 
the same evidence, rather it should only be reconfirmed that the one on the hands of the depositary 
still reflects the ownership of the AIF. 
Custody:  
Article 21(11) of AIFMD provides that depositaries which entrust the custody of financial instruments 
to third parties located in third countries, must ensure that those parties “are subject to effective 
prudential regulation”. Except in case where equivalence decisions have been pronounced by the 
European Commission, this requirement leads depositaries to engage in a complex and subjective 
assessment, with the result that this situation creates inconsistencies across the market.

In relation to question 50, we would like to comment in more detail on the delegation rules (letter-box 
entities): 
Delegation is integral to the EU's open global model and to the UCITS and AIFMD frameworks, 
granting EU investors access to global expertise, resulting in more diversified portfolios, while 
benefitting from high levels of investor protection. It has also made an important contribution to the 
creation of the global UCITS and AIF brands. Changing delegation rules would significantly impact the 
competitiveness of the EU funds industry and impede the Commission's efforts in building a Capital 
Market Union. 
The AIFMD level 1 and 2 rules provide for a solid framework around delegation. Demanding 
qualitative criteria must be met to enable delegation, including: justified by objective reasons; the 
delegate disposing of sufficient resources, being of sufficiently good repute and sufficiently 
experienced; authorisation, registration and supervision requirements relating to portfolio or risk 
management delegates; initial due diligence, effective supervision and ongoing monitoring by the 
AIFM; etc. At the same time, the AIFM must at all times act in the best interest of investors, has the 
right to withdraw the delegation with immediate effect if in the interest of investors and its liability 
towards the fund and its investors remains unaffected. 
ESMA has further complemented these rules, providing guidance in determining and applying such 
regulatory framework. NCAs have implemented this guidance, in particular the Luxembourg CSSF 
issued Circular 18/698, which applies to both UCITS management companies and AIFMs, creating a 
fully consistent regulatory framework across both regimes and ensuring that sufficient resources are 
retained in the delegating entity to adequately oversee the performance of all delegates. 
Fund managers have testified that delegation enables them to offer a wider range of strategies and 
asset classes, to reduce costs and find economies of scale, to develop centres of excellence, to hire 
and retain best-in-class portfolio managers, to access local expertise. All these elements are to the 
benefit of EU investors as end consumers of fund products. Conversely, fund managers have 
expressed that, if delegation rules were to be restricted, this would have an adverse impact on 
investors, notably reducing the EU fund offering (including fund closures, transfers and switches), 
increased costs (without added benefit), fragmenting the market, a loss of talent and of access to fund 
products, reducing competition in the EU market, damaging the UCITS and AIF brands, decreasing 
performance. This would also reduce the EU fund industry's competitiveness when it needs it more 
than ever to provide high quality products to investors both in the EU and globally. Indeed, if the scale 
is tipped too far and the delegation model can no longer be sensibly supported by the EU fund 
industry, third-country managers will continue to seek access to the EU investor base, but EU 
regulators would lose control over such fundraising activity, exposing EU investors to products 
domiciled in third countries that may have lower regulatory standards and levels of protections, while 
EU-based stakeholders would lose their crucial role in ensuring checks and balances, through 
delegate oversight, depositary duties, etc.

The AIFMD level 2 provisions relating to letter-box entities are principles-based, since it is impossible 
to capture the multitude of scenarios by way of a rules-based approach, which would effectively 
require an exhaustive list of cases in which an AIFM is no longer considered to be the manager of a 
fund (also contemplating intragroup vs. external delegation, delegation to other EU-based entities or 
outside of the EU). More importantly, however, the principles set out in the regulation provide for a 
solid basis for NCAs (i) to enforce questionable cases of AIFMs lacking substance or serving as mere 
steppingstones for other entities involved in the management of a fund, (ii) to verify the balance 
between the delegated function and the functions retained by the AIFM,  and (iii) to ascertain that 
there is competency within the AIFM at senior manager/conducting officer level and at board level to 
oversee the delegated function. These principles prevent the creation of letter-box entities. 
NCAs actively engage on the organisational set-up of an AIFM, which translates into granular 
discussions around (i) the number and seniority level of board members and senior 
managers/conducting officers, (ii) reporting lines towards senior management and the board, (iii) the 
internal control functions in relation to risk management, compliance and internal audit as well as the 
incompatibilities between those functions and roles, (iv) delegation and group support arrangements.

fund managers. In addition and as indicated under question 96, convergence tools already exist, e.g.

In relation to question 51, we would like to comment in more detail on the delegation rules (effective 
risk management): 
The function most commonly delegated by AIFMs is portfolio management, meaning that the risk 
management function must be retained, next to other functions such as oversight. This delegation set-
up allows the AIFM to rely on the expertise of a portfolio manager often based, close to the assets of 
the fund, while exercising a centralised function of risk management internally. 
The requirements under the AIFMD level 1 and 2 rules with respect to the risk management function 
are extensive. AIFMs therefore have a very detailed rulebook on the basis of which they can build 
their risk management function, policies and procedures, including considerations around the 
functional and hierarchical separation of the function, safeguards against conflicts of interests, 
qualitative and quantitative risk limits, etc. Liquidity management forms an integral part of the risk 
management function and again the legal and regulatory requirements in this respect are already very 
demanding. 
In practice, NCAs such as the Luxembourg CSSF review the risk management policies thoroughly 
and require that such policies are updated regularly, including when the addition of a new AIF under 
management warrants revisions due to its investment strategy, target investors, leverage 
arrangements or other reasons. The risk management divisions of the NCAs are adequately staffed to 
test the effectiveness of risk management systems and actively engage with AIFMs and management 
companies in this respect. 
In its August letter, ESMA advised the Commission to introduce more specific requirements for so-
called white-label service providers. It is worth reminding, however, that this business model has 
existed for decades without any issues, complaints or difficulties. Even during the Covid-19 crisis, it 
has demonstrated resilience by providing fully independent risk management services to investment 
managers located within, and outside of, the EU. White-label service providers are characterised by 
responsibility, never agreeing to put their reputation at risk or risking to be sanctioned. Reputational 
concerns are key within this type of AIFMs and management companies and therefore they generally 
have very significant risk management and compliance teams.

overwhelmingly have no interest in direct European Supervisory Authorities (“ESA”) supervision in the 
area of financial services.  
Following various contacts with internationally active asset management groups, ALFI has not found a 
single supporter for direct centralised supervision by ESMA. On the contrary:

The above does not preclude a role of convergence for ESMA. The ESAs are the ideal fora for

ALFI would welcome a NCAs’ peer review to assess the actual differences of practices across the 
EU, identifying in particular best practices. Convergence could then be further improved at the level of 
NCAs relying on theses best practices, with the support of ESMA. This would be in line with ALFI’s 
conviction that an efficient/effective supervision requires a qualitative supervisory cooperation. 
 
We would like to explain our answer to question 97 as follows: 
ALFI does not see any need to grant additional powers to NCAs as it believes that the existing powers 
and competences of NCAs under the AIFMD are appropriate. 
 
We would like to explain our answer to question 100 as follows: 
Taking inventory of sanctions applied, as recently detailed by ESMA in its Report ESMA34-32-548, 
most large jurisdictions of alternative fund managers have applied none or only minimal sanctions 
under the current Article 48(1) AIFMD sanctions regime. While some Member States have sanctioned 
alternative managers more frequently, the ratio of applied sanctions vs. managed assets shows that 
the mechanisms within AIFMD are well established and already ensure a well supervised and 
controlled market. This is largely achieved through authorisation processes and application reviews 
on the level of the NCAs, as well as the continued supervision by and transparency towards NCAs 
during the on-going life of managers.  
Furthermore, every jurisdiction has a different focus of manager supervision, driven by core 
competencies, investment strategies, legal structures of fund vehicles and national vs. cross-border 
distribution of products, amongst others. We do not believe that a more aligned sanction framework 
on AIFMD level could adequately address these potentially varied root-causes of sanctions which 
would meaningfully enhance the sanctions regime.

We believe that any alignment on sanctions should take place between ESMA and the NCAs

We would like to explain our answer to question 101 as follows: 
We believe that both regulatory regimes should not be merged. One fundamental point to bear in 
mind is that the two directives are different in scope: the UCITS is a product directive whereas the 
AIFMD is a manager’s directive. This approach was analysed in much detail and considered by the 
European Commission as an important factor of differentiation when AIFMD came into play, and the 
idea of a product directive was deliberately put aside. Introducing a higher level of details for e.g. 
liquidity or risk management to achieve harmonisation is not realistic. Indeed, a UCITS will 
necessarily have a different liquidity and risk management process than a private equity or real estate 
fund. In addition, both types of funds target different types of investors and different asset classes. 
The AIFMD captures a much broader spectrum of funds with an almost unlimited variety of set-ups, 
features, asset classes, etc. Hence the needs of each set-up are different and the robustness of the 
structure should be based on sound principles rather than granular requirements which will likely not 
meet all the various AIF structures available on the market. 
There are reasons why the UCITS and AIFMD rules are calibrated differently: 
- Different investor types (investment restrictions to protect retail investors); 
- Different asset classes (wide variety of structuring requirements when investing in private assets); 
- Different approach: UCITS is a product directive, whereas AIFMD is manager directive; 
- Different risk profiles; 
- Difference in process and requirements for risk management, valuation and portfolio management 
(these processes have to differ widely between AIFs and their UCITS counterparts). 
In addition, combining both regulatory works into a single rule book could present a threat to market 
access for new and smaller industry participants. The objective of the AIFMD should continue to be 
the mitigation of systemic risk; therefore,  adding a layer of regulation (by combining UCITS and 
AIFMD) that is focused on retail investors would result in a much higher market entry cost, which 
would outweigh any success in the initial and crucial objective of systemic risk mitigation. Over the 
past seven years, the AIFMD has fostered growth and innovation within the alternative investment 
market and the above discussed increase in entry barriers could severely hinder further growth and 
innovation within the industry. 
Although we believe that certain definitions and the terminology between the AIFMD and UCITS 
regulatory framework can be aligned, we do not see a need for a single rulebook with the same or 
similar definitions.

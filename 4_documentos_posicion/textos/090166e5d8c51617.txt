III. International Relations – further explanation to some 
questionnaire responses  
 
Question 47. AIFMD and competitiveness  
 
We agree with the European Commission’s conclusion (in its recent report to the European 
Parliament and Council) that the AIFMD regulatory framework has contributed to the 
creation of the EU AIF market, provided a high-level protection to investors and facilitated 
monitoring of risks to financial stability.  
 
AIFMD, when working as intended, has enabled the creation of genuine cross-border 
activity, making it easier for EU investors to access the best investment opportunities, and 
for AIFMs to access those investors. Because it has allowed the European alternative fund 
industry to access pools of investor capital more easily across 27 markets, AIFMD has helped 
the sector to acquire scale (instead of keeping it local).  
 
Naturally, it is critical that the regime also works in a way that enables EU fund managers to 
deliver the best service and returns for their clients, therefore it needs to work with the 
norms of the fund management sector globally. Delegation allows managers to tap into local 
expertise and local liquidity at a competitive cost thus benefitting investor returns. A 
regulatory framework that makes it difficult for EU managers to access the asset 
management expertise, local knowledge and market understanding that is available around 
the world will struggle to be competitive because it will not deliver optimal client outcomes 
(whether those clients are in the EU or in the rest of the world).  
 
The delegation of investment management activity has been vital for the successful 
development of the EU AIFs market and its competitiveness globally. Delegation allows 
AIFMs to acquire additional expertise, to ensure 24-hour coverage of global markets thus 
expanding the range of products and investment opportunities for clients while benefiting 
from the economies of scale that the use of third parties can create. This all amounts to a 
more innovative and competitive EU fund management sector. 
 
Question 50.1 Clarity of delegation rules and letter box entities  
 
The current delegation rules on governance are clear without being unduly prescriptive and 
already provide national authorities with a clear set of criteria that must be taken into 
consideration when assessing delegation arrangements and whether permitting such 
activity would risk the AIFM being considered a letter-box entity – as per Articles 76 – 82 of 
the AIFMR. These rules are also the subject of detailed guidance to national authorities by 
ESMA (in its 2017 Legal Opinion) to ensure a convergent approach to the supervision of 
delegation. These requirements have in turn been incorporated by national authorities 
through specific guidance on required substance (for example, the CBI's CP-86 or the CSSF's 
18/698). More prescriptive rules in the absence of effective regulatory oversight would be 
an additional cost for the compliant without addressing the non-compliant.

Question 51.1 Delegation rules and effective risk management 
 
The delegation rules under the AIFMD/AIFMR framework are effective in ensuring effective 
risk management. Provided there is appropriate regulatory oversight then the requirements 
for appropriate risk management are clear without being unduly prescriptive. More 
prescriptive rules in the absence of effective regulatory oversight would be an additional 
cost for the compliant without addressing the non-compliant. Our experience shows that 
there is robust supervisory oversight over delegation arrangements that M&G has in place. 
 
Question 52.1 Delegation rules and quantitative criteria  
 
We do not think that delegation rules should be complemented further. The nature of firms, 
their structures and ways of operating are extremely diverse. This is a good thing, providing 
innovation that feeds into better products for investors. Detailed requirements about 
delegation mean that the diversity in approach would be supressed. An approach that 
resulted in firms being unable to access wider pools of talent in order to meet an essentially 
arbitrary limit could only result in worse consumer outcomes.  
 
Question 53.1 AIFMD standards and the location of a third party 
 
Our interpretation of the rules is that the standards, which we interpret as the outcome for  
customers, should already be applied no matter where the delegation is to. That is not to 
say that the rules should be the same. There is a need to recognise that different countries 
with different legal or structural arrangements may be unable to comply with the letter of 
EU requirements but this does not necessarily mean that the investor is exposed to any 
more risk, just that the risk is addressed differently. The rules within AIFMD already ensure 
that those standards will be challenged and adhered to. 
 
Question 54.1 Enforcement of the delegation rules  
 
We find that there are inconsistencies in the interpretation of the delegation rules between 
regulators. The specific example we have is in relation to whether it is necessary for an AIFM 
to have oversight of Intragroup delegations. We do not believe that such arrangements are 
necessary and provide administrative and operational burdens for no benefit to customers. 
However, we believe that cooperation under existing frameworks is the best way to tackle 
such issues, and that NCAs and ESMA have the necessary powers already to reach the 
desired outcomes. 
 
Question 55. Delegation rules and UCITS 
 
We believe that the UCITS rules are already satisfactory as they offer greater flexibility 
allowing for firms in deciding how to leverage the greatest skills and expertise within their 
wider Groups. We would support this approach being applied to AIFMD too.

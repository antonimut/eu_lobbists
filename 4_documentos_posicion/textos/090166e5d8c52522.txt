 
 
 
 
 
BPFI feedback on the European Commission public consultation on AIFMD 
 
Dublin 29 January 2021 
 
Introduction 
 
Banking & Payments Federation Ireland (BPFI) is the voice of banking and payments in 
Ireland. Representing over 100 domestic and international member institutions, we mobilise 
the sector’s collective resources and insights to deliver value and benefit to members, enabling 
them to build competitive sustainable businesses which support customers, the economy and 
society.  
 
We welcome the opportunity to provide comments on the European Commission’s (EC) public 
consultation. As our comments are targeted, we wanted to supplement our online contribution 
with the following information. 
 
• An EU depositary passport 
 
Overall, we believe that AIFMD is working well. However, in terms of an area where there may 
be merit in the European Commission making minor changes it is the area of the depositary 
passport. Pursuing this adjustment would, in our view, be consistent with the goal of achieving 
a single market for financial services in the EU, while also developing a fully integrated Capital 
Markets Union, particularly in light of the UK’s departure. More specifically, this would help to 
facilitate Member States who are cur rently challenged in accessing depositary services due 
to the requirement to appoint the depositary in the same jurisdiction.  
 
For example, if a fund manager has to appoint a depositary in the same jurisdiction it could 
prove challenging if there is a lack of expertise within that Member State, and they wouldn’t 
be in a position to leverage the existing expertise across other EU M ember States.  As a 
consequence, certain European Member States are in effect excluded from managing funds 
if they can’t access a depository of sufficient quality in another Member State. That quality and 
expertise is important, given the depositary takes on the liability for losses of assets. 
 
• Delegation and substance requirements 
 
AIFMD rules on letter -box entities are already very clear in our view. In addition to specific 
provisions in the AIFMD on this issue, there are national-specific requirements. In Ireland and 
Luxembourg, for example, the regulatory framework is further underpinned by the following:   
 
• Fund Management Guidance; and 
• CSSF Circular 18/698, which relates to the approval process and organisation of both 
UCITS management companies and AIFMs. 
 
We therefore see limited benefit in amending the current rules. Moreover, th e European 
Commission’s own report on the functioning of AIFMD published in June 2020 did not single 
out the need to reform the current delegation rules, nor were there any issues highlighted in 
the ESMA-coordinated Supervisory Coordination Network (SCN).

We also do not believe that the current AIFMD rules should be complemented by either 
quantitative elements or a list of functions that must be performed internally, for the following 
reasons: 
• Quantitative criteria: very complex as could not adopt a one -size fits all approach, 
given wide universe of AIFMs. This could also diminish the importance of other relevant 
criteria related to AIFMs. 
• List of functions:  could result in loss of access to expertise and efficiencies, 
presenting higher operating costs for EU AIFMs, which could then be passed on to 
investors. Such an approach may also create barriers to entry for smaller/new AIFMs 
and in our view would run counter to the development of Capital Markets Union.   
 
While delegates in third countries will already have to manage a delegated fund/portion of a 
fund in accordance with the AIFMD, we would urge policymakers against imposing any extra-
territorial application of the directive whereby the delegate would need to comply with all the 
provisions.  
 
Likewise, we wou ld caution against any changes to the existing delegation arrangements, 
which have proven extremely effective. In particular, we consider it crucial that investment 
managers based in third countries continue to be able to appoint host AIFMs.  Certain assets 
are based primarily in non-EU jurisdictions where local investment managers have expertise 
in managing such assets.  If the European Commission were to view host -AIFM delegation 
arrangements as not permissible, it would limit the ability of EU clients to  access potential 
investment opportunities and the capabilities of third country managers. The longer -term 
consequences of such an approach would impact the attractiveness of the EU as a destination 
of fund services. The current framework has enabled fund  managers from jurisdictions such 
as the US and Asia to benefit from the EU’s existing arrangements, which could be called into 
question if the current delegation arrangements are made more restrictive.   
 
Regarding National Private Placement Regimes (NPPR), we do not believe it has resulted in 
an un -level playing field. Rather, NPPRs have allowed EU investors to benefit from 
opportunities that would have otherwise been unavailable. If there are any changes 
considered to the regime, like the phasing out of the NPPR, by EU policymakers, we  would 
suggest that this should not happen until the third -country passport has been activated and 
has proven to be functioning effectively.   
 
• The macroprudential toolkit 
 
We believe the focus at this stage should be on ensuring the current set of liquidity 
management tools (LMT) are consistently available across the EU, rather than considering 
whether it needs to be augmented. Experience during the volatility in March 2020 has 
demonstrated the effectiveness of current LMTs, which has been recognised by policymakers, 
including the ECB and the Bank of England.  
 
As a consequence, we do not believe there is a need further guidance regarding NCA powers 
to suspend redemptions or indeed impose leverage limits on funds. In our view, these are 
clearly specified in the AIFMD rules and have been further augmented by recent ESMA 
guidance.  
 
While we acknowledge that these tools have not had to be utilised often, we do not think this 
should be considered to be reflecting a deficiency in the current rules. Moreover, while AIFMs 
will already have to r eport on use of exceptional -LMT, we do not believe this should be 
extended to ordinary-LMT, such as swing pricing and other anti-dilution levies. These are often 
used in the ordinary course of business and in our view will not much value from a financial 
stability perspective. 
 
 
 
 
 
• Reporting and supervision 
 
We believe the current supervisory framework as it relates to AIFMs/AIFs is sufficiently robust. 
In this sense, we do not believe there is any requirement to grant ESMA with direct supervisory 
powers over AIFMs/AIFs, particularly as this was not raised as an issue in the European 
Commission’s Report on AIFMD from June 2020. In our view, national authorities have 
experience and expertise in supervising these entities and we don’t see a case to warrant 
such a fundamental change. 
 
Finally, we do n ot support a supervisory reporting framework being introduced for UCITS, 
considering the prescriptive rules around investment as well as leverage limits in place. Also, 
the risks posed by AIFs are not comparable.

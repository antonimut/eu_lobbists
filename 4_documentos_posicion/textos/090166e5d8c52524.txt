The Association Française de la Gestion financière (AFG) represents and promotes 
the interests of third-party portfolio management professionals. It brings together all asset 
management players from the discretionary and collective portfolio management 
segments. These companies manage at end 2019 more than €4,000 billions in assets, 
i.e. a quarter of continental Europe’s assets under management. 
  
The AFG’s remit: 
 Representing the business, financial and corporate interests of members, the 
entities that they manage (collective investment schemes) and their customers. 
As a talking partner of the public authorities  of France and the European Union, 
the AFG makes an active contribution to new regulations, 
 Informing and supporting its members; the AFG provides members with support 
on legal, tax, accounting and technical matters, 
 Leading debate and discussion within the industry  on rules of conduct, the 
protection and economic role of investment, corporate governance, investor 
representation, performance measurement, changes in management techniques, 
research, training, etc. 
 Promoting the French asset management industry to investors, issuers, politicians 
and the media in France and abroad. The AFG represents the French industry –  
a world leader – in European and international bodies. AFG is of course an active 
member of the European Fund and Asset Management Association (EFAMA), of 
PensionsEurope and of the International Investment Funds Association (IIFA).

I. Executive Summary 
 
 
The UCITS and AIFMD regimes are among the EU’s greatest successes in financial 
services regulation. As such, these two pillars of EU regulation for investment funds and 
their asset management companies will be crucial to the development of the Capital Markets 
Union (CMU) and the post Covid- 19 economic recovery in the EU which we, as an industry, 
are ready to support .  
 
AIFMD gives a current satisfactory framework, both secure and flexible, which makes it 
possible to address both: 
- the institutional market, particularly  via UE passport;  
- the French retail market , excluding UCITS  funds, with a range of nationally regulated 
funds covering all asset classes ( French FIVG, real estate  funds, private equity  
funds, etc.) 
 
Based on the feedback from its members, AFG therefore defends the stability of the AIFMD, 
with minimum modifications . 
 
I. I AFG recommends 3 modifications at the AIFMD level (level 1  of Lamfalussy 
architecture)  
 
1) International employee savings scheme ( question 7.1 ) 
According to its 2.3 f) article, AIFMD is not applying to employee savings scheme s and 
employee participation plan s. However collective investment funds set up for the purpose 
of serving one or more employee savings scheme s fall under the UCITS or AIFM regime. 
But since the entry into force of AIFM, given that French FCPEs, which are AIFs, it is difficult 
to get them accepted in other jurisdictions. The concept of passport is not applicable for 
them. Indeed, French FCPEs are not marketed by AIFMs : it is the  company that is at the 
origin of the implementation of an employee savings scheme operation via AIFs  made 
available by AIFMs. 
In this context it is very important to clarify the regime of distribution of an AIF set up 
exclusively for the purpose of the share purchase plan.  
Consequently, the AFG proposes to modify article 2 .3 f) of the AIFMD to expressly exclude 
from articles 31 and 43 the marketing of AIFs constituted exclusively for the purpose of 
purchasing company shares and proposed to employees of t hese companies, within the 
framework of employee savings plans .

into consideration the date of establishment of the UCITS Directive, AFG  could argue that

3) Liquidity risk management tools (LMTs) (question 56.1)  
AFG believes that level 1 should ask for full availability of LMTs in all jurisdictions . In 
addition, it would be useful to mention the gates (not fulfilling in full the redemption order) , 
as available by nature to all AIFs, in line with suspensions that are available de jure on all 
funds.  
This is to avoid stigma on this tool, useful in comforting investor fairness in exceptional 
market circumstances. Although transparency in the prospectus on the main settings of the 
gate is globally desirable, AFG believes that in general the calibration and triggering of gates 
should continue to be the responsibility of the AIFM, leaving it the possibility to adapt to 
each fund’s situation and taking into account the national regime’s specifications already in 
place.

2) Marketing (questions 21 to 23.1)  
AFG does not want specific nor additional customer categorizations in sectoral regulations 
such as AIFMD. Indeed, to maintain the consistency of the texts, MiFID II must be the 
common reference text for financial products i n terms of client categorization and 
relationship.  
Furthermore, AFG does not ask for AIF retail regulation within AIFMD: 
- AIFMD would then become a "product" directive, like UCITS, therefore with detailed 
management rules, which would risk calling into question the products we have for the 
French market today  and would be counterproductive for innovation ; 
- ELTIF existing already, A FG requests an in-depth review of ELTIF, in the current revision 
of the European regulation;  
- In order to make AIFs more accessible to retail investors, AFG believes that it is not the 
AIFMD that needs to be amended but rather the MiFID II legislation ( the provisions on 
complex and no- complex products and those on the criteria for the opt -in of a non -
professional client to the status of professional client).

Question 38. Are there any additional disclosures that AIFMs could be 
obliged to make on an interim basis to the investors other than those 
required in the annual report?  
⇒ AFG’s answer: No 
 
Annual report is sufficient to allow investors to be informed. Investors do not require more 
frequent reporting, taking into account that AIFs investors are mostly professional investors, 
and they have information through other channels. 
 
Question 40. Are the AIFMD rules on valuation appropriate? 
⇒ AFG’s answer: Yes 
 
In our opinion, the current framework does not require changes in the valuation rules. AIFMs 
invest in very different types of assets (listed and non-listed securities, liquid and illiquid, etc.) 
which obey to different valuation rules. So, it would not make sense to build a “one size fits 
all approach” on valuation between a CTA Hedge Fund and Real Estate funds for instance. 
 
External valuers might be helpful to perform the valuation of very specific assets. However, 
the use of external valuers should remain an option and not mandatory (i.e. at the discretion 
of the management company), and the responsibility for valuation should always remain in 
the management companies. As for the responsibilities of external appraisers, although there 
is no example of recourse to external appraisers outside of real estate funds, they seem to 
us to be correctly defined by current regulations.  
 
Question 41. Should the AIFMD legal framework be improved further 
given the experience with asset valuation during the recent 
pandemic?  
⇒ AFG’s answer: No 
 
Our members consider that AIFMD rules define general principles of valuation. Difficulties of 
valuing assets during the pandemic, if any, were due to a lack of transactions or 
dysfunctioning of some market segments on the underlying markets, and not to these AIFMD 
rules.

Question 50. Are the delegation rules sufficiently clear to prevent 
creation of letter-box entities in the EU?  
⇒ AFG’s answer: Yes 
 
Asset management is by its very nature a global business. To meet investor demands in terms 
of offering them access to a broader set of asset classes and exposures, as well as to best 
manage investment risks through greater portfolio diversification and offer products at lower 
costs, the continued delegation of portfolio management and related functions is of critical 
importance.  
 
In its letter addressed to Commission vice-President Valdis Dombrovskis in August 2020, 
ESMA implies that the AIFMD’s delegation rules would not be sufficient to prevent the creation 
of letter -box entities, while highlighting instances where some management companies 
delegate a substantial part of their activities, including their entire portfolio management, or 
parts of their ris k management ones, to third-party entities. As a result, ESMA questions 
whether these management companies can still effectively monitor such third-party delegates 
and consequently calls for a series of far -reaching legal clarifications in the AIFMD. Unlik e 
ESMA implies, and to the best of our knowledge, there is presently no clear evidence that the 
aforementioned AIFMD delegation requirements would deserve to be altered. 
 
One could presume that the reason why ESMA recommends introducing quantitative criter ia 
as a mean to assess whether sufficient “substance” remains with the delegating management 
company could also be to streamline the delegation assessment process conducted ex ante 
by NCAs. With quantitative criteria, NCAs would have less leeway when reviewing delegation 
proposals, which, in turn, would make it easier for ESMA to monitor NCAs’ enforcement for the 
purpose of promoting supervisory convergence. However, quantitative criteria would 
fundamentally ignore proportionality considerations and subject a very diverse range of

management companies to the same standard for reasons which to this day remain unclear. 
 
However, we invite the Commission to consider ESMA’s suggestion on delegation with the 
utmost caution.  
Effectively, the AIFMD establishes a very clear framework in this respect, with its delegated 
Regulation 2013/231 specifying under Article 82 thereof a series of key parameters against 
which a third-party could be qualified as a “letter -box” entity. Such parameters would in our 
view be exhaustive enough, striking an optimal balance between the twin objectives of investor 
protection and the preservation of a management company’s need to structure its business as 
most appropriate when serving its investors. 
 
Moreover, there is a co nsensual view across the European asset management industry that 
the changes suggested by ESMA to the existing delegation requirements – as per the AIFMD 
and accompanying delegated act (Delegated Regulation 231/2013) – are neither justified, nor 
proportionate. The delegation of portfolio management and other core functions by EU-based 
management companies to delegate entities, both within and outside the EU, lies at the core 
of a global business. This implies accessing local expertise that is uniquely concentrated in 
global financial centres to deliver adequate portfolio diversification and the best return 
outcomes for European investors, both retail and institutional. An extensive review of the 
AIFMD frameworks as suggested by ESMA may result in unintended consequences for end-
investors, notably in terms of investment opportunities and diversification. 
 
This suggestion by ESMA is even more concerning that it goes beyond portfolio management 
delegation, and also tackles outsourcing (e.g. for IT). In both cases of portfolio delegation and 
the wider spectrum of outsourcing of various activities, we have identified the very dangerous 
idea by ESMA to suggest setting a quantitative approach (i.e. thresholds in practice, from which 
it would not be possible anymore to delegate/outsource some specific activity or function). 
In any case, for European asset management companies, we do not see any need for 
changing the current AIFMD framework: legally speaking, they will remain primarily 
responsible for any delegation or outsourcing they would carry out, and as such they would 
remain “at hands” of securities regulators based in the EU too. Both from a systemic 
perspective as well as from a European investor perspective, this principle of remaining at 
hands of EU securities regulators must be the critical point of any approach of delegation and 
outsourcing.

process and governance in place or not.

Question 52. Should the AIFMD/AIFMR delegation rules, and in 
particular Article 82 of the Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 
No 231/2013, be complemented?  
⇒ AFG’s answer: No 
 
Introducing other new delegation rules in the AIFMD framework, such as extending the 
delegation regime to “supporting tasks or introducing restrictions on “seconded staff” proposed 
by ESMA, might also damage the good functioning of the investment management industry. 
 
Firstly, extending the delegation regime to “supporting tasks” when they are closely associated 
with collective portfolio management would be a dangerous idea. There are myriads of ancillary 
functions across the AM value chain that are associated with collective portfolio management: 
risk models & analytics, order management and trade execution systems, cash & position 
reconciliation systems, fund accounting & administration, performance calculation, fund 
reporting, transfer agency services, even depositary services. These are currently considered 
as “administrative functions” under Annex I AIFMD. Management companies are allowed to 
outsource these functions as they see fit while bearing the primary responsibility for selecting 
and overseeing these delegates/third party vendors. An attempt to re -locate such functions 
would cause massive disruptions to say the least, let alone for those services that are offered 
by providers forming natural oligopolies.  
 
Secondly, introducing restrictions on “seconded staff” would also deserve a clearer 
justification. Secondment is a critical element in the way management companies structure 
their operations. Although only a temporary feature of how management companies structures 
themselves, secondment provides firms with vital experience for key projects and initiatives, 
such as transitions and implementation of regulatory changes. Management companies should 
remain free to second key staff/specialist staff as they see fit. Any restriction placed on 
secondments, including the location of where a secondee works, would not be welcomed as it 
is after all a temporary appointment, related to a project or the replacement of an approved 
person with another approved person.  
 
Thirdly, introducing strict requirements on “White labelling” would also be counterproductive. 
White labelling is important for the investment management industry because it allows small 
management companies to focus on developing innovative investment products or to explore 
new markets, while relying on a delegate for administrative tasks, regulatory compliance, and 
risk management. Allegedly, the need to regulate “white-labelling” practices stem from the fact 
there is a risk of a potential conflict of interest between the delegating firm and the delegate 
when the former is a too important client for the delegate.

Question 100. Should the sanctioning regime under the  AIFMD be 
changed? 
⇒ AFG’s answer: No 
 
The point is not to change the sanctioning regime, but to ensure that NCAs enforce the rules: 
since ESMA report issued in November 2020 concerning "penalties and measures imposed 
under the AIFMD Directive in 2018- 2019", we now know that 11 of EEA NCAs have not 
taken any sanction on their territory, raising both an issue of investor protection and market 
integrity over the Single Market and also an issue of level playing field regarding 
competitiveness of AIF managers over the Single Market too. 
 
Question 101. Should the UCITS and AIFM regulatory frameworks be 
merged into a single EU rulebook?:  
⇒ AFG’s answer: No 
 
Our members do not see any need for that – we can perfectly work with the current 
framework. These products are not the same, don’t have the same distribution process and 
are mainly dedicated to strategies and clients that are not the same.

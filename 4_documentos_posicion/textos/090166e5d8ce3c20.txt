Feedback from LERU on ‘The European Health Data 
Space’   
 
First of all, we are very enthusiastic about this proposal. We think it provides a very ambitious framework 
that will definitely help the health sector to move forward in digitalization. We very much approve of the 
federated approach. Furthermore, it pushes all member states to stop thinking and start acting. It is highly 
relevant, and has a high potential to facilitate research and healthcare across the EU.  
 
Here are a few comments ranging from conceptual issues to more concrete points most of which refer to 
phrasing or nuance.  
 
1. Three points on the topic of citizens having control over their own personal health data. This is a 
very popular thing to say, yet it comes at a price. With guardianship comes responsibility.  
 
a)    The EU did a great job with the GDPR securing privacy and currently hospitals act 
as gatekeepers for most EHR data. a more precise definition would be helpful of which data 
types of a sensitive nature with respect to GDPR are to be included in the data space. Yet if 
data will be primarily guarded by citizens themselves, large data corporations will start 
offering money or convenience in return for data provision and citizens will probably just 
accept all terms and conditions. More specifically, more vulnerable citizens will do so, 
which does not reduce inequalities at all (page 1). Such a large step should be accompanied 
by education to empower citizens against data corporations.  
 
b)    The question is that if patients should have full control over their own personal health data, 
patients sometimes may not know what data is relevant for the doctor (or AI) to have, and 
feel e.g. data on psychiatric diseases, HIV status, etc. are too 'private' even for their 
healthcare professional. If patients decide not to share all data, this can lead to suboptimal 
care, or even harm. Again, adequate information and education will diminish this threat.  
 
c) The promise of personalized medicine only holds true if AI can involve (virtually)  all 
citizens. Research has shown that some people (significantly more women, more low SES 
citizens, more migrants) are less willing to share their data. EU should be aware of this 
chance of bias, with subsequent lack of applicability of the findings, and specifically target 
education programmes to these populations. 
 
2. Who benefits the most? Opening up large sets of health care data to the research community will 
lead to data corps and startups making AI tools for which e.g. licenses have to be bought 'back' by 
hospitals and other health care providers. Worst case scenario is that the benefit of AI will not be 
leveraged for citizens, but mainly benefit tech companies, while AI will not lead to a more 
sustainable healthcare system.  Moreover, large wealthy hospitals and wealthy member states will 
profit more as they are able to buy more sophisticated tooling, thereby not decreasing, or even

increasing, inequality (page 1). Business cases for AI tooling are not always obvious. EU should be 
aware of these threats and facilitate collaborative efforts that address this issue. 
 
3. Data availability. We are very happy EU expresses its desire to push for EHR manufacturers to 
facilitate data extraction and data sharing. Local initiatives on this topic are far too lenient. Yet, 
routine care data is incredibly messy. Data availability of routine care data for research, policy 
makers etc. does not immediately solve all problems (e.g. using AI). Even seemingly structured data 
can harbour biases. Some solutions to this problem can probably be found in standardization (e.g. 
FHIR), yet this is mainly interesting for data sharing and connected care since all standardization 
comes at a cost of information loss. EU should state they are aware of the complexity of reusing 
routine care data (e.g. using AI). 
 
4. Medical devices. We are happy to see EU wants to facilitate sharing of medical devices (including AI 
algorithms) across borders. Yet, there are still major implications of the Medical Device Regulations 
that severely hamper the implementation of AI algorithms, especially self-learning ones. If EU really 
strives for AI implementation to facilitate learning healthcare systems, implementation of self-
learning AI (carefully guided) should be made easier.  
 
5. Algorithms. The document seems to imply all algorithms need to be transparent or white box. We 
strongly suggest not to fully impede the implementation of black box algorithms as we may still 
benefit from them. 
 
6. Minor detail on the environmental paragraph. If climate change is EU's priority, one cannot simply 
state 'digital health will not decrease our footprint', but EU needs to facilitate research into and 
maybe even prioritize more sustainable algorithms (e.g. less computationally demanding). 
 
7. The EU cannot force this legislation without encouraging societal discussions in all member states 
on all topics above. Careful implementation of a few easy-to-understand algorithms should 
facilitate uptake of more complex ones and familiarize people with digital health. Algorithms are 
still a touchy subject, if something goes wrong this will harm the implementation of all algorithms 
to come. We believe the document is now a bit too optimistic and may benefit from statements 
(not warnings!) that point towards a careful approach.  
 
8. Lastly, it is important to remember to include means to facilitate exchange within the data space 
also of nucleic acid sequencing data from patients, a type of data rapidly growing in volume as well 
as in importance while it poses special ethical and legal problems regarding personal integrity.

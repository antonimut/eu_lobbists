REVISION ON THE DIRECTIVE ON INTELLIGENT TRANSPORT SYSTEMS (ITS) 
CONTRIBUTION OF EU TRAVEL TECH TO THE PUBLIC CONSULTATION  
 
As part of its new Sustainable and Smart Mobility Strategy adopted on December 9, 
2020, the European Commission will revise the Directive on Intelligent Transport Systems 
(“ITS Directive”) including a multimodal ticketing initiative1. In this document, eu travel 
tech is providing its input as a complement to its replies to the public consultation 
launched by the Commission as part of the impact assessment. In this document, we 
will focus specifically on the facilitation of seamless multimodal travel through data-
sharing2 and more generally the regulation of the relationship between transport 
services suppliers and mobility platforms. 
 
Eu travel tech members3 are providing the technology that is helping to power Europe’s 
travel sector, adding value for travel services suppliers in Europe but also for European 
consumers. Thanks to their position at the centre of the travel and tourism sector, they 
are key players to achieve sustainable, efficient, seamless and smart mobility across 
the transport modes.  
 
The COVID-19 pandemic has had an unprecedented impact on the European Transport 
sector. This new reality needs to be fully taken into account by the Commission when 
designing the revised ITS Directive. Indeed, this strategy shall be an opportunity for the 
European Commission to repair the short-term damage but also to address the long-
term challenges faced by the sector by building a more competitive, more resilient 
travel industry. To this aim, it shall envisage bold steps and reshape the existing 
regulatory framework, including the ITS Directive, in order to build a “fully integrated 
and seamless multimodal mobility system”4, leveraging on digital technologies to 
enable all travelers to opt whenever possible for the most efficient and most 
sustainable travel option.  
 
The European transport system is unique in the world in the variety of transport mode 
it offers to  travellers. Most European cities offer well-developed urban public transport 
systems which are increasingly complemented with a wide array of micro-mobility 
schemes (bike-sharing, e-scooters, to name a few). The rail transport network connects 
the whole continent, with the European Union pushing for more than 20 years for the

The EU should therefore be leading multimodal offerings globally: its transport system 
provides unique possibilities for the combination of all modes of transport accessible 
through online one-stop-shops, making travel more efficient but also more 
sustainable, allowing travellers to adopt the less polluting modes of transport 
whenever possible.  
However, the European transport sector accounts for a quarter of CO2 emissions in 
Europe. And while travel and interconnectivity between European countries and 
regions remain pivotal for the social and economic success of Europe, transport 
emissions continue to grow. The key objective of the new Smart and Sustainable 
Mobility Strategy is to reduce the transport sector CO2 emissions by 90% by 2050, while 
providing more affordable, accessible, healthier and cleaner transport alternatives to 
European citizens.  
Reconciling the need for seamless mobility with Europe’s decarbonisation objectives 
calls for a rethink of European mobility. Intelligent Transport Systems are key in this 
regard to facilitate multimodal passenger transport, hence facilitating the shift 
towards low-emission transport modes by attracting passengers to more sustainable 
alternatives.  
In 2016, the total multimodal market was estimated at approximately 65.7 million 
passengers of the total 10.6 billion passengers, representing 0,6% of travels. And only 5% 
of these 65.7 million passengers used a single contract for their travel6. In order to shift 
the dial towards a fully integrated, sustainable and multimodal travel sector, we need 
to create favourable conditions for users. Developing an EU-wide multimodal ticketing 
and payments system in Europe is a precursor for increased multimodal uptake.  
Europe has the technology for this. GDSs, OTAs and other content aggregators have 
been offering multimodal solutions for many years. Within the ITS Directive work 
programme, the European Commission has also worked with a consortium of 
companies on a number of projects7 which have repeatedly proven that Europe has the 
technological infrastructure to overcome fragmentation.

However, to truly unleash the potential of multimodality, access and the reuse of data 
(including fares & service information) from all travel services providers must be 
ensured. Pooling data from all transport modes into a single interface and ensuring 
this information is transactable is the only solution to overcome what has, up to now, 
been a fragmented transport ecosystem, limiting multimodal mobility of passengers. 
As rightly pointed by the Commission, « planning and purchasing tickets for 
multimodal journeys is cumbersome, as a conducive framework for EU-wide, 
integrated, multimodal information, ticketing and payment services is lacking”8. The 
revision of the ITS Directive shall be the vehicle for the adoption of such a conducive 
framework, addressing the remaining issues related to the availability, sharing and 
reuse of data, including mandatory and non-discriminatory provision of transport 
data to mobility platforms.  
 
THE DEVELOPMENT OF AN INTEGRATED MULTIMODAL TICKETING SYSTEM IS ALREADY PART OF THE ITS 
DIRECTIVE WORK PROGRAMME. 
The Delegated Regulation 2017/1926 on the provision of EU-wide multimodal travel 
information services already provided for the setting up by all Member States of 
national access points for ITS users to static travel and traffic data (schedules) for all 
modes of transport. Such an obligation is partially applied since December 1, 2019.  
This is a very positive first step and its results will still need to be evaluated. However, 
this Regulation has failed to address a key precondition to multimodality: access to all 
travel data from all transport modes. By not imposing obligations on travel suppliers to 
share all data including dynamic data (fares, real-time information) or static historical 
data, whatever the mode, ticketing and payments systems will remain fragmented.  
ENSURING NON-DISCRIMINATORY ACCESS TO AND REUSE OF TRANSPORT DATA, STATIC AND DYNAMIC, 
FOR ALL DISTRIBUTION CHANNELS SHOULD BE THE CORNERSTONE OF A SEAMLESS MULTIMODAL TRANSPORT 
SYSTEM AND SHALL BE SET AS A CLEAR OBJECTIVE BY THE REVISED ITS DIRECTIVE.  
Indirect distribution platforms are uniquely positioned to provide travellers with a 
seamless intermodal mobility experience across Member States. As stressed by the 
Florence School of Regulation (FSR)9, they “can build a network of networks, a 
frictionless coordination of fragmented services across Member States, empowering 
citizens and companies to use transport services across the continent”10. But as 
concluded jointly with the European Commission after a workshop on “EU-Wide 
Multimodal Ticketing and Payment Systems”: “for digitalisation to improve intermodal

However, what is currently seen across Europe is a strong reluctance from transport 
operators to share their data or to allow its reuse with other industry players. As rightly 
identified by the FSR, it “often alludes to the strategies of players hoping to protect their 
market powers”12:   
- In the air travel market, dominant airlines are adopting anticompetitive 
practices to limit transparency on the market (GDS surcharges, withdrawal of 
content, marketing restrictions)13. 
- In the rail travel market, legacy players are also discriminating between their 
own direct distribution channels and indirect distribution channels, preventing 
the latter from providing the same quality of services to their customers (see 
below) or worse slowing their innovations to the detriment of customers 
(example: obstructing automated delay compensation).  
The situation is also a concern for consumers who are currently being deprived of 
access to a wide array of travel options which could allow them to choose based on 
their needs and wishes, such as the most sustainable option. In a recent report14, the 
European consumers’ association, BEUC, calls for “Transport operators [to] better 
cooperate through an obligation to share data about their tariffs and real time 
timetables with third parties for the purpose of providing innovative services to 
consumers (e.g. booking platforms and comparison tools)”. 
Similarly, environmental NGOs, such as Transport & Environment, have clearly identified 
the potential of such facilitated multimodal travel to support a modal shift to more 
sustainable transport modes such as rail: “The EU should create the legal foundations 
for an integrated EU-wide ticketing and payment system[…] To this end, the EU needs to 
oblige transport operators to share static and dynamic data, including fare data.”15 
To achieve a significant upscaling of multimodal travel in Europe, access to and reuse 
of data cannot rely merely on the conclusion of voluntary commercial agreements 
between travel services suppliers and distributors for the combination and distribution 
of their products. Such agreements will always be limited by the geographical and 
modal scope of action of its parties but also by the natural reluctance of dominant

As players of indirect travel distribution, eu travel tech members have the expertise and

SHARED TRANSPORT DATA MUST BE TRANSACTABLE: WITHOUT BOOKABLE DATA, THE MULTIMODAL SYSTEM 
WILL NOT BE ABLE TO RESPOND TO CUSTOMER OR BUSINESS NEEDS  
With mandated data sharing and data reuse obligations, distribution platforms and 
intermediaries can make multimodality a reality: they can efficiently coordinate 
different transport modes, have access to available information in real time to better 
respond to consumer needs, and integrate vast amounts of data spanning across 
transport modes.  
However, imposing data sharing and data reuse obligations is not enough: the 
Commission must ensure such data is transactable. It is crucial that the Commission 
creates favourable business conditions for distribution platforms and business users 
by ensuring that the data is bookable: without bookable data, there is no business case 
for a multimodal distribution model and without a clear business incentive, platforms 
will simply not invest in making this a reality.  
This means that the data shared must allow for it to be ticketed and for an EU wide 
integrated payment system to be established.  
 
ACCESS TO TRANSACTABLE TRANSPORT DATA SHALL BE MADE MANDATORY AT EU LEVEL BY THE EUROPEAN 
COMMISSION 
The need for an overarching EU framework is clearly established: it is at EU level that a 
regulation needs to be adopted, as most long-distance travel is cross-border. 
Furthermore, recent legislations adopted in Finland16 or in France17, while going in the 
right direction regarding access to data, also show that a lack of EU-wide regulation 
will only lead to more fragmentation and therefore hinder EU-wide multimodal 
ticketing.  
The ITS Directive and ensuing Commission Regulations have been unable to prevent 
such fragmentation as they have been implemented differently in neighbouring 
Member States, eg with France requiring the publication of dynamic data while

THE MANDATORY USE OF STANDARDISED DATA FORMAT AT EU-LEVEL IS NOT THE RIGHT INSTRUMENT TO 
TACKLE THE CURRENT FRAGMENTATION OF STANDARDS ACROSS MODES OF TRANSPORT 
eu travel tech does not consider useful to set mandatory requirements for 
interoperability of ticketing-related APIs as a way to facilitate multimodal ticketing. It 
doesn’t mean that eventual interoperability of ticketing systems through APIs shall not 
be the objective. But the imposition of standard interoperability requirements is not the 
most efficient way to progress towards such an objective.  
 
Although a universal standard developed with the buy-in of all Transport Services 
Providers (TSPs)21 would obviously be the simplest solution in an ideal world, it would 
imply challenges which are likely to be insurmountable: it would not only be incredibly 
slow to develop but incredibly costly to implement across the industry – and may be 
prohibitive to many TSPs. Additionally, the governance for evolution of the standard 
would require complex organizational structures and processes for representation at 
global level to adapt to the evolution of new business needs (amongst any sector).  
 
The real challenge is to find the most cost-efficient way to get interoperability across 
multiple transport operators, as required for intermodal trips. Approaches such as 
semantic interoperability, as explored in Shift2Rail, stand the best chance of reducing 
the overall cost of interoperability across the industry. In parallel, existing 
standardisation efforts at industry level shall be strongly supported and encouraged 
by the Commission. For example, it is absolutely key for rail to see the FSM initiative fully 
implemented by all railway undertakings in 2021.

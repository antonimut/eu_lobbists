Johnson & Johnson Policy Position  
on Enabling an EU Healthcare Data Ecosystem 
The European healthcare data landscape 
Digital innovation is key to addressing  many of the major challenges the EU’s health care systems are facing 
today.  Data-fuelled digital technologies are creating opportunities across the healthcare pathway: from wellness 
and disease prevention to accelerated treatment discovery  and enhanced  clinical research, better and faster 
diagnoses and access to personalised treatments for patients. Insights derived from data also have the potential 
to make our healthcare systems more sustainable, by supporting the daily life of patients  and the work of 
healthcare professionals  and management. Importantly, it empowers patients and caregivers, and enables 
improved care pathways and high-quality products and services , creating a patient -centred healthcare system 
based on value and equity. In addition, d ata can help in effectively managing the healthcare working capital, 
improving the value and efficiency of care delivery models. We have already seen the tremendous potential of 
data innovation in real life applications such as precision medicine. 
However, in Europe there are still several challenges preventing healthcare stakeholders from being able to reap 
the benefits of a fully functioning EU healthcare data ecosystem. The lack of a unified data governance model 
along with fragmented infrastructure, barriers to access ing and sharing data, and the lack of interoperability 
across and within national health care systems are impeding the transformation of one of Europe’s most critical 
sectors and limiting the potential of innovative and sustainable patient care.  
Our policy position on enabling a sustainable EU healthcare data ecosystem 
At Johnson & Johnson, we believe that data have the potential to foster a new era for the healthcare sector and 
help us live healthier and longer lives.  Data are at the heart of what we do, from understanding the burden of 
disease and unmet need , to delivering new treatments and quality healthcare services to patients  and 
supporting value-based and sustainable healthcare systems. Data also support the quality and innovation of our 
consumer products, based on continuous  citizen engagement and feed back. We believe that healthcare 
stakeholders and  policymakers must  work togethe r to ensure  data are used for research and innovation in 
healthcare, while ensuring appropriate data protection for individuals.  
In order to realise the full benefits of digitalised healthcare, Europe should create  a connected, interoperable 
and sustainable European healthcare data ecosystem which benefits from individual, private sector and public 
healthcare data.  This will require the development of a holistic healthcare data model built on five key pillars:  
1. A HUMAN-CENTRIC DATA ECOSYSTEM
Trust remains the bedrock upon which the data economy is built. Patients, consumers, healthcare professional 
stakeholders and society as a whole will only confidently unleash the potential of data if there is a clear and 
comprehensive framework of trust , security, and transparency in place, keeping citizens  at the forefront . The 
EU, with its high standards for the protection of human rights, ethical principles, privacy , data security and 
protection, is already leading the way globally . But more needs to be done if we want to fully reap the benefits  
that healthcare data can bring to society. We advocate for:  
• The development of ethical principles in the treatment of personal healthcare data: Building on the EU’s
ethics guidelines for Artificial Intelligence ( AI)1, Johnson & J ohnson urges the Commission to work with
member states and all relevant key stakeholders, including industry, academia, health institutions and
patients to develop similar approaches for health care data generation, use, re -use, and curation that
address security, transparency and privacy. The ethical principles should recognise citizens remain in control
over their personal data, adopting a human-centric approach. This work should fully leverage existing efforts
and guidance developed via relevant IMI initiatives including the European Medical Information Framework
(EMIF) and GetReal, as well as the work of the Institute for Innovation Through Health Data (i-HD).

• The support of Privacy by Design in all data-driven innovation and consistency across the EU: The General
Data Protection Regula tion (GDPR) has established  the foundations of a trust framework for patients,
consumers and other stakeholders to share data, including healthcare data. However, hurdles remain with
regards to its interpretation and implementation, which still differs from country to country . In order to
ensure the full protection of European citizens and enable a  level playing field, we call for a consistent
interpretation of GDPR across member states and the development of guidelines to offer practical support
for compliance in the healthcare and consumer sectors. Close collaboration between regulatory authorities,
especially Data Protection Agencies, the European Data Protection Board (EDPB)  and the European
Medicines Agency (EMA) , is needed to facilitate a harmoni sed interpretation of the research exemption
(Art.9, 2, J GDPR), the use of Real-World Data ( RWD) and especially the re -use of data for secondary
purposes.
• The swift implementation of a robust  EU cybersecurity framework for data, particularly for  healthcare
data: The EU should ensure consistent implementation  of the Network Information Directive  (NIS),
especially with regards to mandatory security obligations for critical infrastructure, network and
information system s in the healthcare sector (e.g. to protect ransomware attacks to hospitals). The EU
should also  advocate for global cooperation on cybersecurity standards and explore the benefits that
innovation can bring to the healthcare sector in granting better security and control of data.
2. DATA THAT EMPOWERS DECISION-MAKING
Data should be collected and used to allow citizens to manage their health and take more evidence-driven 
decisions, while giving healthcare professionals and other stakeholders the ability to improve and personalise 
diagnosis, treatment, services and products. The digital transformation of our society and advances in healthcare 
technology are providing larger and better-quality data sets. However, much of the data remain locked in siloes, 
preventing valuable information from being captured, analysed and applied to care value delivery, 
improvements and decisions. We call on the EU to:  
• Support Member States in enabling all citizens in Europe to have access to their full Electronic Health
Records (including patient-reported outcomes) and be able to easily share their healthcare information
with healthcare professional stakeholders. This will enable the provision of higher quality and safer care
for patients while improving financial efficiencies , support better policy decision -making and ensu re
the evaluation of treatment effectiveness based on real clinical practice.
• Improve access to large volumes of rich, quality data by encouraging data holders to adopt
standardised data collection, implement quality standards and processes to increase data quality
capture and enable data linkage from diverse sources.
• Establish  clear and user-friendly guidelines to facilitate data generation, access to qualitative data sets
that are either publicly owned and/or are voluntarily shared by the private sector. We also believe that
private individuals who wish to donate their data for good should be incentivised to do  so through
concrete initiatives. Control over data should remain with the patient s/citizens themselves and they
should be empowered to access and manage their own healthcare data.
• Adopt a common open source data framework that would allow all players to participate and reduce
dependency on lock-in frameworks from new entrants in the health ecosystem.

Some of t he major  challenges to reaping the potential of healthcare data in Europe  lie in the lack of  
interoperability of the systems where data are collected and held, as well as on the lack of suitable governance 
frameworks for data sharing. The EU should establish a clear and coherent legal framework to encourage access 
to and sharing of healthcare data while protecting privacy of personal data, including: 
• Support data linkage via interoperability:

o Encourage the adoption of standards for healthcare data and open exchange formats for
Electronic Health Records3 (EHR): A critical step to the development of a European Healthcare
Data Ecosystem is to ensure data systems are interoperable, and citizens have control of their
personal data at any time. The EU should promote and enforce the adoption of existing open
technical and data (semantic) standards that are able to accommodate interoperability of all
health-related data such as clinical data , including  high dimensional (e.g. omics) data , or
patient generated healthcare data.
o Extend the European  Health Digital Service Infrastructure (eHDSI)  to facilitate health data
exchange beyond ePrescriptions and patient summaries – to include full EHR.
o Develop guidelines for open healthcare data exchange formats beyond EHRs, including
research, clinical data, longitud inal data (full historic medical records), as well as data
generated by wearable and implanted devices or apps.
o Scale up existing Innovative Medicines Initiative (IMI) projects focused on secondary use of
data, such as EHDEN4 and stimulate the creation of national (member state) health care data
institutes that can act as nodes, like the BBMRI -ERIC5 project on biobanking research
infrastructure linked at EU level via Elixir.
• The uptake of federated data models via regulatory sandboxes6 to facilitate interoperability and
connectivity while respecting GDPR requirements. Federated models have the potential to unlock the
barriers to accessing healthcare data and, in turn, facilitate learning healthcare systems7, while at the same
time ensuring the highest level of protection of personal data and commercial intellectual property.
Through a federated model, the different sources of healthcare data act as nodes in a network. Importantly
the data remain on site, unaltered and uncompro mised. It is only the final output of the data analysis that
is shared within the framework under secure conditions which ensure legal compliance.  For example, the
output can be utilised to inform research, clinical treatment, hospital planning and paymen t models, and
influence the effectiveness of the overall healthcare demand and supply value chain . EU citizens and
patients should be consented and in the centre of such a network and remain empowered  throughout, so
no provider can prevent them from managing or accessing their data.
Johnson & Johnson is leading the way in the development of federated models by leading on EU -wide 
projects such as HONEUR and IMI EHDEN, utilising the OMOP common data model/OHDSI framework , 
which make use of Real -World Data (RW D). Future funding schemes (Horizon Europe, Digital Europe, 
Connecting Europe Facility) could also support such technologies and make them transferable across Europe 
and healthcare settings. 
• Invest in enabling digital infrastructure: Critical infrastructures throughout the healthcare value chain
need resources to meet Europe’s objective of achieving sustainable and high-quality healthcare:
o Support Member States efforts to establish  robust infrastructure to access and share their
health data while respecting citizens’ rights, sharing best practices, and building on  successful
examples from countries such as Finland (Findata) and Estonia (X-Road), and the recent Health
Data Hub initiative supported by President Macron in France.
o Promote t he development of “hospitals of the future” : Hospitals could become digital
innovation hubs  making use of new technologies (such as AI) to improve  the value and
standards of care across the board. Future funding schemes (e.g. Digital Europe,  Connecting
Europe Facility) should  build on Horizon 2020’s current efforts to make “hospitals of the
future” a reality.

4. VALUE THROUGH DATA
Data collected in real  life settings (‘Real -World Data’ or ‘RWD’) can help drive new understandings of value , 
supporting healthcare decision-makers faced with the challenge of assessing different forms of technology  and 
treatments in the context of fixed healthcare budgets . This can include a range of routinely collected data 
sources from EHR, hospital databases, electronic registries and insurance claims to wearables, apps, and device-
generated data, amongst others. A connected and sustainable European healthcare data ecosystem and greater 
trust and acceptability of ‘Real -World Evidence’ (RWE) 9 in Europe will enable the shift towards value -based 
healthcare models and systems, ensure that Europe remains relevant for clinical research (e .g. for platform 
trials and readiness cohorts) , that more patients can join targeted treatment trials and to advance the use of 
outcomes-based payment models – where the cost of care is linked to the outcomes it delivers. This in turn will 
help reduce healthcare inefficiencies to help meet the challenges of today’s healthcare systems and ensure 
sustainable future. We call for: 
• Improving trust and acceptability of RWE for decision-making and reimbursement:
o The EU should communicate guidelines and best practices to ensure that the quality and
integrity of RWD is maintained throughout the lifecycle.
o The EU should provide guidance (e.g. building on the Heads of Medicines Agencies – European
Medicines Agency Joint Task Force on Big Data summary report) and scale up current projects
to support the use of RWE and connected databases to inform decision making . This should
also build on work initiated by the EMA to explore applying a common data model in Europe
to support regulatory decision-making10.
o The EU should support, through the European Semester, a regular and structured comparison
of EU Member States ’ healthcare systems to support effective reforms and track progress
towards value-based healthcare. This could build on the ongoing OECD ’s PaRIS11 initiative in
this field , as well as initiatives by the International Consortium for Health Outcomes
Measurement (ICHOM).
o The EU should leverage Research & Innovation funding (Horizon Europe/future Health Public-
Private Partnership) to further enable and guide the use of enriched, connected RWD to
establish an outcomes-based healthcare data ecosystem in Europe.
5. CREATING A DATA CULTURE
Promoting the uptake of a healthcare data ecosystem depends on whether policymakers, payers, practitioners, 
patients and citizens are well equipped to understand and harness  data. As with all technological innovation, 
acceptance is key. Healthcare stakeholders and policymakers ( at a national and EU level) should take a holistic 
approach to (digital health/data) literacy – thereby creating and motivating a data culture that encompasses 
collaboration and partnership amongst healthcare practitioners, payers, patients and citizens . We call upon

• Ensure every citizen has access to digital  literacy and  skills training: We welcome the incoming
European Commission’s aims to update the EU’s Digital Education Action Plan, which we believe should
focus on ensuring access to basic digital skills training is available for all citizens across the European
Union, no matter their physical location, set of conditions  or age group. Digital literacy is critical to
ensure citizens and patients are empowered to manage their own data and capable of taking informed
decisions. This should extend not only to the use of digital /digital health technologies but also to the
ethics, governance and benefits of  using healthcare data to benefit all citizens , leveraging key
stakeholder-driven initiatives such as Data Saves Lives. We therefore call on the European Commission
to increase available funds for digital skills programmes within Erasmus+ and Digital Europe , and to
work together with Member States to scale up their national programmes and incorporate digital
literacy in school curricula.
• Encourage training and retraining of the healthcare workforce: The EU’s Digital Education Action Plan
should also focus on the healthcare workforce, to make sure no one is left behi nd and that healthcare
practitioners are able to make use of innovative technologies that benefit healthcare . The EU should
ensure sufficient funding under Horizon Europe is dedicated to supporting training, increase funds for
Erasmus+ professional exchang es, and encourage the implementation of national digital skills
programmes for the healthcare workforce.
• Ensure training on digital skills for relevant Member State policy makers and stakeholders: We call
on the EU to further build the digital and innovation literacy of local policy -makers and relevant
stakeholders for them to become real ambassadors and ethical advocates  of the digitalisation of
healthcare in their communities and all circles of influence.
We believe the above actions will help foster the creation  of a patient - and consumer -centric European 
healthcare data ecosystem based on the full respect of the principles of ethics, privacy and transparency and 
urge all stakeholders to actively and jointly work towards that goal s that will enable the shift towards value -
based healthcare models and systems.

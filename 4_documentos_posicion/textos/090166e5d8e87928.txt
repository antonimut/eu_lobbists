ACT | The App Association Comments on the European Commission’s Roadmap for “A European Health Data Space” I/ Introduction and statement of interest ACT | The App Association submits comments to the European Commission’s (EC) Directorate-General for Health and Food Safety (DG SANTE) in response to its roadmap consultation “A European Health Data Space”. The App Association appreciates DG SANTE’s efforts to promote access to health data for research and innovation and to maximise the potential of digital health to provide high-quality healthcare.  The App Association’s Connected Health Initiative (CHI) is the leading policy and legal advocacy effort on connected health driven by a consensus of stakeholders from across the digital health ecosystem, including European small businesses. CHI aims to create an environment in which Europeans can realise the full potential of connected health technologies to improve health outcomes and reduce costs. CHI members are providers, developers, and users of innovative health technologies that promote patient care and engagement across a wide range of use cases. How our members can use and share health data affects how their products function and how patients engage with those products and services. Our members handle and work with health data every day, so they are directly affected by the governance of common European data spaces, especially in the health sector. We are active advocates before the EC and European Union (EU) Member States to advance responsible pro-digital health policies and laws. Our expertise covers areas including reimbursement and payment, privacy and security, effectiveness and quality assurance, regulation of advanced medical devices, health data interoperability, and the rising role of artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare. We appreciate that the Commission wants to address insufficient health data exchanges, improve access to health data, and enable interoperability between electronic health records. Small and medium enterprises (SMEs) will significantly benefit from the Commission resolving these issues.  II/ The proposed legislative framework for the governance of common European data spaces a. Objectives and Problem Identification  CHI supports the European Commission’s general objectives to ensure access, sharing, and optimal use of health data for healthcare, research, innovation, policymaking, and regulatory activities; to foster a genuine single market in digital health; and to enhance the development, deployment, and application of trustworthy digital health products and services. Despite efforts made thus far, huge potential remains for the European health data sector. Making more health data available and facilitating its exchange are important steps in creating a genuine single market in digital health. A single health data market that protects privacy rights puts more data and control

in the hands of European care providers. It enables these innovative companies to do more research, improve customer services, and develop new and better products for consumers.  We agree with the main problems the Commission identifies in its roadmap. It is crucial to address the following to establish a common European Health Data Space: (1) the insufficient exchange of health data, (2) the lack of access and control patients have over their health data, (3) the fragmentation of digital standards and limited digital interoperability between healthcare systems, (4) the limited access to and exchange of health data for research, innovation, policymaking, and regulatory activities, (5) the insufficient free movement and provision of digital health services and products across the EU, and (6) the lack of clear rights and obligations concerning digital health products incorporating AI.   The efficacy of precision medicine, population health, clinical decision support—and AI driven tools in particular—depends in large part on the availability of massive data sets. The free flow of information and interoperability are therefore important and potentially lifesaving for patients. CHI commits to assist the Commission in advancing health data interoperability throughout the continuum of care. In CHI’s view, interference with interoperability and patient access to health information prevent timely and informed care coordination and decision-making.   Electronic health information and educational resources are critical tools that empower and engage patients in their own care. CHI strongly believes that a truly interoperable eCare system includes patient engagement facilitated by store-and-forward technologies (ranging from connected medical devices to general wellness products) with open application programming interfaces (APIs) that allow the safe and secure introduction of patient-generated health data (PGHD) into electronic health records (EHRs). Data stored in standardised and structured formats with interoperability facilitated by APIs provides analytics as well as near real-time alerting capabilities. The use of portals for data streams from multiple and diverse sources will improve the healthcare sector, helping to eliminate information silos, data blocking, and deficient patient engagement. Interoperability must not only happen between providers, but also between remote patient monitoring (RPM) products, medical devices, and EHRs.  In addition to testing and finding consensus on industry standards, CHI encourages the Commission to prioritise encouraging the voluntary implementation of industry standards to ensure interoperability between EHR systems, medical devices, and healthcare products. This practice can also gauge the interoperability of EHR products. A system demonstrating widespread interoperability will provide useable data from various sources, not just from particular EHR technology and EHR systems. For example, we urge the Commission to fully leverage industry-led efforts to establish standardised implementation such as the Argonaut project, which helps standardise the implementation of the Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) standard. CHI notes, however, that even efforts like Argonaut can focus too much on compliance-driven efforts in order to meet perceived regulatory requirements, and the Commission should put incentives in place to drive stakeholders to communicate and pass information from one party to another without regulatory compliance requirements serving as a ceiling (instead, such regulations should be a floor). Such an approach will incentivise a value-based healthcare environment—one which engages patients, reduces costs, and documents quality metrics.

 To reward better outcomes and cost-effective approaches to care, providers must be able to utilise two-way APIs to access, share, and make meaningful use of data about their patients. True interoperability involves not just the ability to access data but also the ability to use it and manipulate it for the patient’s benefit. Knowing the whole story is important for providers and payers to understand the best treatment plan or prevention measures for patients, as well as for patients who seek greater engagement in their own care. Data from previous care settings becomes more important in value-based care because the viability of the provider depends on outcomes—arriving at these outcomes is more efficient when care plans tailor to patients’ medical history, genetics, and other factors. This is especially true for providers in rural areas of the EU, where there are fewer physicians serving people who live further away from healthcare facilities. Rural providers especially need data that shows which care plans or prevention, and treatment measures are likely to work—and which don’t—for the patients they see. Healthcare providers spend about half their time doing paperwork and grappling with EHRs that create friction in their workflow. With fewer caregivers per capita and greater distances to care in rural areas, a system that traps those caregivers in endless stretches of administrative busywork is costly to patients.  We believe the Commission shares CHI’s vision of a seamless and interoperable healthcare ecosystem that leverages the power of PGHD, and we strongly encourage the Commission’s interoperability efforts to prioritise data generated by patients outside of the traditional care setting. Both providers and patients have now come to expect access to seamless and secure patient data across the care continuum, where individuals are able to seamlessly integrate and compile longitudinal electronic health information across online tools, mobile platforms and devices to participate in shared decision-making with their care, support, and service terms. CHI also notes its support for Commission efforts to incent software developers and patients to make use of insurance claims data, and we urge for more claims data being made available and usable via APIs to developers, and coverage for tools that enable patients to use, analyse, and share their claims data. A diversity of APIs is emerging to assist in bringing PGHD into the continuum of care, but we stress that not all of these are necessarily well integrated with EHRs (e.g., some EHR vendors continue to only enable “read only” access, allowing for data to only flow out of the EHR rather than both in and out). Additionally, we are aware that some EHR vendors have not implemented a common approach to API development and lack a consistent implementation of API technical standards. CHI reiterates our request that the Commission utilise standards and incentives to facilitate seamless interoperability.  CHI also appreciates that the Commission explicitly mentions that the increased exchange of health data should not come at the expense of privacy rights. For our members and other SMEs, transparency and a reasonable level of control over data use and collection is essential to building the high level of consumer trust that helps them succeed. For this reason, strong privacy is crucial to maintain consumer trust, confidentiality, access, and integrity of data.   Overall, a harmonised legislative framework for a European health data space would create a level playing field for all actors in the digital health ecosystem. It would allow SMEs to focus on improving their products and using newly available data to innovate new ones. Such a framework,

Proposed policy options   When assessing policy options, Commission guidance and technical measures should have the flexibility and scalability necessary to benefit all stakeholders in the health data ecosystem.   To accomplish its first objective, the Commission proposes three steps. The first step is the establishment of an appropriate legal and governance framework to cover access to and exchange of health data for healthcare provision, research, policymaking, and regulatory activities. A governance mechanism for the primary and secondary use of health data could be useful. We support focusing on existing public sector bodies that hold relevant health data in member states rather than designating new national digital health bodies. The Commission could then establish an EU-wide mechanism to exchange best practices between them. An institutional response based on structural enablers could be a helpful step to create an effective governance framework. This could take the form of an EU coordination body or structure that establishes the explicit processes for health data sharing and access, interoperability, and secondary use of health data. Any revision of the scope and mandate of the voluntary eHealth Network should preserve the voluntary nature of the Network and ensure full compliance with data protection rules. To encourage individuals and businesses to make more health data available voluntarily, the idea of a customisable, common-EU consent form as put forward in the Data Governance Act could be beneficial. Further details on what this consent form would look like are necessary to determine if such a consent form encourages data altruism in a way that respects strong data protection and the responsible use of data.  The second proposed step is to lower technical barriers that hinder the use and re-use of health data, particularly barriers related to infrastructure, interoperability, and data quality. We welcome the commitment of the Commission to lowering these barriers consistent with our recommendations above, as SMEs are usually the ones most affected by them. We also support building on existing efforts like the eHealth Digital Service Infrastructure and ongoing standardisation efforts. When it comes to interoperability, all stakeholders must be involved and needs from all industry perspectives must be respected. Standardisation and interoperability are important but should not come at the expense of innovation and research. We also encourage the Commission to consider obliging the Member States to offer tangible support services to health innovators such as information resources on data quality, public registers, and practical guidance on how to access high-quality data. The App Association encourages the Commission to survey existing efforts to provide such constructs, such as Finland’s Findata,2 and to build on their experiences to expand it to health data specifically.   The third proposed step is to ensure patients and citizens have access and control over their health data. While we support the portability of health data for citizens, we caution the European Commission against mandating the development of specific tools to facilitate data access for  1 https://ecipe.org/publications/dataloc/.  2 https://www.findata.fi/en/.

individuals. Mandating the development of specific tools lacks flexibility and will likely hinder businesses from developing the most innovative tools that can resolve data access best for their specific business. Instead, the Commission could take an outcomes-focused approach and mandate facilitated data access for individuals while allowing businesses to come up with the best solution to meet that goal themselves.   To achieve the second objective, the Commission proposes removing barriers to the cross-border movement of digital health services and products, including patients’ rights to these products and services, and their interoperability with electronic health records and healthcare systems. We support this objective, but the Commission’s roadmap lacks detail on the barriers and specific steps it plans to take to remove them. Overall, establishing a single health data market is a goal we support as it puts more data and control in the hands of European care providers and citizens. It enables innovative companies to do more research, improve customer services, and develop new and better products for consumers. However, as noted above, interoperability should not come at the expense of innovation.   To accomplish the third and last objective, the Commission proposes to analyse the liability rules related to the use of data-intensive digital health services, such as those that use AI, and to complement the horizontal initiatives on AI. A consistently growing body of evidence demonstrates that connected health technologies improve patient care, reduce hospitalisations, help avoid complications, and improve patient engagement (particularly for the chronically ill). These tools, increasingly powered by AI, leverage patient-generated health data (PGHD) and include wireless health products, mobile medical devices, telehealth, and preventive services, clinical decision support, chronic care management, and cloud-based patient portals. To ensure these tools can reach their maximum potential, we believe that analysing liability rules related to digital health services would be beneficial. However, we also believe that precedent may need to evolve to address new scenarios involving AI in particular. We do not believe this is a shortcoming of liability laws, but instead, a result of rapidly evolving technology, and disputes that arise may be decided through future law and policy decisions. However, should the case law demonstrate that there is a need for revisions to liability provisions, the App Association would support such an effort to ensure the fair application of copyright to emerging use cases that involve AI.  III/ Conclusion CHI shares the ambition of the European Commission to facilitate the access to and the use and sharing of health data across the European Union. We appreciate the Commission’s intention for this legislative initiative to benefit SMEs, and we welcome the Commission’s commitment to remove the barriers that disproportionately affect small companies, which are often the most innovative ones. We are encouraged by the efforts to create a single market for European health data. If realised, such a single market would significantly contribute to achieving the EU’s goals and objectives in the digital health space more effectively.  CHI encourages the European Commission to build on existing efforts and design this legislation with SMEs in mind, relying on voluntary guidance and technical measures rather than mandatory provisions.

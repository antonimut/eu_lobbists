Introduction  
EURORDIS – Rare Diseases Europe welcomes the combined evaluation Roadmap/Inception Impact Assessment 
and the ambition set out therein to build a European Health Data Space. 
EURORDIS represents 30 million people who are living in Europe with one or more of over 6,000 identified ra r e 
diseases. A rare disease is defined as rare in Europe when it affects fewer than 1 in 2,000 people. The field of rare 
diseases is characterised by limited know ledge and expertise on each specific disease and geographically disper s e d 
patient populations. In this context, the needs and benefits of capturing, sharing, utilising and federating he a lth 
data to advance scientific research and improve clinical benefits have been well documented in recent years1. The 
creation of the European Reference Networks for rare and complex diseases (ERNs) is a remarkable infrastructur a l 
response to the imperative need to pool expertise and data from various sources across borders. 
EURORDIS shares and supports the ambition of the EHDS to make the most of the use of digital technologies as 
a means to provide high-quality healthcare as well as reducing inequalities while ensuring that citizens have control 
over their personal data. In particular, the description of the policy problems that the initiative aims to tack le is 
clear and comprehensive. Nevertheless, we believe that some specific aspects have been overlooked, notably:  
- the lack of a well-structured multi-stakeholder dialogue on health data to inform policy developments; or 
- the lessons learned from the cross-border exchange of health data between the European Reference Networ k s.   
Cross-border exchange of health data is an essential area for our community. High quality healthcare for pers ons 
living with a rare disease, as well as research on rare diseases, are greatly dependent on having a cohesiv e and 
coordinated strategy on health data at EU and national levels, as well as on well-functioning organisationa l a nd 
technical infrastructures for cross-border exchange of health data for primary and secondary uses.  
EURORDIS welcomes the Commission’s proposal to adopt different sets of measures in order to address the policy 
problems identified by combining mandatory and voluntary guidance; the proposed implementation at EU a nd 
national level as well as the funding from multiple sources. Indeed, the range and complexity of policy aspe cts a s 
well as the interdependencies of the national and EU levels in this sector call for a variety of policy interventions 
that go beyond having new EU sector specific legislation.   
Drawing on consultations with our membership and our Digital and Data Advisory Group, EURORDIS summaris e s 
below the comments and suggestions that we would ask the European Commission to take into account w he n 
defining the regulatory and non-regulatory initiatives that will form the European Health Data Space. EURORDIS 
remains fully committed to supporting these initiatives and would be delighted to take part in any furthe r 
consultations as appropriate.

1. Evaluation  
EURORDIS broadly welcomes and supports the Evaluation, in Part A. Additional aspects to be included in the 
evaluation of the existing framework for cross-border exchange of health data should include the participation of 
patients and patient organisations in the governance and decision-making structures that are currently mana ging 
the use and access of health data specifically for secondary purposes (research, regulation, policy-making etc.) to 
understand the perspective and experience of patients representatives and patient organisations  with the s e 
arrangements.  
Also, we would strongly suggest to incorporate the work of the European Reference Networks (ERNs) in the area 
of registries and cross-border exchanges of health data into the evaluation in order to draw on specialist advice, 
lessons learned as well as views of clinicians, patient representatives and patient organisations.

Access to, and exchange of, health data for scientific research and innovation, policy-making and regulatory 
activities remains very limited in Europe ( secondary use of health data). The lack of health gove rnance 
frameworks that meet  the different needs of all the stakeholders (regulators, HTA agencies, policy -makers, 
researchers, data subjects etc.) could also be hindering the exchange of health data for secondary purposes . 
Specifically, there are five core elements for responsible health data sharing for research that derive from a lar ge 
quantitative survey conducted with patients with rare diseases and family members2 and other consultations w ith 
our membership:  
1. respecting citizens’/patients preferences;  
2. access to data needs to be transparent (especially in cross-border and outside the EU settings) as there is 
often a lack of transparency in data sharing arrangements;  
3. protecting privacy and confidentiality;  
4. having citizens/patients co-defining and co-managing governance;  
5. providing feedback on the results and having incentives for researchers to share openly their research 
results, regardless of the outcome.  
Use of digital health services and products for the provision of healthcare. EURORDIS concurs with the analy s is 
whereby the lack of a coordinated EU response to liability and reimbursement  constitutes a barrier to the free 
movement of digital healthcare services, especially across the borders. Additional barriers hampering the provis ion 
of cross-border remote consultations are: the lack of European-wide clinical governance, clinical management a nd 
clinical practice standards for virtual consultations, as well as the poor evidence on the clinical effectiveness of 
virtual consultations, including a good understanding of patients’ preferences.   
Artificial intelligence. An additional element that is health sector-specific and should be addressed by the EHDS-
related initiatives is ensuring a better understanding among patients, healthcare professionals and care give r s of 
the possibilities as well as limitations and risks of AI-based systems.

Regulation ( GDPR) already regulates the access and use of health data . Nevertheless, we lack a common

respect their pr eferences, while deriving value from healt h data for healthcare, research  etc. Also, a Code of

In this context, we believe that the ERNs provide a testing environment ready to implement and assess new he a lth

Regarding the institutional architecture to drive forward all the initiatives and the ambition of the EHDS, we

 Data access policies, procedures to manage datasets (including enforcing authentication and access rights

 Building the tools to help preserve the autonomy and rights of individuals to control their data; 
 Data standards, specifications and rules for the definition, creation, storage and usage of data; 
 Advice on ethical (legal or social) matters.  
Considerations on sector-specific legislation to complement the Data Governance Act (DGA) 
EURORDIS welcomes the proposal to complement the DGA with sector-specific legislation. However, we would 
like to stress that any new legislative instruments, or amendments of existing ones, should be done in a way tha t 
ensures a truly harmonised implementation across all EU countries and leaves Member States no major leeway to 
interpret the provisions or add new exceptions. This legislation should not contribute to further fragment an alre a dy 
fragmented data protection regulatory environment.  
EURORDIS believes there is a need to complement the DGA with sector-specific legislation addressing a numbe r 
of areas:  
- One-stop-shop at national level for secondary uses of health data. Member States should designate a n 
organisation to act as a primary interface for re-users that seek to use health data. This would be a health s e ctor 
specific task, which would add to the ones already referred to in the Data Governance Act. This body could a ls o 
provide advice and support with ethics committees, as well as help re-users navigate the ecosystem. The s e 
organisations should also be connected to the EU-level institution in charge of steering the sharing of cros s- 
border data for secondary use (as suggested above). 
- Clarify the concept of “general interest”  in the framework of the DGA. EURORDIS calls for furthe r 
clarification on how this concept will be interpreted in the health sector. The term needs to be defined in a w a y 
that takes into account the needs and specificities of this sector, either by providing a sector-specific definition 
or by listing a set of criteria that a ny given activity will need to meet in order to be categorised as being of 
‘general interest’.  
- Clarify the rationale for having two different set of conditions and requirements for health data sh aring 
activities. The draft DGA should provide further clarification on the definitions of data sharing and data 
altruism, as well as the rationale for having two different legal frameworks for data intermediaries and da ta 
altruism organisations and services. Ultimately, both terms refer to the same activity, namely allowing the us e 
and re-use of one’s personal data for specific purposes. Some uses will have a commercial purpose and othe r s 
not, sometimes there might be a direct reward, other times not. Yet, this distinction does not appear to jus tify 
having different regulatory oversight, compliance monitoring mechanisms, governance arrangements a nd 
 
 
6 
 
standards for the organisations offering these data “brokering” services, at least not in the health sector .  In 
addition, the conditions for providing any type of health data sharing service for any purpose, including for the 
common good, should be the same.  
- Additional requirements for health data intermediaries and data altruism organisations. The new sector a l 
legislation for health data should require the compulsory certification of data intermediaries and data altr uis m 
organisations, rather than the notification schemes set out in the draft DGA. We consider accountability a s 
being a very important aspect of trust, which is an essential element in health data sharing. In addition, str ong 
and effective compliance mechanisms, which should be the same in all EU countries , should be in place to 
ensure transparency, avoid abuses and misuses and ensure the enforcement of the GDPR rights and principle s , 
combined with redress mechanisms in case of unlawful use of health data. Additional requirements for the s e 
organisations beyond the ones described in the DGA , will be required if patients are to trust thes e 
organisations. For example, the sector-specific legislation could require them to have specific governance 
arrangements in place to enable equal partnership of patients and patient organisations in the decision-ma k ing 
structures.  
- Prevent misalignment of incentives. Article 11 of the DGA requires data intermediaries to act in the da ta 
subjects’ best interest when facilitating the exercise of their rights, in particular by advising data subje cts on 
potential data uses and standard terms and conditions attached to such uses. The sector-specific legisla tion 
should provide effective control mechanisms to avoid misalignment of incentives between the data subje ct 
and the organisations sharing his or her health data, for any purpose and in exchange of a direct reward or not. 
This is why these organisations should be subject to high transparency standards and have specific governa nce 
arrangements to provide adequate assurance and allow the involvement of patients and patient organisations 
in their decision-making structures. Another way to manage this risk would be to require these organisations 
to have mechanisms in place in order to ensure explicit and continuing consent. 
- Neutrality Principle. The draft DGA requires the neutrality of data sharing service providers as regards the da ta 
exchanged between data holders and data users. As such, data sharing service providers act only as 
intermediaries in transactions and should not use the data exchanged for any other purpose. It is  our 
understanding that research institutions, patient organi sations or hospitals would be prevented from 
establishing themselves as data intermediaries since they could be using the shared data themselves. Yet, s uch 
entities are those that would be more trusted by rare disease patients. The results of the above-mentione d 
survey amongst people living with rare diseases and their families show ed that their level of trust in not-for-
profit stakeholders (medical doctors and other health professionals, researchers from non-profit organisations , 
patient organisations) to handle and use their health information is considerably higher than trust place d in 
“for profit” stakeholders. The sector-specific legislation should therefore clarify the application of principle in 
the healthcare sector and adapt it to this context, if necessary.  
- Common tools and processes for health data collection. EURORDIS strongly encourages the Commission to 
foresee in the sector-specific legislation the co-design common EU-wide consent forms and data collection 
processes for primary and secondary care purposes. Co-designing these common consent forms with patie nts 
and patient representatives would increase their acceptance and use. Once again, the European Reference 
Networks could be used as a testing environment as they are expected to start collaborating and working on 
this in 2021.

c) Ensuring access and control of patients and citizens over their own health data  
Enacting “data portability” is important, yet it is only a very specific dimension that refers to a specific right. In our 
view, “keeping control” over one’s health data is a much broader concept that entails providing the means a nd 
mechanisms to ensure the meaningful involvement of data subjects t hroughout the data lifecycle, as well as  
providing them with the tools to understand risks and benefits so that they can express preferences and act upon 
their data in an adaptive way . We need systems that enable data users to express differen t attitudes a nd 
preferences and allow them to access updated information on how and why the data is used. Ultimately, persons 
living with rare diseases demand the control over access and use of their health data in order to share it when the y 
want or ch oose to keep it private. Ensuring access and control of patients over their health data will only ha ppe n 
when patients and patient organisations contribute to shaping health data governance frameworks, and are pa r t of 
the decision-making structures, ultimately are eco-creators of those frameworks and structures. This applie s to 
registries, the future EU health data infrastructure and any other health database.  
Adequate resources should also be allocated to help develop , and facilitate access to, relevant educationa l 
resources to enable informed choices for patients to share or not to share their health-related data; and to enable 
their contribution in decision-making structures.  
Objective 2: Fostering a genuine single market in digital health covering digital health services and products, 
including tele-health, tele-monitoring and mobile health  
Policy Options will investigate ways to remove barriers to the cross-border movement of digital health serv ices 
and products, including data-intensive ones, as well as the rights of patients to benefit from those services and 
products, and their interoperability with electronic health records and healthcare systems.

Exercising the right to cross-border healthcare is often times difficult for patients who have to travel abroad (lack 
of information, financial  burden, administrative hurdles etc.).  Persons living with a rare disease would greatly 
benefit from having the option of accessing these services via digital means however, this aspect cannot move 
forward while all the othe r barriers linked to access ing in-person cross-border healthcare are not effectively 
addressed. EURORDIS is elaborating on these issues in a separate document that will contribute to the ongoing 
public consultation on the evaluation of the Cross-border Healthcare Directive. 
Additionally, as highlighted above, specifically for cross-border remote consultations, apart from the coordina te d 
EU response to liability and reimbursement, we would need to develop and adopt European-wide clinica l 
governance, clinical management and clinical practice standards for remote/virtual consultations to deliver high 
quality, safe care, have a clear oversight and accountability rules. Having the same standards across countries w ould 
greatly facilitate virtual cross-border consultations and provide adequate assurance for patients. Also, this w ould 
provide evidence on the effectiveness of these remote consultations, thus helping decide what type of care ca n be 
safely and effectively delivered virtually, taking into account patients’ preferences. This body of evidence ca n be 
built as we go, but is important to invest resources in this direction. To illustrate this point, some testimonials fr om 
our Rare Barometer COVID-19 survey3 pointed to the following uses as being adequate for remote care: addre s s 
quick questions a nd questions to relieve anxiety,  discuss results of routine medical analysis, extend/adjus t a 
prescription, follow-up consultations (annual checks) and , as a first c heck, define whether a consultation is 
necessary. 
Once again, ERNs provide an excellent infrastructure that should be used to build a model for cross -borde r 
healthcare services that considers relevant legal and reimbursement aspects, clinical governance , while 
contributing to build evidence.  
Objective 3: Enhancing the development, deployment and application of trustworthy digital health products 
and services, including those incorporating artificial intelligence in the area of health  
 
Policy Options will be analysed on liability rules related to the use of data-intensive digital health serv ices, 
including those incorporating artificial intelligence, also complementing the horizontal initiatives on artificial 
intelligence.  
Whilst we agr ee that sector -specific legislation might be needed to complement  the horizontal initiative s on 
Artificial Intelligence, especially on liability and safety issues, introducing new EU legislation on AI could be 
detrimental for the healthcare sector that is already governed by a complex EU regulatory framework integra te d 
by sector-specific and horizontal regulations. At a time when the healthcare sector is still struggling to impleme nt 
the Medical Device Regulation and the GDP R, introducing new legislation  risks adding a layer of complexity , 
creating inconsistencies between the various rules, introducing uncertainty for users, patients and companies. 
Currently, there is no specific regulatory pathway for AI/ML -based medical devices in Europe. We call the 
Commission to explore how we could have a more rigorous and transparent evaluation of AI/ML-based medica l 
devices, and how they approved to improve public trust, efficacy, safety, and quality of AI/ML -based medica l 
devices4.

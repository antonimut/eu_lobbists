 
 
Roche feedback to the Combined Evaluation Roadmap /
 
Inception Impact Assessment for the European Health Data
 
Space
 
 
Roche welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback to the European Commission’s combined evaluation and
 
roadmap/Inception Impact Assessment for A European Health Data Space (EHDS) as an important step towards
 
informing the legislative proposal for the EHDS and realizing the European Commission’s “A European strategy for
 
data” (the “Data Strategy”) released in 2020.
 
 
General considerations:
 
With the roadmap/inception impact assessment, the Commission aims to make the most of the potential of digital health to
 
provide high-quality healthcare, reduce inequalities and promote access to health data for research and innovation. The
 
objectives set out for the sectoral legislative proposal establishing a regulatory framework for EHDS aim to ensure access,
 
sharing and optimal use of health data, foster a single market in digital health and enhance development, deployment and
 
application of trustworthy digital health products and services (including artificial intelligence).
 
 
 
Roche supports the objectives set out towards establishing a EHDS in support of the Data Strategy and further to the proposed
 
Data Governance Act (DGA) that is currently in consultation. The COVID-19 pandemic has further demonstrated the
 
importance of a common, interoperable EHDS, allowing for cross-border exchange and collaboration of high-quality health
 
data.
 
 
 
Context for Roche Feedback:
 
Roche’s perspective is based on its role within the health sector, as a trusted data partner, to progress the European Strategy
 
for Data. Roche looks forward to any opportunity to provide more detailed feedback as the work to progress the European
 
Health Data Space proceeds in the coming months.
 
 
 
General Comments
 
 
In alignment with or as part of the European Data Innovation Board (EDIB) put forward in the Data Governance
 
Act, we encourage consideration be given to establishing a body with oversight and leadership of the full policy
 
implementation cycle within the EHDS.
 
 
 
To enable effective policy implementation the established body should have mechanisms at its disposal to monitor
 
and enforce compliance with the relevant policies and standardisation measures set. Ultimately having oversight
 
of the full policy implementation cycle beyond coordination/harmonization. Alternatively roles/accountabilities
 
could be clearly defined for each phase of policy implementation ie. what bodies are accountable for each aspect
 
of the policy implementation, monitoring and compliance.
 
 
 
Given the dynamic nature of the emerging data/digital economy it is imperative the EHDS can be agile in its
 
approach to both policy development and implementation. This needs to be supported and enabled by strong
 
leadership with the appropriate authority.

The greatest obstacle to overcoming fragmentation challenges in interoperability is the lack of uniform data
 
models and systems underlying the various datasets and systems in place. For example, while FHIR API
 
standards are available for EHR, in an ideal scenario, this data would be aggregated and anonymised and be able
 
to feed into national (and European-wide) health data registries. EHR, social health insurance data, sickness
 
benefits data, burden of disease registries, and other data collection systems such as biobanks and genome
 
initiatives can also contribute to population data registries. Studies performed by regional networks such as
 
BeNeLuxA (which seeks to build a common data infrastructure for Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg and
 
Austria) found that one of the limiting factors at present is the lack of common data models across the healthcare
 
industry that help ensure data consistency, comparability, interoperability and quality across Member State
 
national health data registries. The 
​
European Commission’s evidence-based API Framework for Digital
 
Governments
​
 suggests a model in which a domain-specific ecosystem network, such as the European Health
 
Data Space would bring together stakeholders, including private industry, government, research, and the civic
 
sector, to identify the most relevant, priority use cases in their domain. This ecosystem network would then
 
collaborate and, where possible, set common data standards. Where this is not possible due to differences in
 
systems, a common data glossary or data dictionary could be created that allows various systems to describe
 
where they align and how they differ so that a move towards common data models can be encouraged over time.
 
Unders uch an approach, stakeholders could first work together on EHR common data models and interoperable
 
API infrastructures with a longer term goal to also achieve uniform and standardise data models for other burden
 
of disease data registries.

​

this challenging landscape.
 
 
 
New legislation being introduced at the Member State level is an improvement, however there needs to be more
 
clarity at the European-wide level. At present, various Member States interpret secondary use of health data
 
differently and have differing systems for assessing. For example, Germany does not allow some stakeholders to
 
access secondary use of health data for research purposes (industry is specifically excluded). Other Member
 
States allow use, but not for innovation, only for health service quality assessment. Others allow secondary use,
 
as long as there is citizen consent for each individual research project, others allow use as long as an ethics
 
board has reviewed the research project. Some agreed definition on secondary use and the appropriate balances
 
between individual privacy rights and the social good that can be generated through anonymised, aggregated use
 
of health data needs to be discussed and clarified at a European-wide level.

B.
Objectives and Policy Options
 
Roche supports the objectives and policy options put forward in the section and encourages the Commission to
 
consider the following in 
​
establishing a regulatory framework for the EHDS
​
:
 
 
Baseline Scenario
 
●
Define and harmonise the mechanisms and parameters including ethical frameworks by which personal
 
health information can be shared, recognizing and balancing the sensitive nature of this data with the
 
need to use longitudinal patient data and forms of data (ie. genetic) that it may not be feasible to deploy
 
privacy methodologies on. Additionally, in the context of digital tools and services, this data is (already)
 
collected and needs to be given consideration for the delivery of healthcare services and further research
 
on this data.
 
 
Objective 1a
 
●
Establish common EU Health Data classification to ensure data partners are speaking the same language
 
which helps them to categorize identifiable, anonymized and pseudonymized data. This contributes to
 
building trust among data partners in the EHDS
 
●
Define anonymization, pseudonymization and aggregation thresholds, specific to the EHDS, to further
 
build trust in data partners execution and delivery of this form of data. These thresholds need to balance
 
deployment of the privacy methodology against the utility of the data once it is applied, otherwise the data
 
will either not fully protect data subjects or not be considered useful to data consumers. These thresholds
 
may also need to be fit for purpose considering the objectives of the data consumer and the form of data
 
being requested.
 
●
Secondary use of health data cannot be subject to multiple procedures or rules under national data
 
protection laws of Member States; a unique set of rules must be defined at EC level that will harmonize
 
the derogations introduced by Member States under article 9(4) GDPR in that regard and allow EU wide
 
secondary use
 
 
Objective 1b
 
●
Data sharing and interoperability create additional concerns in terms of potential unauthorized and
 
unsecure accesses, it is essential that the Commission address these concerns by proposing common
 
rules on authorized accesses (who and under which conditions) and security measures (hosting
 
environment, identification and authentication rules, traceability
 
●
As stated in our comments on section A, we encourage expansion of the reference to interoperability
 
standards to include syntactic and organizational, demonstrating a comprehensive approach to enabling
 
interoperability of health data sources. Additionally, we recommend referencing CMS Interoperability &
 
Patient Access Final Rule in using FHIR standards to enable patient access to this data through digital
 
tools and services.
 
●
Roche strongly supports the ‘1+ Million Genomes Initiative’ and encourages the Commission to continue
 
to drive the roll-out, the linkage of genomic data to other data sources and the secondary use of this data.
 
The initiative should be accompanied by further efforts to improve patient access to advanced diagnostics
 
in all EU member states.
 
 
The main issue with this approach towards a regulatory framework is that Member States have not been able to
 
resource current efforts to maintain their current data registries: many Member States have very patchy health
 
data registries: either incomplete data collection, non-mandatory, low-quality, or missing specific data sets. In
 
some Member States, investment is not maintained to collect data so it is not annually available.

In terms of 
​
economic impacts
​

Other economic impacts to consider include secondary use of health data allows greater market balances to be
 
introduced (a key challenge identified in the European data strategy) as current systems privilege larger
 
enterprises with historical access to some datasets, which makes it more challenging for innovative health
 
startups to enter the market and compete. The lack of uniform secondary use of health data systems also act as a
 
barrier that could enable smaller startups to partner with larger industry stakeholders to complement offerings.
 
 
There is also a value extraction risk from overly stringent data sovereignty laws that define data sovereignty as
 
locally-stored data rather than a focus on generating value for local citizens of data. For example, under the Data
 
Governance Act proposals, data may need to be stored in European data servers. This may lead to convoluted
 
decisions that prevent international tech startups from building a base in Europe, while they still collect data on
 
European citizens. For example, data collected through wearables technologies could be extracted from Europe
 
and used to aid new services and products internationally. Taxes, employment, and innovation experience are all
 
collected outside the EU to enable these new products thus avoiding the fostering of innovation within Europe.
 
There are already instances where some stakeholders in Member States are seeking to classify data collected
 
from wearables technologies as outside the remit of the Data Governance Act, with the end result being that
 
products are built offshore for use only here, leading to further extraction of value of health data from European
 
citizens in uneven relationship.
 
 
 
In a similar manner, international bodies could decide to generate new health data products outside of Europe.
 
For example, Microsoft’s Intelligent Network for Eyecare (MINE) collects international healthcare data on eye
 
health and stores the data in the United States. This is then used to create innovative eyecare products for global
 
markets. Under the Data Governance Act provisions, Europe may inadvertently be locked out of participating in
 
such global collaborative models. The European Health Data Space could lead work on measuring the value
 
extraction risks of health data and make recommendations for appropriate balances that preserve digital
 
sovereignty while reducing the risk of locking out innovation.
 
 
Secondary use of health data also leads to a more efficient health system, reducing healthcare costs.
 
 
In terms of 
​
social impacts,
​
 establishment of an EHDS supports delivery of care in place, which works to achieve
 
the goal of health equity set out in the roadmap. Additionally, it holds potential for delivery of Point of Care Testing
 
and remote patient monitoring the both further deliver this goal and enable the delivery of personalised medicine
 
as set out in the roadmap.
 
 
Also worth noting that secondary use of health data can lead to more equitable healthcare solutions. For example,
 
current large scale clinical trials and use of EHR records often use mass population data and are unable to assess
 
differences in gender, race, age, or co-morbidity impacts, thus reducing the value of healthcare solutions for
 
specific populations that may already be seeing health disparities in terms of excessive health burden. By
 
enabling secondary use of health data, more nuanced data can be used to address specific population needs.
 
Similarly, secondary use of health data allows greater research development on rare diseases for which there is
 
limited population-wide data availability within one country.
 
 
Secondary use of health data also enables greater patient participation in health, which can lead to greater
 
wellbeing, confidence, quality of life, and reduce chronic illness by enabling greater participation in health
 
decisions, more active engagement in addressing health behaviours and can increase preventative health and
 
wellbeing. Again, on an equity dimension, access to secondary use of health data enables patients to have better
 
information flow that may be impacted by gender and cultural differences, and systemic inequalities in healthcare
 
that have led to healthcare professionals reducing their communication with patients. By enabling secondary use
 
of health data, patients are able to communicate with healthcare professionals in new ways.
 
 
In development of new skills to support realising the EHDS this is an opportunity to engage industry partners to
 
enhance the capability building that is required.

 
Feedback on the  Hydrogen and Gas markets Decarbonisation 
Package Evaluation Roadmap and Impact Assessment 
The Clean Air Task Force (CATF) welcomes the European Commission’s climate 
ambition, leadership on decarbonisation and innovation, and pursuit of policies to 
decouple greenhouse gas emissions from economic growth. CATF welcomes the 
revision of the Gas Regulation and the Gas Directive t o ensure compatibility with 
climate neutrality, to facilitate the market entry of renewable and zero-carbon gases, 
and to ensure a level-playing field.  
A performance standard to tackle global methane emissions 
CATF believes that this revision is the opportunity to adopt a mandatory methane 
emissions performance standard effective on all gas sold in the Single Market from 
January 2025 and inspired by the OGCI commitment to deliver a 0,2% methane 
intensity by 2025.  
In the Methane Strategy,  the Commission announced that it will examine options 
regarding possible methane emissions reduction targets, standards  or other 
incentives on fossil energy consumed and imported into the EU. CATF is calling on 
the Commission to adopt a methane performance standard as a measure for limiting 
global methane emissions and to create a level-playing field for both domestic and 
international companies.  
A performance standard for methane emissions would be similar to standards that 
apply to carbon dioxide emissions in EU instruments governing the internal energy 
market. It would therefore be logical and  administratively efficient for parallel 
standards to be introduced for methane emissions in the gas markets legislation. 
A performance standard should first apply to the gas sector as the EU has more 
control and oversight on the gas supply chain than on the oil one and because , 
unlike coal, fossil-based natural gases are projected to remain in the EU energy mix 
for some time.  Moreover, the negative externalities related to methane emissions 
are currently not included in the price of fossil gas in any way and the EU has failed 
so far in efficiently regulating methane emissions. 
Performance standards would allow methane regulation to align with the EU’s 
climate targets  and to establish clear,  understandable benchmarks for reducing 
methane leakage throughout the fossil energy supply chain. By applying to import, 
it would spread EU climate ambition  globally. It would also create  incentives for 
industry to achieve rapid methane  emissions reductions.  Moreover, a methane 
emissions limit measure  is likely to  encourage technological innovation and 
commercialisation to stop methane leakage.

 benchmark emissions but the Commission could initially  start with a “reasonable 
imperfect” methane performance standard based on the best available evidence 
such as limiting  a methane leakage intensity to 0 .2% by 2025 as this is what the 
European industry  already considers feasible. Targets/limitations could then be 
refined at a later stage, through regular review.  
Five foundational mechanisms would enable delivery of a performance standard:  
1) An EC-administered body working in coordination with ACER and the NRAs and 
acting as a European Methane Emissions Observatory . It should be  integrated in 
the existing EU Energy Markets Observatory administere d by DG ENER and 
mandated to collect, audit and make public measurement data available through 
IMEO and other public and private methane emissions data providers; 
2) A clear mandate enabling both ACER and the NRAs to act as well as adequate 
resources to execute on their function; 
3) A mandatory methane procurement standard requiring EU shippers and gas 
buyers to purchase 100% of their fossil gas volumes from producers performing at 
the 0.2% methane intensity standard as of 1 January 2025;  
4) A legal base enabling NRAs, national and regional governments to administer 
proportional and dissuasive penalties such as taxes, levies or tradeable permits on 
all non -compliant gas transactions, incl uding through pipeline, LNG, long -term 
contract or spot. 
5) A coordination between the methane strategy, including any methane 
performance standard, and the hydrogen strategy to ensure that hydrogen 
produced from methane, either domestically or imported, should not be considered 
“low-carbon hydrogen” unless upstream methane emissions are addressed and 
meet any standards or requirements that will be issued via the methane strategy. 
Carbon capture and storage 
According to nearly all modelling scenarios, reaching climate neutrality by 2050 and 
decarbonising the economy will require the development and deployment at scale 
of carbon capture, removal, and storage technologies. Based on the Commission’s 
projections, gas will be present in the energy mix until 2050. CCS will be crucial to 
tackle and reduce emissions in the power sector  and in hard -to-abate industries. 
CO2 infrastructure is therefore a prerequisite for large -scale decarbonisation. As 
geological storage sites are not evenly distributed among Member States , CO 2 
transport will be important to connect CO2 emitters to storage sites. 
As mentioned in the Hydrogen Strat egy, zero-carbon hydrogen c ould be made 
available from natural gas with CCS, as soon as CO2 infrastructure is in place. The 
development of the hydrogen infrastructure, among other trough retrofitting, should 
take place in parallel with the development of the CO2 infrastructure required for the 
production and transport of carbon for CCS, while ensuring there is no lock-in effect.  
 
Moreover, to facilitate the fast  uptake o f carbon capture, removal, and storage 
technologies, the Hydrogen and Gas markets Decarbonisation  Package should 
explore a change in the scope of the Directive to expand the transportation of gas 
beyond natural gas and also include CO 2 in order to allow transmission system 
operators (TSO) to transport the CO 2 as required for the development of CCS 
systems. The legal mandate of the National Regulatory Authorities (NRAs) should 
also be expanded to cover CO2 transport not only onshore but also offshore.

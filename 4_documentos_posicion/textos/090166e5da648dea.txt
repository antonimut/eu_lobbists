Hydrogen and Gas markets Decarbonization Package - Inception Impact 
Assessment 
The Views of the Enel Group 
 
Context 
As underlined by the European Green Deal communication and in the strategies “EU Strategy for Energy 
System Integration” and “EU Hydrogen Strategy” , the transition to a fair and prosperous society, with a 
modern, resource-efficient and competitive economy where there are no net emissions of greenhouse gases 
in 2050 invol ves substantial changes along the entire energy value chain and requires a close and proper 
coordination between policy and regulation in order to move towards decarbonization in an economically-
efficient way.  
In particular , as acknowledged in the roadmap itself, Enel strongly believes that the completion of the 
decarbonization of the power sector and the progressive electrification of final uses, such as transport, 
heating and cooling and industry, along with energy efficien cy, are the key levers to trigger for a clean and 
cost-efficient transition. These measures, in fact, have the lowest CO2 abatement cost to tackle the largest 
part of emitting activities. Green electricity and electrification can deliver in many sectors, t hanks to the 
readiness and cost competitiveness of technologies such as electric-vehicles for the road transport and heat 
pumps for buildings. Going further towards deep decarbonization , for the remaining final energy uses, the 
so-called “harder to abate” sectors such as certain industrial sectors (e.g. steel, cement, chemicals) or 
selected transport segments (maritime, aviation), the “new gases” and in particular green hydrogen will have 
a key role to play in their decarbonization. 
In the light of such goa ls, to further improve and align the current gas legislation with the new EU climate 
targets, Enel welcomes the Commission proposal to review the gas legislative framework to design 
competitive decarbonized gas markets. 
 
Recommendations 
Classification of “new gases” 
With reference to previous dossiers (e.g.. the EU Strategy for Energy System Integration , the EU Hydrogen 
Strategy and, more recently , the revision of the D irective for the promotion of renewable energy), t he 
regulatory definition and classification given to “sustainable” gases and other gases (including hydrogen and 
other derived gases) will be crucial to determine their role in the decarbonization of the energy system. There 
are multiple routes for producing those, which involve varying levels of CO2 emissions. Currently, there is no 
common agreed definition for the different type of gases at EU‐level. Indeed, the terminology surrounding 
potential solutions for decarbonizing Europe’s gas sector can be confusing and potentially misleadi ng. As a 
fundamental step, a regulatory framework requires consistent and clear definitions and criteria to determine 
unambiguously the different type of decarbonized gases and the extent to which each can be regarded as 
“green” or “low carbon”. Without a common understanding on definitions and criteria, a coherent regulatory 
framework is not possible. 
A special effort should be made to keep the classification more exhaustive in terms of type of gases included 
and at the same time as clear and as simple as possible. As a general principle, gas classification should allow

to evaluate the different categories of gas against the sustainability criteria and GHG reduction emissions 
criteria. A given gas source’s climate impact can vary considerably depending on t he energy source and the

Hydrogen infrastructure  
In defining dedicated rules for a potential hydrogen infrastructure (new dedicate hydrogen infrastructure 
and/or repurpose of section s of  infrastructure from the existing gas network), t he upcoming legislative 
proposal should take into specific consideration  the current status and foreseeable developments of 
hydrogen production and demand patterns, taking into account the level of incertitude strictly linked to the 
incipient status of such technologies. T he need for regulatory intervention for hydrogen network 
infrastructure will depend on how the hydrogen sector will evol ve, including the need for transport of 
hydrogen. Indeed, as underlined in a recent ACER/CEER white paper1: “It should be noted that the current 
situation for discussing possible regulation for the transport of hydrogen is very different from the situation  
when regulation for gas and electricity networks was introduced. In the latter cases, when regulation was 
introduced, gas and electricity networks were already in place in most Member States while hydrogen 
infrastructure and market still need to be developed.” At this moment it is impossible to forecast whether a 
dedicated extensive hydrogen network will be needed at all, even more so the convenience to regulate it or 
not. It will depend on the market needs and it should be carefully assessed.  Generally speaking, hydrogen 
should be compared against other decarbonization options (i.e. direct electrification) and chosen only when 
it represents the cheapest and sustainable route, Therefore, developments of dedicated hydrogen networks 
will be proba bly very gradual and start at local level (i.e. in a decentralized manner connecting industrial 
sites). Anyhow, as a general concept, if large quantities of hydrogen would need to be distributed, priority 
should be given to repurpose and reuse the existing  gas infrastructure, where technical feasible and cost 
effective, in order to accommodate it, instead of making investments in new infrastructures. A specific factor 
to take into account here is that the full conversion of an existing gas  network might contribute to a more 
cost‐efficient transition to a low‐carbon energy demand scenario. A brand new infrastructure with a sufficient 
capillarity is costly and increase the risk of stranded asset of the existing natural gas grid, considering also 
that the stranded assets risk will be greater in the future and the infrastructure remuneration will no longer 
be based on the existence of long term contracts . Should a dedicated regulation of hydrogen networks be 
needed, it should be developed only when such a network starts to develop and has reached a certain scale, 
in terms of feed‐in points, customers and volumes. In this respect, once and if a dedicated hydrogen network 
will be deemed necessary, regulation should follow the EU rules already applied to energy networks. In order 
to provide certainty to investors, clarity on the conditions according to which regulation would kick in should 
be envisaged, depending on the outcome of NRAs monitoring activity, and regarding the general principles 
that would be applied to the future European regulation of the hydrogen sectors (in particular unbundling, 
third-party access, transparency, non -discrimination, monitoring and oversight by the relevant NRA).  In

Blending of new gases into existing natural gas grid 
The current Gas Directive and Regulation are designed for the organization and functioning of a fossil-based 
natural gas sector, largely based on imports from third countries. They do neither anticipate the emergence 
of alternative  biogases or  methane gases, such as bio - or synthetic -methane, nor different production 
patterns, in particular decentralized ones. In light of potential growing volumes of such new gases, that could 
affect gas quality and thereby the design of gas infrastructure, the revision of the gas legislation is paramount 
and gas quality harmonization between EU countries should be pursued at interconnection points within the 
EU.  
When discussing options such as the blending of hydrogen into the existing natural gas grid , Enel believes 
that such option should be discarded in favor of other more efficient solutions, in view of using hydrogen in 
those sectors that can truly benefit most from its use (the harder to abate sectors). Blending should also be 
discarded as a way to kick-start a hydrogen market, that should instead rely on dedicated support measures 
for the production and use of green hydrogen in specific end-uses (the harder to abate sectors). Unlike other 
delivery systems, such as  hydrogen transported by trucks or by specific and dedicated pipeline (new or 
repurposed from existing gas grid), that allow to address its use in the most needed and specific sectors, the 
injection of hydrogen as a blend into natural gas entails that such mixture is distributed between the users 
of the network including uses where other more effective decarbonization solutions already exist. As 
recognized in the EU hydrogen strategy  itself, blending is a low efficient use of an expensive and scarce 
"resource", which would diminish the value of hydrogen meanwhile offering just a limited potential in terms 
of decarbonization. Blending also entails several technical and safety issues, for instance, the changes of the 
gas quality consumed in Europe may affect the design of gas infrastructure, metering, end-user applications, 
cross-border system interoperability and other operational issues difficult to be solved, such as how to ensure 
a constant admixture ratio in time and space along the grid  (not mentioning the market related implication 
that these technical issue bring with). Once injected into the gas network, it would be very difficult to control 
the hydrogen flow, which would reach residential users, with a significant risk of causing safety issues to 
existing distribution networks and appliances. Before any decision is taken, blending requires careful and 
independent analysis in terms of  admissible blending share,  its true potential contribution to the 
decarbonization of the energy system and its real need as  a kick-start solution for the hydrogen market , 
versus the technical and market fragmentation issues that such option could entail. 
 
Classification of Power to gas facilities and hydrogen markets 
The EU shall rely on e market competition as the most efficient mean to meet customers’ energy needs.  In 
this sense , sector integration and sector coupling should be achieved via market -based solutions  and 
hydrogen should be considered as a wholesale energy product, just like electricity or methane. Well-designed 
markets should ensure non-discriminatory market participation by all competing technologies and actors and 
give the right economic signals, taking into account the positive or negative externalities.

From a technical point of view, power -to-gas equipment convert electricity to gas (hydrogen or, with 
subsequent methanation, synthetic methane) which may be used for different purposes and provide 
different services (for instance, as indicated in the European Commission’s Hydrogen Strategy, synthetic 
gases may be used for gas congestion management or for storage and flexibility). In this context and as  
already recognized by regulators , the ownership and use of such  installations are already and should be 
considered as market-based activities, which are open to competition among market players. The current EU 
legislation does not have an explicit definition for “power to gas” or for “gas storage”, in terms of hydrogen 
or synthetic methane. Against this background, it is crucial to ensure consistent definitions across relevant 
gas and electricity regulation and avoid overlaps or potential grey area between the different legislations. A 
possible solution is to mirror the current provisions for energy storage to gas legislation or, in alternative, to 
introduce a dedicated definition for power to gas activities both in the electricity and gas legislation following 
the same provisions already set for energy storage by the electricity directive (EU) 2019/944. Eventually, the 
goal of the regulatory framework should to allow the efficient development and use of those installations, 
which should be done by the market, as compared with other installations and technologies providing similar 
services. 
In particular, it should be precluded to regulated entities from investing , owning and managing potentially 
competitive activities such power to gas. Indeed, to develop se ctor coupling in the most efficient way EU 
needs to rely on the market to drive investments. Hydrogen and synthetic gas production are a competitive 
activity and it should be developed by market operators. This would indeed ensure a level playing field 
between electricity and gas and, hence, an efficient sector coupling. On the other hand, the management of 
large‐scale Power‐to‐Gas plants by regulated entities bears the risk of preferential treatment with regard to 
dispatch operations.  
Moreover, t he possibility of limited participation by the TSOs and DSOs  that might help "kick start" the 
development of these technologies” is questionable and needs to be avoided, in fact: 
 if the appropriate policies and rules are established, the development can be done in the market 
 once the infrastructural operators have acquired the skills market participants are likely to be 
displaced.  
Indeed, a market failure can only happen when a market is conceivable, and that is not yet the case. These 
are technologies that are still relatively far away from economic and technical maturity. They can potentially 
be used in a variety of ways, but it still isn’t clear which of those ways will be successful. If they are not being 
deployed now, it is not because of a market failure, but because of this lack of maturity. Therefore, all the 
efforts should first be orientated to address this lack of maturity. Only when they are commercially available, 
the identification of possible market failures could start. 
The boundary between regulated entities core activities and the provision of other services must therefore 
be drawn clearly. In order to facilitate better outcomes in circumstances where the market ca nnot (yet) 
provide the activity.  
 
Tariffs for Power to gas 
Power‐to‐gas assets could be subject to both electricity and gas regulation and network codes with different 
requirements. This could eventually lead to issues concerning differences between physical characteristics of 
the power and gas systems and respective applicable legislation. To review if tariffs could create possible 
distortion, it is crucial to understand the role that these infrastructures are going to fulfil (e.g. electric storage,

hydrogen producer etc.), to which extent they use electricity and gas networks, and if they are in competition 
with other technologies. 
Network tariffs should be focused on recovering grid costs and providing efficient economic signals, hence 
the adoption of specific electricity and gas grid tariff reductions or exemptions to support the development 
of power to gas production units, or to compensate for services they may supply to the grids , should be 
avoided in principle: 
 Tariff structures should be tailored to guarantee technologica l neutrality, so that the most efficient 
solution is adopted (and not the most convenient one compared to a different regulatory regime); 
 Flexibility services supplied through power to gas technologies should be enabled and valued on the 
specific markets those services are targeting  
 
Moreover, h armonized and efficient rules on network tariffs for energy storage are needed. Inefficient 
electricity network tariffs methodologies that could lead to pay double as both consumer (of electricity during 
P2G) and producer (during G2P) could represent a financial barrier and create distortions for the deployment 
of storage technologies. The effect of charges and levies  and taxes affecting energy storage technologies  
should be the taken into account  to avoid financial barriers and distortions.  The energy storage using P2G 
and G2P should have the same treatment given to other forms of electricity storage, like batteries and 
pumping storage. 
Due to the potential, market ramp-up of new gas carriers (e.g. biomethane) , it will crucial to update the 
harmonized transmission tariff structures network code in order to define a common and harmonized 
approach at EU level in a timely manner. In the current phase, with these technologies still constituting a 
niche market, new gas carriers benefit in some countries from exemptions from tariff costs. Access tariffs are 
payments for the use of the infrastructures and should not discriminate among users.  However, due to the 
potential larger deployment of these solutions, the “new gas ca rriers” exemption shall be phase out and 
“new gas carriers” should pay the network tariff on a level playing field with other technologies. Hence, a 
harmonized network tariff system including such technologies should be developed to assure a common 
approach at EU level. In conclusion, as stated by ACER/CEER in a recent paper2, network tariffs should not be 
used to subsidize technologies, activities or users; any subsidization should be done with dedicated 
instruments outside the regulation of network access. 
 
Network planning  
Although natural gas infrastructures could still play an important role in the short and medium term, they 
have a limited lifetime in the transition to a decarbonized system in order to meet the long-term EU climate 
goals. This can lea d to stranded assets as the remaining economic lifetime for parts of the existing 
infrastructure may be longer than the lifetime these parts may be used to serve customers. 
The potential development of hydrogen could contribute to extend the lifetime of at least a part of the gas 
infrastructure, thus reducing the volume of stranded assets. Existing gas infrastructure could be used for the 
transport and distribution of hydrogen, after market’s need & technical feasibility & safety considerations

have been addressed. If power -to-gas technologies mature and become competitive and commercially 
available, gas networks will be necessary in the long term and the risk of stranded assets could be reduced. 
What is crucial is to ensure that gas regulation schemes do not incentivize building more gas infrastructure if 
not necessary. Gas regulation should prevent unnecessary investments in gas infrastructures where possible 
if they do not comply with the goals towards a decarbonized system. To do so, gas regulation schemes should 
be subject to a continuous monitoring of the maturity and competitiveness of hydrogen and power to gas 
technologies and be based on gas demand forecasts that are compliant with the Paris agreement and in line 
with the Green Deal scenarios. 
Against this background and in the light of the growing interdependencies between gas and electricity in 
Europe a more integrated infrastructure planning, coordinated risk preparedness and system operation  
across sector will be required. In this context, sector coupling and the cost efficient use of existing 
infrastructure should be seen as key principles. A stronger oversight by ACER and NRAs will be necessary. The 
increasing importance of links between ga s and electricity infrastructure shall be reflected in a new 
requirement for joint grid planning and scenarios, at both European and national levels. In this contest ACER, 
NRAs and the EC itself should play a key and active role ensuring that these scenarios and grid planning take 
into account the European decarbonization objectives and maturity of different technologies, such as power‐
to‐gas. It is important to ensure that methodological orientations and scenario building are not influenced 
by the sole int erest of TSOs. The overall process of TYNDP development has to provide guarantees of 
neutrality and, as far as gas is concerned, priority should be put on making best use of existing infrastructure. 
NRAs and ACER, as independent authorities, should be resp onsible for properly assessing and, potentially, 
approving the TYNDP, the underlying scenarios and the CBA methodologies. ENTSOs shall conduct an 
extensive consultation process involving all relevant stakeholders, national regulatory authorities and other 
national authorities. Hypothesis and scenarios should take into account technology costs and competitive 
alternatives for clean energy carriers, demand side flexibility potential, energy efficiency and conversion 
losses, the impact of the overall gas volumes on bio resources and on the electricity system, foreseen imports 
and their implications, as well as infrastructure requirements for different types of gases and blends. 
 
Methane emission 
In pursuing the goal to develop a new regulatory framework suited for increasing liquidity, competition, and 
consumer protection to accompany the deployment of new gases, as announced in the roadmap, Enel would 
like to emphasize the importance of strict coordination between the goals of the “Hydrogen and Gas markets 
Decarbonisation Package” and the initiatives already announced in the EU Methane Strategy to reduce EU -
related methane emissions  coming both from fossil fuel and new gases . Against this background,  the 
upcoming legislative proposals should take specific attention to the management of methane emissions from 
new sources of methane such as biomethane and synthetic gases. New sources of methane such as 
biomethane or synthetic methane may cause additional emissions of methane to the atmosphere. There is a 
risk that the development of new plants to produce biogas, biomethane or synthetic methane may cause 
additional releases of methane due to immature design and yet not-optimized operation and maintenance. 
The potential impacts of such phenomena should be carefully as sessed and taken into account in the new 
legislative proposal.

The transmission tariffs may need to evolve in the light of major future changes such as decarbonisation,

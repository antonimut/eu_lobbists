2. Detailed comments 
1. Application of the Internal Energy Market principles to the future H 2 
market 
The transport of hydrogen through pipelines has (or will have, once fully developed) 
characteristics of a natural monopoly  (economies of scale,  optimised network 
planning, network economies in terms of interconnection and control system, market 
power once hydrogen becomes a traded commodity ). Thus, in the same way it is 
rationale to regulate natural gas networks, it is justified regulatory intervention for 
hydrogen networks.  This concept is well sustained and analysed in the report by 
Trinomics and LBST, “Sector integration – Regulatory framework for  hydrogen”, 
recently published by the Commission1, which concludes: 
While existing hydrogen pipeline infrastructure is currently not characterised 
by a natural monopoly, it is expected to represent in the future market 
situation a natural monopoly, based o n the following elements which would 
justify regulatory intervention: 
• Pipelines have a subadditive investment cost curve, and other 
transportation means would for most uses not provide suitable or 
competitive alternatives;  
• Hydrogen is expected to become a  traded commodity with a high number 
of producers/sellers and buyers;  
• Refurbishing natural gas pipelines to hydrogen operations will be less 
expensive than new -build pipelines, and will hence offer a competitive 
advantage to the concerned owners/operators.  
Taking into account the similarities between gas and hydrogen networks and, in 
particular the repurposing of gas infrastructure (to transport hydrogen only) and the 
retrofitting of gas infrastructure (to transport hydrogen blended with natural gas), 
incorporating the rules for hydrogen in the gas legislation would be the most efficient 
way of ensuring regulatory alignment between these two closely related energy 
carriers.  
Enagás is of the opinion that the same market principles should be applied to natural 
gas and hydrogen, such us: unbundling, third party access, transparency rules, 
network tariffs, capacity allocation and congestion management, network planning. 
However, Enagás recognizes that t he hydrogen market and infrastructure is not a 
mature market and it is at a different stage of development compared to natural was 
when the 3rd Package was established in 2009. Thus, some rules might not be 
directly applicable to hydrogen at first stages and the possibility to have tailor made 
regulation at an initial or transitional stage should be accepted.

2. Enabling combined network operators 
The EU gas/hydrogen legislation should explicitly recognise that gas transmission 
system operators can be certified as owners and  operators of hydrogen networks 
through the concept of combined operator. 
Article 29 of the Directive 2009/73/EC already provides the possibility of a combined 
transmission, LNG, storage and distribution system operator; the scope of this article 
should also be extended to hydrogen operators. 
Combined natural gas and hydrogen network operators would allow to benefit from 
efficiency gains and economies of scope, in particular it has the following advantages: 
 Facilitates conversion of natural infrastructure  
 Lower cost of capital  
 Reduced administrative burdens in comparison with other options  
 Increased certainty for market actors  
 Leverages knowledge of CH4 network operators  
Authorising gas TSOs to carry out acti vities related to the development, integration 
and operation of hydrogen infrastructure, including the core hydrogen network, 
would facilitate an efficient way forward due to the experience of TSOs in planning, 
financing, constructing, operating and mainta ining gas infrastructure. Furthermore, 
since parts of the existing gas grids (owned and operated by the gas TSOs) will be 
retrofitted and repurposed to transport hydrogen, this would also be more easily 
facilitated by allowing TSOs to own and operate both gas and hydrogen networks. It 
should also result in a more efficient operation of the future hydrogen network and 
enable existing, well-proven arrangements concerning safety and security of supply 
cooperation to be more easily mirrored to the hydrogen market. 
The Hydrogen Strategy already recognises the potential repurposing of elements of 
the existing pan-European gas infrastructure to provide the necessary infrastructure 
for large -scale cross -border transport of hydrogen. Repurposing may provide an 
opportunity for a cost -effective energy transition in combination with (relatively 
limited) newly built hydrogen dedicated infrastructure. It also acknowledges that  
existing natural gas pipelines are owned by network operators that are often not 
allowed to own, operate and finance hydrogen pipelines.  
The report by Trinomics and LBST, “Sector integration – Regulatory framework for 
hydrogen”, recently published by the Commission, concludes that whichever 
regulatory approach is ultimately opted for, a number of key  principles should be 
respected to enable the hydrogen sector to efficiently contribute to reaching the 
major EU and national energy and climate objectives . One of them is that “Member 
States should be allowed to realise efficiency gains and economies of scope through 
combined natural gas and hydrogen network operators. But this should not 
compromise transparency and cost-reflectiveness of grid tariffs”

3. Electrolysers as energy conversion facilities 
Electrolysers should be classified as energy conversion fa cilities (not being end -
consumers of electricity) and gas TSOs should be allowed to own and operate them  
under tolling/third-party access regime. 
A parallelism can be made between the electrolysers and the LNG terminals, both 
facilities convert energy from one form to another: the electrolysers convert 
electricity into hydrogen and the LNG terminals LNG into natural gas. Both of them 
feed the gas network, though LNG terminals inject much larger volumes. 
Gas TSOs are currently allowed to own and operate LNG terminals under third-party 
access and it has been proved that this activity does not create any conflict of interest 
as long as the operator does not intervene in trading and commercialisation activities. 
This situation is completely different from that of electricity storage by electricity 
TSOs. The restrictions of electricity TSOs to own and operate energy storage is due 
to potential conflict of interests that are not applicable in the case of such operation 
by gas TSOs. The figure of “Combined Operator” (including energy/gas storage 
activity) is part of the Gas directives since 2003. Electricity storage provisions 
adopted in the Clean Energy Package should not automatically be transposed as such 
in the gas legislation. 
Enagás strongly recommend s to explicitly allow discounts at entry points into the 
transmission network from renewable gases productions in order to promote the 
injection of renewable gases into the transmission network. The main arguments to 
support this approach could be, among others, the followings: 
 Biogas and hydrogen entry points are (or will be at the beginning) close 
to consumption points (avoided costs) 
 It brings flexibility to the TSO network by reducing need for IP imports 
(avoided costs) 
 Environmental reasons 
4. Common, clear terminology 
A common EU terminology via clear, accurate and science -based definition of 
renewable, decarbonised and low-carbon gases is urgently required. This will be the 
starting point for the development and scaling-up of hydrogen. 
Up to date the most comprehensive taxonomy for gas has been developed by the 
New Gases Network (NGN). Enagás suggests using the terminology of the NGN 
including the 3  categories of new gases (renewable, decarbonised y low -carbon) 
under the generic term Clean Gases.

5. Hydrogen Target Model with limited exemptions 
First of all, a Hydrogen Target Model should be defined in order to guide Members 
States in developing regulation which is aligned with the EU framework and does not 
hamper the later interconnection of their hydrogen system. 
Enagás is of the opinion that access rules for hydrogen networks should be 
harmonised. However, the regulatory framework should provid e some flexibility 
through transitional measures. 
For this purpose, certain hydrogen network infrastructure could be able to be granted 
exemption in limited and duly justified cases (e.g. direct lines or closed distribution 
systems in line with the meaning contained in the Gas Directive). It should be made 
clear, that such exemptions should be ceased at a sunset date or as soon as the 
relevant criteria are no longer. 
6. Regulatory sandbox at EU level 
A regulatory sandbox allows innovators to trial new products , services and business 
models facing a regulatory barrier in a real -world environment without some of the 
usual rules applying. The exception to rules is temporary. The trial has an explicit 
learning objective to test the innovative project. There is an o bligation to regularly 
report the findings and the results, and knowledge share.  
And at the end of the trial, all rules will apply as normal. The intention of the sandbox 
is not to change regulatory rules permanently. 
These results will be considered during future policy development.

A regulatory sandbox framework approach at EU level would promote innovation, 
scaling up, system adaptation and optimisation for technologies at an early stage of 
development. This can play an important role in encouraging R&D  activities and 
innovative projects. 
It is important to make sure that Member States introduce and apply the regulatory 
sandboxes in a harmonized way consistent with the functioning of the internal EU gas 
market.  
Therefore, the revised gas legislation sho uld allow for the implementation of 
regulatory sandboxes at EU level establishing a level-playing field for the participation 
of TSOs in pilot projects. 
7. Recognition of cost for retrofitting and repurposing gas pipelines 
The majority of the future hydrogen network will be comprised of repurposed gas 
pipelines. As such, some EU level principles should be established to ensure that 
repurposing costs are recognised by the NRAs and that the switch from natural gas 
to hydrogen is planned in a transparent way. Cos ts of retrofitting the network to 
accommodate hydrogen/gas blends should also be appropriately allowed by the 
NRAs. 
8. Financing the development of the H2 networks 
Enagás supports all funding instruments for the development of hydrogen: Horizon 
Europe, IPCEI, TEN-E (CEF), Innovation Fund, InvestEU, Structural Funds and others. 
In particular, i n order to allow for the transport of pure hydrogen, TEN -E could 
support th e repurposing of existing natural gas pipelines and the construction of 
dedicated hydrogen infrastructure/pipelines. 
To ensure the cost-effective and timely development of the hydrogen network, it will 
need to be designed and constructed to meet the longer -term forecast of hydrogen 
demand in terms of sizing/dimensioning, location and timing. As such, this clearly 
has significant consequences for the financing and development of the hydrogen 
network since, in the earlier stages of the market development, the specific network 
charges will either be prohibitively high or the transportation revenues derived from 
initial levels of hydrogen demand will not be sufficient to finance the required 
infrastructure.  
It is therefore essential that arrangements should be established to address this issue 
and thereby better ensure the recovery of investment cost in the hydrogen network.  
For this purpose, there should be a levelised cost between users of the CH 4 network 
and H2 network. Maintaining a similar unit cost (either in terms of energy or in terms 
of volume) between network users of CH 4 and of H2 could be convenient during the 
transition period, meaning that the unit cost paid by users of the network should not 
be balanced and not dependant on the transfer of assets between the CH4 network 
and the H2 network. A stricter cost-reflectivity should be considered in the long-term 
once the transition from CH4 to H2 has been completed. 
Although it is recognised that the details of any funding arrangements are a matter 
for each Member State to determine on the basis of specific national circumstances 
(e.g. gas market size, costs associated with the required hydrogen infrastructure, 
transit or final destination countries, etc), the revised Gas Package should provide a 
clear regulatory framework that enables such financing mechanisms.

11. Blending as an enabler of H2 rollout 
The existing gas transmission and distribution networks, together with the associated 
facilities connected to them, will be an essential enabling factor in delivering the 
decarbonisation of the European economy and the goals of the EU Hydrogen 
Strategy. The retrofitting and repurposing of existing gas networks can be combined 
and complemented with the construction of new dedicated hydrogen infrastructure, 
so that three hydrogen deployment options can co-exist where needed. 
Whilst blending is expected to be a transitional solution, it has multiple advantages 
which are relevant in the context of the EU Hydrogen Strategy: 
 Using gas-hydrogen blends in the short and medium term achieves a larger 
GHG reduction at a lower sy stemic cost than by using only new dedicated 
infrastructure to deliver hydrogen. 
 It allows for building -up hydrogen production capacity and developing 
economies of scale that will foster a positive business case for the transition 
to a hydrogen system. 
 As for sector coupling between electricity and gas, it enhances system -wide 
resilience by integrating surplus intermittent renewable electricity, reducing 
power network congestion and providing short -term flexibility and energy 
storage. 
 Injecting hydrogen int o gas transmission and distribution pipelines provides 
renewable and low -carbon energy to consumers currently connected to the 
gas network. 
 Blending grants market access, on a swift and cost -efficient manner, for 
decentralised hydrogen producers who locate their facilities in areas with high 
renewable energy resources but distant from consumption centres and 
dedicated hydrogen networks. Furthermore, they can benefit from 
participating in the pan-European integrated gas market.  
 Hydrogen blending can be an e specially cost-effective transitional option in 
those European regions without parallel or duplicated networks, or without 
(potentially) available gas infrastructure capacity, which can be easily 
repurposed to hydrogen in the short-term.  
 Regarding production of thermal energy, hydrogen preserves its value when 
mixed with natural gas. 
Hydrogen admixtures in the gas network should be properly recognised within the 
European energy regulatory framework in order to enable its rollout. A robust system 
of certifi cates/guarantees of origin (GOs) would allow monetisation of hydrogen 
injected while reducing the need for public support. Additionally, to facilitate intra -
EU cross-border trade of gas blends, concrete interoperability measures at technical 
and commercial levels would be required.

12. Harmonisation with wide gas quality range 
Quality standards for H2 networks should be harmonised. However, gas quality should 
not impose unnecessary burdens. A wide range of gas quality will facilitate the trade 
of renewable and low carbon gases and the establishment of a core hydrogen network 
at European level. 
13. Encouraging of H2 production - EU quotas for renewable gases 
The EC should fix policy actions at enabling H2 to arrive at competitive prices as soon 
as possible to be able to reach the installing capacity levels and costs of the EU 
Hydrogen Strategy. 
In the future decarbonised energy mix, renewable gases will play an important role, 
especially in the hard-to-abate and transport sectors. Therefore, Enagás supports the 
introduction of EU quotas for renewable gases, underpinned by adequate policy 
support mechanisms. The uptake of renewable gases (e.g. biomethane and 
hydrogen) will also help decarbonising hard -to-abate sectors like heavy -duty and 
maritime transport. 
Enagas takes note of different concrete proposals for sector–specific/concrete targets 
for renewable gases being proposed by different EU associations such as Eurogas, 
Gas for Climate, GD4S, etc. 
Enagás supports the possible upward review of the 32% target for renewable energy 
set at the EU level. However, more than promoting a mere increase of the overall 
renewable energy share, Enagás encourages the European Commission to endorse 
an EU -level binding target for renewable gases to progressively introduce these 
gases. 
Such a target will incentivise Member States to define national contribution in their 
NECPs and develop their own pathways depending on national assets and features in 
term of physical potential (e.g. feedstock availability), national economics (e.g. local 
production costs) or positive externalities (e.g. rural development, local job creation, 
avoided CO 2 emissions, waste management, etc.). This approach would benefit all 
EU consumers while improving investors’ confidence. 
A separate target for low-carbon gases could be set additionally. 
14. Harmonisation of planning requirements 
All certified TSOs should be subject to the same requirements as regards the 
submission to the NRA of a ten-year network development plan. 
There should be a minimum harmonization of requirements for developing the ten-
year network development plan , since this is a fundamental input for the TYNDP 
process. Under the current gas and electricity directives only ITOs (but no other 
certified TSOs) are obliged to submit to the NRA a ten-year network development 
plan at least every two years. This is creating asymmetri es between Member States 
and practical implementation issues as regards the development of PCIs.

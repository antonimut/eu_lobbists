Objectives & Measures of success  
The EU needs to deliver markets that are ‘fit for 55’ by 2030 and ensure that the 
needs of energy consumers are met in a world in which a variety of new energy 
solutions become available.  
These imply a reduction of 29-37% in fossil gas consumption by 20301. A bulk of 
this is expected to be driven through changes in the building sector (>60% 
reduction until 2030).  This implies that for the building sector, readily available 
solutions need to be prioritised over hydrogen, which will only become an at-
scale option in the late 2030s.  
This dynamic will fundamentally transform the shape of the gas market. It will 
make it more fragmented as entire geographical entities are likely to stop using 
gas through bottom up policies (see Table on p. 3). It points to the fact that the 
focus of current gas market policy on promoting gas-on-gas competition is not 
sufficient. Instead, we propose the following benchmarks of success: 
• Mitigate risk of “non-delivery”: Do zero greenhouse gas emission 
products and services have access to the highest value markets such that 
scarce and high value resources are not constrained to low value markets 
where alternatives products and services exist?  
• Enhance competition and cost-effectiveness: Is fair competition across 
the energy sector embedded in infrastructure planning processes to allow 
consumers access to the best value products?

• Deliver a fair transition: Has an appropriate balance been struck

• Ensure climate value: Do the measures lead to a material reduction in

As a result, a framing around essential net-zero emissions services (e.g. a 
package for markets that deliver zero-emissions heat) seems more appropriate 
than a framing around an energy vector like gas. 
 
Critical assumptions and barriers (recommendations in bold) 
1. Manage technology uncertainty 
 
Climate neutrality is a time bounded challenge within which we should enable 
consumers to choose from the best set of high-value options. To do this, instead 
of designing a gas market package, we suggest the policy design is framed 
around delivery of end user services (e.g. zero-emissions heat) that can be 
delivered through many different solutions. 
 
In addition, consumer choices need to be supported by the right investment 
framework to allow early progress in reducing emissions and to create options 
for reductions in the future. This is not the same as keeping all options open, an 
approach that would be prohibitively expensive and take focus away from the 
big system shifts needed.  
 
We need a mechanism to decide which options to keep open and support in an 
innovation journey and which to shut because they are likely to be too 
expensive. This is key for long-lasting infrastructure investments such as gas 
networks where decisions need to be made soon – even more so as bottom-up 
processes need to be taken into account to ensure continued geographic 
cohesion and integrated markets.

considerable evidence challenging this notion and it would mean that we 
may encourage an inefficient transition pathway. For example, 40% of 
current gas use in industry is for heat >100°C and can be replaced with 
heat pumps which can in many cases be more energy-efficient than 
hydrogen5. In addition, the full extent of methane leakage of the network 
is not yet known and it seems premature to assume a smaller molecule 
such as hydrogen is safe to insert into pipes. 
• Blending hydrogen with fossil gas is desirable. Many end users in industry 
prefer pure hydrogen; blending would downgrade the value of a scarce 
and expensive resource in a climate neutral EU economy. The lock in risk 
is also considerable. If blending is pursued as an option, it is important 
that a binding governance mechanism for a full transition to 100% 
hydrogen is set out and that the risk for non-delivery is not borne by the 
citizen.  
 
We support the intention stated in the IIA that the gas package can only succeed 
if the governance surrounding the planning and decision making of our energy 
system is reformed. A reformed governance would greatly aid the design of 
efficient (market and other) delivery mechanisms, enhance our ability to 
respond to innovation as well as strengthen the potential for geographical 
cohesion. There is increasing momentum around the globe behind innovating 
institutional design to deliver climate neutral energy systems6.  
 
2. Citizen consent 
EU citizens need to change from using products and services that depend on 
fossil gas to ones produced by zero emissions sources. This transition will only 
happen if supported by citizens. They must perceive the changes as necessary to 
achieve a better future. The policy mechanism(s) needs to make the best value 
and life enhancing options easily accessible and affordable to consumers, not 
only through funding but also through how we present choices (and absence of 
choice).  
 
In many instances, hydrogen may seem like the easy option now for the decision 
maker but may then leave citizens with high costs or the need to make rapid and 
poorly planned changes later on.

More specifically, some of the essential elements (in addition to funding 
mechanisms outlined in the next section) include: 
• Introduce a combination of regulation (technology specific vs broad 
ranging, e.g. phase out dates for boilers) and fiscal incentives (targeted 
or broad) to enable consumers to make the climate neutral choices. 
• Strengthening the role of citizens in developing local climate 
neutrality/fossil fuel phaseout plans as mentioned in the IIA. 
• Reforming energy taxation, where current incentives are set in a way to 
make choosing climate neutral options more costly (in most Member 
States, excl NL and SE, electricity prices in the residential sector are at 
least double those of gas with taxes and levies contributing a significant 
share7). It is important that the potential extension of the ETS does not 
further penalise consumers who cannot choose to stop using thegas 
grid.  
• Strengthening consumer protection regime on gas as outlined in the IIA 
and ensure the labelling of alternative gases is simple and clear, cf 
suggestions made by BEUC8. Clear certification mechanisms, with strong 
temporal and geographic correlation of any emissions reductions, will be 
needed. 
3. Funding 
Replacing fossil gas will be expensive across networks, production facilities and 
processes, appliances and building fabric. Where the funding comes from and 
who ultimately pays are key policy and political questions, and the gas package 
will need to reflect on this in light of broader energy system integration efforts.  
 
Public funding will be important, at least in the early stages, but over time the 
heavy lifting must be done by private finance. This requires regulations and 
markets that deliver a reasonable return on investments whilst these costs are 
allocated in a fair and equitable way.

Higher costs to the consumer or taxpayer may be justified, but to ensure public 
buy in it requires that  
• a public and/or private benefit is delivered, e.g. significant GHG 
emission reductions in line with EU objectives. 
• That the ’polluter pays’ principle (TFEU Art. 191) is followed in the first 
instance and has been fully exhausted. Using public funding to support 
fossil companies to transition to hydrogen, install CCS or reduce 
methane leakage can in some circumstances be in violation of this 
principle and needs to be carefully evaluated. 
 
We thus recommend the gas package comes up with principles for the fair 
allocation of gas system cost. 
 
Framing the policy options 
The proposed policy options in the IIA are 
1. No change 
2. Rules fostering infrastructure and markets  
3. Integration of RES and low carbon gases 
 
Across all policy options, it is essential that the ‘energy efficiency first principle’ 
is implemented. 
 
The options are currently focussed on H2 replacing gas, but do not look into 
removing barriers for other energy solutions replacing gas use and the 
implications of that. A fourth policy option should be added to explore 
increasing competition between gaseous solutions and other energy solutions.

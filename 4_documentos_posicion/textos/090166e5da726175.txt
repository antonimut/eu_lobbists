HYDROGEN AND GAS MARKETS DECARBONISATION PACKAGE 
This document contains ACCIONA’s feedback on the combined evaluation roadmap/inception  impact  
assessment  regarding the Hydrogen and Gas markets Decarbonisation Package (Revision of Directive 
2009/73/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 July 2009 concerning common rules 
for the internal market in natural gas and repealing Directive 2003/55/EC and Revision of Regulation  
(EC) No 715/2009 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 July 2009 on conditions for 
access to the natural gas transmission networks and repealing Regulation (EC) No 1775/2005). 
 
First and most importantly, we would like to point out that, whe n talking about decarbonisation of 
the energy market, a very clear principle has to be kept in mind: direct electrification is the most cost-
efficient way to decarbonise energy consumption  and, therefore, must be prioritized.  Alternatives 
such as gas shoul d play a subsidiary role and be considered only to the extent that electrification is 
not feasible. In this regard, the continued expansion of “electrifiable” end uses should be taken into 
account (e.g. heavy-duty road transport). 
If gas is chosen based on the above, zero-carbon gases should be the solution of choice, particularly 
renewable hydrogen.  The European Commission’s Hydrogen strategy (July 2020) states that 
renewable hydrogen produced via electrolysis based on 100% renewable electricity is the hydrogen 
type most compatible with EU’s climate neutrality goal. The revision of the hydrogen and gas market 
rules should serve to ramp up the production of renewable hydrogen towards competitiveness in the 
next decade. 
In order to maintain coherence within all the EU energy and climate framework, the EU should not 
adopt or support any measure leading to  a demand for natural gas lock -in. Greenfield low carbon 
projects imply a very certain gross carbon footprint coupled with an, as yet, untested CCUS offset. This 
is not an acceptable combination and will look even more unwise seen from the vantage point of 2030 
or 2035. Furthermore, in light of the projections -given the strong policy push envisaged in the 
Hydrogen Strategy- “low-carbon hydrogen” will foreseeably be rendered uncompetitive  (in purely 
economic terms) around 2030.  
Hence, no investment in new fossil gas capacity should be made . A ny “low -carbon hydrogen” 
investment should be aimed at reducing the carbon footprint of existing fossil gas infrastructure, and 
have a clear expiration date: transitional solutions should not be perpetuated.  Any retrofi tting of 
natural gas transport networks should wait until national and international supply and demand 
patterns are clearly established, and the technical and financial costs of such an exercise are fully clear. 
Consistently, all new public policies and initiatives should be oriented towards the EU 2030 and 2050 
decarbonisation targets and, therefore, we consider appropriate the Commission's proposal to revise 
the EU rules on Trans -European Networks for Energy (the TEN -E Regulation) since it includes: (i) an 
obligation for all projects to meet mandatory sustainability criteria and to follow the ‘do no harm' 
principle as set out in the Green Deal; (ii) an update of the infrastructure categories eligible for support

Also, the future revision of the Commission’s guidelines on State aid for environmental protection and

In this regard, the 2050 projections in the Climate Target Plan Impact Assessment (including a still -
significant weight of fossil gas) should by no means be taken as a given: rapid technological progress

knowledge frontier and allow zero-carbon gases (or even electrification) to cover industrial uses now 
deemed captive for fossil -based gas , rendering Carbon Capture, Utilisation and Storage (CCUS) 
unnecessary. 
Let us bear in mind that the EC Communication ‘A Clean Planet for all’1 already established that CCUS 
is still barely developed and thus, its potential effect in facilitating decarbonisation should not be

In parallel to phasing -out fossil fuels, policies should also be directed at phasing  in new sustainable 
technologies to foster the decarbonisation.  This entails reducing investment uncertainty and 
adopting a stable regulatory framework, helping dispel fears from investors in new technologies and 
preventing future conflict when carbon-based assets become stranded. 
Guarantees of origin for renewable Hydrogen should be part of such stable regulatory framework . 
The 2018 Renewable Energy Directive (“REDII”) foresees that the hydrogen produced via electrolysers 
that are directly connected to renewable generation installations can claim Guarantees of Origin (GOs) 
and will count towards fuel supplier obligations, but there is no clear provision for the hydrogen that 
is produced by electrolysers that are directly connected to the grid. A clear, unambiguous and uniform 
definition at the European level  would ease the interpretation , while the establishment of a swift 
mechanism for the issuance of the corresponding guarantees of origin would facilitate a market 
uptake of renewable hydrogen.  
To this end, a clear methodology and criteria should be set to verify when and to what extent hydrogen 
should be considered of renewabl e origin (namely, traceability criteria). Also , an accreditation body 
should be designated to certify compliance with such criteria and issue the corresponding GOs. Such 
compliance should be able to be proven using the latest technologies available in the market, like 
blockchain, since this technology allows the verification and visualization of the entire hydrogen value 
chain: from the production of 100% renewable electricity in the generation plants for its supply to 
electrolysers, to the consumption of h ydrogen by customers; all this in real time, anywhere in the 
world and in a transparent way.  
This technology could definitely help develop a  tracking and certification system3, without prejudice 
to other verification procedures that may be put in place, like the current system being developed 
under the CertifHy project, and it would be suitable for all purposes provided in the RED II and relevant 
ancillary legislation like (i) customer disclosure or (ii) certification of contribution to the renewable 
energy in transport target and to other possible targets.  
In order for green hydrogen to take off, a quick deployment of electrolysers has to take place. To this 
end, the key to deve loping and deploying hydrogen production is fostering a market for renewable 
hydrogen which incentivises businesses to invest in it, encourages new businesses into the market and 
also builds up green hydrogen demand - which is difficult to happen naturally, due to the higher cost 
of hydrogen compared to carbon intensive fuels.  
Therefore, financial support should be granted for fostering the development throughout the entire 
renewable hydrogen industrial value chain, the deployment of electrolysers and hydrogen valleys, and 
the adaption of end -user equipment to consume hydrogen. R&D aid  is  also essential for achieving

New hydrogen demand will probably take somewhat longer to ma terialise than new supply. In light 
of this, and given the EU’s objective of rapid electrolyser deployment, the injection of renewable H2 
into the current gas grid is a valid option on a transitional basis. Limited blending can be done with no 
retrofitting requirements, and creates immediate demand for renewable hydrogen, which can be 
redeployed as soon as other demand sources for clean hydrogen appear and gas starts to fade. 
Projects developing renewable hydrogen production on a large scale will require larger investments 
and take longer to come to fruition, while smaller scale projects can come online sooner. Initial efforts 
should not leave small -scale hydrogen projects aside ; a t the very least, it shoul d be possible to 
aggregate them at company level to achieve scale. 
In the long term, initial R&D demonstration projects should be able to generate production, processing 
and consumption clusters (industrial zones, ports, intermodal transport zones).  
As mentioned before, retrofitting the gas transport network is a n irreversible and  highly expensive 
exercise; it should be approached very cautiously , with all the necessary information at hand. The 
hydrogen market is currently in its infancy and in a very flui d situation. This applies, first of all, with 
regard to its size, i.e. how far electrification will take us and what residual end uses will therefore be 
renewable-gas based. But also to its internal structure: t he national, intra -EU and extra -EU 
geographical patterns for hydrogen supply and demand are far from clear at this stage (e .g. 
electrolyser close to the energy source vs to the offtaker, production in developing countries) and will 
determine whether it is electricity or gas that will have to be trans ported. The absolute cost of 
hydrogen transport grid (including retrofitting) is unclear at present, as is the attractiveness of this 
option vs other transport alternatives (ammonia, etc.). 
We should allow the hydrogen market to settle and these details to take shape before assuming any 
sunk investments in the transport grid. Furthermore, this discussion should be kept strictly separate 
from the debate on how fast natural gas can be phased out.

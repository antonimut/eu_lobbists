Comments on Gas Package Evaluation Roadmap/Inception Impact Assessment   March 10, 2021   The Regulatory Assistance Project submits the following comments in response to the combined Evaluation Roadmap/Inception Impact Assessment (IIA) for the revision of EU rules on market access for gas networks. The Regulatory Assistance Project (RAP) is an independent, non-partisan, non-governmental organization dedicated to accelerating the transition to a clean, reliable, and efficient energy future.   RAP recommends the Commission consider the following changes to its approach to the gas package:   Ensure that gas package revisions are made in the context of other Commission goals and objectives   The gas package revisions are occurring at a time of rapid decarbonisation of the European energy system. The European Green Deal’s path to climate neutrality by 2050, and the Fit for 55 greenhouse gas emission 2030 reduction targets, require significant changes to the European energy system. The IIA itself explains that the share of gaseous fuels will decline, and the use of fossil gases will become only a small portion of that remainder.  The gas package constitutes an important piece of that transition and needs to be addressed within the overall context of system decarbonisation. Consideration of revisions to gas market rules must therefore mean of how revision to the gas market rules add to, or fit within, a larger energy system transition. As a result, the gas package revisions must incorporate the changing, and diminishing role of gas in meeting end uses. As the Commission noted in the IIA: “Electrification is for most uses the most cost-effective and energy-efficient way to decarbonise final energy demand. The Commission provides direction in the IIA that “reforms should enable fair competition between smart electrification, energy efficiency, and renewable and low-carbon gases” To achieve this fair competition, the gas package must ensure that renewable and low-carbon gases are competing with other options on a level playing field, which incorporates GHG externalities into the analysis of possible solutions. Integration of the gas package into the broader energy system transition will allow the Commission to achieve that competition and arrive at the most efficient decarbonisation solutions.   The IIA indicates, however, that the Commission is instead addressing decarbonisation of the gas sector by developing a policy “that facilitates a progressive phase out of unabated use of fossil gases via gradual replacement of natural gas the uptake of renewable and low-carbon gases.” The IIA thus seems to anticipate that the revisions to the gas market will involve replacing fossil gas with lower-carbon molecules instead of considering the role of gas and alternative energy carriers and end-use efficiency in the energy system overall. The IIA’s specific objectives of “ensuring the emergence of cost-effective hydrogen infrastructure and contestable hydrogen markets,” “facilitating local and decentralised production of renewable and low carbon gases,” further reveal a focus on perpetuation of a broad scale a gas market, rather than a hard look at what will likely be more limited needs. Although hydrogen and low-carbon gases may be a part of a decarbonised energy system, they will likely be used for specific applications in hard-to-electrify processes such as aviation, shipping and seasonal power generation; as the Commission noted, electrification will be the most efficient solution for vehicles, and heating and cooling applications. A focus on replacing fossil gas with lower-carbon molecules will unnecessarily narrow potential options.  RAP’s recommendation to consider the gas market in the context of Europe’s broader energy system transition is in keeping with the Commission’s Energy System Integration Strategy. There, the Commission delineates the need for energy system integration – “coordinated planning and operation of the energy system ‘as a whole’ . . . as the pathway towards an effective, affordable and deep decarbonisation of the European economy in line with the Paris Agreement and the UN’s 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development.”1 Energy system integration is built by creating “stronger  1 Communication from the Commission to the European Parliament, the Council, the European Economic and Social Committee and the Committee of the Regions, Powering a climate-neutral economy: An EU Strategy for Energy System Integration, COM (2020) 299 final (Aug. 7, 2020) [hereinafter “Energy System Integration Strategy”].

Consideration of the gas system transition as a piece of the larger puzzle of energy system transition, rather than as an end in and of itself, will allow for solutions that may meet the needs of consumers and society more efficiently. Although the IIA notes this goal, a limited focus on the creation of gas markets for alternative gases, rather than on a wider range of solutions to reduce the need for fossil gas, may undermine the possibilities for a more efficient and effective solution set.   Plan for infrastructure needs in the context of meeting end uses most efficiently – avoid stranded assets  Because the most efficient way to meet end uses may no longer require gaseous fuels, a critical part of a revision to gas market rules is to consider where gas may no longer be needed. This step is particularly important when considering support or incentives for the development or maintenance of infrastructure to support gaseous fuels to avoid the creation of stranded assets. The development of new infrastructure for fossil gas, for example, once seemed a safe bet, but we are now seeing even relatively old infrastructure facing the threat of diminished usage or retirement well before it is fully depreciated. Although gaseous fuels may be needed in certain applications, as noted above, electrification, energy efficiency, and other system flexibility options may obviate the need for reliance on gaseous fuel in many applications where gas is currently used.   Incorporating integrated planning across the electric and gas systems is recommended to address this issue. Efficient and effective planning includes compiling transparent data about anticipated energy needs in the face of changing circumstances, creating potential scenarios to meet that demand that include a range of demand- and supply-side alternatives, and analyzing those scenarios against metrics, such as reliability, safety, cost, carbon impact, risk (including risk of stranded assets), equity, and resiliency. Such scenario analysis provides an opportunity to determine the most efficient ways to meet energy needs. Predetermined technological (or preferred fuel) outcomes are inconsistent with finding those efficient solutions, or most troublingly, may lead to outcomes that fail to meet GHG emission targets. Furthermore, betting on one solution over another, before system needs and options are considered, may lead to stranded assets when infrastructure is built that may become underused or obsolete. These gases will likely play a role in certain sectors of the energy system, but that role should not be artificially augmented by assuming that fossil gas will necessarily be replaced with other gaseous fuels. The IIA’s focus on hydrogen and alternative gases risks putting focus on less efficient outcomes, and the development of infrastructure in the wrong places.4 The stated objectives of “fostering hydrogen infrastructure and markets” and “integration of renewable and low-carbon gases in the existing infrastructure and markets,” presuppose the demand for hydrogen when in fact, such a focus may incentivize unneeded supply. As the IIA notes, “robust market signals and rules for hydrogen” will “create predictability needed for investments in production facilities, infrastructure and end-applications.” The creation of these market signals and rules will indeed lead to increased investment. The Commission has the opportunity in the gas package revision, however, to determine how much investment in alternative gases is in fact warranted and desirable for an efficient energy system aligned with carbon reduction targets.

Verband kommunaler Unternehmen e.V. (VKU) is the German Association of Local Utilities of municipally 
determined infrastructure undertakings and economic enterprises. These companies provide services of general 
interest in Germany within the framework of local self-government. Working in the context of an economic system 
driven by competition, they serve the interests of citizens by maintaining a service structure that counteracts the 
forces of market concentration and forms an integral part of Germany's social market economy. 
 
VKU represents the interests of the local public utility sector in Germany, which includes both supply and disposal 
services. Founded in 1949, this Association of Local Utilities has its headquarter s in Berlin. In July 2011 the VKU 
moved into the association's new headquarters in the political center of the capital. It also runs an EU 
representation in Brussels. Furthermore, the VKU is represented at federal state level with local offices. The 1,500 
member companies organized within VKU are primarily active in the fields of energy supply, water supply and 
sewage, waste management, municipal cleaning and telecommunication.

VKU welcomes the opportunity to comment on the European Commission’s inception 
impact assessment in preparation of the Hydrogen and Gas markets Decarbonisation 
Package.  
 
VKU supports the EU's decarbonisation target and is committed to accompany this path 
across all sectors and to initiate the necessary steps for the EU's complete decarbonisation 
by 2050. Gaseous energy sources will be of vital importance to achieve the energy 
transformation. They have to be allowed to play a role in achieving an integrated, reliable, 
sustainable and affordable energy system though. New legislation should be technology-
neutral, supporting the dev elopment of local energy value chains, ensuring a cost - and 
resource-efficient use in all applications, such as heating, industrial p rocesses and 
mobility. This way an inclusive and affordable transition can be ensured.  
 
The general assumption that direct  electrification is the most cost -effective and energy 
efficient way to decarbonise final energy demand is misleading. The base -line scenario, 
that is the Gas Directive and Regulation are not being modified, is not feasible. VKU 
supports the EC’s approach to develop a new policy framework supporting the uptake of 
renewable, decarbonised and low-carbon gases. The EU must not hesitate any longer to 
provide a coherent reform of the gas market and network rules that ensure clear long -
term signals to investors, incentivizes the development of innovative gas -related 
technologies and supports the transformation (including decentralization) of the whole 
gas value chain.  
 
This also heavily depends on Members States’ current energy mix and planned phase-out 
of coal and nuclear, gases will continue to bring an essential contribution to the security 
of energy supply for Europe’s industry and citizens.

1. Gas distribution infrastructure for gases – technical compatible with renewable 
gases 
  
The existing gas infrastructure already offers best conditions for transporting renewable-
, decarbonized - and low-carbon gases such as hydrogen, synthetic natural gas and 
biomethane via an extremely high-performance, safe and efficient transport and 
distribution system. Security of supply and reliability of supply will thus be maintained in 
the future.  
 
Technical compatible with renewable gases 
 
Renewable gases  are largely compatible with the existing gas transport and distribution 
system. These gases  can be transported, stored and distributed without extensive 
conversion measures in the gas infrastructure - if appropriate contracts exist between the 
market participants in the area of production, supplier and transport or storage. Synthetic 
methane and  bio-methane can be immediately fed into the grid in their entirety as 
exchange gas and used in gas applications like conventional natural gas. It can also be 
used in other infrastructures (such as storage in underground storage facilities or as a 
network buffer). In this respect, the option of blending and also the conversion of existing 
natural gas pipelines to pure hydrogen pipelines is preferable from an economic point of 
view, compared to new gas infrastructure compared to electrification. The use of already 
existing infrastructure is always cheaper and socially more acceptable than building a 
completely new infrastructure. This also applies to end -use applications. In this respect, 
the complete infrastructure for the use of synthetic gases, etc. is already in place and can 
easily be used further with some adjustments. 
 
Blending of hydrogen 
 
For the blending of hydrogen, the legally permissible proportion in Germany is currently 
up to 10 % by volume. In recent years, the DVGW (German association for gas and water) 
has conducted research projects on H2 injection possibilities and H2 compatibility for 
materials and gas appliances in the existing network. Initial studies confirmed a hydrogen 
compatibility of up to 10 %. Further DVGW research projects already show a possible feed-
in concentration of at least 20 %. In concrete terms, this depends on the respective 
structure of the connected sensitive customers. According to VKU members, an addition 
of up to 30 % hydrogen can be made without significant technical changes to the network 
infrastructure. This would also not require any obligations comparable to new 
acceptances.

2. Making distribution grids fit for blending hydrogen and transporting pure 
hydrogen  
 
Decarbonisation via the gas distribution networks must be pursued in a targeted manner. 
The political focus must also be placed on the heating market in order to reliably supply 
the classic gas application areas with increasingly renewable-, decarbonized - and low-
carbon gases. 
 
Transporting pure hydrogen to all sectors 
 
In Germany, for example, only about 600 industrial and commercial end consumers are 
connected to the transmission grid. The absolute majority of end consumers, namely 
around 1.8 million industrial and commercial customers plus around 13 million household 
customers, are supplied via the gas distribution network.  
 
The path of the distribution grids to become fit for hydrogen blends or, in future, pure 
hydrogen, must therefore be made possi ble now and secured in the long term. In this 
context, regional clusters in particular can ensure an efficient connection of the users. 
Without hydrogen in the distribution grids, a comprehensive decarbonisation of industry 
and commerce as well as the heating sector is not possible with today's technology. 
 
Step by step transformation  
 
The VKU has developed a possible scenario on the matter of the future regulation of 
hydrogen concerning Germany, which VKU would like to inform the European 
Commission.  
 
Until 2030: 
 Increased feed-in of H2 into the gas grid, share in total expected to be between 
10 and 20 % H2, based on Germany as a whole. 
 Prompt conversion of selected transport and distribution grid lines to 100 % H2 
within the framework of pilot projects, start of parallel H2 grid construction. 
 Distribution grids: Mix of p ure hydrogen grids (especially for DSOs with regional 
transport character) and natural gas grids with hydrogen admixture of 20 - 30 % 
depending on the possibilities of existing customers for decarbonisation of the 
heating market.

Until 2040: 
 Further feed-in of H2 where technically and economically viable and development 
of individual H2 networks, share in total up to 50 % of existing DSO grids, based 
on Germany as a whole. 
 Conversion of further transport pipelines to 100 % H2 as well as new construction 
of individual hydrogen pipelines 
 H2 admixture: max. 30 % in existing DSO grids depending on the possibilities with 
existing customers, conversion to 100 % hydrogen everywhere where sufficient 
H2 is available. 
By 2050: 
 Complete decarbonisation of the gas grids with climate-neutral gases. 
 Complete and area-wide H2 transport network throughout Europe 
 Parallel H2 grid expansion or rededication 
 Spatial parallel operation of natural gas and hydrogen grids ("two lines in one 
street") is only economically feasi ble for distribution grids in rare exceptional 
cases due to the high grid costs per metre of line length and per transported 
energy volume. Distribution grids are therefore operated with H2, biomethane, 
SNG or a mixture of climate-neutral gases, depending on availability and the local 
situation. 
 
3. Climate-neutral gases in the heating market 
 
In Germany almost 60% of the households are heating with gas. This amounts to 800 TWh 
of Energy provided by gas only. Through the additional use of climate-neutral gases, such 
as biomethane or synthetic natural gas, the heating sector could be decarbonised without 
triggering substantial replacement demand from end customers and would therefore 
increase acceptance. In the heating market, gaseous energy sources will be needed in the 
medium-term. Renewable-, decarbonized - and low-carbon gases are necessary in order 
to meet the new EU targets for 2030 in the heating sector. However, it is also clear that 
hydrogen is only a limited available energy carrier, especially until 2030. 
 
Potential of H2 in district heating 
 
The assessment that H2 quantities will be low for the foreseeable future and therefore 
cannot cover the needs of all sectors ultimately leads to the prioritisation  in favour of 
(heavy) industry, chemicals and heavy goods transport that is widespread in policy 
discussions. Not only the heating sector would be the one for which sufficient H2 could

no longer be available, but also for many industrial and commercial cus tomers who are 
supplied from the distribution grid. 
 
Policymakers are therefore called upon to push the market ramp -up of H2 even more 
strongly and, in particular, to promote the necessary additional expansion of renewable 
energies and to enter into targeted energy partnerships for the import of larger quantities 
of H2. This concept could be flanked by an ambitious public expansion target for climate-
neutral gases by 2030. 
 
District heating concepts already in place 
 
The district concept, which is already being applied by many municipal utilities today, 
offers considerable potential for synergy effects through sector coupling. Municipal 
utilities as local partners can contribute their know -how, e.g. in concepts with hy drogen 
as district storage or in combination with fuel cells, hybrid heat pumps or hydrogen CHP, 
if this is an economic viable option. The fuel cell  has the potential to become  a 
cornerstone in the end -customer sector. In this case the promotion of such t echnology 
could foster the development of pure hydrogen networks.  
 
The electrification of the heat market is not sufficient. The electrification of the heat 
market would exacerbate already existing restrictions and bottlenecks in the electricity 
grids. At the electricity distribution grid level, this concerns the electrification of high -
density conurbations - where there is hardly any space for new electricity lines - and the 
tunnelling of major cities for new electricity cables is also not a feasible solu tion. At the 
TSO level, the existing bottlenecks at the extra -high voltage level would be further 
exacerbated by the additional power demand. 
 
Hydrogen also plays an important role in existing buildings: with today's renovation rate 
of about 1 % per year, only about 30 % of buildings will have been renovated by 2050. By 
contrast, 70 % of today's existing buildings will be supplied with heat in 2050, just as they 
are today. Even if the refurbishment rate - as desired by politicians - increases 
significantly, a large proportion of buildings will still not be refurbished in 2050. These 
buildings can only become "Paris-compatible" if the CO2 emissions of the energy source 
used decrease over time. Therefore, the use of climate-neutral gases in the heating sector 
- in addition to other measures for building insulation and heating renovation - is essential.

H2-ready – quick and cost-efficient  
 
A significant advantage is the speed of transformation and responsiveness when calling 
up capacities in the distribution g rids: The time required to achieve H2 readiness at this 
grid level is significantly less than required for extensive electrification of the heating 
market. For example, to provide the heat demand for Frankfurt/Main electrically would 
require the constructi on of several new high -voltage lines and thus a multiple of the 
current total connection capacity. In this case, the essential question of storage and - 
subsequently - the provision of only the electrical heating capacity - parallel to the 
necessary capacity of electromobility - in winter during a nationwide dark period after the 
shutdown of the nuclear power plants and the coal-fired power plants is unresolved. 
 
4. Decentralised hydrogen production and its feed into the 
distribution grid level 
 
VKU welcomes the EC’s look into locally produced renewable, decarbonized and low -
carbon gases and addresses potential variations in gas quality. "Local hydrogen clusters" 
are being prepared at municipal level. Local public utilities are experienced in the long-
standing application of various forms of sector coupling such as combined heat and power 
and waste -to-energy. Further design of hydrogen production at distribution grid level 
should also take into account issues of grid and system serviceability.  
 
The focus should not be exclusively on transmission but on the importance of DSOs. Both 
are successively growing together in a two-way flow - long-distance gas TSO and DSO. In 
addition to a clear political signal, H2 penetration depends fundamentally on the r apid 
and sufficient provision of H2, and thus on the abandonment of the current view that 
primarily large-scale industry and thus the transport networks should participate in the 
emerging H2 market. Decentralised approaches also create the basis for value creation 
and thus jobs in the local regions. The decarbonisation of the gas distribution networks 
must be pursued in a targeted manner in order to reliably supply the classic gas 
application areas with increasingly renewable gases. In the future, they will  be able to 
offer applications for hydrogen and other "products" of electrolysis, such as oxygen or 
flexibility in the electricity grid, in sewage treatment plants, distribution and heating grids. 
The level of small, decentralised plants must be included s o that sufficient H2 quantities 
can be produced for successful decarbonisation. In order to achieve a fully competitive 
and liquid market, the source of the required hydrogen must not be seen exclusively as 
gigawatt-scale production with associated transmi ssion infrastructure, but also as local 
H2 production on a smaller scale.

In addition, local solutions can promptly create the basis for hydrogen uses, such as filling 
stations that can be safely supplied. These would not be supplied from transport pipelines 
and international hydrogen production for many years. A hydrogen truck will be able to 
leave the yard very quickly with local projects, H2 blending can be easily implemented in 
the gas distribution network, so that, where necessary, their systems can a lso be 
developed step by step with the customers. A decisive advantage of local solutions is that 
they can be implemented more rapidly.  
 
Suggestions for the supply-side market ramp-up  
 
Decentralised plants for H2 production are required so that sufficien t H2 quantities for 
successful decarbonisation can be produced in an appropriate ramp -up curve. This also 
ensures that competence and value creation for this future field can be built up in the 
municipalities. An increasing admixture of renewable, decarbonized and low -carbon 
gases where possible and feasible for the heating market creates investment security and 
can generate a supply -side market ramp-up of the necessary magnitude. The admixture 
should be balanced across all climate -neutral gases by means of a GHG reduction 
property. In this way, network sections can be optimally converted to 20 % or 100 % H2. 
 
With regard to the competition between national H2 production and H2 imports, it should 
be noted that the H2 transport costs considerably reduce the potential H2 production 
advantages of foreign countries. The Jülich Research Centre concludes that domestic 
hydrogen production is to some extent competitive even in a global comparison. 
 
5. Revisit gasses classification  
 
VKU would like to suggest to use a clear classification or t axonomy for gases, and 
therefore a consistent use of the terms “renewable, decarbonized and low-carbon gases” 
that the Commission uses itself in the Roadmap. We pledge to ensure coherence in terms 
of definitions and objectives across legislative files, not ably on sector 
coupling/integration, to deliver on the 2030 and 2050 climate objectives and to introduce 
the terms decarbonised and low -carbon gases in legal texts and include them in the EU 
GHG emission reduction objectives. Market access and grid access rules for renewable, 
decarbonised and low carbon gases needs to be legally clarified, too.

6. Using transformation potentials and avoiding lock-in effect 
 
The proposed transformation strategy, for instance, through the initially higher blending 
rate, leads to a high decarbonisation effect in the heating market in the short term. In the 
long term, the transformation path of the gas distribution networks will als o generate 
innovation pressure in connected building technology and thus further strengthen the 
effect. Investments in gas distribution network infrastructure thus actively prevent a 
"lock-in effect" and clinging to existing heating market structures. This  is especially true 
given the low renovation rates in the building stock and the insufficient availability of 
alternative energy sources, at least in the area.

7. Market based incentives 
 
Taking into consideration that EU plans to be carbon neutral by 2050 it is obvious that  
renewable and low -carbon gases will become increasingly important in order to fill the 
gap and garuantee the security, flexibility and affordability of supply where electrification 
is technically or economically not viable or in conflict with social acceptance. 
In the absence of a significant market -driven demand for climate -neutral gases the 
development of new infrastructure as well as the retrofitting of the existing infrastructure 
is doubtful. In contrast, the existance of such infrastructure is key for the development of 
forward-looking gas markets providing a level playing field and enabling cross -border 
cooperation.  
 
A common carbon pricing scheme might incentivize the deployment of renewable gases.  
In this light, VKU would like to state it is necessary to create a common European ETS in 
the long term that includes a cross-sectoral carbon price to decrease CO2 emissions cost-
efficiently. However, as this can have severe consequences due to strongly divergent 
abatement costs between the sectors, VKU is against an immediate expansion of the 
existing EU ETS on transport and buildings.  
 
We recognize that additional support schemes might be necessary to promote the 
introduction of decarbonized, low-carbon and renewable gases in order to enable a 
competion for abatement where all these gases can play a significant role. However, these 
instruments must be thought together to avoid negative interferences between the 
separate mechanisms.

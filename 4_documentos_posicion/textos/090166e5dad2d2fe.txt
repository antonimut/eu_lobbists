IN A NUTSHELL 
 
As key stakeholders in the housing and real estate sectors, we fully acknowledge the importance of 
decarbonising the building stock and energy system. Property owners, be they owner -occupier 
households or individual/professional landlords, have a crucial r ole to play to contribute towards 
achieving the 2050 climate goals, and thus we, as an association, are committed to improve the energy 
efficiency of our stock and more generally the sustainability of our built environment. 
In line with the views expressed in the Green Deal Communication and the Renovation Wave Strategy, 
we consider that better performing buildings can ensure healthier and safer environments for all.  
We acknowledge the intention of the European Commission to decarbonise our building stock and the 
objective to have more and deeper renovation. We therefore value the opportunity to share our position 
on the Revision of the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD) 2010/31/EU. 
For our sector to encourage, and for citizens to accept and support the transformation of our buildi ng 
sector, we need to guarantee that any further policy measures and actions to reach these objectives 
are conceived and implemented according to the principle of cost -effectiveness. This is one of the 
guiding principles in the promotion of “ the improvement of the energy performance of buildings within 
the Union” as enshrined in the EPBD, and in particular of Articles 1 and 2a therein, but also in the Energy 
Efficiency Directive .1 Cost-effectiveness principle and housing affordability go hand  in hand. This 
principle guarantees that economy of scale solutions are privileged when feasible and it also ensures 
that the adequate steps can be taken in a progressive way to achieve carbon neutrality by 2050 
prioritising, when necessary, staged deep renovation. It is therefore by no means an impediment to deep 
renovation.  On the contrary, the principle of cost -effectiveness will accelerate renovation process by 
encouraging financing for investments made cost-effective either through energy savings alone or with 
public support.  
 
Therefore, in our view,  the revision of the EPBD should take into consideration the following  
recommendations:  
❖ Approach the integrated vision of building renovation as an opportunity to optimise 
intervention, balance requirements and prevent overregulation; 
❖ Move from experimentation to massification of support schemes such as one -stop-shops 
empowering citizens in their renovation journey; 
❖ Address the concerns of the sector  regarding th e Mandatory Minimum Energy Performance 
Standards (MEPS) and, if MEPS are to be introduced, carefully and progressively introduce them; 
❖ Increase the reliability of the Energy Performance Certificates (EPCs). 
 
We indeed believe that the key stakeholders need to be enabled to follow up with the level of ambition 
to reach the climate objectives , and this can only be achieved with adequate funding measures and 
opportunities with the highest consideration for possible negative impact of the renovation on the 
European households, and by ensuring the mitigation of obstructive consequences.

INTEGRATED APPROACH – PREVENTING SILOS, FOSTERING SIMPLIFICATION  
 
❖ We recognise the importance to have an integrated vision of building renovation to optimise 
intervention, balance requirements and prevent overregulation.  
❖ In parallel, buildings are to be seen as  part of an integrated system, while local and 
neighbourhood approach to renovation should be fostered.  
We recognise the Commission willingness to have a more integrated approach to building renovation. 
Constructing or renovating a building is indeed not only about energy efficiency. Buildings can benefit 
from adopting a broader approach to ensure buildings’ functions and qualities, and to provide a 
positive living environment.  
Having a holistic view can encourage to optimise each intervention at building or neighbourhood level, 
generating maximum benefits  and limiting the costs . It should by no means result in increasing 
regulatory burden, on the contrary. It can balance interest and promote the most optimal and/or 
preferable solutions. An integrated vision is also the opportunity to facilitate synergies in the existing 
and upcoming legislation and avoid competing actions and regulatory redundancy  between different 
climate-oriented rules, but also between those and other technical, social, health and safet y, etc. 
measures.  
Therefore, a holistic approach is the opportunity to have an overall vision and a chance to better 
balance and prioritise, rather than pilling up, requirements to be set in the future legislation.  
In parallel, buildings are to be seen a s part of an integrated system an d neighbourhood, district and 
city level approaches to renovation should be promoted to ease economy of scale, set up local support 
instruments and create large scale system efficiencies. 
 
ONE-STOP-SHOPS – KEY TOOL TO INFORM, MOTIVATE, ASSIST AND SUPPORT 
 
❖ Move from experimentation to massification of support schemes such as one-stop-shops 
empowering citizens in their renovation journey. 
We strongly welcome the Commission’s support towards th e setting up of standardised One -Stop-
Shops (OSSs), thus recognising their crucial role. Besides ensuring well-informed and evidence-based 
decisions, OSSs do not only facilitate the retrofit process by accompanying and unburdening the 
consumers, but also enhance business opportunities for local contractors and companies, while paving 
the way towards a more sustainable environment for all. Despite the positive examples running in 
some European regions and cities, the OSS approach still faces some major obsta cles to its wider 
deployment and long-term sustainment. In particular, the business model on which OSSs are based is 
yet to be fully sustainable from an economic standpoint, given that OSSs are particularly resource and 
capital intensive (due to the investment needs in robust IT tools, development and marketing skills, 
technical competences and trainings, as well as the long duration of renovation projects). 
Therefore, the long-term vision required for the establishment and efficient management of an OSS 
demands a robust political commitment to adequate and available funding and financing 
opportunities so as to materially turn the political will into societal action.

MANDATORY MINIMUM ENERGY PERFORMANCE STANDARDS (MEPS) – PHASING IN 
CAREFULLY AND PROGRESSIVELY 
 
❖ We understand the objective of the measure but have some concerns regarding its 
conception, future implementation and impact.  
❖ Should MEPS be introduced, it would require careful assessment first, flexibility given to 
Member States, a progressive and sectoral approach coupled with additional financial 
means and safeguards.  
One of the most impactful announcements in the Renovation Wave Strategy is undeniably the 
potential introduction of mandatory minimum energy performance standards (MEPS) for existing 
buildings. We acknowledge the need for reinforced measures to decarbonise our building stock and 
the need for specific consideration of the worst performing buildings. Yet, the introduction of MEPS 
can create an obligation to modernise, regardless of the context , feasibility, economic necessity and 
financial ability. Consequently, i t can also potentially lead to an EU enforced ban on commercial 
activities due to the lack of compliance and thus, if the MEPS are not met, it might lead to abandoned 
buildings as property owners may consider it too expensive to meet the requirements. In our view, a 
market based approach on the demand and supply of energy-efficient and climate-friendly properties 
is the most effective way to guide cost-effective renovation investments in the property stock. 
Therefore, although we understand the level of ambition and the objective pursued by this measure, 
we would like to express some concerns regarding its conception and future implementation.  
Should MEPS be introduced, it  would require abiding by the following principles in order to alleviate 
its impact:  
Careful Assessment: Needless to say, a thorough impact assessment of the effects of such a measure 
based on real national data Member State by Member State – and not on average values – is needed 
for a convincing case to be made.  
It is undeniable that the assessment depends on the availability of data. Nevertheless, as stated by the 
Commission itself in the 2016 Impact Assessment of the EPBD , “detailed statistical data on national 
building stocks , linked with socio -economic indicators is a precondition for s etting obligations on 
building renovation; however, this data is currently not available”.2 Despite the tremendous work done 
in the European Building Stock Observatory and some Member States , the availability and quality of 
detailed statistical data is still problematic for some Member States and some sectors even in 2021 –
not  to mention the still remaining lack of reliability of some Energy Performance Certificates in some 
countries used in to scale the quality of the stock.   
National setting: In respect of the conferral, subsidiarity and proportionality principles, Member States 
would need to retain some level of flexibility to set the specific performance standard below which it 
would be mandatory to renovate,  the segments of building stock covered  and/or the scale of 
renovation required to accommodate different economic, climate, political and social conditions. 
Housing and rental law are still a retained competence of the Member States. National and sub -
national specifici ties make housing markets far from being homogenous across Europe, as they 
strongly differ from each other in accordance with a diverse range of cultural, social and economic 
reasons. Member States are rightly entrusted with the necessary powers and the ad equate level of 
flexibility to take into account these differences in their housing policies, which in many instances are

even further devolved to regional or local authorities.  Obviously, building stocks and energy systems 
vary greatly between Member States. Therefore, different measures and approach would be needed.   
Progressive nature: In case MEPS were to be made mandatory, their introduction needs to be of 
gradual character as owners need the time to make the necessary renovations, especially in the multi-
apartment building stock.  
Given the magnitude of the task, imposing a first step cut-off date before (at the very least) 2030 would 
be an unmanageable task at national, regional or local levels to put the necessary accompanying 
policies in place and at individual investors, citizens and co-owners associations’ levels to decide, plan, 
organise and implement the necessary work. Carefulness and progressivity, even beyond 2030, would 
be needed to guarantee not only the acceptance of the measure, but also its realistic outcome.  
As a matter of fact, both France3 and the UK4, which have opted for MEPS, have implemented or opted 
for a very progressive introduction of the obligations. 
No less important, a gradual introduction with a transition period is necessary given the remaining 
quality issues and lack of reliability of Energy Performance Certificates (EPCs) on which the MEPS are 
to be based. The cornerstone needs to be strong for the structure to stand. 
Sectoral approach: The necessity of having a different approach for different segments (i.e. residential 
vs. non-residential, rented vs. owner-occupied, public vs. private) should be guaranteed. Some degrees 
of flexibility, rather than a blank application of the measure to all segments of the building stock, are 
necessary to ensure the cost-effectiveness of the required intervention and to address the challenge 
of affordability in the housing sector. A gradual introduction of the obligation would also be preferable, 
starting with segments that can lead by example (e.g. first for public buildings, then office buildings). 
Incentivising before imposing: Promoting renovation by imposing mandatory minimum requirements 
to be met can fall short of its social objectives unless preceded by the appropriate incentives and the 
necessary support. As rightly observed by the Commission , MEPS could introduce a significant 
challenge in the building sector and “ might not be considered cost -effective in a purely financial 
perspective”5. Therefore, if a cut-off date is to be set  for the introduction of MEPS, a first logical step 
should be to put in place the right level of incentives to lower down renovation costs for specific target 
groups and sectors. The right incentives can also provide the necessary boost to go the extra mile and 
plan beyond the MEPS. 
Therefore, we strongly advise against obl igations without first ensuring appropriate financing 
dedicated to the fulfilment of these obligations. This is the reason why  we need to challenge the 
Commission as, despite the effort made at EU level, there is no way to guarantee that Member States 
will put in place the necessary “financing” that will guarantee that the monthly net expenditure of the 
citizens will be limited.

Caveats and safeguards: Since support cannot be guaranteed, caveats are vital to ensure that citizens 
will not be forced to do excessive investments . Once again, the implementation of the Renovation 
Wave measures should be guided by the principle of cost -effectiveness for all households  as well as 
professional property owners. Both France and the UK have introduced caveats to cap investments or, 
in the case of France , exempt buildings which, because of their technical, architectural or heritage 
constraints, may not be renovated in order to achieve a consumption level lower than the threshold 
mentioned and to buildings for which the cost of the work required to meet this obligation is manifestly 
disproportionate to the value of the property.  
Alternative to MEPS: Acknowledging the fact that the European Commission is currently looking into 
the possibility to extend the Emission Trading Scheme (ETS) to the building sector, it is highly relevant 
to question if the implementation of ETS  – in addition to such a detailed sectoral approach to 
regulation – could generate a risk of over-regulation. Therefore, rather than seeing the MEPS and ETS 
as complementary tools, ETS could be considered as an alternative instrument to the MEPS to reach 
policy objectives providing a consistent carbon price signal, helping to incentivise emissions reduction 
and ensuring technologic neutrality.  
Given their strong and meaningful impact as outlined in the arguments here above, we consider that 
the introduction of MEPS is a delicate matter that needs to be addresse d with the right level of 
carefulness and consideration which would allow to mitigat e any possible negative consequences it 
may bring in its worthily objective to modernise and renovate the building stock.  
 
 
ENERGY PERFORMANCE CERTIFICATES – INCREASING RELIABILITY  
 
❖ We encourage a better reliability of the EPCs that will trigger trust on behalf of its users while 
considering that real performance metrics should complement EPC and its calculated 
performance. 
The Energy Performance Certificates (EPC) should become a more reliable tool in assessing energy 
performance, in particular if the EPC is to become the screening criteria to assess the sustainability of 
real estate investments under the Sustainable Finance initiative ,6 and the benchmark instrume nt to 
set mandatory minimum energy performance level. Experts still consider that EPCs are not acceptable 
as a basis for project financing in the private sector, since they most often overestimate current energy 
consumption resulting in overoptimistic econ omic indicators. Therefore, further efforts are required 
to enhance the required qualifications to deliver EPCs that would also consequently result in a better 
reliability and trust on behalf of its users.  
We consider that real performance metrics should complement the EPC  and its calculated 
performance. This would contribute to  guarantee tailored renovation advice to building owners , 
proper design of measures planned and better coordination of the renovation steps.  It also has the 
potential to increase predicted savings accuracy , which could contribute to guarantee savings  to 
facilitate financing and rental arrangements.  
Real performance metrics are important but not sufficient. It is also important that EPCs include actual 
potential energy savings  expressed in real terms (reduced KWh/year) and not only expressed in 
Primary Energy Demand (PED) or potential cost savings. PED and cost savings are dependent on 
political decisions and price variations respectively, so they are uncertain.

Finally, w e acknowledge the need to address  the synergies and complementarities of different

CONCLUDING REMARKS 
The Renovation Wave and its proposed measures, including the revision of the EPBD, bring along a 
significant level of ambition. We acknowledge the fact that the renovation can bring positive impacts, 
as detailed in the inception impact assessment . Nevertheless, in this pioneering project the key 
stakeholders need to be enabled to follow up with the level of ambition, and this can only be achieved 
with adequate funding measures and opportunities with the highest consideration for possible 
negative impact of the renovation on the European households , and by ensuring the mitigation of  
obstructive consequences. 
We are currently facing unprecedent challenges and consequently actions are needed, also from EU 
citizens, the real estate sector and the construction value chain. The Renovation Wave overall is the 
occasion to address those challenges, but it should be done with and for the citizens, with a better 
balance between means and obligations.

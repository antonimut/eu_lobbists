 
 
 
 
 
Position paper on  the EPBD roadmap 
 
Through this position paper, Aedes would like to give her comments on the inception impact assessment of the 
Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD). Aedes is the Dutch association for social housing providers. 
Together, Aedes members manage 2.4 million dwellings, constituting 32 percent of the total housing stock. Aedes 
supports the objectives of the European Green Deal and the 55% 2030 -target. Our sector is committed to 
improving the energy performance of the building stock.  
 
In this position paper we firstly advocate that  non-regulatory measures (option b) is the preferred option for 
Aedes. This is followed up  by an overview of the position of Aedes on Minimum Energy Performance Standards 
(MEPS), which is the most significant proposal mentioned in the inception impact ass essment. We conclude by 
giving recommendations of elements to consider in the impact assessment of the European Commission for the 
EPBD. 
 
Preferable option: non-regulatory measures 
Aedes supports the objectives of the European Green Deal and the Renovation Wave. Therefore we acknowledge 
that the renovation rate in the built environment needs to increase. We do, however, not favor regulatory changes 
in the EPBD. The latest revision of the EPBD was in 2018 and has only recently b een transposed into national 
legislation. For the sake of regulatory stability, we are not in favor of another revision of the EPBD. 
 
Furthermore, we argue that the main obstacle for an increase of building renovation, is the funding gap. Building 
renovation is not financially viable. In vestments are not earned back by lower energy bills. A recent study 
conducted by the Dutch government and Aedes demonstrates thisi.  
 
Building renovation is a cost item for social housing providers for which they do not always have sufficient funds. 
Introducing regulatory obligations does not solve this issue. Therefore we prefer non-regulatory measures (option 
b), such as an increase of available public funding for building renovation and measures to incentivize highly 
needed cost reductions from the renovation industry.

Position on MEPS 
We assess that introducing MEPS are not the only route to a decarbonized building stock. In the energy agreement 
of 2013, the Dutch social housing sector voluntary committed itself to reaching energy label B (EI 1,4) on average. 
The chart below demonstrates that the sector will reach this target in this yearii. 
 
 
 
 
 
Different European countries have chosen different strategies in decarbonizing the building stock. While the 
Netherlands have chosen for voluntary agreements and a focus on sector level, France decided to legally phase

The graphs above demonstrate that there is no clear indication on which approach works best. The housing stock 
and energy landscape may differ from member state to member state. Furthermore the costs of building 
renovation differs from member state to member state as well, as building renovation is labor intensive and there 
are large differences between the costs of labor across the EU. Finally, the housing market itself differs across 
member states. Some countries, such as the Netherlands, are faced with a major housing crisis which makes it 
necessary to ensure that sufficient investments in new construction are done as well.  
 
Given these large differences, we do not favor one -size-fits all solutions from the European level. There is no 
silver bullet to building renovation yet and the differences between member states are too large to impose a 
European standard for building renovation upon member states. 
 
We point out that the new headline target of 55% reduction in 2030, automatically leads to higher ESR -targets 
for members states. In the Netherlands, a committee of senior civil servants deliver ed a report that sets out  a 
list of measures describing how the new 55% target can be translated into national policy iii. For the built 
environment, this concerns, among other things, measures that work towards an insulation standard from 
different growth paths. We prefer this national based approach as it takes the specific characteristics of the Dutch 
housing market in to consideration.

Consideration for an impact assessment 
While Aedes is generally speaking not in favor of MEPS, we highlight that for any form of MEPS to work; sufficient 
room for implementation at the national level should be given to member states . We advocate for a thorough 
impact assessment on the implications of a possible introduction of MEPS, that takes the following elements into 
consideration 
 
1. Assessment of the funding gap: In order for MEPS to work, sufficient public funding is needed.   
Aedes established that phasing out the worst performing buildings of the Dutch social housing stock alone (energy 
label E-F-G) would cost 5,9 billion euro. The costs of this exercise exceeds the full share of funding available to 
the Netherlands in the RRF, while at this point it remains unclear if funding from the RFF will be redirected at 
building renovation at all.  
 
➢ We recommend the European Commission to assess, in detail, what the required investments are 
for the introductions of MEPS and if sufficient funds are available at European, national and 
sectoral level. 
 
2. Affordability for tenants: The IIA states that the introduction of MEPS may help to alleviate energy poverty. 
This seems to be based on the assumption that investments in building renovation are earned back by lower 
energy bills. In reality, this is often not the case as investments in building renovations have large unprofitable 
tops. A recent Dutch study demonstrates that investments in building renovation are financially unviable. Housing 
corporations chose not to pass on the costs of this to the tenant. If they did, this would actually mean that the 
tenant's housing costs would increase, despite the lower energy bill.   
 
➢ We recommend the European Commission to thoroughly examine the impact of the introduction 
of MEPS on the affordability of housing for tenants. 
 
3. Crowding out investments in new construction: Social housing providers operate from the principle of  
cost neutrality for the tenants when renovating their building stock: after a renovation, rents may be increased, 
but not with a higher amount than the decrease of the energy bill. Since building renovation is not financially 
viable, this means that the remaining costs are borne by the social housing provider. From the perspective of a 
social housing provider, building renovation is not cost neutral at all. A significant sum of the investment cannot 
be earned back by future rent increases and is financed by the social housing provider, which impacts the ability 
of social housing providers to invest in new construction. Without detracting from the importance of building 
renovation, we also emphasize that this should not come at the expense of investments in new construction as 
the Netherlands currently has a housing shortage of 331.000 homes. 
 
➢ We recommend the European Commission to assess to what extent the required investments for 
the introduction of MEPS will crowd out investments in new construction.

4. Cost developments for building renovation: For a renovation wave to succeed, it is essential to decrease 
the unprofitable top of building renovation, by decreasing the costs of building renovation. Industrialization 
is the key word here. Whi le positive developments are occurring, there is clearly no silver bullet yet for 
building renovation. Consequently, the costs of renovation are still too high.  
 
➢ In order to analyze the financial impact of MEPS, we recommend the European Commission to 
(continuously) evaluate the cost developments for building renovation.

performance of the Dutch social building stock.

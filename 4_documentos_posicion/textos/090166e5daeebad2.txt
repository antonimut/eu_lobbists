The Renovation Wave strategy is one of key EU Green Deal initiatives introduced by the European 
Commission to accelerate the renovation of buildings, aiming to reduce emissions and energy use to 
support climate targets, tackle energy poverty, decarbonise heating and cooling. Buildings will be 
critical in delivering an ambitious Green Deal while putting Europe on a sustainable recovery path in 
the context of the current COVID-19 crisis. 
 
The building sector is crucial for achieving the EU's energy and environmental goals, as it represents 
more than 30% of EU’s emissions and energy consumption. According to the European Commission, 
almost 75% of the EU’s building stock is inefficient according to current building standards and 85 -
95% of the buildings that exist today will still be standing in 2050. In EU households, heating and hot 
water alone account for 79% of total final energy use (192.5 Mtoe ). According to 2019 figures from 
Eurostat, approximately 75% of heating and cooling is still generated from fossil fuels while only 22% 
is generated from renewable energy. Moreover, only approximately 16.5% of heating and cooling is 
efficiently generated through high efficiency CHP, with more than 70% of heat today being supplied 
through inefficient boilers . Thus, to achieve the cost -effective and efficient decarbonis ation of the 
building sector, the EPBD revision should better consider the role of energy efficiency first principle 
and energy systems integration. Early actions on energy efficiency accelerate measures to avoid the 
long-term lock-in of inefficient energy use. In that regard, better and more energy efficient buildings 
improve the quality of citizens' life while bringing additional benefits to the economy and the society.  
 
In response to the  EPBD revision Inception Roadmap , COGEN Europe supports option 3 of the 
inception impact assessment, fostering higher ambition  for energy efficiency and system 
integration in the building sector. In our view, the EPBD should be revised and designed in such a 
way to promote those solutions that enable significant energy savings and stabilise the energy 
system in a long -term perspective. Given the imperative for rapid and effective action to reduce 
climate impacts, solutions that provide a proven and fast deployment must be prioritised for early 
action to deliver higher efficiency and climate ambition as part of the EU Green Deal and the fit for 
55 framework, high efficiency cogeneration should be better addressed under the EPBD. 
 
To strengthen the legal framework on energy performance of buildings, a comprehensive policy mix 
is needed to set clear objectives and ambition, allowing for integrated planning at national and 
regional levels. COGEN Europe believes that the following points should be taken into consideration:

1. Foster energy efficiency first principle in decarbonisation of buildings, complementing the 
uptake of renewable energy sources. 
 
It is important to apply energy efficiency across the entire energy value chain, maximising 
system efficiency through the optimum local mix of end use, transmission, distribution and 
supply side efficiency measures. This can be done by focusing on and rein forcing primary 
energy savings and integrated local planning of infrastructure, including the building sector.  
Moreover, “energy efficiency first” requires that efficiency criteria should be applied to all 
possible energy sources used in buildings:  
 
o Firstly, set a level playing field between demand and supply side efficiency , 
ensuring that consumers have the option to choose cost-effective solutions that are 
tailored to their circumstances and which support overall system efficiency.  
o Secondly, end use electrification should be subject to efficiency criteria, ensuring 
that the primary energy used to produce the additional (marginal) electricity used 
for heating or EVs linked to buildings is coming from increasingly renewable sources 
and/or effi cient sources. Promoting cogeneration to complement direct 
electrification will ensure that the electricity produced at times of high heat 
demand from heat pumps (and increasingly EVs) is highly efficient1.  
o Thirdly, renewable-based fuels (e.g., all gases, bioenergy, hydrogen, geothermal, 
waste heat, solar thermal) should be used efficiently to ensure resource efficiency, 
as well as carbon reductions and even negative emissions. Thermally produced 
energy will still be required in buildings, whenever electrification is not technically 
possible (e.g., in heritage buildings) or too costly (e.g., where upgrading power grids 
and power prices would make it too expensive)2. Whenever fuels for thermal energy 
production are used to generate heat and/or power for buildings, cogeneration 
should be prioritised as the most efficient way to maximise the use of those fuels.  
 
2. Buildings should be considered as part of an overall locally integrated energy system. This 
can be achieved linking the EPBD with the Energy System Integration and Hydrogen 
Strategies, as well as the upcoming Green Gas Package.  
 
The diverse European building stock requires customised approaches to account for existing 
infrastructure, available resources, customer preferences and climatic conditions. Mobilising 
all relevant solutions at local level by efficiently integrating electricity, heat and gas systems 
can provide  the optimum mix for a specific region, district and /or building. While direct 
electrification of heat and transport is identified as key solutions to decarbonise  buildings, 
this may not be applicable to all European buildings. Electrifying heating and cooling in 
buildings efficiently and via renewable energy presents challenges related to the limitations 
of the power system to meet supply and demand reliably when and where needed. 
Therefore, end use electrification and the uptake of variable renewable power will need to 
be complemented  by alternative solutions such as district heating, high efficiency

3. Long-term Renovation Strategies (EPBD, Article 2a) and Heating & Cooling Comprehensive 
Assessments (EED, Article 14) should be better synchronised to foster coherence of plans 
and actions in buildings. 
 
Better integrated Member State LTRS and Heating & Cooling Comprehensive Assessments 
would have served as the ideal input and contribution to the Renovation Wave, at the same 
time reinforcing the EPBD, while simultaneously enforcing the implementation of European 
legislation, to the benefit of European citizens. Currently, not all LTRS are even submitted to 
the EC, while 2020 comprehensive assessments are only published for five countries (DE, DA, 
FI, FR, NL). Therefore, both documents should be taken into account in the EPBD revision to 
successfully assess energy performance standards in buildings. It is important to 
simultaneously optimise demand and supply solutions, considering the local context and 
consumer circumstances for heating and cooling. Depending on regional and local options 
and the specific economic situation, different approaches and technology solutions need to 
be considered. Supporting Member States in delivering ambitious and better synchronized 
comprehensive assessments and LTRS can contribute  to more effective identification of 
potentials for district heating, cogeneration, renewable/decarbonised gases and other  
heating solutions. 
 
4. Reinforce the requirement for building codes to account for electricity generation mix 
displaced or used by different energy solutions in buildings. 
 
Building performance methodology should take into account efficiency and carbon factors 
that better reflect the decarbonisation potentials of specific energy solutions. With respect 
to heating and cooling, Member States should aim at accounting for the seasonality of heat 
and the marginal power mix utilised for heating. The use of electricity in heating is as 
efficient and carbon intensive as the electricity mix available in a country. The FFE study 
shows that the EU electricity mix used for heating is different than the average power mix, 
namely it is approximately 35% efficient and has a carbon footprint of 986gCO2/kWh. Even 
when a very efficient heat pump uses this electricity (e.g., COP of 3), the resulting heat ing 
efficiency is around 100% and its carbon emissions could reach 330gCO2/kWh, which in some 
countries may go counter to both efficiency and carbon reductions objectives. It is therefore 
important that Member States consider the marginal electricity mix, which will ensure that 
consumers can fairly compare different heating technologies and would incentivise further 
innovation in demand response and system optimisation. Financing and support schemes 
should be  directed towards the uptake of efficient solutio ns like cogeneration, including 
micro-cogeneration in buildings or cogeneration with district heating, in line with identified 
potentials at EU, national and local levels.

6. Reinforce the Smart Readiness Indicator (SRI) framework for buildings, including its role 
for the digitalisation of heating and cooling. 
 
The revision of EPBD should also serve the purpose of spurring innovation  rate and making 
smarter buildings. Smart Readiness Indicators (SRI) for buildings should be reinforced as they 
would accelerate the process of digitalisation in heating and cooling and enable consumers 
to become act ive contributors to the energy system, through demand response, but also 
through self -production and -consumption of electricity and heat. Innovative energy 
solutions for buildings, like on -site cogeneration including via fuel cells, have the capability 
to self-produce electricity and heat, including at times of peak demand or when intermittent 
renewables are not generating; these appliances can be aggregated (e.g., to MWe -scale 
capacity) and remotely controlled and modulated, in the form of a flexible virt ual power 
plant. Moreover, SRI might be considered to enhance measures on sustainable mobility 
(recharging points for electric vehicles) and promote the more environmentally friendly 
operation of building equipment at system level. While SRI is currently voluntary for member 
states, it should gradually become a common European scheme.

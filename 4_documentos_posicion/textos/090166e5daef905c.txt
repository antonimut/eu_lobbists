Preliminary remarks 
o Buildings decarbonization is now widely considered as of the outmost importance to reach our energy 
and climate goals. As outlined in the EU Renovation Wave, to achieve the 55% emission reduction 
target, by 2030 the EU should reduce buildings’ GHG emission s by 60%. As of today,  97% of our 
buildings are inefficient 1, while 75% of the fuels for heating and cooling in buildings come from fossil 
fuels2.  
o Despite large amount of public and private money invested, we are facing a situation where the 
decarbonization of the existing stock is not happening fast enough 3. On top of this, and despite a 
successful review in 2018 with adequate provisions to speed up the deployment of solutions able to 
significantly reduce buildings GHG emissions and achieve cost -effecting energy savings, such as 
building automation and controls, and to set up long -term renovations milestones for the EU Member 
States building stocks, the EPBD implementation is facing significant delay in its implementation 4. 
o Achieving –55% without implementing the current pieces of legislation (which were adopted under a -
40% GHG emissions reductions ambition for 2030) will be “Mission: Impossible”. The European 
Commission should monitor and track progress of EU Member States, and set up the right incentives to 
support a fast implementation of the current buildings rules. 
o Within this challenging context, the decarbonization of commercial and industrial buildings must be 
prioritized. The relative annual GHG reduction per non -residential reno vation (comparing the 
performance of the building before and after renovation) is estimated to be roughly 18% (compared to 
9% for residential renovation). Tackling non -residential buildings energy efficiency are also easier than 
in residential buildings because of the frequent change of activity (every 3 to 5 years). While targeting 
non-residential buildings, the focus must be on the existing stock.. Converting 30% of the building stock 
by 2030 (i.e. 3% per year) to fully electrified heating with heat pumps  and digitized with 50% energy 
efficiency gains would lead to: – 20% reduction in energy demand – 35% reduction in emissions 
(assuming 40% decarbonization of the electricity mix)5.

1. Introduce Mandatory Minimum Requirements with CO2 milestones for buildings (based on final 
energy)  
 
As highlighted in the Renovation Wave, we need to set-up mandatory minimum requirements in order to deliver 
on the 60% GHG reduction for the building stock by 2030.  We must mandate renovation work at European level 
in order to have an impact across the continent. For this, we need to conceive a regulatory instrument that creates 
both decarbonisation and economic value. The European Commission suggested to introduce mandatory 
minimum requirements in its recent Renovation Wave Strategy 6. We support such dir ection. We believe the 
introduction of mandatory minimum requirements must be based on three principles; 
o We need long -term CO2/final energy reduction milestones for the building sector in order to set -up a 
clear direction and to accelerate the renovation m arket in Europe. The yearly deep energy efficiency 
renovation rate barely reaches 0,2% for both residential and non -residential buildings. Based on the 
current renovation rate, we estimate it would take approximately centuries to renovate the building sector 
in Europe to a way that complies with our climate objective. Such milestones must differentiate between 
non-residential building and residential buildings (non-residential buildings must be early mover because 
of their innovation/cost saving potential 7). We recommend making mandatory the achievement of the 
milestones for tertiary buildings. In that regard, the French tertiary decree must be used as a benchmark 
(see the table below).  The French law prescribed all tertiary buildings to reduce their final  energy 
consumption by -40% by 2030, -50% by 2040, and -60% by 2050. 
o Milestones must go hand and hand with the adoption of metrics that must be upgraded in time of property 
transaction. Those metrics shall act as ‘trigger points’ and must be adjusted to th e type of buildings (in 
particular residential versus non-residential). The metrics must go beyond energy efficiency, looking at 
the systemic efficiency impact per technology in order to achieve economy of scale and avoid silos 
between energy efficiency / renewable energy integration. These shall reflect the state -of-the-art 
technology available per type of building and enable the measurement of either individual building 
performance OR district building performance based on actual energy consumption /CO2 emissions per 
buildings.  You will find in annex I a list of technologies as a baseline to create a metrics for buildings.  
Such set of metrics must be integrated into a reviewed Energy Performance Certificate system (see next 
point). 
o We must change the met hodology baseline of the EPBD. Today, it is based on p rimary energy 
consumption for heating and cooling (in average, 60% to 70% of the real energy consumption of 
buildings). As the BPIE argues, “ the current approach, using the Primary Energy Factors (PEFs)  is 
detrimental to understanding the real energy performance of a building ”8. With the imperative need to

France “Tertiary Decree” – Mandatory final energy consumption reduction targets for tertiary buildings 
• The French Tertiary Decree entered into force in October 2019 in France and specified the implementation 
of the article 175 of the ‘loi Elan’ that prescribed all tertiary buildings to reduce their final energy consumption 
by -40% by 2030, -50% by 2040, and -60% by 2050.  
• The decree target applies to all tertiary buildings above 1000m2 (all public and private buildin gs with a few 
exceptions) and proposes two methods to achieve the targets. 
 First option, building owners/occupants must demonstrate that they fulfill with the final energy consumption 
reduction objectives (2030, 2040 and 2050 – see above) based on a year o f reference that needs to be 
2010 or after. 
 Second option, building owners/occupants takes a commitment to achieve a final real energy consumption 
by a given year based on a set of performance level, which is being set-up by the public authorities (to be 
adjusted per type of activities/buildings). 
• The decree includes a list of actions to achieve the energy performance objective (thermal performance, 
energy management, maintenance, behavioral changes etc).  A digital platform has been introduced where 
buildings owners/occupants must report their final energy consumption (based on a few criteria) to keep 
track of the level of final energy consumption per building. Penalties are being included in case of non -
conformity. 
 
 
2. Toward a new Energy Performance Certificate system (EPBD) 
 
One of the major gaps in the improvement of energy performance of the building sector is the lack of reliable 
instrument to drive investment at European level. On one hand, market incentives such as private labels (LEED, 
BREAM) are far too complex and do not focus enough on reaching a zero-energy consumption for buildings. On 
the other hand, the implementation of national Energy Performance Certificate regimes have led to a diverse set 
of instruments, varying in terms of scope and available information, res ulting in some cases in limited reliability, 
compliance, market penetration and acceptance at countries level. Aspects like smart data usage, energy 
flexibility and the impact of energy management systems are not covered in current EPC regimes. A reform of  
the Energy Performance Certificate is to be one of the critical priorities of the EPBD reform. It must look at four 
points 
o Firstly, A certain number of key indicators must be fully harmonized across Europe, in particular, the 
indication of the building pe rformance in CO2 emission/final energy so to enable a better engagement 
by end-users9. 
o Secondly, alongside the introduction of metrics (see the previous paragraph), the Energy Performance 
Certificate must take into account a wider range of parameters to e stimate the actual energy 
performance of a building, in particular management, control and monitoring mechanisms. Annex I will 
give an overview of key technologies to be taken into account.  
o Thirdly, the closer integration of EPC databases and digital buil ding logbooks (in particular for non -
residential buildings) must be another priority. Digital building logbooks could bring a level of granularity 
which could be critical for some buildings. For instance, the Smart readiness indicators (BMI), BIM 
models and environmental certification systems have to be part of the EPC. In particular, we recommend 
the Smart Readiness Indicator (SRI) to become a mandatory part of EPC for buildings above 290kW. 
SRI is, as of today, the best tool available to asses energy perf ormance of tertiary buildings (see also 
the point number 4 on how to accelerate a data-based performance for buildings).

3. Accelerating digital technologies deployment in buildings  
 
Digitalization has huge potential to accelerate the transition to a low carbon and circular economy. For instance, 
the combination of hardware and software technologies in buildings could generate up to 50% energy savi ngs 
with a ROI 3 to 5 years. Digitalization comes as a key enabler to combine efficiency, sustainability and 
decarbonization within the building environment.  Digital is removing inefficiencies through use of 5D BIM, 
modelling all aspects of buildings, orga nizing the ecosystem of players, ensuring timely execution, offering 
transparency of works, and certifying decarbonization labels. It gives a new perspective to building management. 
We must reflect the importance of digital technology in the EU regulatory framework, we need to; 
• Trigger the deployment of EU indicators and indicators looking at the impact of digital tools for building 
sustainability= Gradually incorporate a data-model approach within the Smart Readiness Indicator’, SRI 
• Reform of the energy performance certificate to promote a data -model approach in reference points 
about their level of performance.  
• Promote digital exchange of product descriptions and service descriptions, based on standardized data 
formats (e.g. ecl@ss advanced). 
• Support/mandate the roll-out of digital design and operating tools (Building Information Modelling (BIM) 
in construction work (and renovation work when applicable). 
• Identify others digital tools providing value-added in term of circularity/efficiency and flexibility. 
 
 
 
4. Set up the right framework to boost smart charging at building level (AFID and EPBD) 
 
The Sustainable and Smart Mobility Strategy unveiled in December 2020 recalled the need for a 90% reduction 
in greenhouse gas emissions from transport, in order for the EU to become a climate-neutral economy by 2050.To 
achieve this systemic change, electri fication of transport and infrastructure will have to be ramped up. In this 
context, electric vehicles (EVs) sales are rapidly increasing. Indeed, if costs of EVs continue to decrease and are 
on a par with internal combustion engine vehicles by 2024 for light vehicles and by 2027 for heavy vehicles, then 
half of all new sale vehicles will be EVs shortly after 2025 for light vehicles and shortly after 2030 for heavy 
vehicles.10 There is thus a need to boost the numbers and availability of electric charging points in all Member 
States and for all modes of transport. On top of this, there is a need to expand private and semi-private charging 
: recent figures show a need to deploy around 290 million charging points by 2040, with home, workplace and 
private commercial charging accounting for 78% of this investment 11, to support growing EV needs. However, 
the electricity system simply cannot cope with the increased uptake of electric vehicles (EVs) unless their use of 
the electricity system is smartly managed. 
The Fit for 55 Package should stimulate the shift towards private charging, while enabling the adequate 
framework for the integration of those distributed energy resources: 
 Set up a target as part of the AFID revision in terms of smart charging capabilities with interoperability and 
bi-directionality obligations :  the Commission should complement the existing requirements set in art. 8 of 
the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD) by mandating 100% smart charging capabilities for 
all common new charging infrastructure in multi-family and large non-residential buildings.  
 Strengthen the provisions for the deployment charging stations in buildings 12: the EPBD has introduced in 
2018 provisions to accelerate the roll -out of minimum cabling infrastructures in both non -residential and 
residential buildings, which are new or undergoing major renovations.

ES-SO, European Solar Shading Organzation  comments on the Inception Impact Assessment 
“Revision of the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive 2010/31/EU” 
ES-SO welcomes the EU  Renovation Wave as announced by the EU Commission last October 2020, 
containing an action plan with concrete regulatory, financing and enabling measures, with the 
objective to at least double the annual energy renovation rate of buildings by 2030 and to foster 
deep renovations.  It is also convinced  that the  revision of the Energy Performance of Buildings 
Directive (EPBD) is necessary as one of the vehicles to deliver on the Renovation Wave.  
ES-SO’s input aim is to focus on provisions that are central to delivering a Renovation Wave that 
contribute to emission reduction, taking into people’s health and well- being  and the attention of 
energy poverty.  
 
Facing reality of global warming and the effect on human beings and buildings 
In the JRC 2020 report on global warming and human impacts of heat and cold extremes in the EU it 
was recorded  that during last decade  heatwaves are becoming  more intense and during  longer 
periods;  while all-time temperature records were broken in many locations in Europe. These events 
are projected to happen more frequently and become more intense with climate change. During 
COVID time last year we also noticed around Europe that increased death fatalities in summer were 
related to heatwaves.  Looking long term when keeping global warming to 1.5°C  limit, mortality from 
extreme heat would reach to around 30,000 fatalities/year whereas milder winters will reduce 
significantly exposure to and fatalities from extreme cold.  
According to the survey on income and living conditions in Europe (EU SILC) approx. 1/5 of the 
European population, this is  100 million people, is unable to keep their dwelling cool in summer, 
which means that overheating occurs. Only half as much, 50 Mio people, report to be unable to keep 
their dwelling warm in winter.  This is in strange contrast with a  footnote (4) of  the Inception Impact 
Assessment footnote  that only refers to 34 Mio Europeans who can hardly keeping  their homes 
warm.  
The World Health Organisation confirms the estimation that people nowadays  spend approximately 
90 % of their time indoors in residential and non-residential buildings, and the Covid-19 crisis has 
highlighted the importance of indoor comfort and wellbeing as many people have to work from/ stay 
at home.   
Besides the effect of global warming, as a consequence of EPBD, overheating occurs now also more 
in new buildings which are more insulated and air- tight constructed.

Reports on overheating of buildings around Europe  
 
EPBD 2010 – 2018  analysis on overheating  
In 2010 the EPBD has laid  the  focus on avoiding overheating by passive measures, at least in its 
preamble, recital 25: “Recent years have seen a rise in the number of air-conditioning systems in 
European countries. This creates considerable problems at peak load times, increasing the cost of 
electricity and disrupting the energy balance. Priority should be given to strategies which enhance 
the thermal performance of buildings during the summer period. To that end, there should be focus 
on measures which avoid overheating, such as shading and sufficient thermal capacity in the building 
construction, and further development and application of passive cooling techniques”. 
Overheating was also explicitly mentioned in the “ANNEX I Common general framework for the 
calculation of energy performance of buildings (referred to in Article 3) 1.  
“The energy performance of a building shall be determined on the basis of the calculated or actual 
annual energy that is consumed in order to meet the different needs associated with its typical use 
and shall reflect the heating energy needs and cooling energy needs (energy needed to avoid 
overheating) to maintain the envisaged temperature conditions of the building, and domestic hot 
water needs.” 
Compare with EPBD Revision 2018,  where in the Annex I, pt 1 the reference to overheating was 
deleted and replaced by the  following :   
“1. The energy performance of a building shall be determined on the basis of calculated or actual 
energy use and shall reflect typical energy use for space heating, space cooling, domestic hot water, 
ventilation, built-in lighting and other technical building systems”. 
The focus on overheating with its potential risk on increased energy use  which was addressed in the 
former EPBD’s has thus been removed in the  EPBD 2018.    
EPBD revision needs a mandatory harmonised approach on the overheating risk 
In some European countries “overheating” has become  a point of attention, but restricted to new 
buildings only as it was occurred as a side effect of better insulated and air tight construction.  
However there is no harmonised approach. In the National Common general framework 
methodology for the calculation of energy performance of buildings where the overheating risk is 
considered mostly it is estimated at building level whereas it should be at room level or at façade 
level; the overheating temperature is calculated as an average estimated temperature at monthly 
level instead of at hourly level; or an estimated number of exceeding hours per year above a certain 
temperature; and that all based on outdated climate data model  instead of a future predicting 
climate which prevents to estimate the realistic need for cooling. New insulated and air tight 
appartments with one façade are particularly highly affected. 
The consequence is that designers struggle with the problem of overheating in sacrificing window 
size in order to reach the building’s energy performance requirements. However, there are plenty of 
studies providing evidence that windows are essential for people’s health and well-being. Daylight 
plays a key role in providing a good visual, biologically effective and energy efficient lighting of indoor 
spaces. Daylight is the light source preferred by humans.  Windows needs to be assessed on their 
energy balance, which is not only on heat losses but also on heat gains useful for reducing energy 
consumption on heating and lighting.

In the previous EPBD 2018 daylight was mandatory to be considered in the Annex I, point 4.  Instead 
of reducing the window ratio we need to increase this preferably up to 33% in our homes for health, 
wellbeing, but also for energy savings.  As overheating is now generally badly considered we notice 
recent years all over Europe ( thus not only in Southern Europe) an exponential growth of high 
energy  consuming mobile airco devices on the market as citizens seek instant cooling for their 
homes. Energy poverty counts also when people are not able to  tool their dwelling.  
To cool a building is to prevent in the first place that the solar radiation becomes heat when entering 
the building. This is the most natural defense and should always be the first choice before applying 
air conditoning, which needs energy production investments, as the second step. 
 
RECOMMENDATION:  the evaluation of the overheating risk has to be evaluated mandatory and with 
a harmonised approach in the next  EPBD revision, not only for new built but also for renovation.  
 
EPBD Revision with recognition of dynamic solar shading as technical building system, respecting 
the energy efficiency first principle   
Dynamic solar shading, especially when it is automated is an intelligent system that makes external 
and internal solar shading devices operate automatically. The solar shading device receives real-time 
input from sensors, such as sun, wind, temperature and combines this with pre-set data and 
thresholds based on the requirements from both facility managers and tenants to save energy and 
provide comfort. By managing the appropriate quantity of daylight and solar gain inside buildings, 
dynamic solar shading helps to reduce dramatically/optimizes air conditioning during the cooling 
season, while decreasing heating consumption during the heating season. In a single-family house, 
external shutters reduce the total energy demand for heating and cooling by 38%. If 75% of the 
windows in Europe are installed with dynamic solar shading the potential energy savings can accrue 
up to 19% saving in heating and cooling energy use (or 49.3 Mtoe/yr) and have a carbon emissions 
reduction of 19% (equivalent to a saving of 117 MtCO2/yr). 
RECOMMENDATION : Include “solar shading” among the technical building systems in the definition 
provided by EPBD Art. 2 and consider policy proposals to enhance the use of solar shading in new 
and renovated buildings to prevent overheating, respecting the energy efficiency first principle. 
Subsidies for solar shading should also be part of the EU Renovation Wave funding.

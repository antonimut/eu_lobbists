  
 
 Energy Performance of Buildings Directive Inception Impact Assessment  Introduction and abstract Delivering the Renovation Wave strategy’s ambitions while addressing the Climate and Biosphere Emergency at the same time, requires changes to the EPBD. The present document presents the main recommendations of the Architects’ Council of Europe (ACE) to tackle the problems identified in the EPBD inception Impact Assessment.  In summary, the ACE recommends:  1. Putting greater emphasis on the role of lifespan of buildings and components to manage the life-cycle impact of buildings;  2. Embedding feedback and the validation of achieved performance in use in the EPBD to accelerate innovation, raise architectural quality and to facilitate accountability for performance;  3. Ensure robust data and evidence base to empower research and promote best practice; 4. Consider and improve all available mechanisms for scaling up renovationswhile improving quality of life in Europe. Details of these recommendations as well as additional points are placed in the Annex of this paper (“ACE key messages in the area of building performance and sustainability” ; from page 3).  ACE comments on the expected impacts of the revision of the EPBD § Economic impacts In addition to the Commission’s points ACE advocates that it is essential to invest in qualitative as well as quantitative performance improvements of buildings Requiring the validation of EPCs with measured data would facilitate the feedback loops required to incentivise this. It is also important to invest in skills, awareness and building resilience before investing in technological and digital solutions that have a short life-span and require a high degree of expertise for design, construction and maintenance. Craftsmanship and skills needed for deep retrofit offer greater returns in terms of economic, social and environmental value than high-risk technological solutions. Again, mandating feedback and benchmarking of achieved performance in use will highlight the whole life costs of high-maintenance solutions and allow the sector for better plan for these. § Social impacts For positive social and cultural impacts ACE recommends  - that retrofit initiatives are accompanied by large-scale retrofit of housing for low-income occupants together with neighbourhood social care, health care, educational and inclusivity measures - to ensure that retrofit initiatives are accompanied by investment in local green renewal, sustainable transport and play infrastructure.  § Environmental impacts The current uptake of low impact retrofits is a major challenge. To scale up retrofit rates ACE recommends enhancing mechanisms for neighbourhood scale retrofits to include green infrastructure and renewal and to encourage community self-organisation.  The future EPBC should provide better incentives for life cycle extension instead of the replacement of buildings. Retrofit in most cases is lower cost and significantly lower environmental impact than newbuild. Most existing buildings, not just historic buildings, have embodied value in their material density and spatial

Most such buildings can become more resilient to the heating climate through design measures to tackle overheating than new buildings that are more lightweight and usually of smaller floor to ceiling height. Building on this resilience and capturing its value should be central to the revision of the EPBD.  ACE main recommendations for the review of the EPBD   1. Put greater emphasis on the role of lifespan   Life span is a major determinant of lifecycle impacts and is influenced by adaptability, durability, resilience, disassembly. ACE recommends:  - combining performative and architectural measures for functional and aesthetic upgrades   - It is imperative that the EPBD focuses on tracking absolute performance, based on measured and as-built outcomes. This process should begin with a condition survey, followed by a retrofit plan, design proposals and followed up by a reconciliation with measured performance in use. Key indicators, metric and otherwise, as set out by the EU Level(s) programme should be declared at key project and lifecycle stages via the Building Passport scheme. Occupant feedback on functionality and usability should be included wherever possible to enhance quality and the ability of retrofits to stand the test of time.  - Both operational and embodied impacts need to be tackled so that impacts are not pushed further into less reported life-cycle stages.    2. Mandate validation of performance  Buildings consume 1.5-3 times more energy on average than planned. This performance gap must not be repeated in embodied carbon and other environmental impact categories or environmental quality. It is essential that the EPBD enables the tracking of absolute performance based on measured and as-built outcomes to  dramatically improve construction quality, usability and interface design of buildings, which are major contributors to life-span..  ACE recommends:  - In the first instance it is essential that the EPBD implements the validation of performance and sets out the reconciliation of asset ratings with operational ratings. –EPCs that have been validated should be visually distinct.   - Greater harmonisation of EPCs, smooth reporting from design to operation to support accountability for performance, energy performance contracting of design projects and removing risk from financing and investment in sustainable building performance.  - Tackling embodied carbon in the first instance by tracking life span in buildings, components and products and by requiring an as designed and as-built bill of materials as part of the building passport – this should include the life-span, maintenance cycles, quantities, recycled content and instructions for recycle and reuse – as well as origin.  Feedback and validation of both qualitative and quantitative outcomes is needed to  deliver long term resilience and value.       
  
 
3. Robust data and evidence base to accelerate innovation and accountability  Improvement measures will be less costly and more effective if regulations target what matters, which is achieved performance in use. The requirement to report as-built and in-use data has a major impact of construction quality, incentivises focus on usability and interface design, which all have a major impact on a building’s life-span.   Feedback from performance in use helps accelerate the adoption of innovation that works – we must ensure that investments in building performance achieve measurable improvements in whole life performance as well as quality of life. Building energy performance and material impact data should be gathered alongside feedback on user experience to improve the longer life-span and quality of retrofits. The data collected at building level must inform the data used to report national and EU data on GHG emissions – from different sectors, building types, energy end uses, manufacture and construction processes. This is to allow a closer feedback loop between policy measures and achieved improvements in practice.  Requiring EPCs validated by measured performance in use alongside an as-build bill of materials, including the data detailed above is the first and most important step towards addressing building’s whole life performance. These are fundamental to be able to transition to a circular economy. Lastly, such a comprehensive collection and analysis of data is essential to tackle any unintended consequences of the legislation. ACE strongly advocates the review of the EPBD to be complemented by an overarching review of all legislative and incentive measures currently in place and envisaged by the Commission as originally intended by the Green Deal. This review is urgent and must not be held up. Robust reporting of qualitative and quantitative data will lead to a cultural change in the building sector, which is currently one of the most fragmented, with extremely low productivity rates.   4. Accelerating renovation rates / scaling up retrofit The EPBD review should consider all possible mechanisms for accelerating renovation rates in Europe. These include: - Mechanisms for member states to maximise consequential improvements to minimum energy performance standards, including planning, finance and regulatory measures; - Recognise that ‘deep renovation standard’ MUST include architectural and functional upgrades to maximise return on investment and life-span; - Strengthen neighbourhood regeneration and the planning process – local one stop shops must include legal, architectural, structural, mechanical engineering advice and landscaping advice to enable effective community self-organisation - Recommend taxation of newbuild and abolish VAT on retrofit - Ensure that Smart Readiness is based on the ability to report and benchmark measured performance, including the recording of energy end use data, indoor environmental quality and occupancy rates.       
  
 
ANNEX ACE Key Messages in the area of building performance and sustainability  ‘MEASURE TO MANAGE’: EMBED FEEDBACK AND VALIDATION IN ALL LEGISLATION AND INITIATIVES: The current EPBD does not mandate the reporting of achieved operational performance nor the validation of the indoor spatial and environmental quality achieved. Validation and disclosure of building performance in use for both operational and lifecycle impacts is the only way to achieve the rapid transformation of the construction sector to respond to the climate emergencies we face, increasing the renovation rate and dept in Europe. It is necessary to ensure that the investment of natural resources and funds deliver outcomes that work, reducing risks. The reporting and validation of both qualitative and quantitative performance data is the most effective way to overcome the fragmentation of the sector and introduce greater accountability. It must permeate all areas of sustainable building performance: energy, material impacts, indoor environmental quality, health, resilience, cultural and community value. Making feedback and related data accessible to the market would grow the evidence base for continuous improvement, accelerate research and allow knowledge transfer building, neighbourhood and at stock level. A good data basis on the condition and characteristics of the building stock, which also reflects regional differences, is a fundamental prerequisite for managing the decarbonisation of the building sector by 2050 and effectively implementing renovation strategies. The availability of data still shows significant gaps. ACE therefore calls for:  Þ The creation of transparent reporting and benchmarking tools on achieved building performance, harmonised across MS.  
Þ A broad disclosure of the actual energy consumption and embodied carbon data: aggregated anonymised data should be made available in the public domain, to ensure the rapid and continuous improvement of sustainability measures and technologies.  
Þ The validation of the actual capabilities of building systems and fabric as well as performance in use to introduce accountability for building performance.  
Þ Expand the data basis on the building stock. The registration of EPCs, both calculated and measured, in national databases is an essential building block for establishing such a data base. It is necessary to expand and standardise methods of life cycle assessment, which should also take regional characteristics into account, in order to anchor them in planning practice. For this purpose, an EU-wide database with verified, reliable, and comparable information on building products should be established and made accessible free of charge. The comparison and evaluation of the CO2 balances of different building materials and component assemblies must be easy for planners to understand and document at any time. In the future, all life cycle phases should be taken into account in the building product information (e.g. Environmental Product Declaration - EPD). So far, there are still large data gaps for many building products, especially for the "deconstruction" phase.   Þ Harmonise energy performance certificates and the subsequent climate certificates (building passports) throughout the EU and develop them into a roadmap system. In principle, it would be desirable to harmonise the energy performance certificate/EPC system throughout the EU, both in terms of the reference values shown and the presentation. Standardisation would help to create comparability for consumers with regard to the overall energy quality of buildings in different Member States. The system of energy performance certificates/EPCs should be further developed into a roadmap system with building-specific renovation roadmaps. The information contained therein takes into account individual 
  
 
circumstances, wishes and financial implications of the user. Building-specific renovation roadmaps subdivide the measures to be taken into sub-steps in a clear and time-phased manner. The EPBD should continue to call on the member states to develop such roadmap systems.  CREATE SUSTAINABLE VALUE THROUGH ARCHITECTURE: Reducing the consumption of natural resources, while increasing the economic and social value of buildings is the imperative of our times. The energy and environmental performance of new buildings and renovations relies on the expert integration of spatial, material and technical solutions that is led by architects. As a regulated profession, practitioners in the EU have to follow high standards of ethics and maximise the long-term resilience as well as the cultural, health and productivity benefits of buildings. It is this value creation that drives the long-term sustainability of buildings, yet it is also the least recognised aspect of building performance. Aligning stakeholder incentives to support holistic value creation is a major challenge for the highly fragmented buildings sector. It is widely acknowledged that the regulatory framework for the built environment must make whole life impacts as well as benefits more explicit for these to begin to translate into value. The implementation of overall efficiency goals must be made visible in good design to achieve greater social acceptance for the more rapid implementation of sustainability goals. The idea of building culture/ Baukultur and quality planning of buildings should be strengthened overall, following on from the NEB.  To this end ACE calls for: Þ A broader definition of building performance in the EPBD in line with the 17 UN Sustainable Development Goals, which includes accounting for both the resources consumed and the value created in terms of societal and economic well-being. Þ The recognition of the power of high-quality architecture as a means to re-cast our relationship with the natural world. Greater priority should be given to research and investment focusing on the role of the creative arts and design in the social, economic, and environmental transformation of the built environment. Þ Taking inspiration from the New European Bauhaus (NEB) to combine good design, sustainability, and inclusiveness.  REDEFINE THE TERM BUILDING PERFORMANCE TO INCLUDE A BROADER AND WHOLE-LIFE APPROACH: There is an increasing body of scientific evidence showing a need to define ‘building performance’ as the use of natural resources for the creation of environmental quality, resilience and value. Considering environmental impacts beyond energy consumption over the full life-cycle of buildings is essential so that designers can balance the multiple and often conflicting factors driving building performance. To prevent impacts being transferred to less reported areas, the role of regulation is to require minimal reporting to maximise benefits, i.e. to use indicators that are transformative. ‘Clever’ indicators canto empower architects to re-design the interface between the built environment, its enabling technologies and its occupants.   Studies already show that a lifecycle approach tackles inefficiencies across the supply chain and can save substantially more carbon compared to targeting operational savings only. To deliver 2050 targets new buildings and refurbishments should already be net zero, so all opportunities for saving carbon emissions should be followed up.  A complete assessment of sustainability only makes sense at the building level, since building products are only intermediate products. The time factor is incomparably more important for buildings than for 
  
 
products since buildings should ideally be designed for a very long service life. A credit for the CO2 storage of existing buildings is therefore a prerequisite for establishing comparability with new construction. The existing stock must be assessed in terms of its value as a carbon sink or in terms of the CO2 emissions potentially caused by demolition and replacement new construction. Building preservation should be prioritised in principle, demolition/replacement should be subordinate options as far as possible. A bonus/malus system could provide the necessary incentives.   Þ ACE therefore calls for a greater acknowledgement in future sustainable buildings legislation of all five pillars of building performance, namely: § Reducing the consumption of non-renewable resources and protect the environment;   
§ Improving indoor environmental quality including indoor air quality, thermal comfort, daylight, acoustics, biophilia;   § Raising occupant satisfaction including occupants’ health, wellbeing and their perception of building functionality, adaptability and accessibility and how the building meets current and future needs;   
§ Reducing the risks presented by the heating climate and ensure that buildings and neighbourhoods are resilient to extreme weather events and future climate conditions;  
§ Transform the perception of value to reflect social, economic and environmental contributions over a building’s life span as defined by the 17 UN Sustainable Development Goals.  Accordingly, ACE advocates the following:  Þ Incentivise the retrofit and rehabilitation of existing buildings and brownfield sites and ensure that energy efficiency retrofits are embedded into the functional and aesthetic upgrades of buildings and neighbourhoods. Þ A greater recognition of architectural solutions over technological ones: the whole life cost and impacts of technical systems need to be fully addressed in policy and practice, otherwise priority should be given to simple, passive, low-tech, locally tested solutions that do not consume energy and are less prone to human error. Þ The Level(s) reporting framework should underpin the reporting of the performance of sustainable buildings in EU green procurement, financial incentives, and legislation and in the longer term to inform the development of an EU Standard for harmonised sustainable building metrics. Þ Include the overall CO2 balance of buildings as a climate protection indicator. ACE advocates the mandatory inclusion of CO2 as a climate protection requirement, as the evaluation of buildings with regard to their overall CO2 balance over the entire life cycle is better suited to depicting climate impacts than primary energy consumption. ACE therefore calls for the specification of CO2 emissions alongside energy consumption (as a climate protection indicator) in order to establish a direct cross-reference to greenhouse gas emissions.  The holistic assessment of buildings with their building materials over the entire life cycle, which is necessary for climate protection, and thus also the bound energy (embodied energy/ total CO2 footprint) should be included as a criterion in the EPBD. In future, the EPBD should not only focus on reducing the energy demand for building use, but also take into account the energy demand for production and demolition. In a climate certificate (building passport), which could replace the current energy certificate in the near future, grey energy and an assessment of the material flows used would be included in the overall assessment of the building in addition to the operating energy.    
  
 
SCALE UP FROM THE SINGLE BUILDING BOUNDARY: Currently, EU legislation on building performance and sustainability essentially focuses on single building units. However, going beyond the boundary of single buildings is necessary to enhance and optimise the economic, social, environmental and cultural value of our built environment.  Nations, regions and cities are unlocking finance to support the transformation of the construction sector to achieve net zero while creating a more circular economy. Meanwhile the global pandemic has revealed an urgent need for green urban renewal and the protection of biodiversity. While the barriers may seem different, enabling large-scale net-zero regeneration and the landscaping of public and semi-public spaces require similar mechanisms and are more effective when deployed jointly. ACE promotes the following measures to overcome barriers to both: Þ Embed community health and well-being in EU Taxonomy, Green Procurement and Recovery Fundcriteria for built environment initiatives   
Þ Deploy multi-scale approaches: consider policy mechanisms for building projects to be evaluated for the potential enhancement they bring to their community in a street, district, and city context in terms of functional mix, density and intensity ratios, environmental restoration, resource flows, etc.   
Þ Make accessibility and green mobility major priorities – to successfully interweave regeneration efforts at building, neighbourhood and city level.   Þ Support local self-organisation to empower the green transformation of neighbourhoods alongside the mass deployment of low carbon measures. For example, One-Stop-Shops , already promoted by the EPBD should be re-cast to offer architectural and landscape design, legal, and procurement advice for local communities to take concerted action. The architectural profession provides an expert role to guide individuals as well as local authorities, to enable participatory design.  
Þ Incorporate criteria for climate change resilience as per the EU Level(s) framework in national renovation strategies to improve green and blue infrastructure, to build resilience to heat-waves, drought and flooding, reduce pollution peaks, etc.   
Þ Incorporate in the Strategy for Sustainable Buildings the application of the Level(s) reporting framework to neighbourhood and city scale regeneration.

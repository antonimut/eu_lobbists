 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Energy Cities welcomes the  revision of the Energy Performance of Buildings 
Directive (EPBD) and sees it as  a major opportunity to effectively drive 
improvements to the sustainability of buildings in Europe and deliver a major 
contribution to the EU ‘Fit for 55’ for 2030, and beyond to 2050. 
 
EPBD must focus on displacing fossil emissions from heating and cooling with 
renewable solutions combined with improving energy savings. There are no 
substantive technical barriers to enabling this change, just a lack of regulatory 
design and policy coherency 
 
Within the policy options proposed by the Roadmap, Energy Cities believes that 
the policy ‘option 3’  presents the appropriate pathway to revise the EPBD. 
However, we are also strongly convinced that the reinforcement of non -
regulatory measures (e.g. technical assistance, project development assistance, 
financing), falling under ‘option 2’ should not be ov erlooked, but deployed in 
parallel. These elements, enabled by an enhanced role of local authorities with 
regional/local intermediaries (one-stop shops) in managing them, are of crucial 
importance to strengthen the EPBD’s implementation framework.  
 
Stemming from the list of measures foreseen under ‘option 3’, we welcome the 
introduction of Minimum Energy Performance Standards (MEPS) for different 
types of buildings. In our view, MEPS are a crucial policy tool to build up the 
momentum for building renovation within the EU building stock.  To help 
achieving the objectives of the Renovation Wave, the following points must be 
taken into account:   
 
• Technical and administrative s upport for local authorities to use  a 
district or neighborhood approach: aggregation of renovations at local 
level can help achieving economy of scales, facilitate integrated heat 
and energy planning and encourage participation of citizens and 
communities, hence maximizing energy efficiency, system integration 
and overall opportunities for decarbonizing H&C at a larger scale.  
 
• Empower citizens to take action on renovations : Engagement of 
citizens and communities is necessary to make renovations happen  (a 
citizen-led renovation wave); their  involvement is determinant to both 
understand their needs and create support for the renovation agenda.

• A successful introduction of MEPS should focus on increasing their 
embrace by citizens; MEPS must therefore be accompanied by an 
overall package of measures that support and empower citizens, in the 
form of adequate financing tools (with dedicated  specific support for 
the more vulnerable households), precise information, opportunity for 
participation, advice as well as technical assistance. Within this scope, 
we believe that a successful reception of MEPS within the energy 
renovation value chain should be facilitated by intermediaries (one-stop 
shops), which, via technical assistance, information campaigns, project 
aggregation, quality control, supplier training /sourcing and financing, 
can inject more trust in the overall building renovation ecosystem thus 
driving up the demand for renovations and ensure a str ong 
implementation framework of the EPBD.1 
 
• MEPS are a powerful tool to address the worst-performing buildings, 
while contributing to improve the living conditions of ‘vulnerable 
consumers’ that experience energy poverty. However, MEPS should not 
be restricted to those buildin gs, and address the transformation of all 
buildings in all segments . Even if the introduction of MEPS for certain 
types of buildings is gradual, it is important to reiterate that any delay 
in introducing these policy tools for  residentia l buildings (which 
accounts for almost 75% of the EU building stock) will undermine their 
potential in achieving the objectives laid out by the Renovation Wave. 
 
• There is however a need to increase the ambition in terms of outcomes 
of renovation and urban regeneration programmes that have to be 
adapted and codesigned with residents. This should happen through 
better cooperation at the local level between housing providers, 
contractors, local authorities, better knowledge of the residents ‘needs 
and local resources (in particular in the field of renewable energy), and 
embracing the concept of sufficiency as a complement to the Energy 
Efficiency First principle. 
 
• Phase out fossil fuels when deep renovations (60% reduction in primary 
energy demand) are carried out 
 
• Implement the call from the Renovation Wave to introduce targeted 
objectives for the integration of RES in buildings, for instance by 
introducing a minimum share at neighbourhood, building stock or 
individual building level

• New buildings’ requirements should prevent future lock-ins by banning 
new fossil installations, while progressively phase -out inefficient and 
fossil-fuel based heating systems from existing buildings. These should 
be replaced by alternative RES, such as renewable electricity that 
powers electric heat pump s and solar thermal heat. Buildings can also 
be connected to renewable district heating networks. This should be 
combined with halting any kind of support to fossil fuel infrastructure. 
 
• At national level, designing MEPS that appropriately address all 
buildings requires better info rmation and data about national building 
stocks (including data for each building typology); this is necessary to 
decide how to best tailor MEPS to each national situation and building 
typology.  
 
• Article 2a on Long-Term Renovation Strategies must be adapted to set 
a target of 100% greenhouse gas emission reductions by 2050 and 
accelerate the decarbonisation of the building stock through renewable 
heating, cooling and energy savings measures.  The Long -Term 
Renovation Strategies should also evolve to integrated planning that 
combines the demand -side (energy efficiency) and the supply -side 
(renewable energy). Integrating the long -term renovation strategies 
with national and local heat transition  plans helps to maximise the 
synergies between energy efficiency and renewable energy measures in 
the building sector. 
 
• Cities must be heavily engaged and involved in the development of 
national long -term stra tegies. Member states must be obliged to 
consult and work with other levels of government to develop a long-
term renovation plan which can be implemented quickly and efficiently. 
Currently there  is no requirement for cities to be consulted in the 
drafting of plans. 
 
• A more dynamic approach to buildings’ energy performance and its 
assessment is necessary, meaning that besides spurring building 
renovations, a review of the EPBD would be important to enhance the 
role of solutions that are both able to keep energy perfor mance high 
over time and better match energy demand and response, such as 
Energy Performance Contracts.

 
 
 
Saint-Gobain answer to the EPBD Roadmap Consultation – March 2021 
Saint-Gobain welcomes the roadmap consultation and the opportunity to express our views on the EPBD 
revision, which we see as a key success factor for the EU Green Deal and the Renovation Wave.  
Our buildings not only represent a high share of CO2 emissions in Europe (36%) but they are also the biggest 
assets in our econ omies - assets which risk losing value if not brou ght to climate neutral compatible 
standards. And since over three quarters of buildings built today , mostly prior to building codes, will still 
be standing and occupied by 2050 means, the focal point of EU and national buildings policies should be 
to accelerate the energy renovation of the ageing building stock. Another key priority should be to factor 
the multiple benefits of renovation in the decision-making process of building policies.    
1) We support option 3 of the roadmap and further enforcement of existing measures (option 2).  
A new, more dynamic and more impactful regulatory framework is needed to boost the renovation rate 
and depth of buildings. In spite of many progress, incl. Long Term Renovation Strategies (LTRS), financing 
and support  schemes, no regulatory perspective is yet visible to owners regarding the needed 
improvements. As a result, the private and public subsidy schemes deployed at EU and national, regional 
and city level, reach a market that  is often only half-prepared and half ready to undertake renovation 
works. The role of buildings in the climate and energy transition needs to be framed within a clear, 
pedagogical comprehensive policy framework, with clear regulatory objectives and explanations of the 
individual and collective benefits.  
We thus fully support the option 3 , namely to develop minimum energy performance standards for 
existing buildings, as a way to create demand and align perspectives among actors of a complex and 
scattered value chain. MEPS should be designed at national level, with sufficient lead time, and in a way 
that is simple and respects the economic rationality of each segment, e.g. residential occupied, rented or 
commercial buildings. They should build on the mapping work done in the LTRS, but be deployed as a 
matter of priority – without waiting for LTRS’ next edition.  
MEPS should be deployed in priority in segments where the energy savings, the economic, climate and 
societal benefits are the greatest. This requires to integrate the monetary value of the multiple benefits 
of energy efficient buildings. Numerous examples can inspire MEPS design, such as the requirements on 
office buildings to reach an EPC class ‘C’ from 2023 in the Netherland, an EPC class ‘E’ for private rented 
properties in the UK, or a rental ban for dwellings with a consumption exceeding 450 kWh/m²/year from 
2023 in France. We note that deploying MEPS in the owner occupied single family segment require 
specific attention in particular in view of dwellings facing energy poverty or vulnerable conditions.  
MEPS should help accelerating demand for energy renovation, notably in the worst performing 
buildings, but they also have a more fundamental role to play in signaling higher performance as this will 
drive more transformative measures . In this sense , any MEPS design should include both short and 
medium term milestones on the road to reaching a 2050 objective (e.g. reaching class ‘C’ by 2030 or 2035, 
and class ‘B’ by 2030 or 2040 ). This point is essential and should avoid that MEPS trigger “shallow 
renovation” and lock-in effect via minimalistic measures. Financing schemes should be designed in a way 
that facilitates the attainment of the longer term ambition, via deep renovations.  
In the case of public buildings, more ambitious objectives should be put in place from the start, given the 
involvement of public funding (class ‘B’ for the renovation of public buildings is suggested in Denmark).  
2) Key tools to deliver more impact and ambition for new and renovated building  
Energy Performance Certificates: The certificates are a key instrument to provide an accurate picture of 
the building’s performance but their role in driving renovation is limited due to their ‘static’ feature. EPCs 
should build on  more comparable methodologies  across the EU. C ertifiers should meet minimum 
qualification requirements, and the building should always be visited  before enacting the document. 
These are important elements to build trust in the tool.  
In addition, it is important that the result of the renovation works performed are being evaluated  and 
ideally certified by the production of a new EPC after the works. The colors of the EPC labels should better 
alert of the remaining gaps towards the best class ( e.g. the class ‘C’ often appears as “green”, which 
wrongly suggests that no improvement is needed). Initiatives towards making EPCs available in a more

timely manner for the rental and buyers’ markets, and making them obligatory and enforceable will also 
raise their quality (e.g. “opposabilité”, as introduced in France).  
Building Renovation Passports (BRPs):  BRPs must complement EPCs in order to equip building owner s 
with tailor made recommendations for energy efficiency measures to deliver improvements towards the 
best energy class. BRPs should suggest an adequate order of works (prioritizing the reduction of energy 
needs via envelope improvements ), support the coordination of works between various professionals 
intervening in a building, and cover key benefits of improving indoor air quality, thermal and acoustic 
comfort and daylight/visual comfort. They could also cover topics such as the structural elements, stability, 
or future living needs. The examples of functioning BRPs (e.g. Flanders) and the learnings of the H2020 
IBroad project should be exchanged as best practices across the EU27 to foster adoption. The realization 
of a BRP should be supported by public funding (like in Wallonia). A BRPs being a powerful tool to ensure 
works’ relevance and improve quality, it could condition the access to public subsidies.  
Near Zero Energy Buildings : The deployment of the nZEB concept needs to be further strengthened. 
Disparities between countries and regions, and especially the lack of alignment with the 2016 Commission 
recommendations on nZEB levels (cf October 2020 report), suggest that nZEB definitions should be more 
ambitious, better aligned with the principle of reducing demand first and with our EU 2050 goals.  
Deep Renovation Standard: We welcome the idea o f developing a deep renovation standard. It should 
support the transformation of the entire building stock towards levels compatible with our long term 
decarbonisation goals (nZEB or equivalent level). In this sense, the role of a deep renovation standard goes 
beyond the channeling of private and public financing instruments towards renovation.  More precisely, a 
deep renovation should deliver the full savings potential of a building and avoid a lock-in effect on the road 
towards nZEB level. This means that typically, a deep renovation will deliver around 60-70% of reduction 
in energy needs, at least for the major share of the building s that are in the ‘D’, ‘E’, ‘F’, ‘G’ or ‘H’ classes.  
The Flemish definition of deep renovation (Ingrijpende Energetische Renovatie) is a good example.  
A ‘deep renovation’ of the EPBD: Given what is needed to make building stock aligned with the carbon 
neutrality goals, we believe that this revision should be comprehensive (not only a targeted revision). Tools 
initially designed to support the evolution of building codes more than 10 years ago, such as cost-
optimality, do no longer seem to be suited to support a forward looking transformation towards climate 
compatible 2050 status. Similarly, restricting the application of an energy performance ambition to “major 
renovations” overlooks that from now on, any renovation step should be part of an nZEB trajectory. The 
concept of ‘major renovation’ should thus evolve.   
3) Beyond energy efficiency 
Driving the reductions of other impacts of buildings is important given the environmental challenges as 
well as the health & comfort  expectations linked to  sustainable buildings . The EU Commission has 
recently launched Level(s), a voluntary framework to assess the sustainability of buildings, which we 
believe should be the reference guiding any evolution of the EU regulatory framework. Such evolution 
could happen step by step, following a progressive introduction of Level(s) core indicators.  
The EPBD should remain the main instrument for regulating buildings impacts. Any evolution of its scope 
should be well articulated with its main purpose to deliver reduction of operational energy use and related 
GHG emissions. This is important to keep in mind, given the features of the EU building stock (dominance 
of existing buildings with drastic cuts to be performed on the operational dimension). In other words, when 
introducing concepts such as whole life carbon  of buildings, one should avoid to consider the embodied 
carbon impact of component s in a way that is disconnected from the role they fulfil in a building  (for 
example: insulation or efficient glazing solutions improve the efficiency and reduce operational emissions).  
In this context, requesting the disclosure of a building’s Global Warming Potential indicator (GWP, as 
defined in Level(s)) could make sense to help gather data for new buildings and for the major renovation 
of public buildings.   
For any evolution, the transparency of impacts  linked to products and buildings is essential. Progress 
needs to accelerate on this front. The European Commission should further support t he disclosure of 
environmental impact via Environmental Products Declarations (EPDs) based on the EN15804+A2, and 
assessment at building level based on EN15978. A level playing field needs to be maintained for all 
solutions and materials. Also , when considering e mbodied carbon, all building components should be 
covered, namely envelope and equipment  (such as equipment for h eating, cooling, pr oduction of 
renewable energy).

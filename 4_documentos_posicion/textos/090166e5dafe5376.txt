Explanatory note on the IED and EPRTR Open Public Consultation March 2021 
 
With this paper ICBA would like to provide additional comments to the questions in the questionnaire of 
the Open Public Consultation and an explanation of our answers in selected instances.  
Question 1: Large industries which are covered by the IED, have already achieved significant reduction of 
emissions (see presentation held by the EEA for the IED evaluation in May 2019).  This has been achieved 
as an outcome of the IED and as an outcome of voluntary action to achieve sustainability goals. 
ICBA generally views the impact of large industrial plants on environmental issues as important, however 
they are continually improving, and it should be noted that they are not the only and, not always the main 
contributor.  
Question 2: ICBA views the contribution of large industrial plants to techno-economic and environmental 
objectives as important.  For instance, carbon black is an enabler for climate neutrality by reducing tire 
rolling resistance, increasing tire durability, and allowing electric vehicles to have a greater range Hence 
large industrial plants which produce these types of materials provide an important contribution to 
techno-economic and environmental objectives throughout a larger value chain. 
Question 3: The role of large industrial plants post-2030 will be important.  As it is the case today, carbon 
black producers deliver important goods for our society which support various environmental objectives. 
This role will become even more important post-2030. Hence, the operators of carbon black production 
facilities can in general act as enabler for a more circular economy and a cleaner environment. While the 
IED provides the right framework for continuous improvement, we caution that double regulation must 
be avoided. For example, we note that the proposed GHG inclusion in the IED scope would introduce a 
major change in the functioning principles of EU ETS. IED currently excludes units falling under ETS. Hence, 
GHGs are an aspect under the IED for all other plants – which is the majority of industrial installations. We 
see an increasing complexity if – in the future – the same units have to comply with ETS and the IED.  For 
further detail see our explanation on Q8.  
Question 4: Question 4 relates to the place where ‘you’ work, study or live. Since ICBA is a global 
organization, we chose not to answer this question. 
Question 5: ICBA has sufficient access to information on the level of environmental impacts of industrial 
facilities. Our members are cooperating with the authorities and fulfil their reporting requirements (e.g. 
via E-PRTR and also on a national level). As such we strongly agree to the topics in this Question 5, that 
sufficient access to information on the environmental impact of plants exists.

Question 6: We see an increase of ‘Public Participation’ in the permitting process of lesser importance.  
This is because trained and highly educated experts/public servants of the respective local authorities 
represent the interest of the broader public in this process.  The process of public participation is valuable 
as it adds to a better mutual and reciprocal understanding. However, there is no reason to change it under 
the IED as it is already governed by the rules of the Treaty of Aarhus and national administrative law.  
Question 7: ICBA members operate facilities in several EU Member States.  These facilities participate in 
the permitting process, undertake monitoring and report data (to e.g. E-PRTR) and ensure the proper 
sharing of information.  We therefore know that the respective information can be retrieved from publicly 
accessible sources. 
Question 8: In general, we strongly agree that the existing IED, supplemented by horizontal legislation 
sufficiently controls environmental impacts. The evaluation of the IED carried out by DG ENV in 2019/2020 
(refer to the staff working document) has shown that the IED broadly serves its purpose and shows 
tangible results (e.g., the reduction of emissions for virtually all major key environmental issues taken into 
consideration). This is also supported by the EEA report shown at the IED workshop in Brussels, May 2019.  
We would also like to note that the proposed inclusion of GHG within the scope of IED would introduce a 
major change in the functioning principles of EU ETS. Until now, with GHG emissions regulated by EU ETS 
(presented as an alternative to a command-and-control approach), no direct limits (e.g., like AELs) existed 
on GHG emissions, provided a corresponding quantity of allowances are surrendered. This is a 
fundamental aspect of the EU ETS functioning, that would no longer apply with the new proposal on IED 
scope. Further, Art. 9(1) of the IED only excluded units falling under ETS. Hence, GHGs are an aspect under 
the IED for all other plants – which is most industrial installations. We therefore see an increasing 
complexity if – in the future – the same units must comply with ETS and the IED.  
Question 9: With respect to the existing sectors covered by the IED we moderately disagree with the 
notion that they still have a significant impact. As the EEA has shown, industrial pollution has, in parts, 
been significantly reduced. Concerning the question on thresholds, we do not consider that the threshold 
for consideration under the IED should be reduced or modified.  Except for the BREF on LVOC (Large 
Volume Organic Chemicals) there are no thresholds for chemical processes.  This approach provides a 
level playing field within the EU. 
We have only commented on BREFs/activities directly or indirectly related to our member’s production 
processes.  
Question 10: We have only answered questions related to our production processes. For areas directly or 
indirectly connected to our facilities we strongly disagree with the need for an extension of the scope. 
These matters are sufficiently regulated via other mechanisms.  
Question 11: With regards to permitting and reporting we believe that no changes are needed as the 
experts involved in the permitting drafting and granting process have sufficient access to the respective 
information and know where and how to find it. The E-PRTR database and regional/local requirements 
for reporting are of sufficient detail and provide the permitting authorities – who represent the needs of 
the broader public – with information.

Question 12: As stated for Q11 we believe that the respective experts on Member State Level, the industry 
and the NGOs do know how to make use of the means for public access to information. We agree that 
obtaining the full benefit of all such tools requires relevant experience. 
Question 13: As stated for Q6, we see ‘Public participation’ in the decision-making process of lesser 
importance.  This is because trained and highly educated experts/public servants of the respective local 
authorities and the European NGOs, well represent the interest of the broader public in this process.   
Question 14: We view most of the information provided in the E-PRTR database as very complete, as 
comprehensive reporting requirements exist. We like to note that the E-PRTR was designed for the 
reporting of actual and relevant emissions in absolute numbers. It was not the intention to report 
performance. The actual performance with regards to pollution reduction is evaluated in much detail via 
the BREF process and later serves as the basis to define best available techniques (BAT).  
Question 15: As stated for Q12 we believe that the respective experts on Member State Level, in industry 
and at the NGOs do know how to make use of the search capabilities for information in E-PRTR. We agree 
that fully benefiting from this tool requires experience, however, it is pretty much self-explanatory.  
Question 16: We did not comment on questions unrelated to our member’s processes. For all other 
aspects we believe the usefulness of the E-PRTR is very satisfactory as it provides a very broad and 
comprehensive overview of actual emissions, augmented by detailed information down to the individual 
plant level. We like to note: E-PRTR was about the reporting of actual and relevant emissions in absolute 
numbers. It was not designed to report performance. The actual performance with regards to pollution 
reduction is evaluated via the BREF process and later serves as the basis to define best available 
techniques.  
Question 17: Removing of any pollutant from the list to be reported under E-PRTR is not necessary, 
provided the reporting thresholds are appropriate and provided the reporting is only required for 
‘relevant’ pollutants. The decision as to which substances are relevant, and which are not is taken via the 
BREF process. Hence IED/BREF and E-PRTR are complementary to each other. 
Question 18: Where our members operate, public access to justice is deemed working well and public 
access to influencing legislation is provided e.g., via the ‘Refit Process’ 
Question 19: Carbon black is a significant enabler contributing contributing towards, among other things, 
a Circular Economy and climate neutrality. The necessary (e.g., mandatory) contribution is comparably 
low, as carbon black producers (and several other industrial sectors) have already significantly reduced 
emissions. Furthermore, carbon black producers have voluntary sustainability programs which address 
these issues and are operating under the rules of the Responsible Care program. 
Question 20: We view the degree of effort needed by Members States as moderate as we believe the 
main impetus for a program is broad and should therefore be handled at the European level. 
Question 21: Climate change, decarbonization, circularity cannot end at borders. As such we moderately 
disagree that these objectives should be achieved by Member States. Further, to ensure a level global 
playing field and to protect the competitiveness of EU industry, measures taken need global alignment.  
This approach would help avoid “leakage” of environmental impacts to other less regulated regions.

Question 22: Question 22 asks for an evaluation of the importance of the main aspects of the EU Green 
Deal: Whilst we fully support the concept of the EU Green Deal, the IED should not become the ‘multi-
tool’ for all kind of purposes. As such, we consider the policy options ‘in the IED and E-PRTR’ as not 
important. After all, there is powerful legislation already in place, with further development in the areas 
of circularity and (energy) efficiency. Recent reports (e.g. the Staff Working Document of the IED 
Evaluation) show that the IED and other legislation have in fact led to (significantly) reduced emissions. 
(Over)-burdening the IED will unavoidably slow down the BREF process, simply due to the sheer increase 
of complexity. Above this, we like to note: Policy options should not only take the level playing field in 
the EU into consideration but should also address this on a global level. Further to our response to Q21, 
“leakage” of environmental impacts to other less regulated regions needs to be avoided as this will risk 
increasing global environmental impacts. 
Question 23: We believe that the main future impacts on IED units which need to be assessed as part of 
the IED review are a) the increasing and excessive legislative burden which will come along with and 
‘extended’ IED, b) the (potential) shift of the main environmental impacts moving to areas outside of 
Europe, it’s effect on the economy in Europe and its global environmental impact and c) the cost-benefit 
and cross-media effects as further reductions of emissions become outweighed by additional efforts or 
unintended consequences (e.g. additional use of energy for abatement, leading to further/other 
emissions). 
Question 24: We wonder why Covid 19 is mentioned in conjunction with the EU Green Deal.   It is our 
opinion that the COVID-19 situation should not be intertwined or mixed up with the Green Deal policies. 
Question 25: We believe the effectiveness of the IED/E-PRTR can be maintained while providing 
opportunities to reduce costs.  Pollution reduction should not be pursued beyond the point where cross 
media effects outweigh the benefits resulting in disadvantages for EU based companies and leading to an 
increase in emissions in less regulated regions. Further, the BREFs should focus on relevant substances as 
consideration of all substances of a potential ‘concern’ will not have a significant benefit and will slow 
down the BREF process. Effort and costs must be in a reasonable balance with the benefits. Monitoring 
must be limited to only obtain data really needed.  
Question 26: Whilst the IED has a good capacity to ensure a level playing field within the EU, the aligned 
implementation of BREFs must be administered by the Member States with consideration given to the 
risk of disadvantaging EU facilities and increasing environmental impacts in less regulated regions. AELs 
and BATs must be based on the knowledge of experts and not on political positions. Fair competition 
within the EU but also globally must also ensure a non-disclosure of confidential data. Competitive 
advantages must not be gained by uneven implementation of BREFs across the EU and the 
competitiveness of the EU industry on a global scale must be maintained. As the OECD BAT project 
shows, significant imbalances in AELs are threatening the competitiveness of EU installations. 
With this explanatory note, ICBA wishes to flag a couple of issues raised by the public consultation’s 
questionnaire. ICBA is looking forward to sharing its ideas and exploring potential policy solutions with 
the European Commission and Stakeholders in due course.

GENERAL COMMENTS  
 
Surfrider Europe supports a thorough revision of the IED to improve delivery in achieving a high level 
of environmental protection from industrial activities, with priority to pollution prevention over 
control and adapting to both existing and emerging pollution sources.  
 
Surfrider Europe regrets the Ocean and water bodies continue to suffer from a high range of pollution 
from industrial installations, both at sea and on land. As stated in the IED assessment, the IED has not 
led to substantially reduced pollutant emissions to water and should be revised to meet this objective. 
The revision is a necessity if the Commission wants to fulfil its Zero Pollution ambition and achieve its 
objective sometime soon of reaching the good environmental status of its waters, an objective it has 
failed to achieve by end of 2020. 
 
Surfrider Europe also b elieves all environmental impacts generated by the industry sector should be 
addressed by the Directive  and sees no reason why certain releases should be addressed elsewhere 
only. It is necessary the IED addresses in an integrated wa y all emissions and their impacts  and 
derogations in that sense should be removed from the revised text. 
 
Surfrider Europe feels the IED should also be used to stop industry sectors using notably harmful 
contaminants and not only set emission limit values for polluting substances.  The BAT should be 
reviewed in many sectors so to ensure that industrial installations operate under strict standards that 
limit as much as possible any significant harm to the environment and to public health.  
 
As regard to pollutants, installations should provide information on the substances they use and not 
only on the substances they release to allow for strict control  and tight risk assessment.  Public 
information and participation should be largely strengthened.  
 
Surfrider Europe would finally like to recall the importance of this Directive to reduce pollution 
impacting the ocean, seas, rivers and generally all water bodies. A s it is now, the IED greatly focuses 
on ensuring that industrial installations do not deteriorate the quality of soil and groundwater, which 
is fundamental to protect our environment and health. Yet, the IED has a key role to play in protecting 
further our ocean and seas , if revised and enforced accordingly. The protection of all waters – not 
limited to groundwater- should be explicitly stated as such.  
 
Surfrider Europe would finally like to stress the need for the Commission and national authorities to 
increase control, ensure enforcement of the Directive and apply the polluter pay principles. Surfrider

Europe was confronted in many occasions with cases of infringements of the IED where strong 
mobilisation from our NGO and our volunteers and legal action were needed:  
 
• Aluminia release, Alteo, Gardanne (France) : since 1967 Alteo, the world leader of the 
production of alumina, has pumped an average of 1095 tons a day of “red mud” industrial 
waste into an offshore pit. Surfrider in collaboration with other NGOs took to court to stop the 
release. In 2018, the Marseille Administrative Court delivered its verdict concerning the Alteo 
plant's derogation from pollution standards: a two -year reduction of permissible derogation, 
and imposing of a study on the cumulative effects of discharges at sea. Unfor tunately, the 
plant is now planned to be moved to Guinea in Africa, which could bring even more concerns. 
 
• Smurfit Kappa, Biganos (France): in 2012 the paper factory Smurfit Kappa released 4152m3 of 
black liquid into the Ocean at Lacanau, France, due to a spill within the facilities. Unexpectedly, 
the release was authorised by the regional authorities through an outfall directly into the 
coastal zone. In response, local fishermen and surfers notified Surfrider Europe to action on 
their side, to legally challenge the authorisation and to hold Smurfit responsible. After a three-
year legal process, Surfrider Europe won the legal action and Smurfit was fined 30.000 euros 
for the harm done. 
 
• Ammoniac and bleeching, Rosignano Solvay (Italy): the famous ‘White Beaches’ of Rosignano 
Solvay have been an attractive touristic spot for decades. The main reason is the white colour 
of its sand, rocks and the turquoise colour of its water making it look like an ‘exotic beach 
paradise’ However, some reports show a close correl ation between the unnatural colour of 
this environment and the presence of the Solvay Industry, located only a few hundred meters 
from the sea. There is clear evidence of discharge of chemical products by the industry directly 
into the sea via a canal. Bea ches are frequently closed in the area and dead fish are often 
observed. A study on those fish showed evidence of high traces of ammoniac inside their 
organisms. In addition, the European Environmental on Agency conducted reports the 
pollution of the site from 2001 to 2016, without any repercussions to date. 
 
These are only a few examples, Surfrider is also at the moment actively monitoring industry releases 
in Martigues, France (Ferferric chloride leak, Kem one) Wijk aan Zee, Netherlands (Cyanide and heavy 
metals, Tata Steel) and Bayonne, France.  
 
 
DETAILED COMMENTS 
 
On pellets, Surfrider Europe recalls the necessity to address pellet pollution through a supply chain 
approach. The IEP does not provide this trans cutting approach, the incorporation of new measures 
under a revised IED are welcomed but cannot be considered as efficient enough to tackle the issue of 
pellet spills in the environment, given the number of  actors from the pellet chain and corresponding 
number of proven pollution sources . While it can allow to set an emission threshold  in industrial 
production and transfo rmation sites, additional measures enshrined in a standalone legislation are 
necessary to prevent pellet pollution. Including best practices to prevent and handle pellets into the 
Polymer Production Best Available Technique (BAT) Reference Document (BREF) under the Industrial 
Emissions Directive is very helpful, and as su ch welcomed but cannot be considered as enough to 
address pellet pollution. 
 
On the inclusion of additional sectors, Surfrider Europe welcomes the Commission looking at including 
extractive industries and urban waste water treatment plants to IED scope and urges the Commission 
to include indeed those two sectors 
 
On offshore oil and gas platforms , they are responsible for several types of pollution and Surfrider 
Europe believes that not enough measures are being taken to report and prevent them. 
First, during the prospection phase companies are  using seismic testing to locate the reserves of oil 
and gas. This seismic testing creates underwater noise which is currently covered by the Directive’s 
definition of pollution (Art.3.2). Seismic testing has grave consequences for the marine life: it deafens 
marine mammals and causes their increased beach strandings. It also has impact on plankton and fish, 
reducing in some cases catch rates for up to 80%1. 
 
Secondly, offshore oil and gas exploitation is accompanied by unavoidable hydrocarbon spills and 
releases: European companies reported 99 of those in 2018 alone, an increase of more than 110% 
compared to the previous year2. It also leads to water pollution by “drilling muds” containing benzene, 
zinc, arsenic, radioactive materials, and other contaminants with irreversible consequences 3. The 
current regulation (Directive 2013/30/EU on safety of offshore oil and gas operations) puts the accent 
on preventing major accidents and does not provide sufficient incentive for operators to reduce  the 
day-to-day pollution of the installations. 
 
Thirdly, the currently applied Directive 2013/30/EU does not introduce an obligation for companies to 
decommission the offshore installations  once they’re no longer in use. However, if they are not 
properly d ismantled, oil and gas platforms become marine waste, as demonstrated in the UK : its  
government recently approved Shell’s decommissioning plan stating their intention to leave the 
concrete bases and part of the metal structure, leaving behind thousands of tons of contaminated 
water. If this sector was  covered by the Industrial emissions r ules, platforms decommissioning could 
fall under the provisions of the Article 67  prohibiting the disposal of waste into any water body  and 
bring a great benefit to the protection of the marine environment. 
 
Fourthly, today there exists little obligation fo r offshore oil and gas exploration sites to provide 
information about their greenhouse gas emissions. Only platforms covered by OSPAR provisions are 
obliged to provide this data, which creates a geographical asymmetry within the EU as this requirement 
is not applied to all the platforms. Moreover, we strongly believe that the absence of the information 
on greenhouse gas emissions constitutes a failure to ensure public access  to environmental 
information in accordance with the Aarhus Convention. Finally, the information of greenhouse gas 
emissions of offshore platforms and the limitations of those emissions is crucial for the EU Green Deal 
efforts.

Protected Areas (MPAs) have high conservation value and were designed to boost biodiversity. MPAs 
conservation is also crucial in the fight with climate change : they help with  both the efforts of

On urban and industrial waste water treatment plants , Surfrider Europe supports the inclusion of 
biomedia releases in the emissions into water under the scope of the Directive. Biomedia are the media 
used as bacterial bioflm carriers in the wastewater treatment process. Large numbers of small plastic 
cylinders have been found washed up along European coasts. Biomedia escaping water treatment 
plants can, like any unnatural element entering th e environment, end up in the sea. They can be 
transported in water courses over hundreds of kilometres from their point of discharge, just as a drop 
of water will also follow the same route through the water cycle. This means biomedia can be dispersed 
over vast areas. They need to be subject to strict control and best practices. Recent cases of pollution 
in Corsica are sad reminders of the urgency to address this pollution.  In addition, in many cases, 
industries discharge indirectly to the urban sewerage system escaping this way the IED requirements. 
We support the Commission addressing this issue as well.  
 
On emerging issues , we support the revision of the BAT on textiles to include stricter measures to 
incorporate microplastic fiber emissions from textiles, including via the obligation to process to on site 
pre washing cycles of textiles. We feel the IED should use BATs to allow more for clearer reporting and 
obligations to reduce microplastic emissions from all sectors.

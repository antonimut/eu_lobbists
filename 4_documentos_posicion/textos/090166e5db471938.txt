Tetra Pak welcomes the European Commission’s 
EU Biodiversity Strategy 2030 and supports its 
goals of protecting and restoring nature.
Tetra Pak is committed to contributing to the 
European Green Deal vision of achieving climate 
neutrality while preserving the environment and 
ensuring resilient and sustainable food systems. 
We strive to deliver the world’s most sustainable 
food package system designed to reduce food 
waste and minimise water and energy use: carbon-
neutral,   fully   recyclable,   and   made   solely 
of sustainably-sourced renewable or  recycled 
materials.
We are committed  to  protecting  biodiversity 
wherever we operate, and we welcome the EU’s 
ambition to play a leading role in the run-up to 
the UN Convention of Biological Diversity (CBD) at 
COP15. We are convinced that a sustainable forestry 
policy is the most relevant aspect to focus on at EU 
level. Forests are home to much of the continent’s 
biodiversity,1 though we do not neglect the greater 
and wider value of biodiversity in less degraded 
and less densely inhabited environments on other 
continents.
Tetra Pak’s purpose is to make food safe and 
available,  everywhere.  And  we   promise   to 
protect  what’s   good:   protecting   food,   people 
and the planet. We fully assume the responsibility 
of our significant role in food systems, aiming to 
contribute to a globally sustainable economy and 
society.
 
As a company that was founded on the 
idea that a package should save more 
than it costs, we instinctively support 
the principle to give nature back more 
than the world takes.2
Protect forests, protect the 
planet
Biodiversity and climate change are fundamentally 
intertwined, and we believe sustainably managed 
forests are key to heading off these twin planetary 
crises.3 As well as being the EU’s largest territorial 
ecosystem,4 forests are the lungs of our  planet  
and are an invaluable ally in decarbonisation.5
Reflecting the partnership spirit of the EU Biodiversity Strategy 2030, Tetra Pak calls for an 
inclusive debate across the value chain through a public-private platform to develop a balanced 
approach that works for all dimensions of forests’ multifunctional role – from ecosystem services 
to productive, sustainable use. This platform should include governments, certification bodies, 
forest owners, downstream users and NGOs.

and 400,000 small and medium- sized enterprises 
already rely on forests for their livelihoods in 
Europe alone; 9 developing this green economic 
sector has the potential to create millions of 
new sustainable jobs in local and indigenous 
communities worldwide.10

Drawing on our experience, Tetra Pak calls on the 
European Commission to consider the following 
recommendations:
Getting it right, together
Nature is complex, as is sustainable forestry 
management that works for all dimensions of 
forests’ multifunctional role. Following the all- 
of-society approach called for by the European 
Commission, it is critical to involve stakeholders 
throughout the policy development process. This 
reflects the partnership spirit of the EU Biodiversity 
Strategy 2030 recognising that ‘all parts of the 
economy and society will have to play their role’ 
and that ‘protecting and restoring nature will 
need more than regulation alone’.
Tetra Pak is therefore calling for an inclusive debate 
across the value chain through a public-private 
platform that would include governments, 
certification bodies, forest owners, downstream 
users and NGOs. This debate should lay the ground 
for how the forest industry can best evolve to 
provide materials for a circular bioeconomy, while 
ensuring biodiversity is protected and restored, 
land regenerated, climate change mitigated and 
resources used in a sustainable way.
As such, Tetra Pak welcomes the European 
Commission’s intention outlined in the strategy 
to ‘build a European Business for Biodiversity 
movement’ as an integral part of the Climate 
Pact.16
Common definitions, criteria 
and metrics
There is currently a lack of science-based and 
commonly used definitions, criteria and metrics 
associated to restoration and protection across 
biodiversity regulations.
To get it right together, we need to 
agree on common goals and targets and 
speak the same language, developed by 
means of a deliberative and fact-based 
process involving all stakeholders. 
For this reason, Tetra Pak calls on the European 
Commission and other stakeholders to create a 
high-level dialogue on the future of sustainable 
sourcing of materials in Europe.  This roundtable 
will help in the process to: 
•  Adopt common definitions and standards for 
products that make sustainable use of forests 
materials (FSC TM, common methodology for 
carbon content of products among others); 
•   Address barriers and loopholes for sustainable 
use of products and the sourcing of plant-based 
materials;
•    Adapt legislation accordingly to create lead ma- 
rkets for bio-based and sustainable products. 
Tetra Pak asks that the proposed biodiversity and 
forest measures be subject to  comprehensive 
and science-based impact assessments. These 
should include economic, human and community 
impacts.
Mandatory due diligence and 
certification
Tetra Pak supports introducing a mandatory 
due diligence label to protect the environment 
throughout the supply chain, especially for 
plant-based products.
In 2007, the beverage carton industry was the 
first to voluntarily commit to full traceability and 
due diligence for all wood fibres we use in our 
cartons17 to prevent illegal timber entering the 
supply chain. This action was inspired by the 
EU Forest Law Enforcement, Governance and 
Trade Action Plan (FLEGT) and the EU Timber 
Regulation, which have been effective in raising 
awareness of illegal logging, contributing to 
improved forest governance globally, particularly 
in partner producer countries, and has reduced 
demand for illegal timber in the EU.18 We share the 
European Commission’s objective of eliminating 
deforestation and forest degradation.19

Building a circular bioeconomy 
that protects food, people and 
the planet
To advance on the Green deal objectives we must 
collectively build a  circular  bioeconomy and  
develop   the   role   of   plant-based   solutions 
in a sustainable way that respects ecological 
boundaries.
Today the amount of total greenhouse gas (GHG) 
emissions from materials used for packaging 
exceeds those from the aviation sector and they 
are expected to increase significantly, thereby 
making the Paris Agreement’s targets harder to 
reach. To ensure packaging can deliver solutions 
towards a sustainable bioeconomy we believe 
that there is an  urgent  need  to  promote the 
use of  climate  neutral  packaging  materials, 
both responsibly sourced and recyclable.
Tetra Pak is investing with partners in recycling 
infrastructure across the globe 25 to expand 
collection and  to radically increase the recycling 
of cartons.26
At the same time the use of sustainably sourced 
renewable packaging materials, such as wood 
fibres and plant-based polymers should be 
incentivised. They are complementary to the use 
of recycled materials, in particular for sensitive 
applications in food contact materials. They  have 
a low carbon footprint,27 grow back  in sustainably 
managed ecosystems and drive  economic  growth 
decoupled from finite resources.
To give an example, Tetra Pak’s cartons have a 
significantly lower carbon footprint compared to 
alternative packaging used for the same product 
category because the materials we use are 
renewable, lightweight and transport efficient.28
Through research programmes and appropriate 
financing schemes, such as Horizon Europe and 
LIFE, the EU should promote the development, 
of plant-based solutions and the circularity of 
materials. Investments in the deployment and 
upscaling of innovative plant-based solutions  
require a clear, stable and forward-looking 
framework.

Biologically diverse forests are the lungs of 
our planet and essential to achieve climate 
neutrality: an ambition we have set for our own 
operations by 2030 and across the value chain 
by 2050. Globally, approximately 2.6 billion 
tonnes of carbon dioxide, one-third of the CO2 
released from burning fossil fuels, is absorbed 
by forests every year. 29 European forests have 
the capacity to absorb the equivalent of nearly 
10% of all European Green House Gas (GHG) 
emissions each year. 30 In addition, forests and 
plant-based products store carbon for the 
duration of their lifetime and substitute fossil- 
based materials.
Forests are a key component of the circular 
bioeconomy we must build together. Plant-
based products, such as the wood fibres are 
renewable, recyclable and climate positive 
when they are sourced from growing, well-
managed forest.
Forests supply goods and services that can drive 
sustainable growth. The evolving European 
bioeconomy has an annual turnover of about 
EUR 2 trillion and employs  22  million  people  
–  over 8% of the total EU workforce. The global 
market for  biobased  products  is  expected  to  
grow  by
 1.8% annually until 2030, leading to the creation 
of one million jobs in the EU by 2030. The forest 
industry alone represents about 7% of the EU’s 
manufacturing GDP.31
Forestry is fundamental for inclusive prosperity 
and a just social transition. Globally, 1.6 billion 
people (nearly 25% of the world’s population) 
rely on forests for their livelihoods, many of 
whom are the world’s poorest. 32 The value-
chain structure offers the possibility for a more 
inclusive distribution of income and jobs over 
an extensive territory, especially in rural areas 
– in line with the Green Deal’s inclusive growth 
ambitions.33
Developing plant-based solutions in a way 
that is consistent with protecting the planet 
can boost EU strategic autonomy as it 
reinforces access to materials in the long term. 
Sustainably managed forests in the EU can 
contribute to shifting packaging products away 
from imported fossil resources, thereby also 
reducing carbon emissions and dependency 
on third countries. On average, more than 
70% of our packaging material is made from 
long, strong paper fibres mostly sourced from 
European forests.34

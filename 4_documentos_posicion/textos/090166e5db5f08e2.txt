EU Nature Restoration Targets- 
Additional feedback 
Background 
The European Commission has opened a public consultation on the development of binding EU nature 
restoration targets for 2030. The EU-Commission presented an Inception impact assessment in November 2020, 
with the objectives and policy options to restore degraded ecosystems.  
Our views 
Selection of areas for restoration 
Swedish Forest Industry Federation, SFIF, welcomes a discussion on nature restoration.  SFIF would like to point 
out the overall effectiveness for society of restoration and nature conservation management measures in 
protected and other areas set-aside for biodiversity or recreational purposes, as this at the same time will 
enable an effective and sustainable forest management in other areas, for circular bioeconomy purposes . 
In the Inception Impact Assessment, it was proposed to develop EU targets for restoration of degraded 
ecosystems, where one example was primary and old-growth forests. SFIF believes that primary and old-
growth forests are not good examples of forest ecosystems where restoration is needed. Even though science-
based, definitions of these two concepts is lacking, primary forests are often described as forests without any 
signs of human activities, and old-growths forests as forest with few such signs. These types of forests do 
normally not need any, restoration, with an exception of conservation burning in some cases. SFIF do therefor 
understand the proposal as a suggestion to restore and manage other forest areas to resemble such older 
forests. It is essential to describe from what and to what, the ecosystems are to be restored.  
Forests will however change over time as trees die, other trees are newly established and species both 
disappear and are added. Competitive conditions change when a sparse canopy closes, and shade-tolerant tree 
species might take over and eventually change the habitat type. Leaving forest for free development will 
therefor often lead to a new forest habitat, why management and restoration is needed to retain the 
biodiversity values. 
One purpose of the initiative is to restore ecosystems with the most potential to capture and store carbon. In 
this context, SFIF wants to underline that promoting increased growth of forests through sustainable forest 
management-and to use the biomass to produce wood-based products to substitute fossil-based materials and 
energy - is superior to all other alternatives to combat climate change, including restoration. Regarding this 
objective, primary and old-growth forests are pointed out in the Inception Impact Assessment why SFIF would 
like to remind that old trees do not capture carbon as well as younger trees do. Furthermore, definitions of the 
two concepts still is lacking and it would therefore not be appropriate to include the concepts in any 
suggestions for objectives.  
Older forests with structures and elements such as dead wood, old and coarse trees of different species, is 
important for biodiversity purposes, but not mainly for capturing and storing carbon in the long run.  
Furthermore, SFIF reminds that forest production and protection objectives can be met simultaneously and are 
not in contradiction. The aim must be to ensure the sustainable use of ecosystems, and to improve knowledge 
and monitoring.

The targets 
SFIF suggests that the targets for restoration focus on the process of change to reach good ecosystem status 
and not referring to a required status. A management process applied to degraded ecosystem s would ensure 
constant monitoring and improvement of the ecosystem. Targets that refer to a specific historical ecosystem 
status as the preferred/required status, or that are tightly tied to Annex I of the Habitat Directive, would not 
necessarily promote this important objective of continuous improvement.  An approach based on the Article 17 
report regarding assessment of favourable conservation status would also risk leading to inefficient measures 
as there are widely disperse ways of reporting the assessment among Member States. The key factors for 
results of the assessment are the reference values used for the habitat types’ areas . The Member States decide 
themselves which reference values to use when deciding the status of a habitat type. These values are then 
used in assessments of the habitat types. While Swedish reference values are 20% of estimated pre-industrial 
areas, many other countries use estimated areas at the time of EU accession.  
Concerning policy options, SFIF strongly supports non-binding measures. Earlier targets are not met, but the 
reason for this is not clear. In our view, voluntary, realistic and concrete targets combined with guidance, and 
financing support, gives more incentive to landowners to than legally binding targets do. The analysis for a 
legally binding target should therefor clearly investigate the reasons why not a bigger proportion have been 
restored and learn from Member States with good achievement, before developing further legislation. 
The preparatory work for restoration targets, and interaction with stakeholders, as Member States and forest 
owners, will be essential. There is a need for definitions and clear, accepted objectives of the restorative 
efforts, while developing targets for restoration Interaction and dialogue should include questions such as: 
• How to define areas to target and to assure that criteria are science-based?  
• What is to be achieved and why? Is the restoration to focus on improving the forest’s carbon uptake 
or biodiversity? Or both? Depending on answer, actions will differ. 
• Can management be carried out in a restored forest area? Nature is dynamic, not static, so the status 
of such an area will over time by definition change. 
• How will Member State and forest type specificities be respected?  
SFIF suggests that a Restoration Plan is built on a scientific basis, respected by researchers knowledgeable in a 
broad variety of European forest conditions. Definitions, measures and result evaluations should be Member 
State and/or region specific. Sustainable Forest Management, SFM, should be the main tool to meet objectives 
and such management should be adaptive. The Plan must create strong forest owner engagement. It should 
furthermore define that meeting several objectives simultaneously, such as restoration, climate change 
mitigation and wood production, is the overarching target. 
SFIF further wants to highlight that any EU restoration policy development must be based on a deep 
understanding that we have a great variety of forests in Europe. A one -size-fits-all solution therefore does not 
exist; instead adjustments for local and regional variations is a must and this  is best done by Member States. 
Acknowledge Member State lead on work related to conservation and protection of habitats and species.   
And finally; In order to embed forest ecosystems in the upcoming legislative framework, it would be essential 
to develop the approach under the auspices of the upcoming EU forest strategy and in particular benefitting 
the expertise of the Standing Forestry Committee (SFC). Active and sustainable forest management is a 
prerequisite for the further development of the circular bioeconomy in Europe.

Examples of Swedish forest owners’ contributions creating and reinforcing natural values 
Swedish forest policy is based on freedom under responsibility and a sectoral responsibility to extend beyond 
the level set by the Forestry Act. Voluntary setting aside land and enhancing nature consideration, often as a 
result of forest owners being FSC- or PEFC-certified, play important roles. Other efforts include various forms of 
education, planning and measures targeting specific environments and species, as illustrated by the following 
few examples carried out by forest owning companies, on voluntary basis without any economically 
compensation as a part of sectoral responsibility.  
 Conservation burning 
Conservation burning creates suitable conditions for many 
species that depended on the recurring fires in the old boreal 
forest.  
 
 
 
 
Lady of the snows thrives better after fire 
Hokaberg in Härjedalen hosts some of the province’s richest 
moss communities. The anemone Pulsatilla vernalis (lady of the 
snows, Härjedalen's ‘province flower’) is protected and red-
listed as vulnerable. The species thrives in open environments 
with sedimentary soils and is disadvantaged by overgrowth and 
competition from, above all, lichens. It has deep roots and is 
promoted by fires, which favour its seed dispersal. Holmen 
carried out a nature conservation burning of 30 hectares of the 
premises. Just three weeks after a nature conservation burning 
of 30 hectares of land owned by Holmen 25% of 100 seedlings 
of the anemone (marked before the fire to allow their 
subsequent development) had sprouted green leaves

The white-backed woodpecker should return 
In 2016, another forestry company (Stora Enso Skog) carried out a 
spectacular felling with the help of a pontoon bridge on an island in 
the river Klarälven. Conifers were cut down and dead hardwood 
was created to help the critically endangered white-backed 
woodpecker to re-establish in central Sweden. This was one of 
10,000 hectares of forest land that the landowner Bergvik Skog Öst 
restored in efforts to meet the woodpecker’s needs. 
 
Exposing sand for the sand lizard 
At Brattforsheden in Värmland, Bergvik Skog Öst restored 150 hectares of 
forest to benefit the unusual sand lizard. Mosaic-like patches of sparse forest, 
dead and dying trees, as well as exposed sand surfaces, have been created. In 
the pine forest that has not been restored, there are dense lichen mats and 
the sand lizard does not thrive. In addition to the sand lizard increasing in 
number, species such as lady of the snows, the bee Andrena argentata and 
nightjar have also benefited from the restoration. 
 
Diversity parks for diversity and people’s wellbeing  
The SCA has established five diversity parks (Mångfaldsparker), 
one in each county in northern Sweden. These are landscapes 
covering several hundred hectares where at least half of the 
area is managed to promote natural and cultural values. An 
example is Märlingsberget in Jämtland, a pine forest with some 
pines more than 300 years old. There are untouched areas and 
hilly terrain with small ponds, bogs, streams and hiking trails. 
 
Confirmation of successful nature conservation burning 
A nature conservation burning at Märrviksnäset in Medelpad was followed up five years later with an 
inventory, which showed that the red-listed longhorn beetle Tragosoma depsarium had established. This was 
the first time in 50 years that the species had been recorded in the province. The burning was carried out by 
the SCA forestry organization, which conducts nature conservation burnings over large areas every year.  
Wild bees receive help with food and housing 
Holmen, together with the Östergötland county administrative board, is implementing 
several measures to help important wild bees. Sand beds have been laid out at various 
sites in the county. The target species are the bees Andrena marginate and Nomada 
argentata, but many more species benefit from the sand beds (Figure 67). The bees also 
benefit from burning, mowing and clearing shady trees and shrubs so that the soil can be 
covered with flowering plants.

About the Swedish forest industries 
The Swedish Forest Industries Federation (SFIF) represents the Swedish forest industries, which refine wood 
resources to bio-based products, such as pulp, paper, board, packaging material, sawn timber, refined wood 
products and advanced biofuels. The core business for SFIF members is industrial ac tivities based on wood 
sourced from sustainably managed forests. Among SFIF members are also some of the largest private forest 
holdings in Europe and forest related European Union policy is therefore of high importance.  
SFIF members take pride in managing their forest holdings sustainably. Historically, we have a much experience 
of restoration of degraded forest ecosystems, as big parts of southern Sweden were deforested in the 
beginning of the 20th century but are now and since nearly 100 years co vered by forest. Focus has been on 
reforestation and silviculture, resulting in a successful doubling of the Swedish forest stock from the early 
1900’s until today. Since the early 1990s, a broader perspective on sustainability, also including biodiversity  and 
social aspects, has been prevailing. Maintaining and enhancing biodiversity is today an integral part of SFIF 
members’ forest management. By engagement in forest certification, Sweden has become one of the largest 
suppliers of wood from sustainably managed forests.  
Today more than five percent of Sweden's forests, about 1,2 million hectares, are exempted from production 
forestry on voluntary basis and managed to preserve and promote biodiversity under forest certification 
schemes or otherwise. These areas and forest ecosystems are not degraded, but biodiversity in some of them 
would benefit of more active management activities to be preserved and developed.

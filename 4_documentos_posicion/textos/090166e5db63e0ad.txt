MTK’s additional feedback on the public consultation on EU biodiversity policy 
initiatives: development of the EU Nature Restoration Plan 
 
 
The Central Union of Agricultural Producers and Forest Owners (MTK) welcomes the opportunity 
to contribute to the development of the new EU Nature Restoration Plan outlined in the EU Biodi-
versity Strategy for 2030.  
 
Agriculture and forestry are livelihoods based directly on natural resources and ecosystem ser-
vices, and thus, the good condition of nature is crucial for MTK and its members as well. In its own 
Biodiversity Programme, MTK has recognized well -targeted nature management and restoration 
as one of the three key elements in safeguarding and enhancing biodiversity. The other two are 
best practices in agriculture and forest management, and landowner-based voluntary nature con-
servation.  
 
In all activities done to promote biodiversity, economic and social perspective must be fully 
acknowledged. Recognizing and ensuring the three pillars of sustainability in a balanced 
way is a prerequisite for the acceptability of the Strategy and for its successful implemen-
tation since the outcomes will largely depend on the motivation, support and future involve-
ment of landowners and other actors on the ground. This applies to the restoration section of 
the Strategy as well since planning and implementation of restoration measures have highly local 
characteristics. 
 
The concept and objectives of restoration 
 
MTK believes that there are many different understandings of the concept and objectives of 
restoration. This is no surprise since the opportunities and practical actions taken to improve the 
condition of nature depend on the case. In some sites the aim is to remove the signs of previous 
human intervention whereas in other areas the aim is to strengthen the role of human-related ac-
tivities. Furthermore, sometimes active restoration is the most effective choice whereas sometimes 
allowing self-restoration is the most suitable solution and leads to the best outcome.  
 
According to MTK’s understanding, there is a fine line between nature management and restora-
tion. MTK points out that these two are mutually supportive and therefore the separation of these 
two is n ot necessarily even needed. Nature values can be created and strengthened by nature 
management, and with restoration the recovery of degraded habitats towards natural or seminat-
ural state can be accelerated.  
 
When it comes to the objectives of restoration, there is probably no general definition neither for 
“good condition“ nor for “degraded”. In most cases restoration is a process with a long and unsure 
time horizon: restoration done today may lead to measurable outcomes only years or decades  
later. What is more, the outcomes of restoration measures are dependent on many external factors 
and uncertainties. For example, climate change is likely to be an increasing threat to the favourable 
conservation status of many habitats and species. Despite all efforts made to reach and maintain 
the good condition, the actions might not deliver the wanted outcomes at least not in a desired

Targeting and prioritization of restoration 
 
There are restoration needs within the current protected area network but also elsewhere. Target-
ing of restoration measures usually requires step by step thinking since effective allocation of lim-
ited resources must be considered in prioritization.  
 
MTK is of the opinion that sites located within existing protected areas should be prioritized 
in restoration to unlock the full potential of those areas . Targeting restoration measures to 
protected areas could also help to avoid possible conflicting interests related to land use. 
 
Outside protected areas, both nature management and restoration can further support the work of 
everyday sustainable agriculture and forestry for the benefit of biodiversity. The continuation of 
sustainable agriculture and forestry is necessary to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals 
and the goals of the EU Green Deal. Thus, integrated approaches combining both sustainable use 
of renewable natural resources and biodiversity protection are needed. This highlights the im-
portance of multifunctionality as one of the guiding elements.  
 
Continued sustainable use of renewable natural resources combined with nature management and 
restoration can lead to win-win solutions. Improving the capacity of ecosystems to deliver a wide 
range of ecosystem services benefits not only biodiversity, but also human needs. Thus, MTK 
highlights that supporting ecosystems’ capacities to provide multiple ecosystem ser-
vices should be a core objective for restoration in sites that are located  outside the pro-
tected area network.  
 
It is likely that the degradation of nature values caused by climate change increases the need for 
restoration in the future . It is necessary to anticipate climate change effects in the planning of 
restoration actions to ensure resilience to changing conditions. Climate change mitigation and ad-
aptation are closely linked to many aspects of restoration as well. In this regard, attention must 
also be paid to e.g. unwanted trade-offs. MTK stresses that making good use of the possibilities 
provided by active sustainable agriculture and forest management is central also from the 
viewpoint of minimizing the future restoration needs caused by the negative effects of cli-
mate change.  
 
Both nature management and restoration require knowledge and resources and therefore , 
measures must be carefully targeted and prioritized to ensure cost-effectiveness. The desired  tar-
geting and prioritization is likely to very different depending on the perspective (Member State level 
vs. EU level).  
 
Due to the many specificities and heterogenous conditions existing across Europe, “one 
size fits all” approaches do not lead to optimal solutions  neither in general biodiversity 
policy nor in restoration. Flexibility is needed to allow the recognition of every country’s 
precise needs, conditions, opportunities and starting points. Therefore, more detailed plan-
ning and implementation of the Strategy in relation to restoration must take place at the 
level of the Member States. The key is to choose right measures for right places, and this under-
lines the need for case by case decisions.

The possible elements of the EU Nature Restoration Plan 
 
MTK is of the opinion that the EU Biodiversity Strategy must be a strategic tool and leave room 
for national and local decisions. This applies also to the restoration section of the Strategy. If any 
restoration targets would be proposed by the Commission as part of the EU Restoration Plan, there 
should be one general target only to secure flexibility. Ecosystem specific or Member State specific 
targets should not be set. 
 
Based on the viewpoints mentioned above, the EU Nature Restoration Plan should focus on 
strengthening different enabling measures instead of defining the actual restoration priori-
ties and measures. There are possibilities to increase the EU added value through supporting the 
work done in the Member States by e.g. providing better EU funding opportunities and enhancing 
knowledge sharing.  
 
The Strategy conveys a message that in addition to regulation alone, protecting and restoring na-
ture will need strong partnerships among different actors at local, regional, national and European 
level. MTK agrees and reminds that open communication and stakeholder involvement are 
crucial to ensure social sustainabi lity and acceptability, and those demand participatory 
processes taking place close to the ones that are affected by the different activities. Instead 
of legally binding restrictions and demands, the focus should be on positive incentives intensifying 
voluntary bottom-up participatory approaches, supporting the expansion of existing best practices, 
and encouraging the development of new business opportunities. These constructive approaches 
based on cooperation of multiple actors create engagement leading to successful outcomes. 
 
The actual restoration work will take place in the Member States  and therefore, also defining the 
needs and priorities for restoration should be decided there. In addition, each Member State must 
have the possibility to choose the general principles and practical methods of implementa-
tion used in the context of restoration . Successful and effective restoration can be based on 
voluntary approaches. The Finnish Helmi Habitats Programme focusing on voluntary-based nature 
management and restoration is an example of a national restoration plan that combines a land-
owner-respective approach and ecological functionality. 
 
National restoration plans must offer a flexible possibility for the Member States to decide 
and indicate their contribution: what, where, when and how restoration is done. To fully embrace 
the country specific character of the national restoration plans, it is highly necessary that the con-
tents of the plans are not restricted by any EU level attributes based on top-down thinking. National 
restoration plans hold great potential for success if versatility is understood as an asset. 
 
Decision-making at the level of Member States is clearly a justified principle in relation to 
restoration. It enables appropriate targeting and prioritization ,  improves cost-effective-
ness, and secures the fulfilment of the rights of landowners. In addition, it reflects the prin-
ciples of subsidiarity and proportionality. 
 
When it comes to monitoring and reporting, MTK points out that it is necessary to minimize all 
administrative burden. Based on the Habitats and Birds Directive s, regular reporting is already 
required. The existing national and EU mechanisms for continuous assessment and reporting on 
progress should be utilized as much as possible to avoid additional burden.

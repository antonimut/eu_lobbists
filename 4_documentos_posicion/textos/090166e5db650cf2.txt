Why is important to restore Mediterranean Wetlands 
 
The Ramsar Convention, adopted at International Conference on the Conservation of Wetlands 
and Waterbirds in Iran (1971), is the intergovernmental treaty that provides the framework for 
the conservation and wise use of wetlands and their resources. In its documents, in addition to 
a detailed classification of wetland types ranging from marshes to lagoons, mangroves, rice 
paddies or salt marshes, it establis hed a principle of " wise use " of wetlands, applying the 
criterion of sustainable development in their exploitation. 
 
This treaty, laid the foundations for action on wetlands, areas that had been traditionally 
considered unhealthy and of little value, many of them being lost through drying up and 
overexploitation of their resources. In Europe, the protection of wetlands is a requirement of 
the European Union's Water Framework Directive and there is a coincidence of objectives with 
the Birds Directive and the Habitats Directive, which address aspects established by Community 
policy on wetlands, especially with regard to integrated water management, from a quantitative 
and qualitative point of view. 
 
Spain is a privileged country from a natural point of view, as it is home to a large part of Europe's 
biodiversity. Almost 85% of vascular plants and more than 50% of its fauna species are present 
in Spain. Its wide variety of habitats (54% of the total number of habitats of the whole European 
Union) is home to rare and diverse forms of life, with a total of 1,500 unique endemic plants in 
the world and others yet to be defined (MARM, 2011). The Mediterranean region is  also home 
to a large number and diversity of wetlands, ranging from extensive coastal lagoons t o small 
ponds of just a few square metres in area.  In this sense, the biodiversity associated with these 
wetland environments, as in other parts of the world, supports the functioning of ecosystems, 
which in turn produce "services".  
 
These ecosystem services are defined as the benefits that people obtain from ecosystems (MA, 
2005) and include provisioning services (e.g. food, fibre, fuel and water), regulating services (e.g. 
climate, flood, disease, waste and water quality), cultural services (e.g. recrea tion, aesthetic 
enjoyment, tourism, spiritual and ethical values), and supporting services necessary for the 
production of the rest of the ecosystem services (e.g. soil formation, photosynthesis and 
nutrient recycling). 
 
A recent meta -analysis indicates that restoration activities that enhance biodiversity are 
positively correlated with increased ecosystem service provision (Rey Benayas y otros. 2009). As 
the objectives of restoration activities have increasingly focused on ecosystem services (Bullock 
et al. 2011), it is important to consider the impacts of wetland use on biodiversity and ecosystem 
functioning. Where the drivers of wetland degradation cannot be reduced or eliminated, 
restoration activities can still contribute to reduce negative impacts and enhance benefits.

Although wetlands occupy only 2 –3% of the land area of the Mediterranean basin, more than 
30% of the region’s vertebrate species depend on them (MWO 2018). Meanwhile, and with local 
variations on the overall trend, Mediterranean wetlands and their biodiver sity are in sharp 
decline. For example, the Mediterranean lost approximately 50% of its natural wetland surface 
area over the twentieth century (Perennou et al. 2012) and a sample of natural wetland sites 
shrunk by 45 –51% on average between 1970 and 2013 ( MWO 2018). Wetland -dependent 
amphibians, reptiles, mammals, fish, odonates and orthopterans have all recently experienced 
large declines in abundance over recent decades (MWO 2018; Fraixedas et al. 2019). Critically 
Endangered species include the Adriatic sturgeon Acipenser naccarii, the Greek red damselfly 
Pyrrhosoma elisabethae, and the beautiful water -starwort Callitriche pulchra (IUCN 2020). 
Historically, Mediterranean wetlands have been drained or highly sanitised for agricultural use, 
development of t ourist areas, and perceived health benefits (Balbo et al. 2017; Parrinello and 
Bécot 2019). Contemporary challenges for Mediterranean wetlands include rapid urbanisation, 
increased water usage, climate change and land conversion (MWO 2018).  
 
Regarding Cl imate regulation , as active ecosystems, wetlands play an important role in the 
global C cycle (Battin et al. 2009) because of their high capacity to fix and store C from the 
atmosphere (Mitsch et al. 2013). Therefore, wetlands are one of the most important ecosystem 
types for global C sequestration, despite the small area they occupy (Mitra et al. 2005). Different 
studies highlights the importance of conservation status on the climate -mitigating capacity of 
Mediterranean wetlands. Based on these studies, re sults, degradation or restoration level 
influences the C balance and the mitigating/warming role of these coastal wetlands on a short

to medium time scale. In Spain, Freshwater and brackish marshes showed capacity for C capture, 
especially in the restored sites ( ∼950 g m−2 yr−1), with a paramount role of helophytes. 
Differences among the study sites revealed the importance of alterations and restoration for C 
exchange. Specifically, a highly altered site showed increased rates of aerobic respiration 
compared to und isturbed sites, with a decrease in C storage capacity to ∼50 g m−2 yr−1. 
Management practices resulting in instability of the organic sediments favoured degradative 
metabolism, with release of stored C and concomitant increases in CH4 emissions. 
hydromorphological alterations in these sites may convert healthy ecosystems contributing to C 
sequestration and climate change mitigation into C emitting ecosystems. (Morant D. et.al 2020) 
 
Degradation causes and current state of conservation of 
Mediterranean wetlands 
 
Mediterranean wetlands are especially vulnerable due to the fragile balance that determines 
their ecological state and also their specific hydrological regime, so it is important to know their 
dynamics to understand how they work and, in the case in w hich deficiencies are detected, to 
act to include them into an Environmental Restoration process. The causes of the degradation 
and loss of Mediterranean wetlands are multiple, although the pressures and impacts they 
receive can be grouped into the followi ng broad categories suggested by the expert and 
researcher Camacho, A. (2011): 
 
• Hydrological (water withdrawals, artificial damming of influent watercourses, artificial 
water contributions, drainage structures, overexploitation of the aquifer, etc.). 
 
• Geomorphological (changes in morphometry, extraction of materials, over-erosion due 
to deforestation in the basin, etc.). 
 
• Water pollution  (occasional discharge of wastewater or specific pollutants, diffuse 
sources of pollutants, alteration of groundwater, etc.). 
 
• Pressures and impacts on biological communities  (exploitation of living resources, 
aquaculture's bad practices, lack of connectiv ity, the introduction of exotic species, 
etc.). 
 
• Use of the territory  (occupation of the basin or the wetlands margins zone by 
urbanization, industries, agricultural operations, power lines, etc.). 
 
• Others (solid waste, hunting, poorly managed recreational use, etc.). 
 
The official data on the general conservation situation of Mediterranean wetlands is relatively 
outdated. According to the Spanish Environmental Ministry's data (MITECO, no date), the main 
and most exhaustive recent compilation of information  on Spanish wetlands is the inventory 
 
 5 
developed by the General Directorate of Hydraulic Works between 1989 and 1991 (DGOH 1991). 
These data were obtained by a multidisciplinary team, in which information on some 1,400 
spaces was compiled. This inventory in cluded the aquatic environments of mainland Spain 
(wetlands and mountain lakes) with a surface area greater than 0.5 Ha, except for rivers, 
watercourses and reservoirs. 
 
From this inventory, it stands out that inland aquatic environments represent 92% of w etlands 
places, although it does not reach 14% of the total Spanish wetlands' area. This is because there 
is a small number of very extensive coastal wetlands that occupy 86% of the total surface, among 
which six stand out for their great extension: the Gu adalquivir Marshes, the Bay of Cádiz, the 
Ebro Delta, the Mar Menor , the Albufera of Valencia and the Aiguamolls de l'Empordà . The 
inland wetlands, those of freshwater (46%) and those of mountain (32%) must be highlighted 
due to their abundance, and also a  large number of inland wetlands correspond to temporary 
lagoons that are located between Castilla-La Mancha and Castilla y León , wetlands that have 
been affected by drainage or desiccation and by the occupation of their margins zone and lagoon 
basin for agrarian uses. 
 
Among the inland wetlands, floodplains have suffered a greater degree of alteration, being in 
their totality modified by man, followed by freshwater wetlands (62% modified), karst (54%) and 
saline (51%). Mountain lakes and wetlands are the o nes with the best state of conservation, 
although their surfaces are only 4%  of the total surface occupied by wetlands . On the other 
hand, more than two -thirds of the inventoried coastal wetlands are considered altered or 
degraded, while less than a third are conserved. 
 
In 2004 a Royal Decree (RD435 / 2004) was published to update the Spanish wetlands inventory, 
but until now National Government has only published data from 8 of the 17 Regions of Spain 
and there are not available public data about the conservation state of th ese environments. 
Nevertheless, from that inventory, it is highlighted that there are at least 734 inventoried 
wetlands with a surface area of almost 231 thousand hectares (MITECO, 2020). The lack of 
updated official data on the c onservation state of Mediterranean wetlands is a challenge to 
establishing a general criterion for their restoration, and therefore the approach for this purpose 
should focus on the service provided by these ecosystems.

Objectives that the restoration law proposal should include to 
cover Mediterranean wetland ecosystems 
 
Schmidt and other authors (2011) suggest that taking into account the current political, legal and 
economic context in Spain, it is necessary to bet on projects, tools and techniques in the restoration 
of wetlands that consider the ecosystem services provided and actively include the potential 
beneficiaries of your post-restoration upgrade. Even more so in cases where the latter are willing to 
pay proportionally for the improvements. The same authors suggest promoting and prioritising 
conservation and restoration projects that intervene on ecosys tem functions over those that 
intervene on ecosystem structures. 
 
The former act on the pressures or causes of environmental degradation or ecosystem services, 
allowing the ecosystem to recover its natural functions and dynamic processes in such a way 
that no further intervention is needed along its trajectory (no return or passive restoration 
projects). The latter approach restoration as partial remediation, requiring updates or 
complementary measures over time. Thus, for example, management guidelines can  be 
established in Mediterranean wetlands that favour their function as carbon sinks, while 
maintaining their ecological integrity and functionality, so that they provide the services of a 
healthy ecosystem and favouring management that considers the climate mitigation service. 
 
Similarly, to guarantee the conservation and improvement of the ecological integrity of 
wetlands, a broad knowledge of the abiotic components, composition, abundance of native 
species, structure of biological communities, rates of change and wetland support processes is 
required to characterise the healthy or optimal operational condition of the ecos ystem. It is 
therefore necessary to implement regulations that require continuous monitoring of these 
parameters in restored wetlands in order to have quantifiable and historical data on the 
evolution of the ecosystem. And understanding that many Mediterranean wetlands are linked 
to cultural activities , in the framework of promoting the prevention and rational use of 
resources, not only the ecological integrity of the wetland should be reached, but also the 
traditional uses that have taken place in the wetl and, allowing, within the limits imposed by its 
capacity to drain, the promotion of these social values and the involvement of the population 
in its conservation tasks. 
 
The objectives of the proposed Law should also be in unison with the aims of the Spani sh 
Strategic Plan for the Conservation and Wise Use of Wetlands, which are as follows: 
 
• To e nsure the conservation and wise use of wetlands, including the restoration or 
rehabilitation of those that have been destroyed or degraded. 
• To integrate the conservation and wise use of wetlands into sectoral policies, especially 
water, coasts, land use planning, forestry, agriculture, fisheries, mining, industry and 
transport. 
 
 7 
• To contribute to the fulfilment of the commitments of the Spanish State in relation  to 
European and international conventions, directives, policies and agreements related to 
wetlands, as well as to the application of the Spanish Strategy for the Conservation and 
Sustainable Use of Biological Diversity and the Mediterranean Wetlands Strategy.

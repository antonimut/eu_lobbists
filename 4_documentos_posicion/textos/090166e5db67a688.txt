Additional comments to the public consultation on nature 
restoration targets under the EU biodiversity strategy for 
2030 
April 2021 
With this document, we would like to provide further recommendations on setting the legally binding 
nature restoration targets which we would like to provide as part of the ongoing public consultation. 
 
The EEB has been advocating for the EU to establish legally binding nature restoration targets since 
2019 in order to drive much-needed nature restoration across the EU. These comments complement 
and emphasise the main points from the joint position Restoring EU's Nature  and Scoping paper for 
free-flowing rivers target (also submitted as a supporting documents to the consultation.   
 
The new restoration law must be additional to existing obligations 
and not undermine them  
While the new law should create synergies with and support the implementation of existing 
obligations, such as the Birds and Habitats D irectives (BHD), the Water Framework Directive (WFD) 
or the Marine Strategy Framework Directive (MSFD), it is of fundamental importance that the new law 
does not weaken or undermine the existing legislation. New obligations must therefore be additional 
to the existing ones.  
Especially Art.6 of the Habitats Directive (HD) already includes some restoration requirements (Art.6(1) 
HD) and sets out a clear non-deterioration obligation in Art.6(2) HD. The absence of a deadline of the 
BHD obligations does not jus tify the lacking or insufficient implementation and enforcement of the 
obligations set out in the Directives.  
A new restoration law that requires the restoration of areas that Member States failed to protect from 
degradation, in breach of Art.6 HD, risks undermining the obligations in Art.6. It does so because it 
allows Member States to count measures towards their restoration target that they have already been 
legally required to take for decades. This is not only problematic from a rule of law perspectiv e, as it 
tolerates and rewards insufficient implementation of long -standing obligations, but also significantly 
reduces the overall level of action and ambition for biodiversity. Measures that go beyond the BHD 
requirements, coupled with the strict enforcement of the existing obligations, significantly increase the 
overall biodiversity (and climate) benefits that are much needed given the urgency and extent of the 
biodiversity crisis.   
In addition, there are risks that the new law will be perceived as ‘let ting Member States off the hook’ 
for not complying with their long-standing obligations more broadly, beyond the restoration obligations 
in the BHD , and will further weaken the implementation of the Directives. It could also delay 
implementation and enforcement steps that could already be taken today  until 2030, so as to allow 
Member States time to comply with the new law. However, given the extent of the biodiversity crisis, 
we simply do not have this time to further delay the proper implementation and enforcement of the key

restoration measures that go beyond the reparation of the breach of the non -deterioration obligation 
by taking measures to restore species or habitats that go beyond  those protected through the BHD. 
Additionality may also be provided by physically restoring beyond the protected habitat which will

Similarly, restoration is required under the Water Framework Directive in order to reach good status of

implementation of the Water Framework Directive.  
At EU level, a significant increase in resources and staff , as well as political will to provide for the full 
enforcement of the existing Directives as well as the new restoration law, is required to ensure the 
proper implementation thereof.  
 
Learning from previous failures for the approach of the law  
As we consider the main reasons for the failure of the previous restoration targets to be their voluntary 
character, we welcome the commitment by the Commission to propose legally binding restoration 
targets by the end of the year.  
The focus on ‘degraded’ ecosystem was also a key factor in slowing down and even preventing proper 
restoration action as it required a lengthy debate to define what ecosystem degradation entails . We 
consider such a definition to be unhelpful in this context as it risks turning what can be a targeted and 
strong law into a rather broad and weak instrument.    
A new, quick -to-implement law on landscape level restoration to high -quality nature, leading to 
biodiversity rich and functioning habitats is needed. While guidance and financial incentives can aid in 
the implementation of the law, they cannot and should not replace legally binding targets. We have 
learnt from the previous Biodiversity Strategy, and also other EU environmental tools, that voluntary 
approaches often result in inaction. Due to the extent of the biodiversity and climate crisis, there is no 
time to lose and we therefore need a strong restoration law that leads to urgent action on the ground.  
 
Criteria to guide restoration priorities  
The law must foc us on ecosystems with carbon storage and adaptation potential and require 
fundamental land and sea use change, rather than incremental improvements. Examples of such 
ecosystems include forests, peatlands, floodplains, free -flowing rivers, wetlands, biodive rsity-rich 
grasslands, coastal areas and marine ecosystems such as seagrass meadows or biogenic reefs.  
Incremental improvements in urban ecosystems, agricultural areas, soil on its own or fishing practices 
should be addressed through other policies and legislation. Radical improvements are urgently needed 
in these areas, but this law should bring ecosystems in any starting conditions to high -quality nature 
rather than slightly improving an ecosystem to a slightly less poor condition. Therefore, ‘nutrient 
cycling’, ‘soil fertility’, ‘gene pool maintenance’ and ‘pest and disease control’ should not be the direct 
focus for this specific tool (although the above will be indirect benefits).

This new law should not aspire to address all biodiversity and sustainability challenges at once and 
should instead focus on achieving ecological restoration to high quality nature for specific ecosystems 
important for biodiversity and climate in 15% of land and sea and res tore 15% of rivers to a free -
flowing state all across the EU. The achievement of legally binding restoration targets must result in 
large-scale land use change in ecosystems that create synergies with climate mitigation and adaptation 
instead of aiming for the sustainable management of ecosystems or incremental improvements across 
all ecosystems, including agricultural, soil and urban ecosystems . Sustainable use/management and 
improvements in agriculture, soil and forestry must be achieved through other instruments, such as a 
bold CAP reform, a new legal instrument on soil protection and a Forestry Strategy that is focused on 
protection and restoration.  
The restoration law must promote natural ecosystem dynamics, connectivity, ensure long -term 
protection, ecosystem resilience and focus on the restoration of ecological functions. Biodiversity must 
be the main priority, with climate mitigation and adaptation benefits as a supportive objective. These 
priorities will also bring a broad range of knock-on benefits, relating to various ecosystem services with 
enormous benefits for human health and wellbeing as well as sustainable jobs and cost -effective 
nature-based solutions.  
We consider  that this new law has the potential to put biodiversity on the path to recovery while 
enabling climate mitigation and adaptation as well as providing broader societal benefits. However, to 
make the most of this opportunity and to ensure that these benefits materialise, the law must be 
focused, targeted and ambitious, and lead to speedy implementation of biodiversity and climate 
improvements.  
 
The target: restore 15% of EU land, sea and rivers  
We propose that the overall EU target must be to restore 15% of the EU land and sea, to restore 15% 
of all rivers to a free-flowing state and should entail a target for CO2 removal by restored natural sinks 
that is additional to the 2030 emissions reduction target. These targets should be met at Member State 
level, i.e. without  effort sharing, so that all Member States contribute their fair share and to ensure 
ecological coherence.  
If ecosystem-specific targets are being developed, they need to focus on biodiversity-rich ecosystems 
with significant carbon storage and sequestration potential , such as peatlands, floodplains, wetlands, 
old-growth forests, biodiversity rich grasslands, free flowing rivers and coastal areas or marine 
ecosystems. The ecosystem specific targets need to be consistent with the EU overall restoration target 
and must accumulate to 15% of the EU’s land and sea area.  
The target should not refer to a proportion of degraded ecosystems but to the EU’s entire land and sea 
area in order to be of the necessary level of ambition and to be easy  and clear to implement.  
The targets should be area -based and should not require a definition of ‘degraded ecosystems’ or 
‘degradation’ as this is likely to trigger lengthy debates that will slow down progress in taking action 
on the ground and in meeting the targets with the urgency that is required.  
The law should build upon existing definitions and monitoring regimes as much as possible while 
ensuring that all obligations stemming from the new restoration law are additional to the existing 
obligations under e.g. the Birds and Habitats Directive, the Water Framework Directive or the Marine

The restoration targets should initiate the implementation of measures that result in fundamental land 
and sea use change, starting an ecological restoration process. Changes in the management must 
occur shortly after the adoption of the new law. Achieving the end result of a restored ecosystem can, 
depending on the ecosystem, take a very long t ime. An outcome-based target would thus risk a lack 
of accountability and enforceability. The restoration targets should therefore focus on an obligation of 
conduct (to implement the necessary management and land use changes) instead of an obligation of 
result, while having a mechanism to ensure that if the measures are not effective in leading to the result 
(identified in national restoration plans), timely changes must be made.    
The targets should be formulated so as to allow active and passive restorat ion as well as restoration 
activities inside and outside protected areas including Natura 2000 areas.   
 
Ecosystem priorities  
The law should focus on biodiversity-rich habitats with potential for carbon storage such as peatlands, 
wetlands, forests, grasslands, rivers, forests, floodplains and marine ecosystems. It should be targeted 
and should not focus on sustainable use or incremental improvements in agriculture, soil and urban 
areas as these should be addressed by other polices. 
The listed ‘landscape features’ are essential to bring biodiversity back to farmland biodiversity, but they 
should not be the focus of the restoration law. Instead, they should be fundamental criteria for 
sustainable farming and are key elements of agroecology. These practices ar e therefore not real 
restoration measures. Including them in the restoration law would water down the targets and would 
weaken the beneficial impact of the law. Restoring grasslands on the other hand, should be a high 
priority for the restoration law.  
While there are many opportunities for synergies with climate mitigation and adaptation, biodiversity 
should be the main priority of the restoration law so that habitats that are key for biodiversity but have 
lower carbon storage potential will also be restored.  
As outlined above, the law should not focus on agricultural, soil and urban ecosystems. Sustainable 
use/management and improvements in agriculture, soil and forestry must be achieved through other 
instruments, such as a bold CAP reform, a new legal instrument on soil and a Forestry Strategy that is 
focused on protection and restoration.   
 
Delivering the targets  
The obligations in the restoration law should focus on timely measures to initiate restoration rather 
than on restored outcome to ensure timely implementation and enforceability. The law must contain 
clear and binding deadlines for milestones, such as restoration plans, the implementation of measures 
as well as stocktaking of the effectiveness of these measures against the objectives set out in the 
restoration plan. Public participation must be ensured at Member State level throughout the process.

Long-term protection  
Member States should detail plans for  the long-term protection of restored areas in their restoration 
plans and regularly report on the effectiveness of the restoration measures in reaching their objectives. 
Public participation throu ghout the process and access to justice provisions are key to ensure 
accountability. 
The specific form of the long -term protection may be left up to the Member States in line with set 
criteria that the protection must inter alia have a legal basis, be long -term and ensure the non -
deterioration of the restored site, including where this requires on-gong management activities.  
 
If the new restoration law is focused on the fundamental need to bring back nature at scale, it can be a 
real opportunity to put biodiversity on the path to recovery as well as to demonstrate the EU's 
leadership on restoration globally, setting an example for other nations and regions.

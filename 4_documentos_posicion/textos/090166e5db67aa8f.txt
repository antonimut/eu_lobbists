 
 
Review of the application of the EU Regulation on Invasive Alien Species 
 
EUROGROUP FOR ANIMALS’ SUBMISSION 
 
March 2021 
 
 
 
 
Invasive alien species ( IAS) have severe and growing impacts on Europ ean biodiversity. EU 
level action is essential, as no single Member State can tackle this issue on their own. By 
introducing an EU-wide system to tackle this issue, with the Union List at its core , the IAS 
Regulation aimed at establishing a  transparent, science -based, process, able to respond 
rapidly to and prevent new invasions . However, Eurogroup for Animals consider s that the 
following factors have hampered the effectiveness of a system primarily designed to prevent 
the impacts of invasive alien species: 
 
• While there is sufficient scientific ground for the Commission and Member States to 
consider the importance of prevention as a more environmentally desirable and cost-
effective strategy while dealing with invasive alien species, this has not been 
sufficiently reflected in the listing process. Presently, the Union List contains species 
already present in the EU, and many with a widespread distribution , while effective 
prevention should address the sources of introduction of IAS. 
 
• The listing of new species is an extremely  burdensome and lengthy process, 
particularly inappropriate to address some dynamic pathways of introduction, like the 
exotic pet trade.    
 
• The inclusion in the Union List of highly invasive species has been opposed by Member 
States to protect their economic interests. For instance, despite its massive impact on 
the European biodiversity and the scientific evidence shown in the Risk Assessment 
presented by Portugal, the American mink has not been included in the Union List .  
This was not only a missed opportunity to save the European economy millions of 
Euros in LIFE projects to control American mink to protect native species, but it also 
compromised the credibility and political ambition of the IAS Regulation. 
 
• Article 9 of the Regulation establishes provisions which allow existing commercial 
activities involving invasive alien species  to continue to operate under a system of 
authorisation, on the provision that they are able to guarantee a strict biosecurity

regime to prevent escapes. The inclusion of this article led to the weakening of the 
regulation. The authorisations delivered by t he EC to Member States operating 
raccoon dog fur farms include weak measures to prevent the escape of the animals 
and are  sought for an indefinite or extremely long (30 years) period, without the 
establishment of a mechanism to periodically evaluate the  enforcement and the 
effectiveness of the prescribed measures.  
 
• The IAS eradication, control and management obligations included in the Regulation 
impact large numbers of animals for su stained periods of time and thereby raises 
serious concerns for animal welfare. This is inconsistent with the  increasing public 
concern for invasive alien animals as sentient beings, and, more in general, with the 
increasing interest by the civil society on animals being treated humanely.  The 
Regulation specifies that non-lethal methods can be used for the management of the 
species and that “Member States and any operator involved in the eradication, control 
or containment of invasive alien species should take the necessary measures to spare 
avoidable pain, distress and suffering of animals during the process”. However, it is up 
to the Member States to select the measures “appropriate to the local conditions” . 
The terms and conditions on the use of non-lethal or humane methods are not binding 
and therefore rarely used . Arguments used to justify this are that they are  more 
expensive or poorly developed and known in Europe.  Furthermore, s anctuaries, 
rescue centres, shelters, as well as private individuals that take in and care for rescued, 
orphaned or injured animals listed as IAS of Union concern (e.g. raccoons, sliders and 
others) face huge obstacles and challenges to continue with their work as there are 
no specific rules or even references for such facilities in the Regulation. 
 
Eurogroup for Animals recommends the following: 
 
• Prevention should be the first priority under the regulation, with full application of the 
precautionary principle to the listing process. Preventive action, as well as being the 
most cost- effective approach, is also the most animal welfare friendly approach.  
 
• The exotic pet trade has been recognized to be one of the main pathways of 
introduction of IAS by the EC, the Bern Convention and by the Convention on Biological 
Diversity (CBD). Based on ever -changing trends, new species regularly appear in the 
European exotic pet trade; therefore, a negative list, like the Union List, does not fulfill 
the precautionary approach  and entails a considerable time lag due to bureaucratic 
burden. In contrast, a positive list of allowed species is  a concise list based on a 
scientific risk assessment that would provide clarity to owners and enforcement 
agencies and would create less regulatory bureaucracy and costs for the governments. 
An EU Positive List to regulate the exotic pet trade would identify acceptable species 
for trade, breeding and keeping, where the risk of invasiveness is low or non-existent, 
among other criteria like the human and animal health risks, species conservation and 
animal welfare.   
 
• Article 9 authorisations should be delivered only in exceptional cases and for reasons 
of real compelling public interest, not to serve Member States’ requests following 
heavy lobbying by the industry. 
 
• Guidance on humane management of animal IAS must be developed by the European 
Commission, and be largely disseminated to avoid or minimise animals’ pain, suffering 
and distress as explicitly requested by the Regulation. This should include guidance on 
the implementation of the  ecological system restoration a pproach, where 
by improving the natural habitat/ecosystem , the resilience against invasive species 
increases naturally1. The most inhumane management methods should be prohibited 
by specific EU legislation, as they are incompatible with the EU recognition of animals 
as sentient beings . In addition,  the EC and Member States should provide adequate 
financial support to the development and implementation of innovative and long -
term effective non -lethal methods.  Finally, clarification should be prov ided to 
Member States to allow rescue centres and sanctuaries to continue to take in and care 
for rescued, orphaned or injured animals listed as IAS of Union concern.

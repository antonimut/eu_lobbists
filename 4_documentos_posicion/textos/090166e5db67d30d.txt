COPA and COGECA’s additional contribution to the 
Public consultations on the development of legally 
binding EU nature restoration targets 
 
Copa and Cogeca believe that any EU initiative in relation to restoration should ensure the 
continued sustainable use and multifunctionality of ecosystems and resources that equally 
recognises all three pillars of sustainability – environmental, social, and economic. 
 
Approach to restoration 
It is crucial to agree that restoration is a process and not necessarily a final outcome 
and that any improvement or effort that moves an area towards a better condition should be 
regarded as a contribution to the restoration objectives. This is especially valid taking into 
consideration negative impacts of climate change, and as the authors of the article1 ”Prepare 
river ecosystems for an uncertain future” pointed out we “cannot look to tools of old: 
conventional management techniques that aim to restore ecosystems to their original state. 
Ongoing human development and climate change mean that this is no longer possible”. 
Restoration should also be approached as an engaging and inclusive process with highly 
local focus. This stems from the fact that restoration is inherently uncertain at every step, from 
the planning (how to balance multiple objectives and conflicting interests?), to the 
implementation (what is the best way to restore?), to the continued management (when a 
project is truly successful?).  
In addition, according to the review published in Restoration Ecology2: Whereas ecological 
constraints deﬁne what is possible and ﬁnancial constraints determine what is realistic, social 
constraints will determine whether a given habitat restoration project is acceptable. Clearly, 
social and ﬁnancial constraints are inter-related. … Unrealistic expectations of what is possible 
may lead to disenchantment among practitioners or the general public and make further 
restoration actions less likely. 
 
EU Restoration Plan and targets 
The EU Restoration Plan should be based on non-legally binding instrument since 
restoration as an obligation is already present in the legislation such as the Habitats and Water 
Framework Directives. If the new legal approach to restoration is introduced, then the Principle 
3 of the current Commission’s working methods should be respected, according to which every 
legislative proposal creating new burdens should relieve people and businesses of an equivalent 
existing burden at EU level in the same policy area. 
Therefore, the Commission should instead of the legally binding instrument present 
an EU Action Plan for restoration. This Plan should:

• Promote bottom-up approaches to restoration that are incentive based and 
voluntary.  
According to the article “Forest Landscape Restoration—What Generates Failure 
and Success?”: “… restoration ambitions could be impaired by projects that ignore 
key principles such as the engagement of local communities in decision making and 
implementation, equitable benefit sharing, and monitoring for adaptive 
management. This entails the danger of continued degradation, disappointed local 
stakeholders, and ultimately, project failure.” 
• Explore the ways to improve the communication and coordination of the 
public and private restoration efforts in the EU and the ones that are integral 
part of different EU legislation, policies and programmes (e.g. LIFE, Interreg and 
Horizon).  
• Ensure that the future restoration actions in the EU should take full account of 
the cost-effectiveness. The good example why this should be done is presented in 
the   
the article published in the Marine Pollution Bulletin3, where it is stated that: The 
‘‘one-out, all-out’’ principle tends to inflate Type I errors, i.e., concludes that a 
water body is below the ‘‘good’’ status even if the water body actually has a ‘‘good’’ 
status. This may cause additional restoration costs where they are not necessarily 
needed”.  
In addition, future restoration actions should respect land and forest ownership 
rights and the need for proper compensation and incentives.   
• Promote the development of economic incentives, especially from the 
private-sector, and business opportunities related to ecosystem restoration 
and sustainable management. 
In case the restoration target at the EU level is proposed (as part of the Action Plan): 
• The target should be non-legally binding and should be in line with the 
subsidiarity and proportionality principles. 
• There should be only one restoration target set at EU level. Any specific 
ecosystem targets at EU level should be avoided and Member States should 
be allowed to make prioritization based on their actual needs and local opportunities 
(which will allow more ecosystems types to be covered by the restoration actions). 
The “one size fits all” approach to ecosystem prioritization is not the most 
appropriate one. 
• The target should be flexible enough and should be focused on efforts and 
not the final outcomes. It is because restoration outcomes are highly unpredictable 
and take time, which challenges the capacity to reliably meet the goals. For example, 
according to the article published in the journal Ecology and Society: “Emphasis on 
restoration success has encouraged practitioners to have very specific restoration 
targets and outcomes, but these good intentions can lead to homogenization and 
simplification of restoration habitats and a greater cost than required to support 
healthy populations (Hiers et al. 2016).”4 
 
Finally, any follow up actions to the restoration process, such as maintenance through the 
protection, should ensure acceptability by the land and forest owners and sufficient long-
term compensations.

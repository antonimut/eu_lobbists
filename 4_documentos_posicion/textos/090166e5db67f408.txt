E.D.Schulze Saarbrueckerstr 20 07749 Jena Germany e-mail dschulze@bgc-jena.mpg.de   Dear President von der Leyen, dear President Michel, dear President Sassoli  We are a group of forest scientists and practical foresters who are concerned about the future of European Forests. We decided to write to you as Presidents of the EU following two public discourses, the first one about the intention to ban the use of energy wood, and the second one about the goal that 30% of the forest landscape in the EU should be under protection. In face of environmental and climate change, we are aware that additional urgent issues exist in the forest sector, including forest fires, pests and diseases, which are high on the European agenda. However, this letter addresses the two above-mentioned public discourses which have highest impact. Here we would like to make the following comments:  We need wood-based fuels and wood products. The present winter clearly shows that wind power cannot safeguard stable energy supply due to thermal stability of the weather systems in winter and solar panels were covered by snow. Also, in post-fossil fuel times, we need a broad array of energy sources to secure the overall demand. We need wood for both fossil fuel and fossil fuel-intensive product substitution. In this context it has been argued that wood has a lower energy density than fossil fuels, thus releasing more carbon to the atmosphere than fossil fuels. Smoke-stack emissions per produced energy unit are very similar for biomass and coal, with emissions from biomass being part of the contemporary carbon cycle, while those from coal are not. Thus, the arguments about energy density of wood are not applicable. Even if forest products were not to be used, trees undergo their natural life cycle and die. Also under protection, carbon would be released, not by incineration, but by microorganisms decomposing dead tree biomass. Moreover, in case we do not use the wood, fossil fuels would be used to generate the required energy in the next decades, with the carbon from these fossil fuels being released into the atmosphere in addition to the carbon emitted by microbial decomposition. Another unintended consequence of protecting forests from management would be that any wood we use as raw material must come from other places in the world, or we would need to severely reduce our wood and energy consumption.  Biodiversity protection can be combined with wood production if forest owners are engaged. The German weekly magazine “Der Spiegel” summarized that all the protection measures of the past had failed to improve biodiversity and that the failure now has to be corrected by implementing even more protection. We fully agree that the past attempts to protect the environment have failed: There are alarming signals, e.g. the increasing number of Red List species or the decline in the number of insect species. However, we doubt that this can be reversed by increased protection of forests. The political debate is neglecting that the diversity of plants is the fundamental basis for the diversity of all other organisms. In this context it is important to note that not a single forest-specific plant species has been lost in German forests since the start of plant taxonomy mapping some 300 years ago – despite 
3  forest management. In fact, it is management that has enhanced the diversity in forests. The abundance of forest birds has even increased. This is outstanding and it clearly distinguishes forestry from other land uses. In this situation, we would like to suggest that a change in paradigm may be more beneficial for maintaining biodiversity: Not more protection, but more obligations and responsibilities of the land owners are needed. You cannot protect diversity without having the land owners or land managers on board, taking on responsibility for biodiversity protection goals. In addition, to implement such goals, we think that maintaining a Red List species should have a price tag that matches other land uses. That is to say, the land owner should be rewarded for maintaining diversity. With such an inclusive approach, the EU would create a new instrument to maintain diversity in Europe and beyond. In addition, a small fraction of the landscape should be set aside as strict reserves, serving as baseline for assessing the impact of other forest management activities.  To sum up, we are concerned about the future of European forests. We suggest that developing and encouraging sustainable, ecologically driven, climate-smart forest management together with the land owners can make forests important habitats for biodiversity, while still providing wood for a bioeconomy, also under conditions of climate change. We believe that a win-win situation indeed exists and that forestry urgently needs your support to realize all the benefits and to achieve our common goals.

 
 
Attachment to the EU public consultation on EU biodiversity policy initiatives, Part II “Review of 
the application of the EU Regulation on Invasive Alien Species” 
 
 
Question 2: To what extent is the design of the Regulation adequate to address the 
threat posed by invasive alien species to biodiversity in the EU? 
The design of the Regulation is adequate to address the threat posed by invasive alien species (IAS), 
as also indicated by the swift increase up to 66 animal and plant species included in the EU list 
between July 2016 and July 2019.1,2,3  
The Regulation already provides the Commission and member states with a wide array of tools to 
prevent, eradicate or manage IAS in the EU according to the specific circumstances of each case,  for 
instance: EU-wide restrictions on the trade and use of species (article 7); a species-by-species permit 
and authorisation system (articles 8 and 9), applied by Finland with regard to the finnraccoon 
(Nyctereutes procyonoides); enhanced regional cooperation among member states for species of 
regional concern (article 11), currently discussed by a number of member states together with IFF 
with regard to the American mink (Neovison vison); national lists for species of concern for individual 
member states (article 12). 
In light of the above, IFF thinks that the Regulation is fit for purpose. 
 
Question 3: does the current list of invasive alien species of Union concern cover the 
most relevant species to be controlled in the EU? 
IFF selected “Fully” among the possible answers. To clarify this choice, this answer only refers to 
animal species within the scope of IFF members’ area of interest. IFF speaks from the very specific 
perspective of the fur supply chain and its expertise focuses on animals bred for their fur. From this 
perspective, IFF believes that the current list covers the most relevant species, the finnraccoon. 
Although the American mink was also discussed in the Committee on IAS, IFF supports the decision 
of the Committee not to include the American mink in the EU list. Other measures under the 
Regulation are under discussion among member states to avoid the accidental escape of animals 
from the farms. 
Concerning other animal or plant species outside of its area of expertise, IFF refrains from 
commenting.

been implemented? The management of pathways of unintentional introduction and spread.

Question 4.8: to what extent have the following provisions on invasive alien species of Union concern 
been implemented? The management of widely spread invasive alien species. 
IFF is overall satisfied with this aspect, especially regarding the two cases of the finnraccoon and the 
American mink. These two cases show how different tools envisaged in the Regulation can apply to 
different scenarios. As the finnraccoon is widely spread in a number of member states but its 
economic exploitation generates a relatively limited socio-economic value, the species was put on 
the list and article 9 was activated to grant authorisation for commercial activities in the interested 
member states. On the contrary, since the American mink generates a significantly larger socio-
economic value (as of article 4(6) and recital 12 of the Regulation) in Europe,6  the Committee on IAS 
agreed not to include it in the list. As a result, member states started discussing other options 
applicable to this species, such as the enhanced regional cooperation envisaged in article 11, also 
through EU LIFE+ funds. Moreover, IFF is developing containment and other biosecurity measures to 
be applied in all mink farms across Europe. The implementation of these measures will be regularly 
checked in European farms through the global on-farm assessment programme WelFur.7 
 
Question 5: In your experience, are the costs of controlling invasive alien species of Union concern 
(i.e. restrictions, surveillance, eradication and management) proportionate to the benefits for 
biodiversity, human health and the economy? 
Looking at IFF’s experience, the finnraccoon is considered a widely spread species. In the specific 
case of Finland, who obtained an authorisation to continue the farming this species, the 
implementation costs for farmers are quite high and easily go in the tens of thousands of EUR per 
farm, depending on its size. These costs are mostly related to the construction of buildings, fences, 
containment and monitoring measures, and the maintenance of the same.  
The proportionality between the control measures implemented so far and the benefits for 
biodiversity is not entirely clear because its management cost is difficult to assess but is expected to 
be very high.8,9  In general, finnraccoon farmers are satisfied with the authorisation system in place.

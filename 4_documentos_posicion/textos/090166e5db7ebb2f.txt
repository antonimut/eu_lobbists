UECBV’s feedback on “Protecting biodiversity: nature 
restoration targets under EU Biodiversity strategy”  
UECBV support the initiative seeing it as a package of Green Deal and F2F where we already 
gave full supportive messages i. We believe that it is very important that the objectives are 
based on the latest and robust scientific data, which recognize equally all three pillars  of 
sustainability – environmental, social, and economic.  
We would like to remind the Commission  that it is crucial that  the following must be 
considered:  
The positive results that were achieved over time -e.g. over the short term between 2012 and 
2017, the grassland butterfly index has grown by 2.7%; the short-term trend for the common 
birds index has been more positive since 2010 and common farmland birds index remained 
stable during this period; gross nitroge n balance per hectare of utilized agriculture area for 
the EU decreased by 10 % from 2004 -2015; the phosphorus surplus on EU agricultural land 
more than halved from 2004-2015. 
Livestock, especially ruminants, can have a positive impact on biodiversity and soil carbon via 
the maintenance of permanent grassland and hedges and optimized use of manure.  The 
contribution of livestock manure with a high C / N ratio (compost, manure)  has a generally 
favorable impact on soil organic matter content and macrofauna (earthworms). Regular 
supply of effluent appears to improve soil biological functionsii and to influence soil microbial 
biodiversity because they are both a source of many nutr ients for native soil flora and they 
are also complex inoculum iii. These effects are recognized at European scale. Permanent 
grassland area is protected by EU and national legislations and livestock seems to be 
concomitant with most of the High Natural Value agricultural areas, notably in grassland based 
ruminant systems even if certain pig farms, horse and buffalo farms may have local 
importance.iv

administrative burden for the public authorities and stakeholders. There are some potential

which make  restoration situ ational and there is no single restoration formula. Restoration 
plan, which worked in one area, cannot always be applied to another one with the same 
results expected. This might entail new administrative burden in preparation and approval of 
different restoration projects, their implementation and monitoring. Physical modifications to 
the sites might entail burden related to permits.   
Restoration efforts that are integral part of different EU legislation and polices – Birds and 
Habitats Directive, Water Framework Directive, Common Fisheries Policy, Common 
Agricultural Policy, Nitrates Directive, Flood protection and programmes, Industry Emission 
Directive, Zero pollution action plan etc. It is important to have a harmonize approach and not 
to duplicate the regulations.   
Depending on the goal of the restoration, the related costs can be very significant. It is 
therefore important to address who will be bearing the costs and how this migh t impact 
specific stakeholders (including the right to compensation and the potential loss of production 
capacity). It would be beneficial to have few concrete examples (case studies) addressing 
restoration of different ecosystems. In addition, there shoul d be a careful approach on how 
biodiversity benefits are attributed to the restoration objectives chosen not because of their 
biodiversity value.

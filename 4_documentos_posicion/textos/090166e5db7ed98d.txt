US Industrial Pellet Association Response to European Commission consultation on the EU 
Biodiversity Strategy 
USIPA supports the ambitious goals of the European Green Deal and believes that sustainable woody 
biomass can play a crucial role in delivering the EU’s ambitious goals to 2030, 2050 and beyond, 
while protecting the environment and promoting healthy and growing forests. The sustainability of 
the biomass we produce and use is paramount and we fully support the robust and binding 
sustainability criteria set out in the EU legislation.  
Sustainably sourced biomass is helping the EU deliver its climate ambitions 
Increased EU climate ambitions require immediate solutions. Bioenergy already delivers over 50% of 
EU’s renewable energy and woody biomass is the main source. Sustainable biomass has proven to 
be a fast and reliable way to decarbonize the economy thanks to its ability to displace coal on the 
grid, provide stable, on-demand back-up power to support deployment of other renewable 
technologies and provide a renewable alternative to fossil fuels in Combined Heat and Power. 
Biomass is also increasingly sought after to displace fossil-fuels in the industrial sector.  Furthermore, 
bioenergy with CCS (BECCS) is now a proven technology in the power sector and is in the process of 
being scaled up to help deliver negative emissions. 
Biomass is part of a wider forest economy that maintains forest growth and promotes 
sustainability 
The US Southeast has a forested area larger than the total land area of France, Germany and the UK 
combined. According to the USDA Forest Inventory and Analysis program, private forest owners in 
this region are growing 40% more wood than they remove every year. Because demand for a range 
of wood products provides financial incentive for private landowners to keep  their forests as 
forests, the US Southeast has seen continuous growth in forested area, forest inventory, habitats 
and forest carbon stocks since the 1950s despite record population growth and urban development 
during this same period. 
Working forested land protects and expands habitats for wildlife 
Conservation groups agree that the greatest threat to most wildlife species is loss of forested 
habitats. Demand for forest products across the US Southeast prevents this loss and encourages 
landowners to keep their lands forested, maintaining and enhancing wildlife habitats and 
biodiversity in the region. Keeping these lands forested supports habitat connectivity across the 
region, which is considered one of the most important factors in maintaining biological diversity by 
allowing species to interact with a larger and more diverse gene pool. 
Working, managed forests have many benefits including creating a variety of landscapes and age 
classes, which each provide habitats and benefits for a variety of wildlife. Contrary to activist claims, 
there is no mass conversion of forests into pine plantations in the US Southeast. In fact, the 
cumulative conversion rate of other forests into planted pine over the last decade has been just 
0.2% of the total forest landscape. 
Thinning, an important part of forest management, improves growth of remaining trees, reduces risk 
of wildfire and disease, and creates open canopies that help support wildlife habitat. These open 
canopies allow for sunlight to reach the forest floor, allowing shrubs and brush to grow, which is 
necessary for wildlife species for nesting, protective cover, and food. Some species in the US

Biomass creates a market for unwanted low value wood 
Using biomass creates a vital additional market for private landowners to sell their low-value wood 
ensuring an efficient use of forest resources. The main biomass feedstocks are thinnings (i.e. the 
managed removal of low-value ‘whole trees’ before a sawtimber harvest that are not productively 
used elsewhere), limbs, tops, crooked and knotted trees and saw-mill residues that would otherwise 
go unused or are undersold in the local market. The use of the expression ‘whole trees’ by activists 
to send the wrong message about the wood energy industry is both unhelpful and misleading. Any 
forestry expert knows that thinning a forest is at the core of sustainable forest management. 
Nevertheless, wood energy is just a small part of the overall US forest products industry sector - the 
biomass industry uses less than 0.1% of total forest inventory in the US Southeast each year to 
produce wood energy and less than 3% of the total wood harvested each year is converted into 
pellets. 
Research from the USDA Forest Service and the University of Georgia Warnell School of Forestry 
projects that the absence of this market for wood bioenergy would result in the loss of up to 
15,000sq km of US forests — an area roughly the size of the Netherlands — over a 10-year period. 
Ensured and independently verified sustainability 
In the use and export of biomass to end users in Europe, the US biomass industry is also highly 
regulated by both US environmental laws and sustainability regulations at the EU and Member State 
levels. The US has a variety of stringent environmental laws, including the Clean Water Act, the 
Endangered Species Act, and the Clean Air Act. These laws are strictly enforced and apply to both 
private and public lands. 
In addition, biomass placed on the EU market must meet the strict sustainability criteria in the recast 
Renewable Energy Directive (REDII), which include requirements that harvesting is carried out 
considering maintenance of soil quality and biodiversity, with the aim of minimising negative 
impacts. 
To independently demonstrate sustainability, US woody biomass exporters to the EU and the UK 
hold sustainable fibre sourcing and chain of custody certifications from internationally recognized 
forest certification schemes such as PEFC, FSC, SFI, SBP and others. These regional risk-based 
certifications require third-party, independent audits and mitigation of any risk of unsustainable 
sourcing identified throughout the supply chain. These audit reports are provided to regulators to 
ensure compliance with EU and Member State climate laws.

Therefore, legislation and forestry certifications effectively address and prevent scenarios where 
forest conversion will have a clear negative impact on forests, biodiversity, or carbon storage.  This is 
addressed through limits on sourcing from areas where conversion has resulted in loss of high-
carbon stock, areas where forest harvesting would impact the carbon-storing ability or productivity 
of the forest over the long-term, and areas where forests have been converted to high-intensity 
short rotation production plantations that lack ecological integrity. Therefore, we do not support 
further blanket restrictions such as the recommendation from JRC (JRC biomass study) to restrict 
biomass sourcing from lands that were ‘recently’ converted away from naturally regenerating 
forests.  These high-risk issues are well-covered in current practice and governance and thus further 
regulation is not required. 
For instance,  SBP has multiple requirements related to biodiversity and land use, including but not 
limited to: 
 SBP Criterion 2.1 Management of the forest ensures that features and species of outstanding or 
exceptional value are identified and protected 
o Indicator 2.1.2 The biomass producer has implemented appropriate control systems and 
procedures to identify and address potential threats to forests and other areas with high 
conservation values from forest management activities. 
o Indicator 2.1.3 The biomass producer has implemented appropriate control systems and 
procedures for verifying that feedstock is not sourced from forests converted to 
production plantation forests or non-forest lands after January 2008 
 
 SBP Criterion 2.2 Management of the forest ensures that ecosystem function is assessed and 
maintained, through both the conservation/set-aside of key ecosystems or habitats in their 
natural state, and the maintenance of existing ecosystem functions throughout the forest 
o Indicator 2.2.2 The biomass producer has implemented appropriate control systems and 
procedures for verifying that feedstock is sourced from forests where management 
maintains or improves soil quality 
o Indicator 2.2.3 The biomass producer has implemented appropriate control systems and 
procedures to ensure that key ecosystems and habitats are conserved or set aside in 
their natural state 
o Indicator 2.2.4 The biomass producer has implemented appropriate control systems and 
procedures to ensure that biodiversity is protected 
 
 SBP Criterion 2.3 Management of the forest ensures that productivity is maintained 
o Indicator 2.3.1 Analysis shows that feedstock harvesting does not exceed the long-term 
production capacity of the forest, avoids significant negative impacts on forest 
productivity and ensures long-term economic viability. Harvest levels are justified by 
inventory and growth data. 
 
 SBP Criterion 2.9 Regional carbon stocks are maintained or increased over the medium to long 
term

About USIPA 
The US Industrial Pellet Association (USIPA) is a trade association bringing together over 30 members 
based in the southeastern US, representing all aspects of the wood energy export industry and 
supply chain, including wood pellet producers, large landowners, traders and brokers, equipment 
manufacturers, bulk shippers and service providers.  
USIPA promotes sustainability within the wood energy industry and advocates for the wood energy 
sector as an effective solution to climate change. USIPA members have been supplying European 
energy producers with sustainable woody biomass from managed forests in the southeastern US for 
over a decade, enabling them to reduce their emissions and deliver on the EU’s climate and energy 
goals.

Review of the bank crisis management & deposit insurance framework 
Intesa Sanpaolo’s position paper 
Intesa Sanpaolo welcomes the opportunity to comment on the European Commission 
consultation on the review of the bank crisis management and deposit insurance framework 
(CMDI).  
We have a positive judgment on the track record of the resolution regime during its first 6 years 
of existence. This fact should be the starting point of any discussion together with the recognition 
that very extensive progress has been made in enhancing the resolvability of large systemic 
institutions. Banks have made significant progress in recovery and resolution planning, raising 
MREL and enhancing resolvability, and more efforts are ongoing. Now, it should be the time to 
further reinforce this progress by bringing to completion the still unfinished Banking Union project.  
We would like in particular to focus on three main elements which , from our perspective, are 
needed to complete the current framework: i) the harmonization of the national bank 
insolvency procedures in the Banking Union, to address  the potential crisis of Less -signification 
Institutions (LSIs) outside the scope of resolution; ii) establishing a flexible framework for national 
DGSs interventions and iii) starting a step-by-step process towards the establishment of the third 
pillar of the Banking Union.  
This long-term vision of the Banking Union would encompass two clear tracks for banks’ crisis 
management: the EU resolution framework for systemic European banks, and a harmonized 
liquidation regime to be managed at the national level for the remaining banks.  
1. Complementing the resolution framework with a harmonized insolvency regime across 
the Banking Union 
As often reminded by the Single Resolution Board itself, resolution is for the few and not for the 
many. We do not believe that the resolution framework is flexible nor proportionate enough to 
address the potential crisis of all banks, especially small and medium-sized ones (LSIs). Systemic 
banks and LSIs differ in terms of liability structure, capacity of accessing capital markets  for 
issuing bail -inable liabilities, and in their ability to generate internal resources to finance a 
resolution strategy. 
One of the  problems of the current framework derives from a situation where a disorderly 
liquidation is the only possible outcome for the crises of small and medium-sized intermediaries 
which are not suited for resolution. In this sense, we believe that the resolution framework could 
be accompanied by the harmonization of national insolvency procedures set ting common 
principles and conditions across the EU.  
The objective of this harmonization of national insolvency procedures should not be to substitute 
national laws with a European regime, but to ensure a level playing field and common principles 
around at least some fundamental elements: i) homogenous insolvency triggers (addressing the 
so called “limbo situations”), ii) coherent treatment of deposits across the EU on the basis of the 
depositor preferences already existing in some Member States  and iii) adequate source of 
funding. In this context, the proposal made by the German Presidency of the Council for an EU 
Orderly Liquidation Tool is worth exploring in more detail, having in scope all those banks which 
do not qualify for the so-called Public Interest Assessment for resolution. 
When it comes to funding issues, realistically medium and small sized banks would see precluded 
their access to the SRF given their inability (due to a peculiar liability structure) to ensure an 8%

2. The importance of a flexible framework for DGS alternative measures 
We believe that access conditions for funding in resolution and funding in insolvency should be 
adapted to the realities of banks’ business models. While access conditions to the Single 
Resolution Fund currently in place are broadly adequate  and consistent with the resolution 
framework, the access to funding of the DGS in insolvency should be based on a clear and 
harmonized “least cost principle”.  Avoiding atomistic liquidation, whenever possible, should be 
the main goal in order to minimise overall costs on the economy, including , ultimately, the 
recourse to taxpayers’ money. 
According to this principle, interventions of the DGS other than payouts should be based on a 
test comparing the direct costs of any payout of deposits with the cost of alternative actions 
(such as a transfer of assets of the failing institution). The methodology, to be defined in EU 
legislation, should take into account also indirect costs associated with financial stability or risks 
of contagion. If this least cost principle is applied correctly and in a consistent way across the 
Union, moral hazard by certain market participants would be avoided and going concern 
values of failing banks would be preserved . Financial stability would be ensured without the 
need to extend burden sharing to depositors and senior creditors of the bank.  
In this context, DGS preventive measures have an important function in the current regime, 
allowing authorities to intervene before the point of non -viability of the institution is reached, 
with the possibility to reduce the costs of a disorderly bank fail ure. From an Italian perspective, 
DGSs preventive measures are not to be assimilated to public interventions as clearly 
demonstrated also by the ruling of the European Court of Justice C -425/19 P (the so -called 
Tercas case). Of course, we understand that i n the EU many DGSs are a more or less a direct 
emanation of public authorities, and therefore it is important to maintain a certain degree of 
flexibility in the overall crisis management framework. A review of the State aid regime could  
then be envisaged in order to take into account the outcome of the Tercas ruling. 
Finally, it should be kept in mind that large institutions continue to be the predominant source of 
financing for national DGS. Therefore, it remains crucial that the overall CMDI framework 
achieves a cost-effective design without increasing existing contributions and duplicating 
efforts. For this reason, we believe that in the long term a fully -fledged EDIS remains the only 
possible evolution for the Banking Union. 
3. Completing Banking Union with a fully-fledged EDIS 
The major missing block of the CMDI framework remains the persistent lack of important safety 
nets at the European level, most importantly a fully -fledged EDIS and the provision, by an EU 
authority, of liquidity in resolution. It remains essential to establish a common European scheme 
in addition to the national DGS in order to increase the firepower in case of systemic crisis and 
achieve a more cost-effective design.  
We are aware of the fact that it will be a long-term project requiring a step-by-step approach. 
In this sense, in a first phase, we would support the creation of a liquidity support facility as a 
preliminary step. However, from our perspective, it would still be important to start to 
progressively build a central fund  during this hybrid model. In the build -up phase to a fully -
fledged EDIS we understand that certain limits are needed to ensure an adequate transition to 
a common scheme. For example, we would accept the need to put in place initial limitations

Intesa Sanpaolo is keen to continue the dialogue with the European Commission in the coming 
weeks leading to the legislative proposals on the CMDI review and will continue to support the 
efforts in the direction of a complete and strong Banking Union.

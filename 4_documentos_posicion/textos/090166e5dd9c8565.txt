Contribution of Ornikar/Onroad to the roadmap for the revision of the Directive on Driving Licences 	Onroad (ONROAD SPAIN S.L.) is the Spanish subsidiary of Ornikar (MARIANNE FORMATION SAS)  Ornikar/Onroad is pleased to contribute to the revision of Directive 2006/126/EC -commonly known as the “Driving License Directive” (DLD)- by sharing its perspective as an increasingly important stakeholder in driving education, the digital driving schools. Ornikar/Onroad has operated in France since 2015 and in Spain since 2019 with the aim of providing a more affordable, enjoyable and impactful alternative to traditional teaching methods to all Europeans willing to obtain a driving license.  We celebrate that “the primary objective of the new initiative will be to improve road safety” and the fact that the Inception Impact Assessment acknowledges the societal and technological changes that need to be addressed by the initiative. Given the nature of our activities, our contribution will be focused on the second key objective of the revision: “Increase safety on the Union’s roads by improving driving skills as well as by increasing risk awareness and knowledge on road safety”. In the Assessment, the European Commission has identified different solutions to achieve the objective, some of which we would like to share our views on. However, as it will become evident, these solutions depend largely on the capacity of the regulatory framework to adapt to new technologies that can improve road safety. This should be done also at the training level, by formally recognising the validity of e-learning and eliminating obstacles to it, such as mandatory in-class lessons for theoretical training. For the new generations of students, Internet has become an essential part of their academic education. This trend has been particularly significant during the Covid-19 pandemic, in which online classes allowed the upcoming lawyers, doctors, psychologists, engineers, etc. to continue learning while being safe. It does not make sense that the upcoming drivers would not be allowed also to learn through online lessons, combined with on-road practice.   Knowledge sharing Given that the Commission is considering the adoption of measures to increase knowledge sharing between examiners and drivers, we argue that the exchange of information should be more ambitious. As portrayed in other initiatives of the Commission, such as the upcoming Data Act, the use of data is an essential pillar to the digital transformation of any sector. In the case of digital driving education, there are several ways in which knowledge and data sharing can improve road safety and the learning of future drivers.

Digital education can allow for greater feedback on the reception and assimilation of content by students, enabling educators to continuously improve it. This information flow is not unidirectional, as online learning, through personalised tutoring, also allows for a more tailored interaction between teachers and candidates than traditional in-class lessons. Furthermore, there is potential for a stronger cooperation between digital driving schools and the national competent authorities for Traffic. While respecting all the rules on privacy and data protection, digital driving schools could provide data on their students’ training trajectories, allowing for a data analysis that could improve policymaking and public campaigns related to road safety and risk-awareness.  Lifelong training  Lifelong training needs to be part of the future of road safety education. For most drivers, obtaining their driving license is the first and last formal training in driving education that they receive.  This approach needs to be changed, as it fails to create long-lasting behaviours. Trainings after acquiring their licenses should help drivers reinforce their risk-awareness and keep updated on the constant development of new traffic regulations and mobility technologies.  Lifelong training can only work efficiently if it is based on e-learning, as traditional in-class lessons cannot adjust well to the needs of most drivers due to their time restraints. The possibility of accessing digital classes at any time rather than having to be present at a certain time in a physical classroom is a key factor in order to retain the attention and the interest of drivers in lifelong training.  Harnessing digital education to improve risk-awareness The Digital Education Action Plan (2021-2027) of the Commission sets an ambitious plan to promote the integration of digital tools and skills in the learning process. Regarding driving education, there are no reasons for Member States to require the attendance to physical classes to obtain the driving license. Online classes for theoretical training, combined with on-road training, are equally effective as in-class lessons in conveying risk-awareness and risk-assessment behaviours in candidates. In a context in which competent authorities have carried successfully hundreds of road safety awareness campaigns using social media and other telematic tools, it cannot be argued that e-learning would be less effective than in-person classes. Focusing on the EU countries, the Road Safety Annual Report 20203 of the International Transport Forum (ITF) and the OECD shows that the two Member States with lower road fatalities per 100 000 inhabitants have systems in which there are no requirements for the theoretical training part for the driving license, in contrast to systems in which attending a physical driving school is mandatory. Additionally, the six countries with the five lowest rates are equally divided between those requiring attendance to a course at a driving school, and those without this requirement. Furthermore, making in-class lessons mandatory would eliminate consumers’ choices, limiting fair competition in the driving schools’ sector. Recent developments in the regulatory level support this approach. For instance, following an attempt in 2018 by the traffic authority in Spain (Dirección General de Tráfico, DGT) to make in-class lessons mandatory, the Spanish competition authority (Comisión Nacional de los Mercados y la Competencia, CNMC) issue a report4 against it. In the report, the CNMC argued that implementing such a requirement would have a negative effective effect on competition “without any justification for it”. This report caused the DGT to eliminate any references to mandatory in-class lessons. At the EU level, Directive (EU) 2018/645 and the Annex to Directive 2003/59/EC recognise the validity of digital and online training for drivers of certain vehicles for the transportation of goods and passengers by road. Hence, it is logical that with its revision, Directive 2006/126/EC follows these developments and is updated to the current technological context: e-learning must be recognised as a valid alternative for theoretical training in driving education. It is worth mentioning that requesting in-class lessons is not the only obstacle that digital driving schools are facing. Two additional ways in which the regulatory framework might pose risks to the development of this growing sector are the requirement to have a physical “school” or place in which students can carry out administrative procedures, and the requirement to own the learning vehicle. Both of these requirements would seem outdated in the digital economy, as they are meant for traditional business models and do not allow for the flexibility needed to accommodate modern driving schools.  Conclusions Like many other sectors, driving schools are currently experiencing a digital transformation. Evidence shows that e-learning is as effective as in-class lessons in conveying students risk-awareness of road safety. Furthermore, it provides an efficient and affordable alternative and can contribute significantly to other necessary tools to increase road safety, such as knowledge sharing and lifelong learning. Therefore, the revision of Directive 2006/126/EC should validate e-learning and provide a safe and stable regulatory framework for its development.

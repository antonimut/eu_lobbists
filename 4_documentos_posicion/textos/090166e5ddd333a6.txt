IFRA response on the EU Commission Inception Impact Assessment on the revision 
of the CLP regulation. 
The fragrance industry, represented by The International Fragrance Association (IFRA) supports the 
general objectives of the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability (CSS)  aiming at better protecting 
citizens and the environment against hazardous chemicals and encouraging innovation for the 
development of safe and sustainable alternatives. 
IFRA considers that the introduction of ne w hazard classes in the CLP regulation should take into 
account consistency with international regulatory instruments and definitions. The inclusion of new 
hazard classes should first be done under the UN Globally Harmonized System of Classification and 
Labelling of Chemicals (GHS) framework. It is worth noting that the CLP regulation is designed to 
implement the GHS in the EU. This approach would ensure a level playing field for the European 
fragrance industry at global level. As highlighted in the Commiss ion roadmap, ‘divergence from the 
UN GHS global standards may affect hazard communication for exported EU -manufactured 
chemicals.’ Therefore, the fact that EU companies would benefit from an early adoption at European 
level before full acceptance at a global level would require further substantiation. IFRA considers that 
this would be highly unlikely and is very concerned by the negative impacts on the European industry 
competitiveness. Potential divergent criteria in UN -GHS framework would need follow -up 
implementation in the CLP regulation, which leads to further inconsistencies and re -labelling, 
increasing cost and regulatory burden. Moreover, the current intention to adopt the EU decisions 
before discussion at UN level is in contradiction with the Europe an approach to favour global 
harmonization and may infringe the World Trade Organization’s rules . Proposing and implementing 
new hazard classes first in CLP and proposing their inclusion into GHS afterwards, follows the wrong 
path and minimizes the chances to reach a UN consensus. Even if such a consensus is eventually 
achieved, it will most likely differ from the initial proposal meaning modifying CLP to bring it in line 
with the UN GHS. Such changes would cause unnecessary cost and administrative burden along the 
supply chains (training, reclassification, la belling…) and possible misunderstandings for chemicals 
users. 
IFRA supports the Commission’s objective to establish horizontal hazard identification across EU 
legislation fully aligned with the existing criteria ( e.g., PPPR and BPR) and international recog nized 
definitions (WHO). 
IFRA does not support the introduction of a new hazard class on endocrine disruptors (ED) under 
the CLP. Adverse effects of substances which can act via an endocrine mode of action are already 
captured by existing GHS/CLP hazard classes via CMR classification. CLP and GHS are designed for the 
classification and communication of hazards and not modes of action. Confusing communication of 
such information could lead to reduced attention to labels by people handling chemicals and with 
that, ultimately may lead to a loss of overall protection. For these reasons, a separate hazard class is 
not justified and does not serve the purpose. 
REACH is intended to identify and manage potential risks to health and the environment, including 
endocrine disrupting modes of action.  ED assessment should be conducted in a similar manner to PBT

The same would apply for PMT/vPvM and PBT/vPvB substances as it may result in double classification 
of some substances as many of the elements are already captured in CLP Regulation.  This would result 
in unclear communication to the users of chemicals including the public. 
Regarding the clarification of obligations to classify mixtures and some complex substances, IFRA 
would like to stress that the concept of MOCS (More than One Constituent Substances) is not 
substantiated by any legal basis under REACH, CLP, or other EU legislation. It encompasses and would 
affect virtually all chemical substances produced, imported, and placed on the market in the EU. The 
only exception would effectively be substances with a purity of greater than 99.9 %. 
The data and subsequent classification of a MOCS in the REACH dossier of the substance should be 
given preference over theoretical  classification based on constituents. The classification based on 
constituents should only happen when there is no data on the MOCS itself. 
It is not viable to isolate and test individual MOCS components down to 0.1%, especially for substances 
of unknown or variable composition (UVCB). If that would be required, it would hamper the whole 
hazard and risk information at a substance level concept in REACH and substantially increase animal 
testing, time and costs for data generation and evaluation, complicating it and making it unworkable. 
This is especially relevant for essentials (natural complex substances - UVCBs). IFRA strongly supports 
the fact to consider essential oils as substances regarding the hazard classification according to CLP 
regulation. In no way, an essential oil could be considered as a mixture of components. Indeed, by 
nature, essential oils are defined by 3 key parameters: the botanical source, the manufacturing 
process, the composition. The constituents are intrinsically parts of the substance and cannot be 
considered in isolation. Essential oils are defined in ISO standards and identification of essential oils 
are also described in sector guidance’s available on ECHA website. 
Assimilating an essential oil as a mixture of components would lead to an unfair situation, where NCSs 
are knowingly and significantly disadvantaged compared to synthetic mixtures, where substitution of 
components is possible. 
IFRA questions whether allowing ECHA to develop new harmonised classification and labelling 
(‘CLH’) dossiers would bring added value and how downstream consequences are being considered. 
Disproportionate action should be avoided for substances that are merely “suspected” of hazard 
properties. Regulatory links should be legally clear, also to avoid negative impact on SME’s being not 
fully aware of all regulatory changes . Industry should have sufficient time to demonstrate safe uses 
and authorities to evaluate them (e.g., automated ban for CMR 2 unless positive SCCS opinion).  
IFRA would welcome simpler procedures and ability for industry to directly submit CLH dossiers to 
existing CLH dossiers to revise them when new data becomes available that could change the existing 
classification.

Extending the scope of the CLP regulation requiring hazard labelling of additional products (cosmetic 
products) will not add any value to the current requirements which we consider sufficient to protect 
consumer health. Adding CLP hazard symbols would generate a large wave of reformulations to avoid 
certain symbols appearing on pack. 
IFRA would like to highlight the value of the impact assessment and the need for stakeholders’ 
dialogue from the start considering the magnitude of the proposed changes and the level of ambition 
set in the Strategy. The European fragrance industry is characterized by a wide range of operators, 
from multinational companies to micro and small companies ( SMEs). Assessing the impacts of these 
changes for the hundreds of SMEs operating in t he European fragrance industry is of significant 
importance to ensure an appropriate screening of economic, social, and environmental impacts, in 
light with Better Regulation principles. 
IFRA is committed to contribute fully and effectively in the upcoming European discussions.

About The International Fragrance Association (IFRA)  
The International Fragrance Association, founded in 1973, represents the interests of the fragrance industry 
worldwide. IFRA comprises seven multinational companies, 21 national associations in four global regio ns 
representing hundreds of small and medium -sized fragrance ingredient manufacturers, and seven supporting 
members. Its mission is to promote the safe use of fragrances. 
Fragrances are a key platform technology used by consumer goods companies – for fine fragrances, personal 
care products, household care and more. 
IFRA’s flagship safe use program, the IFRA Standards, applies safety management measures based on scientific 
assessment and the evaluations of an independent Expert Panel. The program is at the h eart of the IFRA Code 
of Practice, which applies to all IFRA members globally, including members of IFRA’s 21 national associations.

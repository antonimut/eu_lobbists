 SUBMISSION PAPER
MAY 2021
 
Cefic submission on Inception Impact assessment on CLP changes  
 
Effective and coherent implementation of the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability will lay important foundations for achieving the goals of the European Green Deal, which Cefic fully supports.  The chemical industry is ready to play its part in building on the most advanced chemical safety legislation in the world, and continue our efforts to further develop and invest in safe and sustainable substances. We all share the same goals as the Commission for a system that ensures there is no harm to people or the environment and supports the industry’s investments into more innovative solutions manufactured on European soil. Both the UN GHS and the CLP Regulation have proven to enhance the safe handling, storage, trade and disposal of chemicals. GHS creates an international level playing field for a growing number of countries, hereby contributing to the implementation of SAICM (Strategic Approach to International Chemicals Management), which is a key objective of the Commission’s global agenda reiterated in the CSS.  Until now, the EU has thoroughly implemented the UN GHS building blocks and by doing so, established exemplary standards for implementing GHS.   Recent reviews of chemical regulations in the EU confirmed that CLP is an effective and powerful tool to enhance the safe use of chemicals. Despite these findings, the Commission sees a need for a CLP revision with the introduction of new hazard classes. The revision is expected to take place before UN GHS discussions, thus leading to divergence between the EU and global framework for at least several years. Cefic and its members believe that a harmonised way of identifying and communicating hazards at national, regional and global level is essential to ensure the safe use of chemicals and to facilitate commercial exchange of goods. 
Cefic welcomes the opportunity to comment on European Commission inception impact assessment on 
CLP. Please find below our comments to EU Commission proposals as presented in CLP IIA document (in 
bold) as follows:  
 
 introduce new hazard classes (such as endocrine disruptors) and corresponding criteria.  
 REACH is intended to identify and manage potential risks to health and the environment, 
including endocrine disrupting modes of action, PBT/vPvB and newly proposed PMT/vPvM 
substances. ED and PMT/vPvM assessment should be conducted in a similar manner to PBT 
assessment under REACH and could be achieved through a dedicated Annex (like Annex XIII for 
PBT’s) to allow for additional data generation where warranted. In addition, communication 
on safe use of substances in articles is foreseen under REACH.  As a result, we would like EU 
Commission to consider also a REACH route as a policy option.

 The UN GHS (Globally Harmonised System of Classification and Labelling of Chemicals) is a key 
contributor to global harmonisation of chemicals legislations by an ever-growing number of 
jurisdictions throughout the world. Europe, as well as other jurisdictions, are committed to 
promoting the UN GHS process and outcome as a world standard for successful chemicals 
management. Hence, in order to achieve global harmonisation, jurisdictions should avoid 
introducing regional specificities and implementing classifications above and beyond those 
covered by the UN GHS.  
 
 The European Commission’s decision to implement changes to CLP before a discussions at the 
GHS level may send a wrong signal to other countries participating in GHS and may lead to 
those countries losing faith in the GHS approach and disregarding it. 
 Any proposal to add new hazard classes and/or to amend existing ones should first be made 
under UN GHS before modifying the CLP Regulation to minimise divergence. Divergence from 
the UN GHS global standards will strongly affect hazard  communication for exported EU- 
manufactured chemicals as the classification of substances may differ from local classification 
and due to the intro of new hazard classes be stricter and misaligned with what the local rules 
would require. Cefic would like to emphasize that before the introduction of additional hazard 
classes, these need to be clearly defined and scientifically assessed by the appropriate experts. 
 Proposing and implementing new hazard classes initially in CLP followed by the proposal to 
include them into GHS, minimises the chances to reach a consensus at UN level. Even if such a 
consensus is eventually achieved, it will most likely differ from the initial proposal meaning 
modifying CLP to bring it in line again with the UN GHS. Such changes would cause unnecessary 
cost and administrative burden along the supply chains (training, reclassification, labelling...) 
and possible misunderstandings by the users of the chemicals. Furthermore, intensification of 
efforts at international level is required to remove the already significant differences between 
local implementations of UN GHS. 
 The European Commission’s proposal to include a separate CLP classification for endocrine 
disruptors, would likely result in double classification of some substances regarding their ED 
mode of action/hazard.  This would result in unclear communication to the users of chemicals 
including the public. Horizontal approach to identify Endocrine Disruptors under REACH, in line 
with the WHO definition and consistent with existing EU legislation (e.g. Plant Protection 
Products Regulation and Biocides Products Regulation) should be followed.  
 Rather than introducing new hazard classes, consideration should be given, in the impact 
assessment (and in any GHS discussions), to “flag” options whereby modes of action such as 
ED can indicated. 
 
 In case the CLP option is pursued further, for ED properties we believe that classification based 
on in vitro alerts should be avoided due to the high number of false positives and also because 
it is not in line with the agreed WHO definition for EDs which requires adverse effects in intact 
organism; and for PBT/vPvB properties the criteria should be used as per Annex XIII to REACH 
3 
 
Regulation. Similar to PBT/vPvB approach, for PMT/vPvM the classification should not be based 
on screening criteria (i.e. log Kow/log Koc, respectively).  
 
 We note the importance to identify and communicate intrinsic hazards of chemicals as CLP 
requires. However, the real impact from CLP hazard classifications is the so-called ‘automatic 
bans’ that are triggered by some classifications in REACH (e.g. Art 57, 59) or other sector 
legislation (e.g. use of CMR’s in consumer products). As the Commission intends to introduce 
more ‘generic restrictions’ (following the goals from CSS) driven simply by the hazard 
classification of substances or mixtures, the classifications will lead to more bans and 
restrictions. We regret that the Commission does not intend to consider these consequences 
in its impact assessment.  Furthermore, mechanisms and enforcement must be put in place to 
ensure level playing field for both EU manufactures and EU importers; in particular in case of 
articles imported to the EU. 
 
 
 introduce an obligation to provide information of some hazards on the label for products currently 
outside the scope of CLP.  
 
 If products have already specific labelling requirements according to EU regulations that are 
complementary to CLP, then they should remain subject to that regulatory regime to avoid 
confusion with labelling obligations from CLP. 
 
 clarify the obligations to classify mixtures and some complex substances.  
 
 Cefic supports this clarification. Any change to the current approach should be assessed in the 
Impact Assessment.  
 
 Such clarification needs to fully consider and build on Article 12 of CLP which addresses specific 
cases. 
 
 As no single substance is 100% pure, this implies that, in practice, the majority of the 
substances may qualify as MOCS (More than One Constituent Substances). It is not viable to 
isolate and test individual MOCS components down to 0.1%, especially for UVCB substances. If 
that would be required, it would hamper the whole hazard and risk information at a substance 
level concept in REACH and substantially increase animal testing, time and costs for data 
generation and evaluation, complicating it to the point of making it unworkable.  
 
 The test results on the MOCS itself allow to take into account the interactions between all its 
constituents that mimic the complex exposure. This approach is more efficient and realistic 
than slicing the MOCS into single constituents. 
 
 For regulatory consistency, the classification of the MOCS should be entirely based on the data 
in the REACH dossier; tests should be given preference over theoretical classification based on 
constituents.  Therefore, when there is existing information on the MOCS itself, there should 
not be a requirement to generate further data on a constituent level.

 When there is no data available on the MOCS itself and there is information available 
demonstrating that a constituent of the MOCS would have CMR classification, it should not 
trigger CMR classification of the MOCS itself when its concentration is below GCL/SCL.  
 
 introduce specific rules for online sales.  
 
 Cefic welcomes measures aimed at improving enforcement of existing regulation and 
monitorability of new provisions. We believe the rules on online sales should be improved and 
accompanied with the right enforcement measures. Enforcement needs better resourcing, 
more sophisticated digital tools, and better coordination between Member States’ competent 
authorities (customs, enforcement authorities) and between Member States and market 
actors.  
 
 introduce the possibility to submit proposals for and set harmonised environmental and safety values 
for some substances. 
 
 DNEL and PNEC derivation belongs to hazard assessment under REACH and therefore those 
elements should stay in the REACH.  CLP is about classification & labelling and is framed under 
the UN GHS. Classification is the starting point for hazard communication. Any such 
consideration on DNEL/PNEC harmonisations should not take place in the context of CLP, as it 
would introduce unnecessary complexity.  
 
 The benefit of deriving harmonised DNELs and PNECs is unclear. We consider that submitting 
proposals and setting harmonized environmental and human health safety values will 
considerably increase the burden on RAC and this comes in addition to the RAC having to assess 
more harmonised classifications in the future as a result of the new hazard categories intended 
to be introduced in CLP. The Impact Assessment needs to consider quantitatively the increase 
workload on the RAC and quantify the additional staff and budget this entails for ECHA and MS 
authorities.   
 
 
 require importers and downstream users to submit information on substances classified for physical 
effects or health hazards to poison centres and clarify obligations for distributors to submit such 
information, through an only representative or other means.  
 
 Expanding the PCN notification obligation from mixtures to substances would not bring any 
added value on safety for human health and environment perspective as the information on 
the substance(s) is already available in the ECHA substance information tool. This would lead 
to duplicating information in C&L inventory, PCN tool and REACH databases. 
 
 We support clarifying obligations for distributors for the notification of mixtures. However, we 
believe a survey on the benefits/added value on poison centre database from the point of view 
of appointed bodies would be beneficial.  
 
 introduce a mandate for Commission to request ECHA to develop new harmonised classification and 
labelling (‘CLH’) dossiers.  
5 
 
 
 
 Many open questions need to be addressed to ensure this would lead to improvements in the 
current CLH process. Additional resources, both at ECHA and in the industry, will have to be 
planned accordingly and assessed in the Impact Assessment.  
 Cefic would welcome simpler procedures and ability for industry to directly submit CLH 
dossiers to ECHA to revise existing CLH cases in case new data becomes available that could 
change the existing classification. 
 
 allow multilingual fold-out labels.  
 
 We support the possibility of allowing multilingual fold-out labels to ensure clear hazard 
communication on small packaging and global harmonisation like digitalisation under UN GHS. 
For certain supply chain scenarios, Cefic would welcome a possibility for placing information 
on packaging labels covering several GHS jurisdictions (EU and non-EU). 
 
 introduce tailored labelling rules where there is not enough space on packaging.  
 
 Generally, support simplification of labelling that would be in line with digitalisation under UN 
GHS. 
 
 introduce a prioritisation mechanism for harmonising the classification of certain chemicals.  
 
 Cefic supports clear prioritisation mechanism in line with ECHA’s Integrated Regulatory 
Strategy (based on hazard classes of highest concern (e.g. CMR-s), uses, volumes etc. 
 
 simplify and reduce unnecessary administrative costs.  
 
 Cefic is in favour of simplification and strongly believes in general reduction of administrative 
costs for all actors, including the high costs for re-labelling. The Impact Assessment will need 
to clarify which administrative costs or processes will be simplified resulting in administrative 
cost reductions.  
 
 The revision should be carried out in full conformity with the one-in-one-out principle laid out 
in the recent Communication on Better Regulation (April 2021). In addition, the impact 
assessment should comprehensively cover adjustment costs and provide an overview of 
possible targeted compensation measures. 
 
Concluding remarks 
The proposed changes to the CLP legal text will have far-reaching consequences not only on the chemical 
sector but also downstream. The revision should be done with the one-in-one-out principle laid out in the 
6 
 
recent Communication on Better Regulation1[. Having a thorough impact assessment is pivotal to assess 
the consequences and benefits of any proposed changes. This impact assessment should integrate the 
outcomes of the planned supporting studies, comprehensively cover adjustment costs and provide an 
overview of possible targeted compensation measures.    
Stakeholder dialogue is essential from the very beginning. Cefic stands ready to engage with the European 
Commission and other stakeholders to secure CLP will deliver its full potential.

About Cefic 
Cefic, the European Chemical Industry Council, founded  
in 1972, is the voice of large, medium and small chemical 
companies across Europe, which provide 1.1 million jobs 
and account for 15% of world chemicals production. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
While contributing to this consultation we remain very aware we are all still experiencing unprecedented times. As 
Cefic, we aim to maintain a high standard in our responses. Europe’s industries will have to cope with the impacts of 
this crisis while ensuring investments support a climate-neutral Europe. We therefore look to the European 
Institutions to undertake the appropriate assessments and set a framework ensuring the EU’s post-crisis 
attractiveness as an investment destination while achieving EU Green Deal objectives.

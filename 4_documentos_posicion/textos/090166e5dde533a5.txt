REVISION OF EU LEGISLATION ON HAZARD CLASSIFICATION, 
LABELLING AND PACKAGING OF CHEMICALS 
ARES(2021)2969734 - 04/05/2021 
The European steel industry, represented by EUROFER, welcomes the opportunity to contribute 
to the Inception Impact Assessment undertaken by the EU Commission on the Revision of EU 
legislation on hazard classification, labelling and packaging of chemicals (CLP revision), as an 
ambition set by the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability, to better protect the environment 
and citizens against hazardous chemicals and at the same time to encourage the development 
of safe and sustainable alternatives.  
We think that this revision process will give an opportunity to improve the legislation. To this 
end, we are of the opinion that: 
o The current regulation places a substantial and unnecessary burden on European 
businesses due to its complexity and, in many aspects, restrictiveness.  
 
o The unbalanced rigidity of the regulations sometimes translates into significant costs for 
companies, due to the adaptation to new standards. Consequently, this burden, beside 
to other financial and professional obligations arising from it, borne mainly by EU 
producers operating in a global market, does not offer a level playing field. 
 
o The resulting threat of increased imports of articles from outside EU, which are not 
subject to the regulatory rules comparable to those within the EU, including the 
development of safer alternatives, is of prime concern for our sector. 
 
o Before taking any regulatory actions, an overall impact assessment on industry needs to 
be explored, taking into account issues such as a substantial increase of prices for 
customers, potential production losses, or even a shift of production outside the EU. 
 
o Industry needs a balanced constructive approach of the regulatory authorities in 
searching for a more effective and efficient use of chemicals and safer production 
process rather than just restrictive attitudes that the legislation defines. Measures need 
to be balanced with various kinds of support for industry, and research & development, 
including a financial support, in addition to general public education. 
 
EUROFER, on behalf of its members, would like to raise the following specific comments on the 
Inception Impact Assessment.

Introduce new hazard classes (such as endocrine disruptors) and corresponding criteria 
With reference to the introduction of new hazard classes, we believe that the development of 
these new criteria should be addressed through legislation such as REACH which can regulate 
the use of these chemicals and ensure their communication on the safe use. 
The introduction of these new criteria should take place with longer transition periods than 
usual, in order to allow laboratories to obtain a better set up of tests and, consequently, to 
obtain more reliable results. To this end, the cost of fine-tuning these new procedures, 
alongside staff upgrading, could be substantial. 
Furthermore, the revision of the CLP, which is likely to take place before the UN GHS, should 
not be underestimated. Indeed, it could lead to inconsistencies and undermine the 
competitiveness of the industry on the global market outside the EU. We recommend that any 
proposals to add new hazard classes and/or to amend existing ones, should first be made under 
UN GHS. 
 
Introduce the possibility to submit proposals for and set harmonised environmental and 
safety values for some substances 
We have concerns about the suggestion to ‘’submit proposals for and set harmonised 
environmental and safety values for some substances’’. With reference to this point, we see 
that the introduction of occupational limit values into the CLP only based on hazard 
classification, without taking into account any socio-economic impacts or feasibility 
assessments, which is normally evaluated before risk management measures are adopted, 
could lead to consequences on industry’s competitiveness and the implementation of 
Occupational Safety and Health (OSH) Directives (Carcinogens and Mutagens Directive and the 
Chemical Agents Directive). The OSH Directives are very known tools used for protecting the 
health of employees from chemical risks via OELVs limits which takes also into account the 
working conditions. On the contrary, the CLP establish DNELs, which are exposure levels 
derived in the context of exposure scenarios. If, in addition to OSH Directives, also in the CLP 
occupational limit values will be set, we are afraid to lose the coherence among the legislations, 
with consequent legal uncertainty, administrative burden for operators which have to comply 
with them due to the confusion it will generate.  
This way to proceed will also be against the OSOA “one substance, one assessment” principle 
called in the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability, which aims to ensure that the same data 
does not need to be generated/submitted twice for different regulatory processes. If properly 
implemented in a way that achieves full transparency, the OSOA is welcome by EUROFER 
members, as we can benefit from consistent and coherent legislation, avoiding overlaps of 
regulations for different sectors using chemical substances.

Introduce a prioritisation mechanism for harmonising the classification of certain chemicals 
We could support the ‘’prioritisation mechanism for harmonization of the classification of 
certain chemicals’’ (for instance for the endocrine disruptors) across existing EU legislation. This 
prioritised (fast-track) harmonization could also help to ensure that the same substance is only 
assessed once for the identification of its specific properties, so it’s in line with the OSOA 
principle as well. However, we call that a proper assessment has to be done, in a robust and 
transparent way, before deciding for the prioritisation, in particular with regards to metals and 
inorganic metal compounds.  
Metals and inorganic compound have very specific properties incomparable with organic 
chemical. For many metals and compounds, data is already available from the scientific 
literature. If these data will be combined with a robust assessment of the chemical properties, 
in a weight-of-evidence approach, the need for additional animal testing could be limited to a 
minimum. The management of such type of chemicals should be specific and risk-based, taking 
into account the exposure considerations and the specificities of metals. On top of that 
classification should be based on science. Classification should therefore be limited to the route 
of exposure on which the positive CMR effect has been observed and concluded. The automatic 
classification for alternative routes of exposure is unnecessary precautionary and should be 
evaded. 
In this context it is particularly important to consider the alloying effect which occurs in many 
alloys. Although an alloy might contain some hazardous substances the matrix of the alloy 
prevents those substances to be released. They do not become bio-available.  
The same applies to substances in slag from iron and steel production, which are often firmly 
integrated into the mineral phases due to production and are therefore not bioavailable. 
In the present CLP this is dealt with in article 12 b) where is stated that if conclusive scientific 
experimental data show that the substance or mixture is not biologically available and those 
data have been ascer­tained to be adequate and reliable; this should require further evaluation. 
This article has led to many uncertainties. In particular with regards to the interpretation of the 
concept of ‘not biologically available’.  
Furthermore, test methods to prove conclusive scientific experimental data are subject to 
conditions mentioned in Article 8(3) stating that the test methods shall be conducted in 
accordance with one of the following methods: 
(a) the test methods referred to in Article 13(3) of Regulation (EC) No 1907/2006; or 
(b) sound scientific principles that are internationally recognized or methods validated 
according to international procedures.  
It is for the metals industry very necessary that bio-elution is acknowledged as a proven 
methodology to demonstrate bio-availability.

Conclusions 
 
EUROFER welcomes the European Commission’s Inception Impact Assessment on the CLP 
revision as an opportunity to improve this legislation, and the possibility for the industry to 
comment on this topic. However, we are of the opinion that the points addressed in this 
document should be clarified to allow the European steel industry to be fully engaged in the 
revision of the EU legislation on the classification, labeling and packaging of chemicals.

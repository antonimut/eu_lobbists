Linking the SPI with the transition to a circulareconomy
A circular economy is based on three principles, all driven by design: eliminatewasteandpollution; keep products and materials in use; and regeneratenatural systems. Thismodelreplaces the ‘end-of-life’ concept by stimulating upstreaminnovation tocreateanindustrialsystemthat is regenerative and restorative by design. It delivers solutions that help tackleglobal environmental challenges such as climate change,pollution, and biodiversity loss.
The forthcoming Sustainable Products Initiative (SPI), announced as part of the 2020 EUCircular Economy ActionPlan, canbeconsideredasacrucial steptowards“supportingthecircular design of all products based on a common methodology and principles”, ashighlighted in the European Green Deal.

Assess the circularity improvement potential
The current EU Ecodesign Directive aims to optimise product performance within alinear economy framework, focusing on selected priority aspects, such as energyconsumption in the use phase. Instead, theimplementingmeasuresunder theSPI couldbe more powerful if they take into account the conditions for different value creationopportunities in a circular economy. Identifying, prioritising, and defining relevant circularaspects and potential requirements to support solutions at material, product, service, orcompany level is key.
Therefore, meaningful implementing measures under the SPI require a comprehensiveassessment that reviews the circularity improvement potential along the relevant valuechains within a broad product category, such as furniture or textiles. To understand thisimprovement potential better, we need to distinguish between three main circular valuecreation drivers:
1. Increase the use of the productby an individual userand/or the number of usersper product2. Consider cascading usesfor the product, its components,and materials3. Give a supporting but limited role to LCA methodologies
Once the circularity improvement potential for a specific product category or valuechain has been identified, it is important tosubstantiatetheexpectedenvironmentalbenefits linked to the proposed implementing measures. In this context, theEuropeanCommission refers to the role of Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) and the EU ProductEnvironmental Footprint (PEF) methodology. LCA, in general, and PEF in particular, havebeen developed as tools for evaluating the environmental impacts of a specific productthrough all the defined stages of itslifetime. However, insteadof calculatingLCAresultsorPEFscoresfor aspecificproduct’suseintoday’slinear system, theSPI needstoenvisagehow products will be used differently in a circulareconomy.
AlthoughLCAtoolsarewell suitedtooptimisingindividual choicesunderfixedconditions,thesolutionstheypoint towill not alwaysleadtothebestcollectivecircularoutcomesovertime,and may create confusion when used as the basis for consumer communication. Takeelectric vehicles (EVs) as an example: manywouldagreethat amobilitysystemsupportedby EVs and renewable electricityisamoreattractivetarget statetoworktowardsthanoneperpetually reliant on combustion engines and fossil fuels. However, not all LCAs wouldsupport thisconclusion. If appliedexclusivelytotheproductlevel (i.e.apassengercar),PEFwould probably point towards the immediate benefit of improving the efficiencies of petrolcars (i.e. minimising impacts in today’s system), while not pointing towards the need toinnovateandimproveEVsfor thefuture(i.e. buildanintegratedmobilitysystempoweredbyrenewable energy that, through better design, will work for more people andleadtofewerindividual cars).
Bearing these limitations in mind, LCA and PEF could still be used for sensitivityanalysisofthecircularityimprovementoptionsidentifiedundertheSPI.Thiscouldhelpquantify some key environmental benefits related to requirements and benchmarks for aspecific product category versus the status quo, or identify and address potentialenvironmental trade-offs. The SPI assessment methodology could build on LCA and PEFmethodologies to investigate the impact of different scenarios, such as changes inconsumption patterns, improvement of recycling infrastructure, or availability of newtechnologies. Ingeneral, thesetoolsshouldonlybeusedtocomparesolutionsthatarefairlysimilarandonlyonenvironmental aspectsthatcanbecapturedandmeasuredwell withintheLCA/PEF boundaries and relevant impact categories.
Incentivise circular outcomes, not business models
Businessmodelsthat combinerelevant circularproduct-designcharacteristics,focuson value retention, and employ reverse cycle processes stand the best chance ofachievingcircular economyoutcomes. Followingthisrationale, theeconomicandmarketincentivesthat areputinplacecanbemostimpactful iftheyrewardcircularoutcomes,whichcan be monitored and measured. An outcomes-basedapproachallowsbusinessesrelativefreedomtofocusonreducingenvironmental impactswithout havingtoadopt aspecifictypeof business model.

Create policy alignment, avoid regulatory fragmentation
Whenidentifyingthecircularityimprovementpotentialforspecificproductgroupsanddrafting relevant implementing measures under the SPI, the process could considerother pieces of legislation to promote policy coordination and harmonisation.Combining market pull instruments (e.g. publicprocurement or reputational incentivessuchas labels and certificates) and market push instruments (such as EU-wide mandatoryminimum information and design requirements) is key to deliver the necessary change atindustry scale.
Given the systemic nature of the circular economy transition, the implementingmeasures under the SPI need to be aligned with a range of other regulatoryinterventions. This alignment could entail horizontal product or due diligence legislation,labelling requirements, rules on legal warranties and consumer protection, end-of-wastecriteria, waste shipment regulations, and infrastructure investments that apply to thesameproduct categories under the scope of the SPI. When it comes to price signals and othermarket mechanisms, these policy instruments are often decided at national level, but theEuropeanSemester processcouldplayanimportant roleinpreventingorovercomingpolicyfragmentation among EU Member States.

Glossary of key circular economy terms
This annex contains a non-exhaustive list of definitionsfor key circular economy terms thatshould be clarified in the legislative text of theEU Sustainable Products Initiative.

It is underpinned by a transition to renewableenergy and materials.Composting Microbial breakdown of organic matter in the presenceof oxygen. It is a practicethat retains the highest value of aproduct while extending its use period.Sharing The use of a product by multiple users. It is a practicethat retains the highest value of aproduct while extending its use period.Repair Operation by which a faulty or broken product or componentis returned back to a usablestate.Reuse The repeated use of a product or component for itsintended purpose without significantmodification, but potentially involving cleaning orsmall adjustments so it is ready for thenext use.Refurbish Return a product to good working order.

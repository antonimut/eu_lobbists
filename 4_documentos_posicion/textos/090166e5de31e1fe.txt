 
 
 
 
 
The International Wool Textile Organisation (IWTO) represents the collective interests of the global 
wool industry. Our members come fro m all stages of the wool textile pipeline, from farm to retail.  
Through scientific research, wool textile education, and knowledge sharing, our goal is a sustainable 
future for wool.   
We thank the Commission for this opportunity to voice our concerns on the topic of the Sustainable 
Product Initiative. We would like to raise the following points: 
(1) The current PEF methodology is narrowly drawn and fails to sufficiently take into account 
other wider issues of concern to consumers including biodiversity and, especially, 
microplastics. If based on the current PEF scoring, a label risks misleading customers around 
the full picture of sustainability. The PEF system is intended to deliver truth in labelling, but 
currently, it doesn’t assess natural and synthetic fibres equally. 
There is a growing body of scientific evidence that synthetic textiles discharge significant 
amounts of microplastic fibres through laundering, releasing microplastics into the 
environment, oceans, and the human food chain. Estimates suggest that as much as 35% of all 
primary source microplastics in the marine environment are fibres from use of synthetic 
clothing. It is also estimated that 46 mg of microplastic particles are lost per kg of synthetic 
textile washing while synthetic fibres account for approximately 60% of the total global fibre 
production, with nylon and polyester as dominating fibres.  
However, microplastic pollution from synthetic materials does not carry any negative scoring 
under the PEF methodology. Therefore, synthetic materials are given a better environmental 
rating, ignoring the environmental advantages of natural fibres that biodegrade and 
misleading consumers. 
  
(2) Like the impacts of microplastic pollution, the full environmental impacts of f orming the 
biomass that ultimately leads to crude oil are not accounted for in the PEF score. By contrast, 
all the plant and animal impacts of forming natural fibres are fully taken into account (i.e. the 
amounts of land water used, and greenhouse gasses emitted). As a result, consumers are not 
being shown the true environmental cost of clothing made from fossil fuel -based fibres. 
Furthermore, renewability is not rewarded since the fact that natural fibres, such as wool, can 
be regrown indefinitely is not considered in the current PEF system. 
This omission is important because textile fibres often show the greatest lifecycle impacts 
during the fibre formation stage. Consumers are not given the option to make fully informed 
and conscious choices for the market to increase its demand of sustainable options.  
  
(3) Renewable resources include energy and materials that are not depleted when used. For 
example, naturally occurring materials are renewable if they move through biological cycles at 
rates that can be replenished by living systems. Raw materials grown on farms are inherently 
circular as they can be grown again every year and at the end of life they biodegrade, returning 
their nutrients to the soil to be used again. Even though carbon, nitrogen, sulphur and other 
elements in natural fibres such as wool and cotton have been recycled in nature for years, the

PEF system does not recognize this natural cycle as recycling and does not reward natural 
fibres in the PEF score.  
On the contrary, the extraction of petroleum or natural gas for the production of fuels and 
plastics is not a renewable process. For this reason, the subsequent production of 
petrochemicals into products such as fuels and plastics (i.e. polyester, nylon, p olypropylene, 
acrylic) hampers the EU’s sustainability efforts and goes against the Green Deal’s objective.   
  
(4) The European Environmental Agency (EAA) recognises that animal‐based fibres and natural 
fabrics, such as wool, have better durability and lower impacts in the use phase and end -of-
life, compared to synthetic fibres. Consumers wash wool clothing less frequently than other 
fibre types, saving the water, energy and detergent associated with laundering. 
If the EU continues with the PEF methodology i n its current form, products made from wool 
and other natural fibres are at significant risk of being rated poorly compared to fossil -based 
synthetics.

   
Position paper on the material efficiency requirements in Ecodesign  
 
Impact on professional imaging and printing equipment 
I&P Europe – Imaging and Printing Association e.V. | Mainzer Landstraße 55 | 60329 Frankfurt am Main | Germany 
A s s i s t a n c e  i n  H e a l t h ,  S a f e t y  &  E n v i r o n m e n t  R e g u l a t o r y  C o m p l i a n c e  I s s u e s 
April 2020 
 
While we encourage the introduction of material efficiency requirements to facilitate the transition to a 
Circular Economy, we would like to emphasize the circular nature of professional imaging and printing 
equipment which has long been designed to be durable, reparable, upgradeable and at end of life recycla-
ble.  
 
Current policy developments show a shift from energy requirements to material efficiency requirements in 
Ecodesign regulations. Our equipment incorporates several products that are in scope, such as motors, fans 
and displays. We would like to highlight several concerns the following developments could have on profes-
sional imaging and printing manufacturers.  
▪ Material Efficiency standards are being developed to support Ecodesign regulations (EN 45 55X 
series by CEN-CENELEC JTC10, JRC reparability scoring).  
▪ Material Efficiency requirements have become part of Ecodesign regulations (such as for Displays 
and Servers) 
We have observed that the focus of the material efficiency standardisation developmen t is on consumer 
products, such as reparability of laptops. It may be that from a reparability perspective it is beneficial to 
encourage ease of disassembly, limit the number of disassembly steps and promote the use commonly avail-
able tools. Looking at lar ge, complex professional product s however, disassembly steps for a product con-
sisting of thousands of parts can be a huge unnecessary burden. The ability to use commonly available tools 
for a product that requires safe disassembly by a professional is not a measure of reparability. Most im-
portantly, requirements must never lead to unsafe situations, where ease of disassembly may conflict with 
the safety requirements in an industrial environment. We therefore encourage inclusion of the specific needs 
for professional products in standardisation developments. 
 
The Circular Economy principle is well established in the professional imaging and printing industry. Our 
equipment is designed to last more than 10 years. The lifetime of many products can be up to 20 -30 years. 
These products are suitable for a second life by a refurbishment/remanufacturing process. We therefore do 
not understand the rationale that the spare part exemption is limited to only 7 years in the motor regulation 
and are very concerned. This greatly impacts our products  and damages the existing Circular Economy. We 
believe legislation should support the use of spare parts for products with long lifet ime according to the 
‘repair as produced’ principle (as applied in the RoHS Directive). Limited spare part exemptions will lead to 
expensive re-design and unnecessary waste creation which is counter-productive to the Circular Economy.  
 
Information requirements could be beneficial when it supports consumers making a more resource efficient 
choice. However, we are concerned that information requirements, which are increasingly part of Ecodesign 
regulations, can lead to an excessive administrative burden for large, complex, products, without benefit to 
the professional user or the environment. Furthermore, from a safety and competitiveness point of view, we 
advise against any obligation making product diagrams and repair/disassembly information available to third 
parties. In our experience, repair and end of life treatment operators will ask if they require such information. 
 
Finally, I&P Europe members know how to build and design products that are part of the Circular Economy. 
Regulatory developments should support the circular nature of professional imaging and printing equipment 
by minimising administrative burdens, harmonisation and by supporting the circular business model of re-
pair, reuse and remanufacturing/refurbishment. Lastly, we would like to emphasise that reduction of overall 
complexity will also greatly benefit SMEs, which, together with larger companies, play a vital role in the fur-
ther transition to a Circular Economy.

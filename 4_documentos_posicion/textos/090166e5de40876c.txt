PRE contribution to the SPI questionnaire 
 
Plastics Recyclers Europe (PRE) welcomes the Sustainable Product Initiative (SPI) as a unique 
opportunity to foster the transition to a green economy. By setting sustainability and circularity criteria 
for products to be placed on the Single Market, it will allow for a harmonization of measures across 
the Member States and establish the link between product and waste policies. 
The purpose of our contribution is to focus on the environmental aspect of sustainability, without 
disregarding the social and governance scope. The framework of the present document is the 
sustainability assessment of plastics manufacturing, as provided by Annex 1 (3.17) of the EU 
Taxonomy Regulation. There, sustainability is assessed for plastics originating from mechanical 
recycling, or (provided it is not technically feasible or economically viable and resulting in lower GHG 
emissions than virgin production), also chemical recycling, and renewable plastic processes. At PRE, 
we support this approach and would welcome the adaptation from sustainable industrial practices to 
sustainable products. 
In this context, PRE would like to address the following points: 
 
1. Recycled content alone is not enough. 
The focus of the questionnaire provided to stakeholders seems to exclude recyclability among the 
requirements (durability, reusability, upgradability, and reparability) for products to be considered 
sustainable.  Recycled content, which the questionnaire touches upon, represents only one side of the 
circularity of plastics products. To achieve full circularity for plastics, sustainable products made with 
recycled content should also be designed for recycling to accomplish more recycling cycles. Design 
for recycling supports circularity and a wider end-applications for plastic recyclates (products made 
from plastic waste such as flakes or pellets). 
For packaging, PRE has established RecyClass1, a value chain platform aiming at making plastic 
packaging compatible with existing plastic waste streams. The initative developed scientific-based 
protocols for each packaging type, upon which guidelines on design for recycling are developed. In 
this respect, recyclability is key for the environmental objectives of sustainability.  
Further, PRE has a clear definition for recyclability, which considers the where and when a product is 
recyclable. It can be summarized in the following 4 points: 
a. The product must be made with a plastic that is collected for recycling, has market value 
and/or is supported by a legislatively mandated program. 
b. The product must be sorted and aggregated into defined streams for recycling processes. 
c. The product can be processed and reclaimed/recycled with commercial recycling 
processes.

2. Design for recycling: standardization to setup a harmonized set of requirements to foster the 
sustainable design of plastics products at the EU level. 
Based on  this design for recycling guidelines, the value-chain of plastics has launched the Circular 
Plastics Alliance (CPA), to tackle design needs through industry-led standards, to place on the market 
products and packaging that are recyclable. The existence of such standards for products to be 
claimed sustainable, represents for PRE a relevant assessment tool. 
 
3. Recycled content can originate only from waste  
Taking into consideration, our experience on recycling as well as the EU legislative framework on 
waste we believe that any assessment on recyclability or recycled content must be well defined in the 
legislation, to avoid unharmonized implementations across the EU Member States’ national 
legislations.  
In this regard, a key requirement for recycled content in sustainable products is that it must originate 
from waste. The concept of waste, especially for plastics, is very difficult to assess both for pre-
consumer and post-consumer “waste” materials (not defined in the EU legislation but referred to in 
standards). End-of-waste criteria for plastic waste were never delivered by the EU, and the concept of 
by-products varies today from one Member State to another. Without a clear distinction between 
waste and non-waste, the assessment of recycled content can become difficult to prove for plastics.2 
In this case, we suggest that sustainable claims are linked to a certification system which allows for 
the traceability of waste. Once audited, the transfer of plastic between the different actors of the value-
chain can be traced and reported, from waste to a product. This chain of custody model could become 
a unique system to assess the sustainability of plastic products being placed on the market, if 
transparency and clarity throughout all the recycling steps can be supplied by all stakeholders 
involved. Where this is not possible, i.e., where book and claim or allocation models are being 
implemented, the sustainability of products cannot be valued.  
 
In other words, the SPI should consider as recycling and recycled materials only recycling processes 
that convert end-of-life materials back into products to be placed on the Single Market. In this regard, 
we would like to provide our expertise and support on a methodology for traceability that assist this 
endeavour, namely, to ensure that only waste is recycled back in final applications, and no by-products 
are to be considered as sustainable products because of unharmonized understandings for recycled

4. A chain of custody model should be in place per material streams, supporting the objectives 
of the SPI. 
The SPI should push for a chain of custody model throughout the different actors of the value-chain, 
provided that transparent methodologies to certify the traceability of waste are in place. For instance, 
a study from ISEAL4 describes different chain of custody models, of which batch-level mass balance 
could perfectly fit for plastics. As the document states, “this model maintains segregation until the 
final point of blending or mixing for a specific batch of a product. Mixing with non-certified product is 
controlled and recorded, so the proportion of certified content in each final product is known. This is 
where certified and non-certified components are mixed within an actual product. This type of mass-
balance ensures the end-product contains at least a proportion of certified product, which allows 
specific end-use claims to be made.” 
 
5. Environmentally sustainable recycling technologies 
Circularity and GHG emissions’ reduction are two key concepts for sustainable plastic products that 
claim recycled content. In a circular economy, sustainable products must support, among others, 
environmental benefits in “the use of energy, renewable energy, raw materials, water and land, on the 
production of waste, and greenhouse gas emissions, or on its impact on biodiversity and the circular 
economy”, as provided in the definition of sustainable investments (Regulation (EU) 2020/852).  
Therefore, where a plastic product is produced from an industrial activity that fails to bring such 
benefits, even if legally falling under the definition of renewable, reusable or recycling, it shall be pre-
empted from being considered sustainable. 
 
6. Product passport 
A frequently put forward notion is that a product passport specifying chemical composition would 
greatly facilitate the appropriate separation and recycling of plastics waste from products. The idea is 
that products would be equipped with a barcode, RFID chip, or other means of identification that would 
make a link to a database with more information on the product. Recyclers could then pick up 
individual articles, look them up by scanning them and make an informed decision on what to do with 
the individual items. 
Despite being an inspirational approach, this might not be the ideal solution for the recycling of plastic 
waste. Recyclers utilise systems that, extremely rapidly, separate plastics based on a certain property 
(e.g., polymer, density, size), with throughputs of several tons per hour. If one would assume a

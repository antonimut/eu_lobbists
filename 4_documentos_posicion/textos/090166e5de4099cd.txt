SPI as a step towards a circular future  
The European Commission launched the Sustainable Products Initiative (SPI), as a flagship project 
of the ambitious EU Green Deal and the new Circular Economy Action Plan (CEAP).  
The Belgian Federation of Enterprises  (VBO-FEB) represents more than 50 sector federations 
actively working towards a more circular economy, as explained in our recently published Vision 
on Circular Economy1. We support this legislative initiative that is targeted to make products more 
durable, reusable, repairable, recyclable and more energy-efficient. The SPI is now at the stage 
of public consultation (until 9th June 2021). The following paragraphs highlight our key messages 
and our concerns to ensure a successful and impactful SPI, beneficial for both the environment 
and European companies delivering on the twin objective of  a cleaner and more competitive 
Europe.   
 
1 Ensure a harmonized development at EU-level 
To achieve an efficient transition towards an EU circular  single market, it will be crucial to have 
a harmonized and coherent development at the European level. 
- A strong dialogue with industry  will be essential since there is no ‘one -size-fits-all’ 
solution for all products on the  EU market. Furthermore company feedback is needed  to 
make sure that new proposals are aligned with other (existing) directives and sectoral 
legislations to avoid legal uncertainty  or even contradiction . This dialogue will also 
enhance chances for successful implementation by industry after legislations are adapted.  
- Joint action by all stakeholders: business partners in the value chain, consumers, waste 
management -both public and private- and public entities. We all need to be involved and 
collaborate to have a successful paradigm shift from linear thinking towards a truly circular

2 Transition period to ensure a successful 
implementation 
The SPI is a n ambitious building block that will serve as the main framework for sustainable 
products in a renewed European (greener) approach towards product policy. It has a very broad 
scope with a potential enormous impact on many sectors  because it may entail a lot of new 
requirements, adaptations and innovation plans in the companies.  
Those new requirements should first of all be aligned and coherent with existing requirements 
for products. Furthermore we need to avoid potential conflicting targets and make sure that they 
are all efficient. For example, the requirements regulating the availability and delivery of spare 
parts should be flexible enough to avoid unintended negative effects such as overproduction or 
excess inventory of spare parts. Last but not least, these new requirements should not put a too 
high administrative and financial burden on the companies that must implement them. Special 
attention is needed for the added burden imposed on small and medium-sized enterprises.  
We therefore want to put forward a flexible transition period of at least some years. The exact 
length should be establ ished in dialogue with each value chain  and if needed, tailored to the 
specific value chain. This will give all companies the opportunity and space to successfully

3 Digital Product Passport  
The Digital Product Passport (DPP) is a very ambitious building block of the SPI. It aims to make 
information to market actors along value chains more accessible and visible.  
We need to make sure that the scope of the DPP is limited to those products where it would 
be useful. One of the key question thereby is: for what purpose will the collected data be used? 
Sensitive information -if not well protected- may get in the wrong hands . Therefore, before the 
introduction of any new product passport system, a thorough impact assessment needs to be 
performed to assess the benefits and disadvantages related to the specific value chain.  
Intellectual property of European companies is the key of a competitive position of Europe in the 
world. We need to make sure that a high level of confidentiality is maintained to keep sensitive 
information from falling in the wrong hands. We therefore support the position of the European 
Parliament to open the door for input by relevant stakeholders and to their close involvement in 
developing the DPP methods.  
Since primary data  is  currently dispersed, not homogeneous and systems used are not uniform 
among the different value chain actors, there is a need for a well-designed, smart IT solution, 
taking into account those differences amongst actors and value chain5. All actors should facilitate 
an effective, data-secure and fast information exchange.  
A lot of questions remain unanswered: Are the exact data requirements per product established? 
How will this data be shared? What with the IT archives  and t echnical requirements? These 
uncertainties make it difficult for enterprises to plan their innovations to correctly and efficiently 
implement the SPI.  
We recommend to implement the information requirements in stages, starting with pilot cases 
and with a set of selected priority products. We recommend to start with pilot cases developed 
in collaboration with industry, then launch it for some priority product types, progressively adding 
data requirements where useful. A phased implementation will give a clear view on the data and 
information requirements, the reason for which it will be used, the availability of the data and its 
confidentiality. This will provide companies sufficient time to organise , adapt and make the 
correct innovation plans.

4 Lifecycle assessment and Green Public Procurement 
 
Lifecycle assessment (LCA) will be an essential aspect of the environmental footprint of the end 
product and to ensure that sustainable alternatives become the n orm6. It can combine 
functionality, environmental, safety and sustainability considerations during extraction, 
production, use and end -of-life7. To have a successful LCA all significant stages of the end 
product’s lifecycle need to be considered, as they will enable to assess the overall impact when 
conflicts arise during the different stages. This also applies for imported products ( e.g. the high 
material/ CO2 -footprint of imported virgin (unprocessed) materials versus materials from 
recycling).  
Another effective measure is making public procurement more fit for circular solutions. The 
implementation of Green Public Procurement (GPP) could accelerate the shift to a sustainable 
built environment while delivering on key European Green Deal goals.

 
 
ANIE Federation Position on Sustainable P roduct Initiative and Digital 
Product Passport 
ANIE Federation is one of the largest business organizations in Italy in terms of weight, size and 
representativeness. ANIE is joined by 1,500 companies in the electrotechnical and electronic sector. The 
sector employs 500,000 people with an aggregate turnover (at the end of 2019) of 84 billion euros. 
 
Below we would like to submit some additional considerations with respect to the contribution sent to the 
public consultation of the Commission regarding the Sustainable Products Initiative and the related Digital 
Product Passport.  
 
Sustainable Product Initiative 
 
Key messages 
 
Generally speaking, while appreciating the intentions underlying the future legislative proposa l, we briefly 
highlight some aspects that we consider fundamental to consider for the finalization and implementation of 
any regulatory initiative on products. 
 
• An impact assessment must always be conducted to ensure that their implementation will be workable, 
proportionate and will contribute to a circular economy. There must be proven environmental benefits 
that exceed the costs to industry. 
• To secure the functioning of the Single Market requirements must be harmonised at EU level. 
• New requirements for p roducts must follow the  New Legislative Framework (NLF)  including the 
application of the CE marking and declaration of conformity. 
• Maintain the Ecodesign procedure currently in force for the definition of implementation measures . 
We believe that the excell ent results achieved so far are proof that the method of developing 
implementation measures work s very well and is able to meet the environmental needs of consumers 
and industry. 
• The “SMERC" principle must be applied:  
─ Specific - requirements must be considered on a product group -specific basis. Even within the same 
product group and within individual categories of equipment in our sector, the products and their 
environmental impact differ significantly, especially depending on ambient and operating conditions.  
─ Measurability - the parameters must be clearly determined and measurement methods must be 
accurately defined. 
─ Enforceability - it must be possible to verify and enforce requirements through market surveillance.  
─ Relevance – new parameters and corresp onding requirements must be relevant for the 
environment, the users and applicable even within the specific life cycle phase(s). There must be 
evidence of clear and significant potential for improvement.   
─ Competitiveness - there must be no significant neg ative impact on the industry’s competitiveness 
and the competition must be fair.  
• We support information on product level when it is possible and relevant. 
• The industry should be involved as early and as much as possible in the development of the SPI and the 
DPP. The sector know-how is extremely important. 
• The burden put on companies must be  proportionate. A dditional efforts should be kept as low as 
possible, must be manageable and affordable for SMEs. 
• Information should respect confidentiality related to protectable trade secrets and secure IP protection.

• There is no one-size-fits-all approach. SPI requirements and DPP must be established on a sector by 
sector and product by product basis  taking into account the differences in products (and differences 
between B2C and B2B products) and the information that is relevant to them.  
• Requirements should be technology neutral  to ensure a variety of technology options applicable to 
sustainable design requirements and choices related to material efficiency. 
• Requirements should not hinder the development of new innovations, business models and products.  
• The EU should support European companies to put in place theses new SPI and DPP measures as well as 
the EU Member States to control these measures.  
• Ensuring effective enforcement and market surveillance  will indeed be of utmost importance for the 
success of the application of the SPI and DPP and will be even more needed than today to ensure a level 
playing field. 
• We strongly oppose third-party certification or inspection. Self-assessment is just as valid a procedure, 
and offers the  same level of safety  benefits, as any conformity assessment pr ocedure supported by a 
third-party (e.g. notified body).  
 
 
Detailed comments 
 
First of all, it should be underlined that ANIE appreciates the general approach of the proposal to regulate 
sustainability requirements by intervening at product groups /families level. In fact, we believe it is 
fundamental that the upcoming regulatory framework safeguards as much as possible the method ology of 
the Ecodesign directive to finalize the implementation measures. The Working Plan methodol ogy, which 
provides for a prior categorization of product groups that will be analysed trough preparatory studies, takes 
into due consideration the intrinsic differences between product families and carefully avoids the use of 
horizontal measures that would not be appropriate or effective. 
 
On the other hand, we are quite perplex in reference to the following statements, reported in the text of the 
consultation in section 2A, on which it is necessary to make further comments with respect to what is 
possible in the consultation itself: 
 
– “e. Require producers/importers to ensure information on repairability is provided on or with a 
product”. This needs to be analy sed to access the environmentally added value compared to the 
costs for companies and for consumers . It might be interesting for some products while other 
products, for example products of low value, should rather be designed for recycling. Again it is 
relevant to distinguish between B2B and B2 C products and further distinguish between products a 
consumer can repair for themselves without safety concerns and products which only authorized 
personnel should repair. In addition, it depends on the detailed info rmation that is provided to the 
end user. Due to safety reasons, some information might not be provided and some products must 
be repaired only by professionals. We  also have several questions such as: how will the criteria be 
designed, w hat information on  reparability should be included  or which l evel of details will be 
required. There is a  risk of unfair competition  and as a minimum the requirements should be the 
same for all products in a given product category . We note that the exi sting French Circular 
Economy law already requires this information and the scope is limited to B2C products. We 
recommend to start with a small number of products. 
 
─ “l. Ban the use of a substance or substances in a given product, s hould such substances be fo und to 
inhibit prod uct recyclability”.  With a well -established and consolidated legislative framework on 
substances, such as the REACH Regulation or the RoHS Directive, it seems incomprehensible to regulate 
or ban the use of substa nces through a product directive. If there w as a need to do so, it would be 
advisable to include these issues in the impact assessments and risk management option analysis of the 
relevant legislation.

Digital Product Passport 
 
Key messages 
 
With reference to the possible establishment of a Digital Product Passport (DPP), we would like to present 
some detailed assessments. In fact, we believe that the introduction of a similar tool would bring undoubted 
benefits to consumers and industry, however it will be necessary to take into account some key aspects. 
 
• The DPP must not provide redundan t information with respect to existing dat abases (EPREL, SCIP, 
etc.) in order to avoid further administrative burdens. The DPP must be connected to the other 
databases and extract the existing data avoiding duplication and overlapping. 
• To ensure the functioning of  the internal market, t he DPP must be based on harmonized regulations 
which leave no room for national drifts. 
• Provide a preventive impact assessment that confirms the environmental benefits. 
• The information in the DPP must be meani ngful, easy to understand, reliable, comparable, verifiabl e 
and have a n improvement potential from sustainability point of view . The data must have added value 
for environmental purposes. 
• The design of  DPP must  involve industry stakeholders that can provide  knowledge and experience 
necessary for the DPP to work in practice. 
• The "SMERC" principle (Specific, Measurability, Enforceability, Relevance, Competitiveness) must be 
applied. 
• The burden on businesses must be proportionate, especially for SMEs. 
• There is no horizontal solution. The DPP must be calibrated by sec tor and by product , taking into 
account the intrinsic differences (e.g. B2C and B2B) and relevant information. 
• A phased approach would be preferable  starting with simple products and criteria that have data 
already available, rather than starting immediate ly with an excessively wide range of complex products 
and criteria. 
• The content of DPP should be limited to existing regulatory r equirements. Otherwise it would be 
impossible to request non-mandatory information from global and complex supply chains. 
• The DPP must respect the confidentiality relating to industrial secrets and intellectual property. 
• It is important to ensure effective application and adequate market surveillance. The information must 
be verifiable to ensure a level playing field between EU and non-EU companies. 
• The data required by the DPP must be based on scientific evaluation me thods, recognized by 
European and international standards, and must be reliable and verifiable. 
• An appropriate and reasonable level of detail of information must be determined. 
• There must be clear accountability for each actor who provides data, accesses and shares information. 
 
 
 
Detailed comments 
 
While showing openness and full willingness to collaborate on the Commission's initiative, our companies 
see in the Digit al Product Passport (DPP) a possible source of criticality if the latter is not implemented 
correctly. In general, we would support the Commission’s idea of creating an address similar to the URL  
(Uniform Resourc e Locator) for the product. When combined wi th a tag (QR code, Radio frequency 
identification (RFID), Bluetooth tag) the different stakeholders could connect directly to access the product’s 
unique digital profile with quantitative and qualitative, static and dynamic, standardized and machine -
readable data.

Through the DPP it would be possible for European companies to enhance their environmental 
commitment and demonstrate clear superiority in terms of  ability to collect and provide information to 
customers and consumers. 
Moreover, the use of  a digit al tool able to collect and standardize data would be appreciated by the 
electrotechnical and electronic sector , which has always pushed for a replacement of paper documents 
which, in most cases, represent only an additional cost for companies  and for the environment. There 
would also be an undoubted benefit for consumers in terms of greater transparency and the possibility of 
direct interaction with those producers who have always focused on customer loyalty and invest in 
maintaining relationships with the supply chain. 
 
For the above, it is therefore essential that the practical definition of the instrument takes place with an eye 
to the following critical aspects, which could be extremely penalizing  if not adequately addressed by the 
European legislator: 
 
─ ─Management of confidential data. The main concern of the industry is that confidential information is 
made available to anyone without control. Should it be required to upload data relating to corporate 
know-how and commercial information, it would  be nec essary to ensure security and prevent 
indiscriminate access to this information . Importance of cybersecurity when it comes to the 
development of Digital Product Passport . Access to data is sensitive in many ways (IPR, trade secrets 
etc.). T herefore, it mus t be ensured that the dat a remains secure and confidential. The number one 
concern for the industry is that the confidential business know -how and trade secrets could be exposed 
to their competitors. This is why there must be clear responsibilities for acc essing and sharing 
information for the Digital Product Passport. 
 
─ Adequately consider the complexity of products and supply chains, as well as the amount of data 
needed, to make the DPP effective. The electrotechnical and electronic sector represents products with 
a high degree of complexity, sometimes consisting of thousands of components and with value chains 
and industrial sites located globally. Consequently, we consider  essential that the DPP provides for the 
possibility of application only for “ representative products of the category ”, an option that would allow 
to exemplify and facilitate the loading of data, thus excluding tailor made products. Furthermore, in 
finalizing the infrastructure of the DPP it would be advisable to involve experts from the various 
industrial sectors, in order to calibrate the type of data required for each category of products; 
otherwise it would be difficult to imagine that information suitable for furniture and textile products 
could fit for electronic equipment. 
 
─ Ensure the relevance and reliability of  information in DPP. Especially for complex products it may be 
necessary to enter large amounts of information, which would risk being difficult to manage and con sult 
both for the Authorities and for consumers. Among the criteria that will guide the implementation of the 
DPP, it will be necessary to consider who will be the recipient of the individual information and the use 
that the latter shou ld make of the data provided. It should not be forgotten that all information shoul d 
be updated periodically, thus increasing the volume and degree of complexity of the data and metadata 
to be kept and consulted. Finally, we cannot fail to ask ourselves ho w the information uploaded to the 
DPP by non-EU companies would be verified and controlled by the Authorities. 
It is essential to avoid what has already happened with other legislation (e.g. WEEE, RoHS, REACH) 
where European companies are penalized compared to non -EU companies that systematically evade 
charges and responsibilities without any consequence.

─ Reduce administrative burdens by reusing data already present in other databases and ensuring the  
interoperability of the DPP.  At the state of the art, there are multiple ways of managing and storing 
information by companies, rangin g from a simple excel sheet to more complex systems such as SAP or 
specific management software, usually this plurality o f methodologies already constitutes the main 
obstacle in the collection and exchange of data between companies. Consequently, it would be useful to 
provide for the highest possible degree of interoperability of the DPP with respect to the different

As already mentioned, there are numerous similar databases in force in EU (e.g. SCIP), therefore it would 
be necessary to provide for a mechanism for direct import of data already loaded into other digital tools, 
eliminating the need for re -processing and loading which would only involve a waste of time and 
resources. 
 
─ Minimize costs and impact of the DPP. It is essential to note that the greater the number of information 
and the level of detail required by the DPP, the greater the time and cost in terms of resources required 
to fulfil it will be. This is why we ask th at a preventive impact assessment be conducted that adequately 
considers the impact and the cost/benefit ratio of each possible information field of the DPP.

─ “c. Quantities of materials and substances present in the product”. Our industries are fully committed 
to reducing the content of hazar dous substances in their products to sup port a more circular economy 
and achieve sustainability goals. A meaningful exchange of information between partners in the value 
chain, focusing on substances of very high concern, can improve the product life cycle  footprint, from 
design to recycling and  therefore contribute to a circular economy. When looking at legislation to 
achieve these goals, our industries believe that any proposal should be evaluated on the basis of its 
demonstrable and real improvements for  the environment. But the question is no t limited to materials 
and substances of very high concern and even not to hazardous materials and substances. Therefore, we 
strongly disagree with this proposal because this could mean a “full material declaration w e understand 
that Full Material Declarat ions are intended to become mandatory. Full Material Declarations are 
confidential business information and must not be made available to the general public and to (non EU) 
competitors. This information is not available for the OEM through the supply chain . IP plays a role as 
information is protected by supply. The protection of European Intellectual Property Rights regarding 
confidential business information on products is crucial for th e competitiveness of our European 
companies and must be guaranteed. For complex products it is very difficult to create a full list of 
materials and substances. Especially if we want data that are reliable and verified across international 
supply chains. For electronics for example such a list will be very long.  We question how such a long list 
would improve the sustainability of products. Level playing field should be guaranteed between EU and 
non-EU manufacturers . The workload and administrative burden of such a requirement would far 
outweigh the positive aspects. We should stick to existing concentration limit (RoHS, SCIP database) and 
not go beyond it. Another point regarding the quantities. Even processed food products don’t have to be 
labelled with a Full Material Declaration. Many quantities of ingredients are not disclosed. A 100% recipe 
is not revealed.  Material is a technical choice. Technical neutrality should be guaranteed. No relevant 
use for customers (apart SVHC).

can this be stru ctured? Very complex and with low benefit. For complex produc ts there are many 
components and very complex supply chains. This requirement would lead to meaningless information. 
For complex products, information should be provided only at product level, not at component level. 
Risk of IP loss and intentional disruptions of supply chain. This requirement must be aligned with existing 
trade/internal market rules. This is not an environmental criterion (beyond the transport /CO2 aspect)

─ “a. Economic actors at the origin of information”. We question if this is feasible in practice following the 
bad experience with ECHA SCIP database.  Risk of disclosure of th e supply chain to our competitors. Risk

─ “i. Information relevant for testing, disassembly, maintenanc e, repair or reassembly ”. Providing 
assembly and disassembly instructions is a common practice for many products but again it depends on 
the type of prodycts, the difference between B2B & B2C products, on who will receive this information 
and on the level of confidentiality (reference to standard EN 45559:20 19 - Methods for providing 
information relating to material efficiency aspects of energy-related products), possible IPR-issues. 
For consumer products which consumers can repair, basic information regarding disassembly, 
maintenance, repair or reassembly co uld be relevant and reasonable to be included in a product 
passport. More detailed in formation and test protocols could be relevant and accessible only for

professionals, operated by professionals and serviced and repaired by professiona ls - all of them 
requiring specialized training and tools. For many products or systems this information must kept in a 
close group of professionals due to safety reasons . An example are the control and stearing systems for 
elevators. The DPP should be sim ilar to the EPREL database where some information can only be 
accessed by the authorities and have a higher security.

o SMEs are not ready for this 
o Data is hard to compile and very costly. 
o There is no general method available to compare the data based on global standards. 
o International companies want to use international standards and not EU ones.

in our Position Paper. For a comprehensive assessment of the sustainability of products, a consideration

─ “p. Any possession of sustainability labels, such as the EU Ecolabel”. As not all products are in the 
scope of such labels and also as not all products do have such sustainability labels, we agree with this 
proposal only for products already having these labels. We disagree for products not yet having such 
sustainability labels. A recent research done by the French Government and presented during an EU 
workshop “Substantiating Green Claims” shows that in Europe there are already 400 labels, logos and 
procedures to identify the sustainability aspects of a product.  
These labels must be harmonized at EU level and based on standards and be the unique basis to be used 
by national labels/logos in order to prevent any hampering of the functioning of the EU internal market.  
As many consumers are overwhelmed by the variety of environmental and product labels, these labels 
must be product-specific, simple and comprehensible. The EU Ecolabel remains a good voluntary tool for 
stimulating a reduction in the environmental impact in the product’s life cycle. We advocate for 
maintaining EU Ecolabel as accessible tool for all companies, especial ly SMEs, and the prevention of the 
introduction of expensive preliminary studies like LCA in order to apply for the label. 
 
─ “q. Information on how the product should be recycled and /or handled at the end of life”. It is 
important to communicate the end of life information including the parts related to material efficiency. 
Our opinion on this proposed requirement again depends on the type of products, on the level of detail 
and also on how products are collected and sorted. We agree at the condition that the information on 
recycling and handling at the end of the products life is the one already requested under the existing 
WEEE Directive. If applicable due to WEEE or Ecodesign implementing measures, information should 
then be defined there or in associated standards. The I4R platform provides treatment and recycling 
facilities and preparation for re-use operators with access to WEEE recycling information in line with the 
requirements of WEEE Directive 2012/19/EU. Mandating the OEM to prescribe how to recycle could 
hamper innovation in the recycling space.

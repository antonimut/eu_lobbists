Cefic views on the future of the EU Ecodesign Directive 
This paper outlines Cefic’s proposed way forward with regards to the widening the scope of the EU 
Ecodesign Directive1. Cefic considers a coherent policy framework in which REACH and Ecodesign work 
together, as a key prerequisite for the success of the Sustainable Products Initiative (SPI). 
Cefic proposal in short: 
• Ecodesign should refer to the central processes foreseen in the overarching existing regulatory 
framework (e.g., REACH) to address the safety of chemicals in products. 
• Ecodesign may be used to ‘flag’ substances in products potentially impacting recycling, via the 
Ecodesign preparatory studies, for assessment by the existing expert committees under the 
REACH process (e.g., RAC and SEAC). 
• The decision-making process under Ecodesign has a limited involvement of interested 
stakeholders. As chemicals are included in the scope, the chemical industry would like to have a 
seat in the Ecodesign Consultation Forum. 
 
1) Current policy 
There are four major environmental policy tools on product policy: 
• Ecodesign2 Regulations (mandatory minimum requirements for a given product category) that 
may be accompanied by an Energy Label 
• Energy Label3 for the products that fulfil these minimum requirements, equally mandatory 
• Green Public Procurement4 (GPP) criteria  
• EU Ecolabel5 criteria. 
According to the objectives described in the SPI  Inception Impact Assessment6, the new initiative aims to 
rethink products to become sustainable. However, we note that a proper definition of sustainable in the 
context of the SPI is not yet available. Alignment with the ongoing developments to develop Safe and 
Sustainable-by-design criteria should be ensured, especially for product-specific regulations. 
SPI should be mindful that one -size-fits-all criteria for all products could be challenging. Sustainability 
requirements should consider each specific application/sector and leverage the existing produc t

2) Addressing chemicals under a coherent regulatory framework 
 
The existing EU regulatory framework  on chemicals is considered as the most advanced at global level . 
Ecodesign requirements are currently adopted to improve the energy efficiency of specific energy -using 
and energy-related product categories, while hazard properties, risk assessment, and risk management of 
chemical substances are covered by REACH7 (including assessing which substances need to be restricted or 
granted authorisation). 
 
2.1 While hazardous substances are also covered under the current Ecodesign Directive (Annex 1.3.d), a 
dedicated process should be included into the revision of the Ecodesign Directive to establish the link with 
REACH to avoid overlaps between different legislations . REACH should be the place of origin  for any 
proposed regulatory management measures of substances , to ensure policy coherence , a consistent 
implementation of EU law in the framework of product policy, the right of all interested parties to be heard, 
and the One substance, One assessment principle . Opening a nother policy instrument to regulate 
chemicals would create confusion and limit the predictability of the decision -making process. Therefore, 
Ecodesign should refer to the existing central restriction and authorisation procedures under chemical 
legislation (e.g., REACH) to address the safety of chemicals. Ecodesign should adapt it to the use conditions 
of the specific product category. 
2.2 In this context, Cefic suggests including a cross-reference between REACH and Ecodesign that provides 
a coherent way forward when addressing chemicals in product policy. This framework is meant to ensure 
relevant expertise in the decision-making process under REACH that takes into consideration not only the 
hazard and exposure assessment of a substance over its life cycle , but also recycling aspects (Ecodesign, 
Annex 1.3.f). 
In practice, should the Ecodesign preparatory studies find potential concerns over the safety of certain 
chemicals in a given product category , these concerns should be  addressed through REACH as the 
overarching chemicals legislation and transferred to ECHA via the Commission. ECHA will then consider the 
opinions from the existing committees of experts (e.g., RAC and SEAC, as applicable). In addition, in case a 
substance hampers mechanical recycling (of a product), alternative and more innovative technical solutions 
(e.g., chemical recycling technologies) could be considered. Cefic proposal aims to simplify the current 
regulatory framework for product policy and streamline the assessment of chemicals through a 
consolidated process. Therefore, duplication of work from other committees should be avoided. 
3) Ensuring representation in the process 
In line with the recently published Communication on Better Regulation8, the decision -making process 
under the Ecodesign product regulations should involve all the interested stakeholders. As chemicals are 
increasingly included in the scope, the chemical industry would like to have a seat in the Ecodesign and 
Energy Labelling Consultation Forum.

Annex to the SPI Questionnaire 
Considering the limited space to provide comments in the questionnaire, we opted for an answer in written 
form for the questions which require further clarification from our side. We remain at your disposal for any 
clarification you may need at any stage of the ongoing process on the Sustainable Products Initiative. 
 
1.A. To what extent do you agree that the following market-related statements explain why products 
sold in the EU are not more sustainable? 
Cefic considers the adoption of a life -cycle approach during the design and manufacturing process es, as 
well as during the consumer use phase, as an essential prerequisite in delivering solutions which serve their 
intended function, are e nvironmentally safe, cost-effective, and positively contribute to society over the 
entire lifecycle, from the design to the end-of-life phase. Life-cycle approaches (LCA) are multidimensional 
and need to include a balanced approach based on commonly recognized standard methods for circularity, 
resource-efficiency, energy use, water and land use and contribution to greenhouse gas emissions 
reduction. When assessment/accounting methods are to be used, preference should go for LCA that better 
reflect the carbon flow reality than Product Environmental Footprint (PEF).  
1.A.f. “Materials used in products are more and more complex and difficult to recycle” 
As indicated in the questionnaire, Cefic disagrees with this statement. First and foremost, the 
characteristics of the materials are designed to fulfil a function and contribute to the performance in the 
application they are used in. This can entail an increasing complexity.   
Manufacturers have already been taking action to overcome complexity of products, start ing from 
investments in R&D to advance design of products and in the development of new recycling processes and 
lower carbon footprint (e.g., recyclable monomaterial pouch solutions for packaging, technologies for 
store-drop recycling). In many cases, thes e processes require a significant amount of time for companies 
to be implemented, especially when the regulatory framework is subjected to changes. Adaptation to 
processes in terms of know-how and resources is a phase which needs to be considered during the decision-
making process.  
Complexity of materials, often linked to lack of recyclability, could further be overcome by adequate 
recycling infrastructure and technologies (e.g., chemical recycling technologies  and dissolution recycling 
technologies). Chemical recycling technologies need to be brought at scale urgently to increase recycling 
rates, supported by an enabling policy framework (Read more).  
Recyclability alone does not define sustainability of products. This concept should also reflect the benefits 
of products (e.g., durability, safety, reparability) – including those considered as complex – with a trade-off 
approach, identifying environmental, societal and economical value.  
 
1.B. To what extent do you agree that the following policy-related statements explain why products sold 
in the EU are not more sustainable? 
1.B.d. “Diverging national rules and lack of a harmonized set of EU rules discourage large businesses, which 
operate across various EU Member States, from offering more sustainable products”. 
 
4 
Cefic supports EU harmonized legislation to foster the free movement of goods and other policy objectives 
within the Single Market. In this contex t, secondary raw materials standards would help increase 
downstream customers and consumers’ confidence in recycled materials, as well as  mutual recognition 
(e.g., in the case of End of Waste) at EU level. This is fundamental in a circular economy, where materials 
manufactured for one Member State may end up in a different one when recycled and recovered after 
their end-of-life. The lack of harmonised rules could hamper circularity and sustainability of products. 
 
1.C. Other relevant market or policy -related challenges to making products more sustainable in the EU 
(please specify) and/or other comments you may have. 
In terms of global competition, it is worth noting that more sustainable products cannot compete with less 
expensive and unsustainable pr oducts imported from third countries, which are expected to deliver the 
same performance. The chemicals sector needs an access to bio -based raw materials at world market 
conditions and guarantee a security of supply. 
 
2.A. Design for sustainability - sustainability requirements for products. In your view, how effective 
would the following measures be in achieving these objectives? 
2.A.a. “Set binding rules detailing, at product group level, what actions producers are obliged to take to 
improve their product s’ durability, reusability, upgradability and reparability (for example, for 
electronic/ICT products, setting a minimum number of cycles during which the battery must function 
properly)” 
The chemical industry is of the opinion that binding rules would not necessarily fit for purpose in this case. 
In this context, alignment with ‘Safe and sustainable-by-design’ criteria that are under development should 
be strived for to ensure chemical innovations can enable downstream users to supply the market with more 
sustainable and circular products. The chemical industry is looking for an incentivising policy framework 
which ensures an improved environmental footprint and performance of products, while ensuring market 
flexibility and boosting innovation. 
2.A.l. “Ban t he use of a substance or substances in a given product, should such substances be found to 
inhibit product recyclability”  
As already included in the Ecodesign position paper and in line with the “One Substance, One Assessment” 
principle, Cefic considers t hat Ecodesign should follow the central processes foreseen in the overarching 
existing regulatory framework (e.g., REACH) to address the safety of chemicals in products. Therefore, we 
do not consider Ecodesign as the proper regulatory instrument to ban sub stances. Ecodesign can be used 
to screen substances in products to be further addressed under chemicals legislation, but the procedures 
used to restrict or prohibit substances should be those provided for under REACH. 
In case a substance hampers mechanical recycling, alternative and more innovative options (e.g., chemical 
recycling technologies) should be taken into account. As mentioned in the answer 1.A.f., complexity of 
recycling should be considered in light of the overall environmental footprint and function of a product, 
including its durability, contribution to sustainability and benefits in the consumer use phase.

2.B. Responsibility for information, including Digital Product Passport  
Digital Product Passport 
The Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability calls for more transparency in the value chains regarding 
substances of concern in products. Recyclers and waste managers need more information on chemical 
ingredients to ensure secondary raw materials are safe, which is crucial for boosting circular economy. 
Cefic members support this objective and are looking at practical ways to provide such information on a 
need-to-know basis, building on new digital technologies (e.g. blockchain) while preserving suppliers’ 
“know-how”. 
Cefic has identified a set of recommendations for the development of a digital product passport. 
1- Establish sectorial approaches for priority material streams based on potential health and/or 
environmental impact, and/or based on their relevance in contributing towards achieving circular 
economy targets, in dialogue with concerned parties. Consumers, business partners in the value 
chain, recyclers and authorities do not have the same data needs so data access should be 
modulated to deliver only the useful/needed data. 
2- Build on the experience from value chains (e.g., SCIP database discussions) and existing initiatives 
(e.g., ChemChain and Circularise). We consider that SCIP database should undergo an evaluation 
process before adding data sharing obligations on other hazardous substances. In fact, initial 
reporting from the value chain identifies the implementation of SCIP as very complex and 
challenging for article manufacturers, due to the different origin of the substances, many of 
which come from third-countries. 
3- Develop standardised requirements regarding the communication on relevant chemicals in 
products (see for instance the approach adopted by the Proactive Alliance of which Cefic is an 
active member). Communication on chemicals could include substances that are already 
regulated by the EU chemicals legislation (e.g., REACH). 
4- Develop a mechanism (or process) to protect confidential business information when passing on 
substance data along the value chain to meet the objective of the Circular Economy Action Plan 
and to increase recycling rates. 
To conclude, the chemical industry is committed to provide all the relevant information to ensure a safe 
and sustainable use of chemicals by consumers and workers in a circular economy value chain 
perspective and to achieve safe and high-quality recycled products.  
In addition, we are committed to engage actively in the process of testing available and new technologies 
to provide a smooth and efficient way of transferring information throughout the value chain. This 
procedure will have to ensure that confidential business information is protected. Discussions and 
alignment with the different levels of the value chain will be needed to ensure the right amount and type 
of information is made available.  
 
2.D. Circular business models 
The chemical industry considers the business models indicated in the questionnaire (e.g., product -service 
system, collaborative and sharing economy, reverse logistics, on -demand production) all positive efforts 
towards more sustainable production and consumption patterns, in principle. In addition, consumers and

public administrations would play a key role in the implementation of these models and therefore a certain

Platform-as-a-Service (PaaS) can be considered as another potential business model for several categories 
of products and services (e.g., automotive, smartphones, electronic households, solvents, catalysts,

A switch to sustainable biomass-based production of circular products could provide an alternative to more

criteria). 
 
2.E. Incentives for circularity. In your view, how important are the following measures? 
2.E.e. “Better use and promotion of voluntary sustainability labels, such as the EU Ecolabel” 
Cefic believes that voluntary schemes and sustainab ility labels would create a dynamic market for 
companies competing in a positive manner. Voluntary schemes leave room for companies to claim 
substantiation methods beyond the requirements of legal frameworks. EU Ecolabel is a significant 
component of the current environmental policy tools on product policy. 
 
3. Compliance, enforcement and enforcability of sustainability requirements for products  
Cefic supports a strong and coordinated enforcement policy under REACH and other product legislation to 
ensure that EU produced and imported substances/articles are subject to the same rules and level playing 
field is respected. Recent RAPEX data suggest that more than 80% of REACH non-compliant articles comes 
from outside the EU/EEA 5. This clearly shows that enforc ement on imports is still a weak point of EU 
chemical legislation. [1]  
Cefic supports the involvement of customs authorities with REACH enforcement activities to tackle 
potential cases of non -compliance. It is critical that relevant enforcement authoritie s have required 
resources to effectively deliver on their mandate. To that extent, the development of the Digital Product 
Passport can help market surveillance and efforts to ensure level playing field, complementing existing 
enforcing measures, by tracking the entry of imported products into the Internal Market. 
To be most effective, enforcement resources should be focused on the most critical market sectors (e.g., 
online sales or imports from countries that repeatedly raised alerts in the past) and  should make use of 
more advanced digital tools (for instance allowing each player in the supply chain to report suspected illegal 
trade, improving and harmonising the Single Window[2] tool for customs). 
Enforceability of regulations must be a key factor d uring the development of the regulatory process to 
ensure that proper resources for controls are available. This means that no restriction or ban should be 
adopted if the harmonised analytical test methods for detecting presence of substances in articles are not 
available.  ).

1) We do not agree to the following provisional definition for coercive practices by non-
EU countries (first question in the consultation survey). 
“Coercive practices by non-EU countries are measures which seek to, or could, coerce public 
authorities in the EU to take, not take, or withdraw, particular policy measures. These 
practices may include the use or threat of coercion, possibly in the form of trade or 
investment restrictions. The coercion may or may not be based on existing legislation, and 
can affect any field in which the EU or its Member States are active.” 
In their August 2015 article entitled “Coercive Trade Policy, ” Vincent Anesi and Giovanni 
Facchini stated that “[t]rade coercion occurs when a government (the “sender”) makes a 
demand backed by threats to use retaliatory sanctions against a trading partner (the 
“target”) if the latter does not acquiesce to this demand.”1  
We see under coercive practices primarily laws, regulations, orders, tariffs or sanctions 
with extraterritorial implications (“Coercive Practices”) that could harm businesses like 
Infineon that have worldwide operations and sales.  .  Because Infineon has established 
locations in many countries, we are bound to adhere to the respective relevant national 
laws.  
The consequence of these Coercive Practice to Infineon and other similarly situated 
companies is that we will have to “sit between the stools” and become victims of the 
enforcement of such Coercive Practices.  As global companies  are established in multiple 
countries with diverging jurisdictions under  retaliatory Coercive Practices, they are likely 
to become subject to penalties or sanctions or damage compensation (in case of suit by 
e.g. state owned enterprises) or impairment of their business. 
One example is the Final expanded US Foreign Direct Product Rule (“FPDP”) Rule 
restricting sales of foreign-made semiconductors produced or developed from certain US 
technology or software to listed entities (e.g. Huawei) and the responding MOFCOM Order 
No. 1 of 2021 on Rules on Counteracting Unjustified Extra-territorial Application of Foreign 
Legislation and Other Measures. The regulations in the MOFCOM order open the 
opportunity for Chinese citizens, legal persons or organizations in China to claim 
compensation in case they are prohibited or restricted by foreign legislation and other 
measures from engaging in normal economic, trade and related activities with a third 
State (or region) or its citizens, legal persons or other organizations.  In other words: If IFX 
holding legal entities in USA obeys to the US- FPDP Rule stopping sales and shipments to 
Huawei then under certain conditions set in this MOFCOM order it may be sued by Huawei 
in China (IFX also is established in China) for damage compensation.     
2) Contermeasures 
We believe this situation also cannot be remedied by European Union’s blocking statute 
(Council Regulation (EC) No 2271/96) protecting EU operators from the extra-territorial 
application of third country laws. On the contrary, an Anti-Coercion Instrument  (“ACI”) 
going in this direction would rather be detrimental for global acting enterprises.

We also believe that countermeasures should not be unilateral because of its perceived 
illegitimacy a unilateral coercion significantly reduces the likelihood of a target conceding 
in comparison to multilateral approach not bypassing the dispute settlement process of 
the international organizations. Such unilateral measures also could trigger a spiral of 
further measures and countermeasures and escalation.    
Further we believe that the EU is pursuing open and free markets and a multilateral, rules 
based approach will harm these goals and themselves if they counteract to these 
principles. 
In addition, have doubts on how countermeasures set unilaterally by EU could effectively 
be enforced in international law. 
Our wish is that countermeasures within ACI should deliver an effective response to 
contradicting / combatting laws/regulations/orders with extraterritorial scope (as outlined 
above – see example) and not to revert to traditional patterns and without violating WTO 
rules. 
ACI also should clearly be differentiated from existing Trade Defense Instruments (“TDI”). 
Measures/Countermeasures also should be in assessment and weighing of their possible 
harmful effect for European businesses in a globalized and interdependent economy. 
Further it has to be ensured that the introduction of measures/countermeasures within 
ACI only happen reactive/reciprocate and only after following a democratic procedure 
(e.g. European Parliament) including a consultation with stakeholders.

EU	Sustainable	Product	Initiative:	an	opportunity	to	accelerate	the	transition	to	a	circular	economy	June	2021			H&M	Group	is	a	firm	believer	of	the	EU	Green	Deal	and	the	green	recovery	as	the	only	responsible	way	out	of	the	crisis.	H&M	Group	is	committed	to	tackle	climate	change,	resource	depletion	and	biodiversity	loss.	One	of	the	levers	to	deliver	on	these	targets	is	to	become	100%	circular	in	all	our	business	operations.	Being	fully	circular	to	the	H&M	group	 means	we	 need	 to	 change	 how	 fashion	 is	 designed,	 produced	 and	 used.	 Fashion	 should	 never	 be	 seen	 as	waste,	but	instead,	should	be	treated	as	a	valuable	resource	by	reusing	and	recycling	again	and	again.	By	2030,	we	aim	for	100%	of	our	materials	to	be	either	recycled	or	sourced	in	a	more	sustainable	way,	including	delivering	30%	recycled	content	in	materials	used	by	2025.	In	addition,	we	prioritise	prolonging	the	life	of	garments	by	keeping	pre-loved	garments	in	use	for	as	long	as	possible	through	investing	in	new	circular	business	models.			As	 a	 Strategic	 Partner	 of	 the	 Ellen	 MacArthur	 Foundation,	 H&M	 is	 working	 together	 with	 other	 companies,	governments	and	cities,	academic	institutions,	emerging	innovators,	small	and	medium	sized	enterprises,	and	more	to	accelerate	the	transition	to	a	circular	economy.	Through	the	Make	Fashion	Circular	initiative,	we	work	closely	with	the	Ellen	MacArthur	Foundation	to	identify	opportunities	to	create	value	through	circular	practices,	and	to	develop	and	implement	targeted	programmes	across	our	activities.			H&M	Group	welcomes	the	EU	Sustainable	Product	Initiative	as	a	milestone	towards	an	EU	circular	economy.	To	achieve	a	full	circular	ecosystem,	we	call	on	the	EU	Commission	to	complement	this	framework	beyond	product	policy	and	business	models	with	legal	and	non-legal	policy	incentives	to	ensure	a	systemic	change.	These	policies	should	support	the	economy	transformation	across	the	board,	including	future	of	work	through	up	and	re-skilling,	define	collected	textiles	as	resources,	the	need	for	new	technologies	and	infrastructures.		
					
Executive	Summary			H&M	 Group	 welcomes	 the	 EU	 Sustainable	 Product	 Initiative	 (SPI)	 as	 a	 milestone	 towards	 an	 EU	 circular	economy.	To	achieve	a	full	circular	ecosystem,	we	call	on	the	EU	Commission	to	complement	this	framework	beyond	 product	 and	 business	 models	 with	legislative	incentives	and	policy	initiatives	to	 ensure	 a	 systemic	change	in	resource	approach,	innovation,	infrastructures,	as	well	as	Pact	for	skills.			The	future	product	requirements	covered	by	the	EU	SPI	should	drive	and	enable	circularity	by:	- Applying	four	core	principles	along	their	development	and	implementation:	create	a	positive	systemic	change,	focus	on	circularity,	enable	a	flexible	framework	and	coherence	with	other	EU	policies.		- Establishing	 the	 EU	 SPI	 as	 the	 umbrella	 framework	 legislation	 and	in	 addition	 develop	sectoral	approaches.	In	practice,	the	EU	SPI	would	cover	the	EU	Eco-design	directive	for	energy	related	products	and	a	sister	legislation	for	the	new	sectors	foreseen	under	EU	SPI.	- Extending	the	scope	of	the	EU	SPI	to	circular	design,	for	both	products	and	materials,	while	addressing	durability,	recyclability	and	design	for	purpose	principle.	Common	definitions	are	a	corner	stone	of	an	impactful	common	framework,	together	with	a	common	measurement	approach.	- Supporting	 and	 incentivising	 circular	 business	 models.	While	 the	 EU	 SPI	 cannot	 and	 should	 not	regulate	 business	 models	 directly,	 the	 implementing	 measures	 can	 define	 requirements	 and	benchmarks	and	incentivise	their	uptake.		- At	its	core,	the	Digital	Product	Passport	shall	foster	a	true	shift	to	circular	economy,	and	concentrate	on	gathering	data	enhancing	reuse,	sorting,	recycling	and	new	business	models.		Whilst	we	fully	recognise	the	importance	of	tackling	social	challenges	in	our	value	chain,	we	recommend	the	EU	Commission	to	continue	addressing	these	issues	at	a	value	chain	and	company	level	through	the	upcoming	EU	due	diligence	mandatory	legislative	framework.

Essential	principles	for	an	impactful	Sustainable	Product	Initiative	(SPI)		We	identify	four	core	principles	to	create	long-lasting	impacts	at	product	level.			1. Target:	create	positive	impacts	and	systemic	change		While	developing	the	various	policies	listed	in	the	EU	SPI,	the	EU	Green	Deal’s	overall	goal	should	not	be	lost.	These	policies	must	support	the	European	Union	to	achieve	climate	neutrality	and	contribute	to	a	fair	and	just	transition	to	a	circular	economy.			Hence,	we	call	on	the	EU	Commission	to	keep	as	primary	objective	of	the	EU	SPI	to	support	economic	actors	to	decouple	economic	growth	from	resource	use.		2. Focus	on	circularity		Recirculation	and	circular	design	requirements	for	both	products	and	materials	should	be	at	the	centre	of	the	EU	SPI.	Encompassing	materials	and	products	will	define	a	true	close	loop	system	from	the	very	beginning	of	the	design	stage.	The	transition	from	a	linear	to	a	circular	model,	will	require	that	all	policies	are	reoriented	towards	prolonging	product	life	and	recirculation	of	resources	in	a	safe	and	transparent	manner.	A	circular	and	sustainable	textile	and	fashion	model	must	be	based	on	two	pillars:	1)	Circular	design	requirements	to	make	products	more	durable	 and	 recyclable	 according	 to	 product	 purpose,	 that	 design	 out	 waste	 and	 pollution,	 keep	 products	 and	materials	in	use,	and	regenerate	natural	systems	and;	2)	A	functioning	secondary	raw	materials	market	ensuring	post-consumer	 products’	 value	 is	 retained	 through	 prolonging	 product	 use	 and	 recycled	back	 into	 new	 quality	products	at	end	of	life.			Product	and	 material	design	 requirements	shall	 embed	 both	recyclability	and	durability.	 Rewarding	durability	and	recyclability	will	incentivise	producers	to	further	invest	in	business	models	that	keep	pieces	in	use	for	 as	 long	 as	 possible,	and	improve	 the	 product	 design	 to	 increase	its	durability,	longevity	 and	 recyclability	requirements.			Whilst	we	fully	recognise	the	importance	of	tackling	social	challenges	in	our	value	chain,	we	recommend	the	EU	Commission	 to	continue	 addressing	 the	 social	 impacts	 at	 a	 value	 chain	 and	company	 level	 through	 the	upcoming	 EU	 due	 diligence	 mandatory	 legislative	 framework.		It	 should	 be	 aligned	 with	 the	 UN	 Guiding	Principles	 on	 Business	 and	 Human	 Rights	 (UNGPs)	 and	 the	 OECD	 guidelines. H&M	 Group	 supports	a horizontal framework that	 can	 be applicable	 to all companies	 of	 various	 size	 and	sectors that can include both human rights	and environment in	its scope.			3. A	framework	allowing	some	flexibility	for	future	innovation					Circularity	 innovations	 are	 speeding	 up:	 the	 consultation	 and	impact	 assessment	 should	think	 beyond	what	 is	possible	today	and	what	will	be	feasible	in	a	few	years	already.	To	remain	relevant	in	the	future,	the	legal	framework	shall	recognise	the	very	dynamic	context	in	which	the	industry	is	evolving.	This	can	only	be	done	by	allowing	some	flexibility	for	innovation	related	to	information	carriers	(Digital	Product	Passport),	unknown	new	business	models	and	new	materials.			Flexibility,	through	vertical	legislation,	will	also	be	required	to	recognise	the	specificities	of	the	various	sectors	regarding	design	requirements,	information	requirements	related	to	repairability,	etc.			4. Coherence	with	other	EU	&	international	initiatives	under	development		The	 EU	 Green	 Deal	is	providing	a	 fantastic	opportunity	 to	 drive	 systemic	 change,	 should	 the	 coherence	between	all	the	initiatives	be	guaranteed:		- EU	common	 definitions	on	 circularity	 principles	to	 be	 applied	in	 product	related	 initiatives:	the	 EU	 SPI,	Product	Environmental	Footprint	methodology	and	EPR	eco-modulation	definitions,			- One	 clear	 and	 consolidated	 framework	 addressing	customer	 information,	 with	 the	 Green	 Claims	 and	Empowering	consumers	initiatives,		- Alignment	 with	 legislation	 on	 end-of-life,	 in	 addition	 to	 EPR	 schemes:	end-of-waste	 criteria	 and	 waste	shipment	rules.

Ensure	the	need	for	sectoral	legislation	to	drive	circularity	further			We	recommend	establishing	the	EU	SPI	as	the	umbrella	framework	legislation	defining	the	overall	circular	design	 requirements	 while	 allowing	 sectoral	 approaches.		This	 common	 umbrella	 will	 allow	 a	 common	approach	 across	 the	 industries	 (setting	 horizonal	 sustainability	 principles)	 while	 ensuring	 sector	 specificities	 to	remain	 at	 core	 and	 so	accelerate	circularity	further.	 In	 practice,	the	 EU	 SPI	 would	 cover	 the	 EU	 Eco-design	directive	for	energy	related	products	and	adopt	a	sister	legislation	for	the	new	sectors	foreseen	under	EU	SPI.		In	addition	to	creating	the	necessary	level	playing	field,	we	identify	several	success	factors	in	developing	sectoral	legislation:		- Align	 and	 build	 on	existing	 sectoral	 legislation	 and	 methodologies:	Given	 that	 in	 many	 cases	 some	sectoral	legislation	or	assessment	methods	already	exist,	EU	SPI	product	requirements	should	build	and	align	 with	 those.	 This	 could	 save	 time	 in	 getting	 design	 requirements	 in	 place.	 For	 instance,	 the	development	of	the	Apparel	and	Footwear	Product	Environmental	Footprint	methodology	could	be	used	as	a	key	method	to	assess	the	environmental	footprint	of	textile	products.	However,	the	PEF	method	as	it	stands	today	does	not	sufficiently	consider	circularity	and	reuse	in	the	end-of-life	phase.	This	must	be	kept	in	mind	when	the	PEF	methodology	is	finalised	in	the	next	year.		- Develop	sector	and	product	specific	requirements:	In	the	same	manner	as	the	Eco-Design	Directive,	it	will	 be	 key	 that	 also	 the	 requirements	 developed	 for	 the	 extended	 scope	 will	 develop	 product	 specific	requirements.	This	will	only	give	the	right	granularity	required	to	drive	the	change	needed.		- Set	up	individual	sectoral	circular	design	workplans:	Ahead	of	developing	the	implementing	measures,	it	will	be	necessary	to	set	up	product	work	plans	for	all	the	individual	sectors	falling	under	the	scope	of	the	EU	SPI.	e.g.	one	for	apparel,	textile	and	footwear	products,	one	individual	for	other	sectors	covered	etc.	This	should	be	more	efficient	as	the	sectors	under	discussion	for	the	scope	of	the	EU	SPI	have	very	different	characteristics	and	specificities.		- Involve	 the	 appropriate	 set	 of	 stakeholders	 when	 implementing	measures	 and	 product	requirements	are	developed:	e.g.	as	eco-design	will	move	to	become	more	focused	on	circular	design,	it	is	key	to	ensure	that	the	whole	value	chain	relevant	for	circular	design	is	represented.	For	instance,	not	only	 brands	 and	 manufacturers	 should	 be	 represented,	 but	 also	the	 expertise	 and	 input	 from	 all	 re-circulators	of	products	and	materials	(i.e.,	repair,	resell,	rental	actors	etc)	will	be	essential.		- Appoint	 the	 appropriate	 Commission	 services	 to	 implement	 the	 EU	 SPI:	Ensure	 that	 future	 EU	 SPI	requirements	for	non-energy	related	products	are	lead	and	coordinated	by	those	DG’s	with	the	appropriate	expertise	on	the	new	product	categories	to	be	regulated	i.e.	DG	GROW	and	DG	ENVI.			Extend	the	legislative	scope	from	eco-design	to	circular	design			The	new	EU	design	framework	should	drive	the	transition	to	circular	design	through	its	legal	structure,	principles,	and	definitions.	By	integrating	the	following	recommendations,	we	believe	the	EU	legislation	could	accelerate	the	transformation	beyond	eco-design	to	circular	design,	for	both	products	and	materials.			Key	principles	for	success:			To	fully	capture	the	industry	circular	economy	potential,	the	EU	SPI	should	include	all	the	life-cycle	stages	listed	in	Annex	1	(Part	1.	1.1.)	of	the	Eco-Design	Directive,	together	with	recognising	crucial	stages	for	circular	design:		reuse	(under	point	e)	and	recycling/reuse	of	materials	for	new	manufacturing	(under	point	f).	The	EU	SPI	must	better	consider	the	importance	of	material	impacts	through	the	full	life	cycle	to	enable	sustainable	product	design	(e.g.	through	using	renewable	energy,	regenerative	practises	in	material	production).			The	‘design	for	purpose	principle’	should	be	integrated	as	an	overarching	principle	in	the	EU	SPI,	supporting	companies	 to	 implement	 circular	 design.		The	 intended	 product	 purpose	 will	 influence	 both	 the	 choice	 of	materials	 and	 the	 design	 strategies.	 Designing	 for	 intended	 product	 purpose	 will	 be	 influenced	 by	 how	 long	 the	product	 will	 be	 relevant	 for,	 how	 often	 the	 product	 will	 be	 used,	 how	 much	 wear	 and	 tear	 it	 is	 exposed	 to	 and	depending	 on	 the	 product	 access	 models.	H&M	 Group	 is	 currently	 developing	 a	 Circular	 Product	 Development	Guideline,	a	tool	that	will	support	our	teams	to	take	more	circular	design	decisions	according	to	intended	product

purpose.		We	are	rigorously	testing	how	to	work	according	to	this	principle,	for	example	Monki	has	developed	their	circular	capsule	collection1. 	The	EU	SPI	should	drive	companies	to	make	safe	chemical	and	material	choices	compatible	with	the	circular	economy,	enabling	recirculation	of	materials	without	creating	hazardous	legacies.	Tackling	the	presence	of	chemical	legacy	and	hazardous	chemicals	in	recycled	materials,	will	be	a	key	part	of	realising	circularity.	In	line	with	the	EU	Chemical	Strategy	for	Sustainability,	H&M	Group	has	a	vision	of	working	towards	toxic-free	fashion	by	2030.	H&M	Group	believes	the	EU	needs	to	take	a	stepwise	approach	to	tackle	the	issue	of	chemical	legacy:	Firstly,	ban	the	use	of	hazardous	chemicals	in	virgin	materials.	Secondly,	ban	their	presence	in	recycled	materials.	This	would	enable	recycling	today	while	steering	towards	safer	chemicals.	For	virgin	materials,	H&M	Group	advocates2	to	establish	an	acknowledged	and	harmonised	hazard	assessment	methodology	for	chemicals,	ensuring	that	brands	can	assess	what	the	best	available	chemicals	are	from	a	safety	and	recyclability	perspective.	Any	chemicals	hampering	recycling	and	material	recovery	should	be	restricted.			Definitions	and	measurement:		Common	 definitions	 are	 a	 corner	 stone	 of	 an	impactful	 common	 framework,	together	with	a	common	measurement	 approach.	The	EU	 SPI	 should	 define	key	 design	 principles	–	e.g.		durability,	 repairability	 and	recyclability.	We	recommend	integrating	the	definitions	developed	by	Ellen	MacArthur	Foundation,	such	as	their	definitions	of		- Durability:	 the	 ability	 of	 a	 product	 to	 remain	 functional	 and	 relevant	 when	 faced	 with	 the	 challenges	 of	normal	operation.	It	often	applies	to	the	physical	attributes	of	a	product	(its	ability	to	resist	damage	and	wear),	though	with	some	products	durability	can	be	technological	(for	example	the	ability	of	software	to	be	upgraded	many	times),	and	in	others	it	can	be	emotional	(for	example,	the	ability	of	certain	clothes	to	stay	relevant	and	desirable),	- Repairability:	The	ease	with	which	a	product	can	be	repaired,	- Recyclability:	The	ease	with	which	a	product	or	material	can	be	recycled	in	practice	and	at	scale,	- Circular	 products:	In	 a	 circular	 economy	 for	 fashion,	 products	 (apparel,	 footwear,	accessories)	 are	 used	more,	made	to	be	made	again,	made	from	safe	and	recycled	or	renewable	inputs.		Anchoring	definitions	of	key	terms	in	the	legal	text	will	have	dual	benefits:	fostering	a	harmonised	understanding	and	application	of	circular	economy	concepts	across	the	industry,	as	well	as	enable	policy	alignment	with	legislation	addressing		- the	product	at	end-of-life.	Basing	future	eco-modulation	fees	under	EPR	rules	on	the	EU	SPI	definitions	will	be	a	key	for	policy	alignment.		- the	product	environmental	impacts.	The	same	concepts	are	currently	defined	with	different	approaches	through	the	Product	Environmental	Footprint	–	and	so	the	upcoming	Green	Claims	legislation.			Common	sound	metrics	and	measurements,	to	assess	circular	economy	improvement	potential	at	product	level,	could	be	set	on	the	basis	circular	design	requirements	and	definitions.	Once	the	circularity	improvement	potential	 for	 a	 specific	 product	 category	 or	 value	 chain	 has	 been	 identified,	 it	 is	 important	 to	 substantiate	 the	expected	environmental	benefits	 linked	 to	 the	 proposed	 implementing	 measures.	 In	 this	 context,	 the	 European	Commission	refers	to	the	role	of	Life	Cycle	Assessment	(LCA)	and	the	EU	Product	Environmental	Footprint	(PEF)	methodology.	LCA,	in	general,	and	PEF	in	particular,	have	been	developed	as	tools	for	evaluating	the	environmental	impacts	of	a	specific	product	through	all	the	defined	stages	of	its	lifetime.	However,	instead	of	calculating	LCA	results	or	PEF	scores	for	a	specific	product’s	use	in	today’s	linear	system,	the	EU	SPI	needs	to	envisage	how	products	will	be	used	differently	in	a	circular	economy.	It	is	important	to	note	that	the	EU	PEF	methodology	should	reflect	the	transition	to	circular	economy	by	integrating	the	multiplication	of	uses	of	one	product	in	the	end-of-life	stage,	before	being	considered	as	the	right	method.								  1 https://www.monki.com/we-are-monki/planet-power/about-planet-power/projects/wearloverecycle/		 2	https://chemsec.org/hm-groups-position-on-safe-by-design-chemicals-definition-to-drive-circular-economy/

Incentivise	circular	business	models	to	prolong	product	life	and	material	use			In	the	case	of	the	fashion	industry,	customers’	need	for	self-identification	through	fashion	and	clothes	will	likely	continue	in	the	future.	Circular	business	models	constitute	a	solution	to	provide	continued	access	to	fashion	for	customers,	whilst	staying	within	planetary	boundaries.			Upcoming	 European	 policies	 should	 support	 companies	 to	 decouple	 growth	 from	 virgin	 resource	 use	 by	accelerating	 the	 circular	 business	 models’	 market	 penetration	 and	 by	 providing	 a	 clear	 framework.	The	uptake	of	circular	business	models	will	deliver	on	EU	Green	Deal	targets:	reduced	climate	impact,	lower	resource	depletion,	and	reduced	pressures	on	biodiversity	loss.	H&M	Group	is	internally	assessing	the	CO2	reduction	benefit	of	circular	business	models	and	their	contribution	to	the	Group’s	2040	climate	positive	goal.	We	see	differences	in	climate	benefits	per	business	model,	hence	we	will	focus	on	those	with	the	largest	positive	climate	benefit.			By	2030,	H&M	Group	sees	a	great	opportunity	for	increasing	the	share	of	circular	business	models	in	the	fashion	and	 clothing	 industry.	 This	 transition	needs	 to	be	 supported	 by	 the	 full	 ecosystem,	 including	 governments	 and	industry. - Increase	consumers’	convenience	in	accessing	these	new	models,	- Adjust	 companies’	 internal	 accounting	 rules	 and	bookkeeping	systems	 to	 recognise	 and	 account	 for	products’	second	(and	third)	life,	either	if	they	are	donated,	refurbished	(e.g.	write-offs)	and	resold. 	The	EU	SPI	should	support	and	incentivise	circular	business	models,	as	well	as	enabling	traditional	linear	companies	to	become	circular.	Circular	business	models	will	require	a	new	business	relationship	dynamic,	based	on	a	 strong	 collaboration	between	existing	 brands	 and	 emerging	actors	working	 with	 recirculation	 across	 the	ecosystem.	While	 the	EU	 SPI	cannot	 and	 should	 not	 regulate	 business	 models	 directly,	the	 implementing	measures	can	define	requirements	and	benchmarks.	We	expect	an	enormous	amount	of	innovation	to	happen	in	the	coming	years.	Any	new	regulation	should	not	limit	new	models	from	being	introduced.	Hence,	we	recommend	incentivizing	their	uptake,	but	only	regulating	where	there	is	strictly	a	need.	We	call	for:	- Supporting	a	new	business	approach:		o Enhance	business	collaboration	under	the	EU	resilience	and	recovery	approach	and	the	EU	textile	strategy,		o Develop	common	performance	 indicators	 for	circular	business	models	or	 a	 scoring	 system	 for	their	promotion	beyond	sales,	o Develop	an	industry	system	to	structure	product	data	as	a	key	enabler	of	circular	business	models.	- Review	tax	and	financial	incentives:			o Adopt	 lower	 VAT	 for	circular	business	models.	 Ensure	 that	 beneficial	 VAT	 systems	 or	 other	economic	incentives	for	circular	models	are	combinable	with	traditional	linear	models,	to	foster	companies’	shift	from	linear	to	circular	business,	o Reform	 current	 tax	 system	to	 integrate	traditional	 retail	into	new	circular	 business	models	e.g.	retailers/brands	are	currently	required	to	pay	tax	twice	when	engaging	in	recommence	models,	breaking	the	profitability	of	new	models,	o Clarify	and	harmonise	rules	on	tax-deduction	related	to	write-offs	and	donations, o Provide	 financial	 support	 to	 SMEs,	 starts-up	 and	 others	 developing	 circular	 business	 models	 to	achieve	industry	scalability.		- Foster	the	needed	feedstock	to	scale	up	circular	business	models:	o Develop	collection	infrastructure,	sorting	and	recycling	hubs	for	textiles	in	all	EU	regions,	o Establish	an	 effective	 market	 for	 secondary	 products	through	future	EPR	schemes	 prioritising	reuse	over	recycling	where	possible,	o Define	collected	textiles	as	a	resource	through	setting	end-of-waste	criteria	at	both	product	and	fibre	level.	This	will	allow	collected	textiles	to	be	easily	movable	across	borders,	and	so	facilitate	rental	and	recommence	models,	o Allow	companies	the	flexibility	to	implement	the	‘right	to	repair’	in	their	service	offer,	e.g.	through	offering	repair	networks	to	customers.			H&M	 Group	 welcomes	 the	 ban	 on	 destruction	 of	 unsold	 goods	 and	 supports	 its	 implementation	immediately.	Incineration	of	goods	should	only	be	limited	to	those	few	garments	not	suitable	for	recycling,	e.g.	due	to	health	and	safety	risks	and	for	which	disposal	should	be	conducted	through	incineration.	Textiles	should	never	be	 sent	to	 landfill.	Circular	 business	 models	 will	 give	 a	 second	 life	(and	many	 more)	to	 these	 unsold	sustainable	goods	through	reuse,	remake,	refurbishment,	etc.				Digital	Product	Passport:	Enable	circularity	first			H&M	 Group	 has	 identified	 a	 business	 and	 use	 case	 for	 a	 standard	 data	 gathering	 protocol	 to	 achieve	product	circularity:	At	 its	 core,	 the	 Digital	 Product	 Passport	 shall	 foster	 a	 true	 shift	 to	 circular	 economy,	 and	concentrate	on	gathering	data	enhancing	reuse,	sorting,	recycling	and	new	business	models.		The	 following	 principles	 should	 guide	 the	 EU	 Commission	 when	 developing	 the	 Digital	 Product	 Passport	governance	and	structure:		- Core	principles:		o Digital	 product	 passports	 should	 be	scalable	 and	comprehensive	 enough	for	 all	 economic	operators	to	be	able	to	use	them.	Hence	the	EU	Commission	should	prioritise	data	collection	and	disclosure	on	a	‘need-to-know’	basis	for	those	actors	that	are	dependent	on	it.		o The	relevance	and	reliability	of	the	information	included	in	the	passport	should	be	a	corner	stone	of	 the	 digital	 passport	 approach.	 The	 verification	 principle	 should	 not	 lead	 to	 additional	
	 7	
audits,	 nor	 increase	 the	 audit	 fatigue	that	already	is	very	 high	 in	 the	 textile	 industry.	 Addition	audits	will	only	diverge	resources	from	capacity	building.			o Building	trust	for	all	actors	of	the	value	chain	around	the	digital	product	passport	framework	will	be	a	parameter	to	its	successful	implementation.	Special	care	should	be	given	to	data	storage	and	data	ownership	aspects.		- Target	audience:	The	digital	passport	should	support	economic	operators	acting	in	the	circular	economy	(e.g.	re-users,	sorters,	recyclers,	etc),	as	well	as	surveillance	authorities.	Consumer	communication	could	be	 enhanced	 by	 digital	 product	 passport	 but	 should	 not	 the	prioritised	end	 goal.	Clear	 customer	communication	should	be	realised	through	the	green	claims	and	empowering	consumers	legislation.		- Step	wise	implementation:	We	support	a	two-phase	approach	with,	first,	a	voluntary	scheme	to	define	best	 practices	 and	 test	 potential	 solutions;	then	 mandatory	 implementation	 to	 support	 the	 industry	circularity	journey.		- Structure:		o Digital	product	passport	should	be	based	on	an	open	standard.	o Ensure	interoperability	with	existing	databases,	i.e.	the	SCIP	database	and	the	Ask	REACH	app.	Companies	should	not	be	required	to	enter	information	twice:	there	should	be	a	system	to	link	the	digital	product	passport	to	information	submitted	through	other	portals/databases.		o In	 principle,	 H&M	 Group	 would	 support	 a	decentralised	 system,	 but	further	 investigation	 is	needed.	A	decentralised	system	shall	define	common	quality	criteria	to	secure	a	high	level	of	trust	in	the	data	collect	in	the	satellites	databases.		o The	structure	should	ensure	data	confidentiality	-	e.g.	for	example	making	sure	that	information	is	only	available	to	those	entitled	to	access	it.	- Data	enabling	product	and	material	recirculation:	o Priority	should	be	given	to	data	necessary	to	sorters,	recyclers,	resellers	and	re-users,	keeping	in	mind	the	scalability	and	comprehensive	principle.		o A	dynamic	approach	would	be	welcome	to	capture	alterations	and	processes	applied	to	the	initial	product	 throughout	 its	 multiple	 uses–	e.g.	resell	 business	 with	 some	 changes	 on	 the	 garments	(shorten	 sleeves,	 etc).	It	 will	 inform	brands	 and	 retailers’	 strategy	 on	 circular	 product	 design,	moving	currently	from	assumption	on	product	longevity	and	usage	to	primary	data.		- Technology	flexibility:	The	EU	framework	should	not	be	prescriptive	regarding	the	type	of	carriers	for	the	 information	 exchange	 between	 economic	 actors	 (e.g.	 QR	 code,	 watermarks,	 etc)	 to	 allow	 upcoming	innovations	to	be	integrated	as	smoothly	as	possible.	- Build	 upon	existing	industry	 and	 international	 institution	 initiatives	to	 complement	 them	 and	create	 added	 value.	The	 EU	 Digital	 Product	 Passport	 should	 not	 create	competition	 with	 existing	standards.	 While	 developing	 the	initiative,	 the	 EU	 Commission	 should	 engage	 within	 standard	organisations,	 international	 authorities	 and	 industry	 experts	-	e.g.	 GS1	 standard,	 UNECE	 traceability	project11,	International	 Trade	 Centre	 (ITC),	ISO	 upcoming	 working	 group,	 industry	 efforts	 to	 create	common	ID	for	suppliers	such	as	Open	Apparel	Registry12.

Comments on Sustainable Products Initiative 
It is crucial that the sustainable product policy legislative initiative will provide a stable 
framework. At the same time, it must uphold the basic principle of technology neutral-
ity to ensure a variety of technology options applicable to sustainable design require-
ments and choices related to material efficiency. 
 
Digital product passport must bring real added value  
 
The Commission has brought forward a proposal on digital product passports that ena-
ble tracking and tracing of products and contain information on origin, composition in-
cluding the presence of substances of concern, critical raw materials content, recycled 
material content, possibilities or instructions for re-use, repair, and collection upon dis-
carding, dismantling and handling as waste, among others.  
 
We support the idea of introducing a product passport for products produced within 
and imported to the EU. It must be carefully considered what kind of information is 
needed in the product passport, however. Company sensitive information must be con-
fidential by default, the information in general bring real added value and there cannot 
be a standard product passport for all products. The information in the value chain 
needs to be adopted sector by sector or product by product and need to be based on a 
true need. Introducing a digital product passport should not bring excessive financial 
burden on the producers.  
 
A digital product passport can be beneficial if it does not become a bureaucratic burden 
for manufacturers. It has the potential to facilitate communication across value chains, 
to support market surveillance, inform users (business to business communication) and 
enable the consumer to make more informed choices.  However, to ensure a proper 
analysis what information and how the digital product passport will be realised, there 
must be sufficient transition time to implement the plan.

Lifecycle approach of products 
 
The environmental performance and the resources used across the full lifecycle of a 
product (the supply chain, its’ use and the end-of-life) is important to understand. All 
products have an impact on the environment. What these impacts are, at what stage of 
the life cycle of the product they occur, how these impacts are measured and opportu-
nities to reduce them should indeed guide the product design. Understanding the im-
pact of products from the cradle to grave will be essential to ensure that more sustaina-
ble alternatives become the norm. 
 
Regarding the widening of the scope of the old Ecodesign directive the following princi-
ples should be considered: 
• Specific – requirements must be considered on a product group-specific basis. Even 
within the same product group and within individual categories of equipment in our 
sector, the products and their environmental impact differ significantly, especially de-
pending on ambient and operating conditions. 
• Measurability – the parameters must be clear to determine, and measurement meth-
ods must be accurately defined. 
• Enforceability – it must be possible to verify and enforce requirements through mar-
ket surveillance. 
• Relevance – new parameters and corresponding requirements must be relevant for 
the environment, the users and relevant even within specific life cycle phase(s). There 
must be evidence of clear and significant potential for improvement. 
• Competitiveness – there must be no significant negative impact on the industry’s 
competitiveness and the competition must be fair.

When it comes to defining the environmental impact through for example a textile 
product’s life cycle, it is not easy or simple to carry out. In general, it is difficult to esti-
mate the life cycle of a textile product as it strongly depends on its user(s) how long or 
short it is. Also, the way a textile product is repaired affects its total life cycle. Concern-
ing the sustainability requirements, they differ from one textile material to another. 
Textile products are manufactured for very different needs and de facto cover a variety 
of product groups ranging from consumer textiles to textiles made for health care sec-
tor. The planned Ecodesign criteria therefore cannot be the same for the different tex-
tile product groups.  
 
Level playing field between EU products and those that will be imported from third 
countries  
 
Level playing field between EU products and those that will be imported from third 
countries needs to be maintained and reinforced, while respecting the EU’s interna-
tional commitments both at multilateral and bilateral levels. For instance, imposing de-
sign or remanufacture requirements, increased durability, fulfilment of extended pro-
duce responsibility obligations when applied to the product etc. must be required to all 
products entering the EU through an effective customs control and market surveillance. 
Ensuring effective enforcement and market surveillance is important for the success of 
the application of the new sustainable product framework. 
 
Green Public Procurement  
 
Mandatory green public procurement is certainly a powerful tool to boost the demand 
for sustainable products, provided that the requirements are feasible to implement, to 
measure and to control. In addition, public procurement should be based on life cycle 
costing to incentivise innovative technology uptake. To fully tap into the purchasing 
power of public authorities, we call upon the Member States to make more enforce-
ment efforts at the end of the green public procurement process to secure a level play-
ing field. 
 
Product requirements and design rules 
 
As set out in the Circular Economy Action Plan, the Commission intends to set product 
design rules and general product requirements to foster the overall sustainability of 
products in the EU. Among other aspects, these rules should cover: improving product 
durability, reusability, upgradability and reparability, addressing the presence of hazard-
ous chemicals in products, and increasing their energy and resource efficiency; increas-
ing recycled content in products, while ensuring their performance and safety; enabling 
remanufacturing and high-quality recycling; reducing carbon and environmental foot-
prints; restricting single-use and countering premature obsolescence. Product require-
ments must be based on applicable internal market regulations, to ensure a level

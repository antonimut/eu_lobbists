Unilever ’s  Position on the Sustainable Products Initiative  
 
Unilever believes that pivoting to a new , low-carbon, circular economy is the way forward 
to thrive within the limits of the planet. Driving systemic change, while making products fit 
for a climate neutral, resource efficient and circular economy , is at the heart of our mission 
to ‘’Make Sustaina ble Living Common Place’’ for the 2.5 billion consumers we serve every 
day in the European Union and across the globe.  
As a company, we are committed to using our technology and scale to drive this agenda 
and support the ambitions of the Sustainable Produ cts Initiative (SPI). We believe that 
information, comparability, and transparency about carbon footprint will be an accelerator 
in the global race to zero emissions, and it is our ambition to communicate and minimise 
the carbon footprint of every product we sell. 
A holistic approach towards sustainability, which includes environmental and economic 
aspects, should be taken into consideration, as well as the acceleration of future-fit 
solutions through digitalisation. The SPI will be an  important pillar of EU policy, leading to 
more sustainable production and consumption , along with the proposals to tackle 
greenwashing and substantiate green claims. We believe the SPI should build on existing 
policy instruments, as well as existing industry initiatives, while setting up overarching 
principles, frameworks and definitions to deliver on the circularity potential of products, in 
particular plastics and chemicals.  
Our contribution focuses on t wo key Unilever priorities: 1/the circularity of chemi cals, and 
2/sustainable packaging (based on Unilever’s approach of “no, less and better plastic”). 
 
1. Tackling the ‘ invisible environmental footprint’ of  chemicals 
and materials as part of the ‘sustainability -by -design’ principle  
Unilever’s ambition is to go for net zero carbon emissions from all our products from cradle 
to shelf  by 2039 , h alving the greenhouse gas (GHG)  impact of our products across their 
lifecycle by 2030, and aiming to make our ingredients biodegradable by 2030. Through our 
Clean Future ambition – through which we want to deliver clean homes, planets & futures – 
we also aim to replace 100% of the carbon derived from fossil fuels in our Home Care 
formulations with renewable or recycled carbon by 2030.  
To date, the contribution to glo bal warming from the carbon embedded in chemicals and 
materials has received little policy attention. For most primary petrochemicals, 65 to 73% of 
their carbon footprint results from the carbon embedded in the molecules.1 Therefore, fossil 
fuels remain the prime feedstock to make most chemicals and materials. Most of the carbon 
embedded in petrochemicals is expected to end up in the atmosphere one day or another, 
either through incineration or (bio)degradation.  
Efforts to increase materials efficiency in the final products, i.e. remove or reduce the use of 
the most carbon intensive chemicals whilst delivering the same performance , are an 
important part of the soluti on. Rapid development in biosciences and catalytic chemistry

The SPI is a unique opportunity to fully integrate embedded carbon into the sustainable 
design of materials  and chemicals . We believe that a n appropriate life -cycle assessment  
should underpin and guid e the selection of non-fossil carbon sources for chemicals in 
formulation. In the short -term, this would tend towards a gricultural by -products or 
chemical recycling of non -recycled plastic into ingredients , while in the medium -to-long-
term we would see more benefits from capturing carbon chains in the environment either 
through biomass (land or marine based, ideally from biowaste) or industrial air capture. As 
in the case of the circular economy of plastic packaging, the circularity of chemicals must 
rely on renewable energy. 
In addition, biodegradability of ingredients and products can offer  an important 
contribution to circularity and should therefore become an important part of the SPI. 
Indeed, biodegradability of product formulations contributes to the circularity of carbon, 
less reliance on new, fossil -based carbon, and ultimately increases the sustainability of 
products. This potential can be fully leveraged  with biodegradable chemicals made from 
renewable carbon and manufacturing powered by energy fully generated from renewable 
sources. At Unilever, we aim to use as many biodegradable ingredients as possible  when 
designing our products – ones that break down quickly, easily, and naturally once they have 
been disposed down the drain , returning to the biosphere and degrading completely into 
simple molecules such as CO2 and water.  
 
Our recommendations  
To improve the sustainability of materials and products, the SPI should fully integrate 
ultimate biodegradability and carbon embedded in chemicals and materials through:  
• A definition of renewable or circular carbon.  Adopting a common, science -
based defini tion of renewable carbon will be a critical starting point  for the 
circularity of materials and chemicals . It should be included as a key component 
in the definition of what will constitute a sustainable chemical under the SPI and 
sustainable-by-design cri teria, as announced in the Chemicals Strategy for 
Sustainability. Establishing a  roadmap for closed -carbon loops that includes a 
carbon hierarchy to  guide the  allocation of  carbon according to  societal needs 
and overall environmental impact will further support the move to more circular 
and sustainable chemistry. 
• The extension of the Ecodesign Directive to account for embedded life -cycle 
CO2 emissions in final products, setting limits that will decrease overtime, like the 
Directive did for energy use. This means ensuring that life-cycle assessments take 
into account the degradation of chemicals at the end of life when assessing the 
carbon footprint of products and materials, with an appropriate, common 
methodology.  
• This would go hand in hand with th e development of standards for repo rting 
embedded carbon in products, and the presence of this information in the Digital 
Product Passport.  
• The obligation for chemicals that present harmonised biodegradation 
requirements to express complete (ultimate) and rapid (readily or inherently)

2. Towards more sustainable packaging  
In 2019, we pledged that we would halve the use of virgin plastic in our packaging by 2025 
and remove more than 100,000 tonnes of plastic entirely.  We are starting with design , 
reducing the amount of plastic we use, and then making sure that what we do use , 
increasingly comes from recycled sources. We want all our plastic packaging to be reusable, 
recyclable or compostable by 2025 , and will also use at least 25% recycled plastic in our 
packaging by 2025.  
In October 2 020, Unilever shared an update on progr ess one year on . We are on track in 
Europe to deliver on our commitment to include at least 25% post-consumer resins (PCR) or 
recycled plastic in our packaging by the end of 2025. It  is a step-by-step approach whereby 
we were able to include around 13% recycled plastic by the end of 2019 and approximately 
20% by the end of 2020 in Europe. We hope to make significant further progress by the end 
of this year. 
While specific packaging measures should be developed under  the Packaging and 
Packaging Waste Directive (PPWD), we believe that SPI represents a unique opportunity to 
develop qualifying criteria for sustainable or low carbon products . It is therefore important 
that the SPI addresses packaging horizontally. 
 
‘Better plastic’: Leveraging the full potential of advanced  
recycling technologies  
Recycling technologies are evolving and can help tackle plastic pollution . By stimulating 
and scaling advanced recycling technologies, more plastic waste can be re-processed into 
new chemicals and plastics, keeping both the material and the value of plastic out of the 
environment and in the circular economy. This is the case for chemical recycling for instance. 
Although this technology is still at an early stage, Unilever supports its development as it 
can play a com plementary role to mechanical recycling for hard -to-recycle materials. 
Chemical recycling provides a concrete contribution to reducing GHG emissions, reduces 
waste, increases recycling levels, and can help boost the supply of high -quality secondary 
materials, especially when it comes to food-grade plastics.  
We are already working with different partners to support new technologies to increase the 
supply of high quality recyclates: 
• Cooperation with scale -up company Ioniqa , based in the Netherlands, to ensure 
difficult to recycle (e.g. coloured) PET waste can be infinitely recycled. This will allow 
our Lipton and other iced tea bottles in Europe to be made out of 100% rPET.  
• Cooperation with SABIC to ensure plastic waste that cannot be e asily mechanically 
recycled is converted back to its starting chemicals, further purified , and then re -
used in food grade plastic packaging applications . For example, Magnum became 
the first ice cream brand to use recycled polypropylene plastic for its tubs. This year, 
the brand will roll out 25 million tubs globally, saving an equivalent of 605 tonnes of 
virgin plastic.

• Cooperation with Recycling Technologies in a 3-way partnership with Nest e, to 
support the development of a first chemical recycling plant in the UK for polyolefins, 
to process hard-to-recycle materials including flexibles. 
A key benefit of chemical recycling technology is that it enables the outputs to be used in a 
wider range of packaging and product applications.  A lot of the plastic waste that is 
collected today is not segregated to sufficient homogeneity and purity to allow traditional 
mechanical recycling to convert it to secondary raw materials that can be used in closed 
loop plastics applications. The output is instead only used in applications that will tolerate 
lower quality, secondary material downcycling2. 
Chemical recycling technologies offer a solution to this sorting constraint  as it can take 
mixed and difficult -to-recycle waste and convert it to quality secondary materials  or 
chemicals (upcycling, up to virgin quality, food grade ). This is important for some of our 
packaging applications  for which we  rely on m aterial specifications , including safety and 
regulatory criteria , that cannot yet be achieved using traditional mechanical recycling 
processes due to quality limitations and/or high engineering and sorting requirements. The 
availability of such materials remains scarce in Europe, limiting the possibilities to increase 
recycling.  
The SPI should fully consider the potential of chemical recycling and other advanced 
recycling technologies to play an important role in develo ping the circular economy, while 
making sure chemical recycling does not compete with mechanical recycling  for waste 
feedstock. This should be part of a holistic approach, together with e co-design 
requirements, improving collection infrastructure, as well as sorting and recycling capacity. 
Furthermore, t he economics need to be further optimised as they are currently not at an 
acceptable level , and the technology needs to be rapidly scaled to provide industrial 
solutions. We believe that sufficient quality supply and competitive pricing are essential for 
Europe to compete with other markets like the US, China and Japan , who are already 
heavily investing in  advanced sorting technologies , which  means higher volumes of 
recycled plastics are available at a more competitive price.  
More generally, we notice that recycled materials remain significantly more expensive (on 
average mechanically recycled plastic represents an oncost of 15%  virgin plasti c, while 
chemically recycled plastic for food grade represents an oncost of 100%) and that there are 
insufficient incentives to reward products based on their different sustainability 
performances.  
Finally, a lot of  information on the sustainability of pr oducts is already available to 
consumers. We advocate for the harmonisation of best practices across the EU, but also call 
for further improvements, particularly regarding the traceability and measurement of 
recycled plastic content in products.

Less and no plastic: Reuse, refill and rethink 
One other solution is refillable, reusable packaging formats. Our partners at the  Ellen 
MacArthur Foundation  have outlined four models for refillable and reusable consumer 
products that we agree have very strong potential: 
• Refill at home:  where consumers reuse their packaging by refilling at home, for 
example with a concentrated version that is diluted with water . For example, since 
Cif launched ultra-concentrated refills for its Power & Shine spray bottles in 2019, the 
brand has saved ove r 170 tonnes of plastic. Refills are now on sale across several 
markets in Europe , including Portugal, France, the Netherlands, and Denmark. The 
refills require 75% less plastic than ordinary packs and are fully recyclable once 
wrappers have been removed. By diluting at home, 97% less water is transported, 
resulting in 80% fewer trucks needed to transport the product.  
• Refill on the go:  where shoppers refill their containers through dispensing systems 
in stores. In October 2020, our Unilever UK & Ireland team launched our biggest refill 
trial to date in Europe, in partnership with retailer Asda.  Seven Unilever brands, 
including Persil, PG Tips and Radox, are trialling their products across three refill 
formats – machines that dispense liquid products into ref illable bottles, self -serve

containers and refill -at-home products.  During lockdowns, these types of 
experiments have been more challenging to test at scale.  
• Return from home:  where consumers can arrange for packaging to be collected 
from their home and refilled. In 2019, we began our trial with Loop – a business which 
delivers products in returnable packaging to consumers at home, through an online 
subscription model.  
• Return on the go:  where consumers return packaging at a drop -off point, and it is 
collected and refilled by manufacturers.  
Although the  refill and reuse approach is in its early days , the pilot projects we have  
invested in have shown there is no one -size-fits-all. Different consumers have different 
needs and wants, which depend on a ho st of factors , such as where they live or how they 
shop. Likewise, different product categories work better on -the-go than at home, and vice 
versa. Therefore, success depends on tailoring solutions accordingly, removing barriers to 
entry, and keeping systems as simple as possible. It also heavily relies on cooperation with 
partners and the support and buy -in from consumers , manufacturers, delivery services,  
retailers, and governments. 
 
Our recommendations  
By making refill and reuse formats widely available, a ccessible and affordable, we hope 
to use our scale and reach to drive lasting change.  We would be happy to further share 
what we learned from our pilot projects across Europe and the world. From our experience 
so far, we would recommend the EU to consider the following:  
• Create the right enabling environment for these business models to flourish by  
setting harmonised standards and incentives for refilling and reusing 
packaging, and other innovative business models. The nascent nature of refill 
models means that regulatory guidelines and public policies can be vague, and 
we have come across challenges with this in sever al of our pilots.  When it comes 
to new packaging formats, collaboration with partners along the entire value 
chain has proven key to develop technical breakthrough that can unlock viable, 
scaled materials and design solutions.  
• Support for digital solution s, such as apps or online subscriptions.  They offer 
huge potential when it comes to refills, making it easy for consumers to order top-
ups of the products they need.  E-commerce is a fast -growing channel that can  
provide invaluable insights and data which c an help shape and support our 
relationship with consumers.  Furthermore, we need flexibility on providing 
consumers with the required information to support the transition.  
• Support the communication of the benefits of refilling and other innovative 
business models to consumers. Our in-store pilots have shown that refill stations 
alone will not deliver results. They need to be amplified and promoted with clear, 
engaging communications and shoppers may need assistance from in-store staff. 
We believe that widespread public communications campaig ns are needed to 
clarify the benefits of refills – alongside smaller-scale campaigns for specific refill 
or reuse solutions.

3. Other important elements to consider in the Sustainable 
Products Initiative  
As a company operating globally and across Europe, we are often facing fragmentation of 
the Single Market, and lack of harmonisation of the legislation, its interpretation or 
enforcement is often an obstacle. We believe a key principle of the SPI should be to prevent 
a patchwork of local rules and enforcement regimes which place extra burden and 
regulatory uncertainty on businesses. For example, an EU definition of “recyclability” is 
needed to ensure a common approach by the EU Member States , as well as a clear 
framework for advanced recycling (see above).  
Furthermore, collection and recycling targets alone will not be sufficient to supply the 
volumes of high -quality recycled plastics required in Europe. The Circular Economy Action 
Plan recognises this issue and helpfully foresees a harmonisation of national collections 
systems, the development of solutions for high -quality sorting, the removal of 
contaminants from waste, and the introduction of quality standards. 
Our experience in innovative  circular business models has shown that there is no  silver 
bullet to enhance the sustainability of products . While sometimes voluntary approaches 
work well, support and incentives from regulation can be necessary to unlock  further 
actions. The SPI and fur ther EU actions in this matter should acknowledge that there is no 
one-size-fits-all and continue to consider the specificities of each sector.  
Finally, the potential of digital technologies should be fully le veraged to accompany this 
transition to more sustainable products, by offering more options for digitalised consumer 
information. With packaging becoming smaller  (or even disappearing) , so does the 
available room for physical labelling. Therefore, b usinesses need flexibility on providing 
consumers with the required informat ion. For example, a ccess to the most recent and 
updated information is available at any time digitally, while such information can also be 
provided on a leaflet at point -of-sale or through in -store scanners in cooperation with 
retailers for consumers who need it. It is also important to differentiate which information is 
relevant at product level, and which one is more relevant at company or brand level, such 
as information on how producers have prioritised materials that are safe and sustainable -
by-design and have substituted chemicals of concern with safer ones whenever possible .

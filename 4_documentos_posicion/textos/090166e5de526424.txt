2 Clarity and consistency key for Sustainable Product Policy 
Our position  
June 2021 
Introduction 
 
The Circular Economy Action Plan and the Sustainable Product  Initiative (SPI) roadmap emphasi se the 
importance of improving information flows across value chains and to consumers  as well as  the benefits 
digitalisation could bring in this respect . They clearly recognise the need to revise the Ecodesign Directive and 
propose additional legislative measures as appropriate, with the aim of making products placed on the EU 
market more sustainable. 
Consumers, the environment and the climate will benefit from products that are more  durable, reusable, 
repairable, recyclable and energy -efficient. Whether the harm produced by products is to the environment, 
society or directly to people, all damage results – directly or indirectly – in a threat to human health. 
The Sustainable Products  Initiative is one of many initiatives that are currently being drafted or submitted for 
consultation or that have recently been published by the Commission  which are  aimed at  fostering the 
manufacturing and consumption of more sustainable goods. Such initiatives include: the EU Strategy for Textiles; 
the ‘Safe and Sustainable-by-Design’ concept in the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability ; the Strategy for a 
Sustainable Built Environment ; the review of the rules on end -of-life vehicles; the proposal on Substantiating 
Green Claims; and the Circular Electronic Initiatives, among many others (including eg eco-design requirements 
for specific product groups). Coherence among all policies is therefore critical to guarantee predictable and clear 
regulatory frameworks. 
Enforcement mechanisms, market surveillance and custom control are, moreover, fundamental to ensure a level 
playing field. AmCham EU’s members take regulatory compliance very seriously and support authorities in their 
efforts to reinforce controls and ensure that only compliant products are placed on the market. 
The roadmap, as described in the consultation now published, aims also at extending digitalisation processes as 
a way to improve identification of the products and responsibilities of the manufacturers  as well as  to enable 
the tracking and verification throughout complex value chains and appropriate end -of-life treatment. In this 
respect, the digitalisation of information and tracking of product performances and characteristics has been 
recognised as a turning point in the internal market.  
In such context  - and after consideration of the recently published Batteries Regulation which provides a 
blueprint of the European Commission’s possible approach on information to consumers, product sustainability 
and digitalisation - we would like to recommend a consistent and clear approach.  
 
Recommendations in line with the Sustainable Products Initiative public consultation 
 
1) Scope of Ecodesign 
• Design is a crucial phase to improving product sustainability and we take note of the need to extend 
the scope of the Ecodesign Directive. A harmonised and broadened eco -design framework is an 
important contribution to a level -playing field for the assessm ent of different products and material 
applications. Diverging eco-design product rules amongst Member States represent a barrier to intra -
EU trade and makes it harder for consumers to access and for companies to offer products cross -
border. The Commission must take decisive action in line with the principles established by the Single 
Market Transparency Directive (EU) 2015/1535 to avert a fragmentation of EU product rules.  
• It is worth noting that the Ecodesign  Directive is not the only policy in place setting sustainability 
requirements. Therefore, the sustainability criteria should be mindful of existing sector -specific 
frameworks to avoid double regulation.  As an example, the Ecodesign Directive should not cover 
packaging, as this is already regulated through the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive (currently 
under review).

• Impact assessments on the potential inclusion of new priority product groups identified in the Circular 
Economy Action Plan (ie electronics, ICT, textiles, furniture, cement, chemicals) must be thorough and 
include consultation with representatives from all relevant stakeholder groups across the value chain.  
• We encourage the Commission to utili se existing international product standards as a basis for future 
eco-design i mplementing measures  and to take stock of industry -wide and internationally oriented 
standards that facilitate international trade and reflect the technological state of the art.  
• We agree that the Ecodesign Directive offers a comprehensive and reliable reg ulatory instrument to 
promote sustainability requirements covering the entire lifecycle of products, based on scientifically 
based life-cycle assessments. Where extensive assessments have been conducted to define eco-design 
requirements for certain product  categories, these must be recognised and taken into account when 
considering measures under legislation that is more narrowly focused. In particular, substance 
restrictions and assessment should be dealt within the Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and 
Restriction of Chemicals  (REACH) and Restriction of Hazardous Substances ( RoHS) processes and not 
under the Ecodesign Directive in order to avoid overlap and ensure policy coherence and a consistent 
implementation of EU law.  
• Enforcement and market surveillance will be key moving forward. The Commission should build on 
existing instruments under the Ecodesign Directive to ensure that national enforcement authorities can 
verify that requirements defined at the EU level are being respected through different  conformity 
assessment methodologies.  
 
2) Sustainability criteria 
• AmCham EU members welcome the approach adopted by the Commission in its public consultation on 
Sustainable Products Policy to define sustainability criteria , not horizontally , but rather at product 
group level. 
• In addition , we believe that the definition of criteria should be carefully considered in light of the 
following points: 
o Comparing products according to their carbon footprint offers the advantage of having an 
indisputable and easily comparable metric in terms of emitted tons of CO2. However, a clear 
and standardised methodology to assess carbon footprints is not  yet available at EU or 
international level for all product categories covered by the scope of the future SPI. 
o Assessing the overall environmental footprint of a product is very complicated. While we 
welcome the efforts made to develop the Product Environmental Footprint (PEF) methodology 
and recognise that  Product Environmental Footprint Category Rules  (PEFCRs) have been 
successfully developed for some product categories , the EU pilot phase has shown it can be 
difficult to achieve consensus across industry and to establish comprehensive methodologies. 
Product differentiation based on life-cycle assessment  (LCA) may be premature for some 
product categories.  
o Similarly, assessing products according to their percentage of recycled content is premature 
in the absence of a legal definition of what ‘recycled content ’ and ‘recyclability’ concretely 
mean. In this respect, developing definitions is a matter of priorit y. In our view, s uch 
definitions should be open enough to encompass content coming from mechanical and 
chemical recycling in order to foster the uptake of innovative recycling technologies and to

The preliminary final analysis of the preparatory study for the Ecodesign and Energy Labelling 
Working Plan 2020-2024 is extremely cautious regarding mandatory reincorporation of post -
consumer recycled content 1. In this respect, we fully s ubscribe to the recommendation of 
launching a more detailed study and pilot projects on this topic before considering any broad 
regulatory measure ; taking into consideration the availability of technological options and 
access to feedstock by sector. 
o Regarding the presence of hazardous substances that may  hamper recyclability, we believe 
that increased recyclability should not be seen as the precondition but rather, in a dynamic 
perspective, as the outcome of a successful circular economy transition. If we adopt a strict 
approach on this, the EU economy will lock itself in one single direction where every product 
must to be designed to fit only recycling requirements  based on currently available 
technologies. This is not the best solution to improve the over all sustainability of products, 
reduce their carbon footprint and grasp the benefits of innovative recycling technologies  – 
indeed, it may  hamper innovation and investments  in new products and recycling 
technologies. It must also be stressed that there is not one possible single definition of 
substances that may hamper recyclability. Substances are added in products to fulfil certain, 
sometimes important , technical characteristics . F or instance, flame  retardants may inhibit 
recyclability but cannot be simply removed without having a proper alternative at hand just 
for the sake of recyclability. 
o It is worth noting that regulation of hazardous substances is currently covered by REACH and 
CLP and  that these existing legislation should remain the instruments by which the safe 
handling and use of substances in product s is ensured; regardless of whether they are made 
from recycled or virgin material and especially when it comes to addressing substances of very 
high concern. Moreover, considering the Commission’s recently communicated intention to 
revise REACH and CLP and to develop criteria within the ‘Safe and Sustainable -by-Design’ 
initiative, it is essential that overall consistency in EU legislation on pro ducts and chemicals is 
maintained. We believe that the Sustainable Product Initiative  should not be the means by 
which to regulate the presence of substances in products which could hinder recyclability and 
eventually ban them.  
• Product sustainability must  therefore be addressed in a holistic way. The sustainability criteria 
identified (durability, reusability and reparability) must concur first and foremost with the key objective 
of reducing the overall environmental footprint of products along their lifec ycle. As trade -offs are 
inevitable among these criteria, they must not be cumulative in principle. Manufacturers must rather 
have the flexibility to strike the right balance between them, for each of their products.  
3) Sustainability incentives 
• Rules on environmental/sustainability performance of products and services must be accompanied by 
economic incentives for customers to opt for such products and services. This would create coherence 
between supply and demand along the environmental goals. Incentive s should be harmonised at EU 
level or at least follow some clear EU guidelines to avoid a fragmented market for both industry and 
consumers. Harmonisation should be done by product/sector and avoid one-size-fits-all solutions. 
• Consumer education pla ys a key role in encouraging the market adoption of sustainable technologies 
and products. Products that comply with the sustainability criteria should  clearly express their

• Incentives should be based on standardised performances (ie standardised methodologies for product 
performance measurement) and – where applicable – on product scores according to the relevant 
harmonised criteria. In addition, where products and services have a safety -related application, 
incentives should consider both the environmental/resource efficiency aspect as well as the safety

design of packaging and products. We support further harmonisation of criteria for EPR fee modulation, 
eg through Commission Guidance. EPR schemes sho uld be based first and foremost on the net cost 
principle (revenue from the sale of secondary raw materials should be used to offset operational costs). 
The criteria and definitions applicable for eco -modulation must be harmoni sed at EU level and  be 
coherent with EU legislation and international standards while also taking into account product/sector-
specific conditions.  
• The implementation of EPR schemes varies widely across the EU ; both between Member States and 
between different product categories. This has led to complex and confusing EPR requirements which 
create significant administrative and financial burdens and barriers to intra -EU selling. By creating an 
EPR ‘one -stop-shop’ for registration and reporting, the Commission could drastically reduce the  
administrative burden faced by sellers of sustainable products across the Single Market.  
• We also welcome the idea that producers’ voluntary commitments to increase product sustainability 
should be recognised but expect clarifications on how this would be concretely implemented.  
• We agree that there is room to mobilise public procurement (ie through green public procurement  
[GPP]) in order to boost the uptake of sustainable products. We support synchroni sed EU rules for 
minimum requirements on GPP based on a sector -by-sector assessment. As for other purchasing 
incentives, we support the principle that GPP should favour those products which meet the highest 
performance level from an environmental, safety and resource efficiency perspective. More work could 
be done at the EU level to ensure sustainability criteria are consistently applied for public procurement 
exercises carried out at Member State level, such as public tenders and auctions.  
4) Market surveillance and custom controls are key to ensure the sustainability 
agenda is implemented 
• Illegal trade is a significant issue that can jeopardise the goals of the European Green Deal initiative , 
especially when it comes to the Sustainable products initiative. 
• If sustainable products are to become the norm, we must ensure a level playing field and enforce 
market surveillance in the EU to ensure that only products compliant to the EU regulations are placed 
on the market.  
• Many improvements have been included in t he text of  the recent revision  of the legislation on 
‘Compliance with and Enforcement of Union Legislation on Products’ adopted in 2019, which also aims 
to support joint activities promoting compliance, providing an EU -wide legal basis for market 
surveillance authorities to join forces with organisations representing economic operators .  
• The provisions of the new legislation on market surveillance will help protect manufacturers from unfair 
competition as soon as the legislation becomes fully applicable (July 2021).

• However, notwithstanding the steps forward this legislation will hopefully deliver, authorities are still 
struggling to react to illegal imports and non -compliant/counterfeited products placed on the market. 
It is therefore essential that further steps are taken  such as improv ing enforcement; imposing 
harmonised and dissuasive penalties ; and quickly introducing the EU Single Window Environment for 
Customs. It is essential that customs authorities stop and confiscate illegal imports and products not 
compliant with applicable EU regulations.  
• Sales of certain chemicals through e -commerce has increased significantly over the last few years . 
These products that are sold on unconventional internet platforms often do not comply with European 
regulations, are difficult to track and are placed illegally on the market; undermining climate goals and 
posing a serious risk to end users. 
 
5) Digitalisation of information and Product Passport Agenda 
 
a.  Objectives and overall architecture of the digital product passports and databases 
• Communication and transparency along value chains as well as  information to the end-user is 
important. Such efforts should help drive consumption choices towards more sustainable products and 
improve waste sorting and recycling. The Commission’s ambition to reap the benefits of digitali sation 
by developing digital product passports  (DPP) is welcomed , but should be  carefully calibrated and 
assessed against its potential drawbacks. 
• We believe th at the comprehensive system currently prepared by the Commission, if based on the 
model of the Batteries Regulation, would be extremely time-consuming and will likely lead to a massive 
increase in red-tape and costs throughout value-chains. In this respect , it is worth highlighting the 
effects that a massive increase in data-gathering and processing  can have on  product cost, 
competitiveness and trade as underlined in the Batteries Regulation impact assessment. 
• It is worth noting that - even in the proposed regulatory framework on batteries - the requirements for 
the Electronic Exchange Platform have been limited to industrial and electronic vehicle batteries, while 
portable and automotive  batteries have been excluded. This raises serious doubts regarding the 
practical implementation of such costly system s for fast-moving consumer goods ( FMCGs) and other 
everyday consumption products.  
• The question of interoperability and coordination between several electronic exchange systems  and 
product passpor ts is also important given that the Sustainable Products Policy Initiative  foresees 
addressing both final and intermediary products. In both cases, special attention must be paid to 
interoperability and coherence with existing or legally required databases  (the Substances of Concern 
In articles as such or in complex objects [Products] database; European Product Database for Energy 
Labelling [EPREL] database ; and the Information and Communication System on Market Surveillance 
[ICSMS] platform in particular) in order to limit administrative burden and duplication. 
b. Different information requirements are needed 
• Information requirements should first and foremost be adapted, proportional and relevant for the 
intended purpose. 
• Information to end-users must be clear , concise, relevant, comprehensible, reliable and easily 
accessible at the time of purchase in order to provide a real added value. Information requirements

• We support the Commission’s objective to increase access to digital information. The DPP is the right 
tool to make product information required by law (which does not need to be readily available whe n 
the product is used) available to consumers, regulators and other stakeholders such as entities involved 
in packaging waste management activities.  The DPP can also provide greater prominence of essential 
information on pack by reducing information overlo ad on the packaging; promote the functioning of 
the Single Market by allowing products to satisfy the legal requirements in more EEA markets: and help 
reduce the costs and waste associated with packaging/label changes resulting from legal and/or 
product changes over time.  
To ensure that all consumers can access the Digital Product Passport, several access options should be 
available:  
o Scanning the product by the consumer itself; and/or  
o Possibility to scan the product at point of sale through machines made  available by retailers. 
• To make the Digital Product Passport effective and cost-efficient, we support the following principles:  
1. The  DPP should  contain information that is legally required by EU legislation (eg chemical 
legislation, Ecodesign, product specific legislation). On that basis, information requirements should 
be tailored to different product categories;  
2. Interoperability with other datasets (eg SCIP) must be ensured.  
3. Different information could be available to different groups:  
a. Information for consumers: On -pack information should be targeted and sharp (mainly 
provided through icons) to avoid information overload and the need for additional 
packaging materials. When m ore detailed information is to be provided, this should be 
made available and accessible online through a digital passport.   
b. Information for economic operators along the value -chains:  A digital passport can help 
increase communication and transparency al ong the value chain and with waste 
management operators to ensure safe and efficient recycling operations. In that 
framework, access to the passport should be exclusively limited to accredited economic 
operators. In such a secure environment the following information could be shared: 
information on proper dismantling and waste treatment of products ; the presence of  
Substances of Very High Concern (SVHCs) through a link to the SCIP database; etc.  
c. Information for authorities on compliance of products with EU  legislation or information 
on poison centres for relevant product categories.   
4. The unit of reference of the Digital Product Passport should be established on the basis of the 
nature of the specific product. For instance, for articles, the default unit of  reference should be 
the product model as defined by existing legislation (eg  New Legislative Framework legislation). 
This will facilitate the uptake of the DPP, as companies can use their existing data systems to 
implement the DPP and avoid unnecessary costs and complexities associated with DPP defined 
by individual products. Individual DPP would result in significant economic costs associated with 
the maintenance of millions of DPP and large environmental impacts. The high cost of DPP by

c. Economic operators’ responsibilities and sensitive data protection 
• When it comes to the responsibility of economic operators to share information, we acknowledge that 
the Batteries Regulation considers that the economic operator placing a n industrial or electric vehicle 
battery on the market should be responsible for their attribution of a unique product passport and for 
filling in the relevant information. In this framework , it is important to clarify which markets shall be 
considered.  
• In the case of complex products, such as electric and electronic equipment, there could be several 
electronic exchange systems to be updated and product passports  to be established. This may 
complicate the tracking of information and the allocation of responsibilities for data sharing.  
• Similarly, in the case of repurposing or remanufacturing  a product, it should be clear that the 
responsibility to update the information is transferred to the remanufa cturer who will be placing a 
remanufactured product on the EU market. 
Clarification of data protection obligations and the potential liabilities in case of data breach is essential, 
and must be clarified upfront.

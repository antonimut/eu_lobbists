Right to Repair response to Sustainable Products Initiative
Consultation
The European Right to Repair campaign supports the
EU’s ambition to make sustainable
products the norm. Here we outline additional comments
in relation to the public
consultation on the SPI.
Design for circularity
-
Expanding ecodesign to non-energy products is a major
opportunity to increase
the application of material eﬃciency requirements
on short lived product
groups, such as textiles and furniture, but also to
address small electronics and
connected devices not prioritised so far in the “product-by-product”
approach of
ecodesign.
-
New material eﬃciency requirements should address
enablers of repairing
rather than replacing products, i.e. designing products
for durability and
repairability, including non-destructive disassembly,
making aﬀordable spare
parts available, and making complete repair and maintenance
information
available.
-
The revision of the MEErP methodology should result
in a shift in focus from
energy eﬃciency towards material eﬃciency as the
primary focus. This should
include the calculation of the embedded emissions
in products, and estimating
the eﬀective savings (in CO2 eq and material footprint,
tonnes) of extending the
lifetime of these products through regulatory measures.
-
Consumer electronics which are unlikely to be “prioritised”
in future ecodesign
work plans (notably small electronics like kitchen
appliances and small
connected devices such as headphones) should be urgently
addressed through
comprehensive horizontal material eﬃciency measures.
The current focus of the
Circular Electronics Initiative on computers and laptops
will leave many products
unaddressed - particularly in the context of a growing
digitalisation and Internet
of Things.
Existing ecodesign framework
-
The existing Ecodesign framework and workplan should
not be neglected. It is
clear that the Commission team responsible for ecodesign
is understaﬀed,
considering that no new measures have been adopted
since 2019. Furthermore

-
The Commission has notably failed to deliver its commitment

Information for circularity
-
The opportunity should not be missed to develop a
repair label as part of the
energy label for smartphones and computers as part
of their ongoing
investigation in the ecodesign framework. This is
a critical policy window to
provide citizens with reliable information on the
repairability of products. This
has already been shown to change the market in France.
-
It should be stressed that Spain and Belgium are currently
exploring repairability
labels. While we welcome these initiatives, the EU
must have a vision for
establishing a harmonised scheme as part of the SPI
in order to avoid market
distortion.
-
In the future ecodesign framework repairability and
durability labelling should be
a “default” policy option under consideration when
developing measures. This
should complement other types of information such
as environmental
performance based on PEFCRs.
-
Repair information, such as manuals and diagnosis
software could be made
widely accessible using the digital product passport
concept.
Circular business models
-
Circular business models should be supported when
they are genuinely circular,
and this means result in absolute resource savings.
Many product as a service
models simply represent new forms of consumption.
Additionally, when led by
major companies, product as a service can be used
to monopolise after-sales
markets ruining the businesses of independent repairers.
-
In general, voluntary commitments should not be accepted
as an alternative to
regulatory measures. Existing ones on games consoles,
complex set-top boxes
and printers have all been shown to underperform when
compared to material
eﬃciency measures set through regulations.

Incentives for circularity
-
A critical gap in the EU’s product policy framework
is its failure to develop
credible market-based incentives which favour value
retention over linear
approach to product stewardship. Reducing the cost
of labour relative to using
virgin resources should be the primary priority to
do this - this can only be done
through genuine tax shifts.
-
Social economy actors and repair cafes can help to
address the cost of labour
through subsidised and voluntary work, however existing
repair measures at the
EU level exclude these actors in their loose deﬁnition
of “professional repairers”
and thus potentially block the repair of low-value
goods.
-
Small incentives through VAT reductions on repair
activities or using EPR
modulation can provide further incentives
Other measures to make sustainable products the norm
-
We would welcome close collaboration between the Sustainable
Products
Initiative and the ongoing review of the initiative
on Industrial Design to ensure
that the production of “spare parts” by third parties
is not prevented using
intellectual property law, thus permitting manufacturers
to monopolise
after-sale markets.
Who we are
The European
Right to Repair
campaign is a coalition
of 47 organisations from 16
European countries representing civil society groups,
repair businesses, community
repair initiatives and public institutions demanding
longer-lasting and more repairable
products. The campaign is steered by the European
Environmental Bureau, ECOS, iFixit,
Repair & Share, The Restart Project, Runder Tisch
Reparatur. We are working at the
European level but also within member states to remove
the barriers to repair our
products and implement a universal Right to Repair,
for all products and for everyone.

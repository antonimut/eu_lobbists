1 Introduction and key issues  
Based on the in ternational framework of ISO 14040 and 14044, the PEF Method provides 
substantial additional requirements to increase the reproducibility of results of product 
and organization LCAs. Sphera welcomes the initiative to derive strong policy instruments 
based on the EF to strengthen the reliability and the extension of the Ecodesign Directive 
to not energy -using or energy -related product categories. Sphera welcomes that the PEF 
Method is aiming at ensuring a fair competition among products and organizations, wi th a 
science-based approach, and we acknowledge that substantial progress has been made to 
achieve this aim.   
However, among the literally hundreds of provisions and requirements, the PEF Method 
has some key weaknesses that critically endanger the success  of the whole effort.  These 
key weaknesses must be corrected before policies are put in place.  Some of the 
weaknesses are very technical, yet with a huge leverage on the outcome, others more 
general, including governance.  While we will detail these issues  here below, we want to 
highlight at the same time that these remaining issues must not be used as an ostensible 
reason to ask for additional development and testing rounds or for weakening policy 
options. In principle, the PEF have seen more than sufficient development and testing, with 
thousands of stakeholders across many industries, for almost 10 years, building from in 
between 30 solid years of use in industry for strategic and daily business and techn ology 
decision support. It is overdue to finally fully harness the power of quantitative life cycle 
approaches. They are indispensable, now, to effectively move forward to achieve the 
ambitious aims of the EU in combating climate change and a range of othe r environmental 
issues that ultimately threaten the existence of mankind and the life support system on 
earth, to come to a Circular Economy.  
Having said that, we consider the following ones such key issues (or sub -issues) of the PEF 
method to overcome be fore putting a policy in place, that would initially use pilot and 
transition PEFCRs  and develop Implementing Measures requirements in dedicated efforts . 
We want to highlight moreover that all of these key issues do not necessitate any or only 
very limited additional testing:  
•  Correct the flaws of the CFF  model for recycled content and end -of-life modelling, 
making it thereby match the results of the formula in the annex in EN 15804, while 
with an additional simplification to always calculate closed loop ( making it fully 
attributional, in effect).  
• Reduce the excessive number of over 93,000 EF 3.0 elementary flows to all at least 
somehow potentially relevant 10,000 to 15,000 elementary flows

2 General issues  
2.1 Background database approach for PEF  
Issue: Public policies, s uch as any PEF based mandatory policy, will need to work without 
requiring any specific, mandatory fee -based data or software. At the same time, high data 
quality is needed and - unlike in other domains with static data - LCI data quickly gets 
outdated, an d benefits from some updates on a yearly basis, with underlying production 
data updates every 3 years. The required expert resources and associated costs are 
substantial. Other leading LCA database developers therefore may expand the number of 
datasets, but at less complete inventories and representativeness, and without or infrequent 
updates of the existing data, letting them slowly get outdated. As there is yet no suitable and 
objective data quality scheme, data quality is hard to judge for users, often n ot recognized, 
and often not considered when selecting data sources. Overall and as an indirect 
consequence, data quality of widely recognized LCI databases differs still substantially. Also, 
long running government co -financed price dumping has arguably l ed to lower data quality 
and only very few remaining providers. While experienced companies and associations 
understand the necessity to use quality data and are willing to pay the price premium, 
prominently for Sphera’s GaBi data, new actors and researche rs with budget restrictions or 
more methodological interests often go for the cheapest data. If companies did so, it would 
be of concern if actual decisions on technologies, sourcing, and design were based on results 
obtained with data of low quality . It would be much wor se if public policy schemes did so. 
What is needed is a full understanding of the quality differences of data on the market and 
mechanisms that incentivize to improve the data quality further. At the same time, a 
provider lock -in is to be avoided in policy schemes. The EC approach to the EF database 
however is not (yet) having a reliable working mechanism for quality data, as detailed in a 
separate key issue in this position paper. Even more: data quality was only one relatively 
limited asp ect in the past EF data tenders by the EC, as data quality did not carry an 
appropriate weight in the awarding (aside the other problematic issues of how data quality is 
defined and aggregated, so far). The available budget to prepare the data according to the EF 
methodology and other requirements and review etc. was moreover far lower than the 
actual cost which has led to two of the other EF pilot secondary data providers officially 
dropping out of the next round of data calls, even though these would just  have seen a less 
demanding update to their pilot phase data. Sphera has however continued to provide its 
data (same as a few others, those mostly with association -backed data sourcing). We have 
done so out of the deep conviction that a successful EF needs  quality data and that poor 
data from other sources could mean that the entire EF initiative and a much wider use of 
LCA fails. The updated underlying energy, transport, packaging and end -of-life data that are 
mandatory to be used for all PEF studies are p rovided by Sphera. In context of the costs of 
PEF studies, we would like to highlight that any kind of LCA studies, also PEF studies under a 
PEFCR, causes many other costs than secondary data license fees, such as measurements of

(as detailed more above) plus having stricter requirements on the review/verification

minor differences due to the currently used two -digit data quality rating differentiation 
which is in any case spurious - data from a source higher in the hierar chy shall be preferred. 
Importantly, the hierarchy needs to have integrated a mechanism that incentivizes using 
higher quality data, where available, particularly for the last level, wherefrom currently the 
bulk of data for the EF is sourced and where mark et alternatives on data exist but 
competition based on data quality needs to be much stronger incentivized. This would be by 
always giving preference to higher quality data within that source level.

3 Technical issues  
3.1 Data quality rating (DQR) definitions  
Issue: Valid PEF studies indispensably require high quality data, to ensure users are 
informed about the specifics and quality of the data for proper data choices and to ensure 
differences among products are sufficiently accurately depicted by  the results produced by 
the users. They must not be distorted by data of too low or unclear quality that lead to 
increasingly random results or results that can be engineered. Based on comparisons of our 
own GaBi data – that is to a large extent based on data that has been measured or calculated 
and validated or assessed with producing industry - with alternative market relevant data 
sources, overall EF results for the same product frequently differ significantly. The 
differences are often found higher tha n the actual differences among many products to be 
expected in PEF studies. At the same time, applying the current EF data quality ratings, both 
compared data sources yield almost identical, “very good” or even “excellent” DQRs. How 
can this be?  
The PEF Method and EF -compliant data guide have several, more or less logically coherent 
DQR tables. The definitions of the quality levels have been adjusted several times since the 
beginning of the EF pilot phase, new tables for specific settings have bee n spun off. They all 
share however four serious weaknesses that we here explain and illustrate:   
3.1.1 Some ratings are unbalanced, in some cases the rating “1” is unattainable, and in 
single cases the rating may even inverse the true quality and the rating  
Example: EF compliant data Guide table 2, Geographical representativeness, GR -EF and 
GRSD, “1, excellent”: “ The processes included in the data set are fully representative for the 
geography stated in the “location” indicated in the metadata”.   
Problem: As the tendered EF -compliant data sets are representing whole countries or 
regions or even global production, rating “1” necessitates having data for all sites of that 
country or region, or even globally, or ensure the full representativeness. This is en tirely 
unattainable. The next two levels are hard to distinguish, and we note that the wording of 
rating level “3, good” is moreover only applicable to aggregated datasets and not to unit 
processes, while the current logic of assigning DQRs is also for unit processes.  
3.1.2 Some definitions are unclear and included terms lacking any definition  
Example: The current PEF Method (“Suggestions for …” document), defines in Table 23 i.e. , 
when using company specific information, time -related TiR -EF and TiR -AD, D QR “2, very 
good“: “The data refers to maximum 2 annual administration periods with respect to the EF 
report publication date”.   
Problem: Does it mean two years are to be averaged? Or it may not be older than (the end 
of?) 2 years before the publication date?

3.2 Data quality assignment and aggregation  
Issue: As initially mentioned, we note that the Data WG under the EF is currently looking 
into improving the data quality assignment and aggregation approach . While there is a good 
support to a be tter solution, the final outcome is yet open, why we reiterate here t he need 
for substantial amendment: In the current, transition EF-compliant data guide (and similar in 
the (“Suggestions for …”) PEF Method) the data quality is assigned to each process of the - 
potentially very extensive - life cycle models that underlie a final aggregated or level -1 partly 
aggregated dataset. The data quality on that underlying level is assumed to be assigned by 
expert judgement. So, the core data quality is generally expert judged, not der ived from 
some large sampling sizes or similar, which are rarely existing. In practice, given the sheer 
number of datasets and lack of more specific information for individual datasets, the basic 
quality is understood to be even assigned generically across  many datasets along patterns, 
by major database developers. This expert judgement or even automatic approach is a 
necessity to which we agree. The aggregation to the final data quality ratings (DQR) of the

aggregated or level -1 partly aggregated datasets is then to be calculated, according to the 
current EF rules, by summing up for the most relevant 80% processes the individual datasets’ 
DQRs, weighted by each unit process’ contribution to the normalized and weighted EF single 
point result. So, the more im pacts a dataset has, the more its DQR contributes to the overall 
DQR. This seemingly practical and sound approach has a serious flaw: those datasets that do 
not have any emissions or resource flows – however often fundamentally determining the 
quality of t he final aggregated or level -1 dataset - hence do not contribute to the overall EF 
results, do not count at all. Among such datasets are importantly BOM -type and other mixer 
and averaging datasets (such as for an import mix of a material, a grid mix from d ifferent 
fossil and renewable power plant types, and so on). That means that a completely wrong 
BOM of a product or a flawed grid mix dataset, which in reality will render the entire dataset 
plain false (“5, poor”), does not contribute to the final DQR. Th e DQR of the final dataset is 
calculated exclusively based on the other datasets of the model that do have direct impacts, 
which are however often dominated by electricity supply and raw material chain impacts 
from the common EF background data. Due to the  not considered DQR of the important 
mixer, BOM etc. datasets, the calculated DQR would hence still be e.g., “very good”, for a 
dataset that in fact would be truly “5, poor”, and hence ineligible, as rendering the entire 
PEF study results false. Next to su ch BOM/mixer datasets, also poor processing datasets that 
miss emission information, but are merely based on stoichiometry plus some estimated 
energy consumption will currently receive calculated DQRs of “very good” while the actual 
quality is far less. Th at means that a final aggregated dataset, built from better processing 
datasets with emission data (direct process emissions) but with lower -than-average quality 
assigned to the datasets, receives a lower calculated DQR compared to the poorer 
aggregated dataset from another provider that misses in its processing datasets in the model 
the direct emissions and that hence do not count for the aggregated DQR calculations. On 
top of this fundamental flaw, we highlight the huge effort needed and hence costs incur red 
to assign DQRs to tens of thousands of datasets in the background system of the models, 
which in any case are assigned by expert judgement.  
Solution: So far, a viable DQR approach via calculation that yields reliable final DQRs is 
lacking. We argue th at neither a static nor a dynamic calculation approach are promising. 
The dynamic contribution i.e., varying each dataset’s inventory values to some degree to 
judge each dataset’s relevance and use this as basis to model the overall DQR, leads straight 
to the Monte-Carlo-Simulation (MCS) approach that previously has been used by individual, 
relevant database developers to calculate overall DQRs. Already years ago, this has been 
stopped, as MCS leads to substantially too positive DQRs. The reason is that MCS  
necessitates independency of the single input values, here the product, waste and emission 
quantities of all the datasets. However, product and waste output amounts are always 
quantitatively linked to material inputs to the same dataset (and to the direct ly connected 
downstream datasets) and must not be varied independently. Also, many emissions are 
equally linked ( e.g., CO2 in link to fuel C input) or correlated ( e.g., NOx and CO in 
incineration). In conclusion, MCS is simply the wrong approach for LCI da ta uncertainty/DQR

counterclaims on data quality, data quality can be expert judged reliably, avoiding the pitfalls 
of the only seemingly sound curren t calculation approach and of Monte -Carlo based 
approaches. To make PEF study results sufficiently reliable and to incentivize a data market 
that improves the data quality, competition based on the data quality is indispensable. 
Sphera is a leading global provider of high quality LCI and EF datasets and we are willing to 
accept this independent, external and qualified (and possibly EC -run or accredited) approach 
to compete with our data in the market.

3.3 Circular footprint formula (CFF) / end of life modelling 
and crediting in general  
Issue: The CFF has been developed by  the EC in a longer effort with three stakeholder 
workshops, where a large number of approaches has been presented by the EC and 
participants; this was in reaction to fundamental flaws that had been found in the 2013 end -
of-life formula, the so -called “50: 50 approach”. The resulting and currently enforced 
formula, which is one of the EC proposed formula variants, lacks a systematic inner logic, but 
is instead combining its elements rather freely, leading to several relevant downsides: Firstly, 
from a practi cality, robustness and efficiency perspective,  the current approach to model 
end-of-life recycling and recycled content, the CFF, is complicated to use and hence costly 
and error -prone, since actually achieved benefits (e.g. 100% recycling at full quality)  are 
artificially reduced to a 80% credit, for example. This also means that the real benefits of full 
and high quality recycling are not adequately recognized. The formula is also distorted 
towards recycled content over recycling, as the factor (1 -A) is i n its logic of capturing the 
market situation of supply and demand of recycled materials almost fully redundant to the 
price ratio of recycled to primary materials (Q S/QP) (and hence correlated). As both are 
multiplied in the formula, the CFF shifts the re sults in favour of recycled content, which may 
come even basically burden free ( e.g., secondary post -consumer textile fibres, which is 
appropriate), while the maximum recycling credits at End -of-life are capped to 80%, even at 
perfect recycling; 100% credi t would be a fair equivalent to burden -free recycled content. It 
is important to understand that this only seemingly small difference of 20% can mean that 
the impacts of the product can be several times higher than with a full credit (where 
appropriate; an example here would be a metal sheet made from 100% primary metal and 
recycled at close to 100% and full quality). At the same time, the CFF gives 100% credit to 
recovered energy, as the factor B is set to zero in all cases, hence is biased – all other aspects 
the same – towards waste -to-energy over recycling. The factor “A” is problematic also for 
another reason: This is a market-based parameter which is incoherent with the attributional 
approach of the entire EF. While we argue that A should also be 0, at  the moment having 
only B = 0 is inconsistent with the approach for materials (factor A). Energy recovery also 
lacks the Qs/Qp factor, which in some cases may inappropriately further favour energy 
recovery; the distinction between material recycling and en ergy recovery (and parts reuse, 
which is currently to be modelled using the material part of the formula), could even all be 
joined, leading to a further simplified formula and consistency. The CFF also has an 
unnecessary consequential LCI modelling aspect  in it making it scientifically incoherent and 
inconsistent for the attributional approach of the EF: For open loop recycling, it gives the

primary energy carrier, in analogy to materials. A sentence in the text body p rescribes this, 
but in fact means that a formula variant is to be used. This does not correct the other flaws, 
but only one, while it adds complexity and another potential error source.  
Solution:  We propose the following modified approach and formula. It is simpler to use 
and to review, scientifically sound, and also fair across competing materials and energy 
carriers and different end -of-life treatments. It fully captures the material and product 
specific different benefits of using recycled content and achieving high recycling rates and 
quality. This approach is the combination of the Integrated formula IF+ 
(https://makiconsulting.com/2016/09/12/improved-umbrella-formula-uf-and-integrated-
formula-if/), that was already proposed during the EF pilot phase workshops and had been 
tested for practicality. In further development, we recom mend always using the logic 
proposed for open-loop recycling in a journal article by Koffler&Finkbeiner (2018),  
https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/are-we-still-keeping-it-real-proposing-a-
revisedparadigm-for-re/15142436. In this new formula, A and B are always 0 and the 
downcycling quotient Qs/Qp is defined as either the economic value ratio of secondary to 
primary material (i.e. the default approach also currently used) or the amount -ratio (e.g. 
mass) of secondary to primary material that is being replaced on average. Annex C of the PEF 
Method which currently provides default values for A can also still be used, but instead the 
values would be the default Qs/Qp values. This combined approach solves each and any of 
the listed issues of the current approach, by a quite minor modification and also simplifying 
the open loop part of the formula ( i.e., E*v is always Ev). Another advantage is that this 
formula is mathematically identical to the modular variant i n the annex of EN 15804 (while 
that one still foresees using future replaced materials in open -loop recycling, as it is derived 
from and mathematically identical to the IF+ formula). We note that the level -1 partly 
aggregated datasets allow for simply adju sting the CFF to the proposed corrected formula, 
by setting A to 0 and using the A values of Annex C of the PEF Method as Qs/Qp values. So, 
the EF 3.0 secondary datasets can be adjusted, and new aggregated ones generated without 
any actually remodelling, b ut by changing two parameter values. Further testing of this 
formula is not needed, as it has been used and tested extensively already, also in the PEF 
pilot context; the additional principle to always model closed loop is also not different from 
what is k nown and also applied in most cases anyway, and hence does not warrant any 
testing.

3.4 Biogenic carbon (EN 15804)  
Issue: The way how biogenic carbon uptake and release (mainly CO 2, CH 4) is currently 
allowed e.g., for food products under the EF in the  so-called “simplified approach”, is not 
only a key method difference to EN 15804, but also generally inferior: biogenic CO 2 uptake 
and release as CO 2 is only implicitly considered in the PEF as “CO 2-neutral”. I.e., emissions of 
C as CO 2 that stem from a b iogenic C source somewhere upstream have no impact and – 
importantly - do not matter in decision making. This, even though it is the very same CO 2 
molecule that is emitted into the atmosphere and where capturing and storing, reduction or 
even avoidance of the emission would actually mean the same environmental advantage as 
for the fossil CO 2 emission. In other words: While the overall LCIA result may be the same as 
explicit modelling uptake and release, the “climate neutral” approach to biogenic carbon 
modelling is entirely unsuitable for decision support. The previous EF approach was more 
suitable and may have been changed due to lobbying or from communication perspective: 
The main argument in favour of the current EF approach is that consumers may get the 
wrong message if PEF results do not cover the full life cycle, namely carry negative CO 2 
emissions and lower -than-zero climate impacts due to uptake of biogenic CO 2 from the air. 
This however implies that consumers should get such results of partial life c ycles, that are 
anyway not foreseen in EF studies with their cradle to grave approach (except for 
intermediate products, but these are not for communication towards final consumers).  
Solution: Revert to the explicit consideration of biogenic CO 2 uptake an d release with 
corresponding Climate change CFs of -1 and +1 i.e., use the EN 15804 approach. PEF study 
reports on intermediate products ( e.g., metals sheets) or food products will exclusively need 
to have both the results of the partial life cycle and a d efault consideration of the end-of-life 
emissions. For consumers, only the full life cycle results are to be communicated (unless 
anyway only a logo or label would be used, without numbers). Even better, consumer 
information can be added how their behaviou r will result in less (also biogenic) CO 2 
emissions and hence in better or less good overall life cycle performance e.g. , via indication 
of the effect of increasing recycling rates of wood-based products, and the like.

3.6 Level-1 datasets  
Issue: Next to aggregated LCI results datasets (such as “ecoprofiles” of producing e.g., 1 kg 
of a specific plastic material), the PEF always foresees corresponding so -called level-1 partly 
aggregated datasets (in fact these are little models). The purpose of these partly aggregated 
datasets has been to break open the last step of the product ion of these products, singling 
out the individual energy carriers and parameterized transport steps that are used, as well as 
partly relevant included materials of the resulting product (in so far they may be produced 
also from secondary materials,) plus the corresponding end -of-life treatment data sets for 
production waste of these materials. All other materials of the BOM and consumables are 
represented by one – typically, but not always - combined aggregated dataset. Finally, the 
foreground emissions of  the last step are captured in another distinct dataset. The original 
idea of having these models was to allow suppliers to adjust these little models by adjusting 
the transport parameters to their individual distance or other conditions, to change the 
transport mode or sourced energy, change recycled content. And to change the direct 
emissions of the last step i.e., their manufacturing to represent their production. Before 
explaining the downsides of this approach in terms of relevance, accuracy and 
reproducibility/neutrality, we would like to make clear that from all the EF pilot phase 
datasets that have been tendered, only a small share has been approved and made available 
for PEF studies as level -1 datasets. It is also important to understand that the ef fort to 
prepare these level-1 data sets is more than half of the overall effort. In other words, about

18  
  
half of the resources and produced EF pilot secondary datasets are not used.  Now on the 
downsides of these datasets/models: The PEF method data needs ma trix foresees for 
Situation 2, Option 2: “ Use an EF -compliant secondary dataset and apply company -specific 
activity data for transport (distance), and substitute the sub-processes used for electricity mix 
and transport with supply-chain specific EF compliant datasets (DQR≤3.0).”  
Now, the production at a supplier will differ in many other ways from the one used in the EF 
secondary dataset model. Changing exclusively the formally foreseen “activity data” and 
other parts of the dataset/model may be beneficial  for some producers, why they would 
select this option (situation 2, option 2), while their true, overall product impact may be 
higher. What is missing is also adjusting the site’s own foreground emissions, replacing other 
fuels than electricity, and the m aterials that enter the product. To give a concrete example: 
the metal part production of brass, copper, zinc, aluminium largely goes through a melting 
process. Some companies use electricity to melt the metal, others natural gas. If only the 
electricity a mount and source is changed, this would mean that suppliers that use natural 
gas, would reduce the electricity amounts to close to zero, while not adding the natural gas. 
Also, the additional emissions from the gas burning would not be considered. The revi ewer 
may point out a limited technological representativeness, but the key problem is that such 
partial solutions lack reproducibility and allow results optimization or at least selection of a 
more favourable option (1 or 2).   
Solution:  It is necessary t o make sure all relevant differences at any specific supplier are 
captured. That means effectively, that the supplier anyway needs to have all production 
information available. In that case it is better to just develop a new EF compliant dataset that 
then fully represents the supplier. To do so starting from a level -1 partly aggregated dataset 
costs a lot and helps little. Such supplier -specific datasets could even be made available to 
feed into a growing, common EF secondary database. Hence, we recommend removing the 
option to use level -1 partly aggregated datasets for PEF studies altogether, with the 
exception of energy, transport and end -of-life datasets (i.e., also excluding packaging): Here 
the level-1 datasets face the described issues less strongly. W here supplier production is not 
well represented by the suitable EF background dataset, and also, as a new element, where 
the contribution to the overall EF single point result is above a certain threshold, the supplier 
should be required to provide a new,  supplier-specific EF -compliant dataset i.e. , to select 
option 1. Another benefit of removing this option 2 (and the various other related and even 
more specific rules on adjusting DQR calculations) is the contribution to the much -needed 
reduction in complexity of the PEF (including in PEF enabling SME tools).

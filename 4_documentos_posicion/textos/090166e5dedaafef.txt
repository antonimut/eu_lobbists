EIGA Position on Gas Market Reform 
 
The European Industrial Gases Association, EIGA, is pleased to share its views on public consultation on gas market 
reform. As EIGA member companies are the only ones already operating hydrogen infrastructure in Europe today, 
we believe we are uniquely placed to comment on the steps needed to develop the hydrogen market.  
 
Supplementing the points we have raised as part of our consultation, EIGA would like to further comment on a 
number of specific points of interest.  
 
Safety and technical expertise 
 
EIGA supports the vision of the European Commission in recognizing the role of hydrogen in a climate -neutral 
Europe and at the same time underlines the safety-first approach which must be taken when scaling up hydrogen 
operations. While hydrogen as an ener gy vector has many advantages, the risks are well documented and 
mitigated in daily operations of EIGA members. EIGA cautions that public confidence in hydrogen cannot be 
assured, especially in the early stages, and will only be built through a robust appr oach to safety. Because safety 
incidents with hydrogen have a number of specific characteristics, EIGA underlines the need for Gas Market 
Reform to be complemented by provisions for robust safety information and procedures for users, carriers and 
first responders.  
 
For its part, EIGA has a library on hydrogen safety and is contributing to a worldwide repository of safety 
information which can support the European Commission in this area. We would be pleased to share more of this 
work as it continues.  
 
EIGA member companies are also familiar with the diversity of current usages of hydrogen (mainly industrial, but 
also increasingly for transportation), understand the hydrogen consumers’ requirements (notably in terms of 
quality and reliability) and are able to properly design the industrial systems that will meet such users’ 
requirements. EIGA would be pleased to further share elements of this technical expertise with the European 
Commission, notably with regards to the quality of hydrogen (see details bel ow). 
 
Low carbon hydrogen as a facilitator for the green hydrogen 
 
EIGA believes both green and low -carbon hydrogen are essential contributors to reach the European Union’s 
carbon neutrality targets. The development of the hydrogen economy will be defined by scarcity. The significant 
expected uptake of hydrogen in industrial applications can only be achieved with ready supplies of low -carbon 
hydrogen to complement renewable hydrogen . To kick start the development of hydrogen applications, and 
encourage investment in hydrogen infrastructure, supplies of renewable and of low carbon hydrogen are needed. 
Industrial applications especially will rely on continuity of supply which cannot be guaranteed by renewable 
hydrogen alone for the foreseeable fu ture. Renewable hydrogen will increasingly be prioritized in consumption, 
even as low-carbon hydrogen ensures system reliability and security of supply.  
 
EIGA members are amongst the largest investors today in electrolysers for renewable hydrogen production, and 
firmly believe in their potential as part of a decarbonized economy. We further recognize that industrial sectors 
are urgently demanding flows of l ow-carbon fuels and feedstocks as they pioneer advanced production 
technologies. Low-carbon hydrogen derived from fossil fuels , in combination with abatement technologies like

The important role of private networks in our economy 
 
EIGA recognizes the complexity of the growing hydrogen ecosystem, and the balance that must be struck between 
acknowledging existing infrastructure which serves specific purposes (i.e. connecting industrial producers and 
consumers thereby meeting the techn ical specifications necessary for their processes),  while growing a public, 
liquid market for hydrogen to ensure its mainstreaming in new areas of the economy.  
 
Existing private hydrogen networks, linked with dedicated hydrogen production, have been devel oped in a 
competitive market place to serve mainly industrial customers in the most cost -efficient way, meeting the 
technical specifications necessary for their processes, thereby making regulatory intervention unnecessary. The 
importance of private networks can be summarized under three key principles: quality, stability, and liability .  
 
Quality: Industrial consumers of hydrogen and other gases as both fuels and feedstocks have stringent 
requirements for gas quality. EIGA members are responsible for delivering these gases at precise levels of quality, 
with variations incurring significant costs. The consequences of a variation in hydrogen quality can include 
unnecessary downtime at a facility, spoiling of goods and other feedstocks, and damage to equipment .  
 
An example is hydrogenation, which is used extensively in the food industry to make a large variety of goods, like 
spreads and shortenings, from liquid oils. This process requires very pure hydrogen to preserve the life of the 
catalyst; itself often a precious metal like palladium. Variations in hydrogen quality delivered to these processes 
could spoil product, damage catalysts and result in financial losses.  
 
Stability: EIGA members are also responsible for ensuring security of supply to their customers. With established 
value chains in interconnected industrial ecosystems, IG companies can, to a reasonable extent, guarantee supply 
to consumers who rely on an unbroken flo w of feedstock and fuel. Moreover, with clear oversight of the entire 
value chain, from the production to the delivery of hydrogen at precise pressures and purities, EIGA members can 
manage the risks that come with the responsibility of delivering for our customers.  
 
Liability: Following from the above points, it is important to underline that a key element of EIGA Members’ 
relationships with their customers is a clear designation of liability. Where there are shortcomings in supply or 
variations in quality for whatever reason, at any point in the value chain, contractual relationships are explicit on 
the distribution of costs and liability. This gives assurances to customers and reduces risk across the system. Such 
a defined set of responsibilities would be impossible in a public hydrogen network.  
 
Private markets should be respected : Following from these three considerations we propose that, with the 
framework of a generally regulated future hydrogen infrastructure, private networks will require special provisions 
in order to avoid roadblocks to existing and future industrial clusters. Such provisions should be structured for the 
long term, rather than temporary, for as long as the relevant regulatory authorities do not identify market failures.

We also highlight the importance of maintaining the level playing field between the self -production model for 
hydrogen vs. the outsourcing model where the consumer is connected to a third- party producer via a private

Finally, EIGA understands and believes that the continued operation of private networks should not undermine 
the ramp-up and development of the hydrogen market as a whole. We remain open to working constructively 
with the Commission to support the development of a robust and open market for hydrogen.  
 
Hydrogen Quality – Standards for public markets 
 
An important factor to be considered as hydrogen markets are developed will be the appropriate quality and 
purity standards. These will be influenced by cost, demand- side requirements, supply- side feasibility and 
infrastructure needs. At the volumes needed for a fully scaled hydrogen market, it is possible that the optimum 
purity will be combustion grade hydrogen. This would be appropriate for industrial heat, though not as a feedstock, 
while being economical to supply. At the same time, fuel cells and specific applications in industry –  some 
feedstocks for chemicals for example – will require higher purit ies. For t his reason, sufficient flexibility must be 
given to hydrogen suppliers and their customers to determine needs and deliver hydrogen through dedicated, 
private infrastructure.  
 
EIGA would be happy to further discuss and provide evidence on this point, including with specifics on fuel grades 
and implications.  
 
Furthermore, the EU should set out a clear framework assigning responsibilities and liabilities with regard to gas 
quality for market participants. Lacking a clear liability framework adds complexity t o the interactions between 
market participants, and will slow down both intra - and extra-EU trade. This framework should be established at 
the EU level, and infrastructure operators should be obligated, to the greatest extent possible, to carry hydrogen 
which complies with quality standards.  
 
Responsibilities within Hydrogen Markets 
 
EIGA would like to underline the continuing importance of the principles set out within the Third Energy Package. 
The continued unbundling of infrastructure owners/operators and fuel suppliers must continue if the hydrogen 
market is to grow in a competitiv e way. Specifically, EIGA would like to stress that, due to the role and 
responsibilities of Transmission System Operators (TSOs) at the centre of the energy system, they should continue 
to be precluded from the production and ownership of fuels. This should be reflected in principle as well as in law, 
and applied to all companies operating under the umbrella of a TSO; that is, TSOs should not be allowed to 
establish separate legal entities for the purpose of producing or trading in hydrogen.  
 
This princip le is echoed in many of the specific questi ons highlighted within the consultation. For example, 
scenario planning for infrastructure development, network development plans and the growth of cross -border 
trade will all be impacted if those responsible for ensuring a level playing field are also active in the market.

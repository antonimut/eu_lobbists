 
Hydrogen and Gas markets Decarbonisation Package 
The Clean Air Task Force (CATF) welcomes the European Commission’s 
climate ambition, leadership on decarbonisation and innovation, and pursuit of 
policies to decouple greenhouse gas emissions from economic growth. CATF 
welcomes the revision of the Gas Regulation and the Gas Directive t o ensure 
compatibility with climate neutrality, and to facilitate the competitive market entry 
of renewable and zero-carbon gases.  
It is anticipated that some sectors of the European economy will continue to 
require gaseous fuel even in a high electrified future. The existing gas network 
may be the most efficient way to deliver gaseous molecules to those sectors 
where electrification alone may not be sufficient to achieve the carbon reduction 
targets. Additionally, it is likel y that the existing gas network will be the most 
efficient manner to deliver hydrogen to new sectors, such as long -haul freight. 
Therefore, it is crucial that a robust and resilient climate strategy is in place to 
transport and delivery hydrogen through the gas networks.  
The revision of the Gas Package is an opportunity to create the  necessary 
framework to decarbonise the gas system by allowing the  uptake of renewable 
and low-carbon hydrogen and remove potential barriers to the deployment of the 
hydrogen as a decarbonisation enabler. 
The gas package revision should also address the issue of methane emissions, 
in accordance with the Methane Strategy, and to facilitate the deployment of 
carbon capture and storage technologies in the EU. 
I. Ensuring the fast uptake of low-carbon hydrogen 
CATF considers that aligning energy markets to the ambitions of the Green Deal 
and implement ing the Hydrogen Strategy should be key priorit ies for the EU 
institutions to achieve the EU climate targets.  
Today gaseous fuel in the EU account for 22% of the total energy consumption. 
It is estimated that by 2050, the share of low-carbon and renewable hydrogen in 
Europe’s final energy demand could be approximately 15%1 or more. Therefore, 
low-carbon and renewable hydrogen are expected to  become part of the EU 
energy mix and are key to decarbonise hard -to-abate sectors where direct 
electrification is not likely to offer an efficient, economical or short -term viable 
solution to replace fossil fuels.  
Hydrogen Certification 
An EU hydrogen certification scheme based on lifecycle GHG emissions is 
required to s upport the development of a transparent and technology neutral 
hydrogen market , and to promote the uptake of renewable and low -carbon 
hydrogen into the EU energy mix. Hydrogen certification should be denominated 
primarily in terms of carbon dioxide equivalent per unit of hydrogen, rather than

Infrastructure  
The deployment of low -carbon gases will require the development of the 
necessary infrastructure to transport renewable and low -carbon hydrogen to 
support the EU decarbonisation targets. CATF supports the repurposing of the 
existing gas infrastructure to transport low-carbon and renewable hydrogen  in 
areas where  a holistic assessment, including  technical, safety and economic 
analysis indicate that repurposing is the most effective scenario. This includes 
the repurposing of  networks that may otherwise be decommissioned. The 
assessment should include an integrated infrastructure planning that includes all 
the energy mix components and prevents the continued used of unabated fossil 
energy (lock-in). 
It is expected that an international hydrogen trade wi ll emerge as Europe 
pursues the decarbonisation of its economy. Infrastructure readiness to receive 
hydrogen will be crucial in the future. CATF calls for developing the necessary 
framework to facilitate low-carbon and renewable gas imports into Europe. CATF 
believes that all options should be assessed, including determining the role that 
LNG terminals and similar infrastructure could play in the energy transition.  
Furthermore, storage facilities are likely to be required in the EU due to the 
projected adoption of hydrogen as a decarbonisation enabler. The gas package 
revision should include hydrogen storage regulation , building on the existing 
methane storage regulation.  
Quality Standards 
The deployment of hydrogen as a component of the EU energy mix will require 
gas quality  standards management  and standardisation. CATF calls for the 
setting on harmonised standards for hydrogen across the EU . This will support 
efficient coordination across member states and facilitate cross-border hydrogen 
trade. However, a distinction should be made for other uses of hydrogen , such 
as in industrial facilities  and private networks , where quality levels depend on 
the design characteristics of industrial installations and should not be subject to 
gas quality standardisation rules. 
Hydrogen Market Access 
Production sites should have access to the gas infrastructure including pipelines 
and storage facilities. CATF calls for the creation of a regulatory framework that 
supports the uptake of low-carbon and renewable gases. This includes ensuring 
transparency and competition between energy suppliers and providing network 
access to third parties.  
 
II. A performance standard to tackle global methane emissions 
While the consumption of unabated gas will need to drop as fast as possible if 
the EU wants to reach its climate targets. However, based on the Commission’s 
projections, gas will be present in the energy mix until 2050 . In the Methane 
Strategy, the Commission announced that it will examine options regarding 
possible methane emissions reduction targets, standards or other incentives on 
fossil energy consumed and imported into the EU. In addition to the MRV and 
LDAR requirements currently being dev eloped by the Commission, CATF is 
therefore asking the Commission to seize the opportunity of the revision of the 
Gas Package to adopt a methane pro duct standard on all gas sold and 
consumed in the EU.  
Why is a performance standard needed? 
First, a product standard on methane would be needed to align the Gas Package 
and the EU gas system with the EU climate targets. So far, the EU has not 
sufficiently regulated its methane emissions: methane is not effectively covered 
by an emission trading scheme or a price on fossil gas reflecting the negative 
externalities related to methane emissions , despite the availability of existing 
cost-effective technologies to address most of the methane emissions from the 
fossil energy sector.  
Since methane is 86 times more potent than carbon dioxide over a 20 -year 
period but does not accumulate in the atmosphere, cutting methane emissions 
would show results for the climate in the short-term and be key to slow down the 
rate of  climate change  and avoid some tipping points  of global warming. A 
methane performance standard will be needed for the EU to reach its climate 
goals. 
Second, as the EU imports about 80% of its fossil gas , over half of all globally 
traded fossil gas are due to EU imports . Most of the methane emissions due to 
EU consumption take place outside of its borders and the EU is consequently 
driving global methane emissions. However, the position of the EU as a main  
buyer of gas also means that the EU could lead the way in addressing global 
methane emissions by adopting a performance standard.  
With a methane performance standard, in addition to the MRV and LDAR 
requirements and a ban on venting and flaring , the EU could strongly incentive 
methane emissions reductions throughout the EU fossil energy supply chain . A 
methane product standard  would create a  clear benchmark for reducing 
methane leakage throughout the whole supply chain of companies wishing to 
export toward the EU market. This would match the EU ambition in fostering 
international changes to address climate change. 
Third, a performance standard would send a strong signal to gas and hydrogen 
companies and foster innovation and investment in methane -cutting 
technologies. An EU product standard with clear deadlines would create the 
regulatory and legal certainty needed for companies to innovate and invest as 
 
they could otherwise loose an important market  and suffer commercial losses. 
By creating the needed incentive to the broad deployment of methane -cutting 
technologies, the EU is likely to contribute in  bringing down their costs and 
encourage further innovation to improve cost-efficiency. 
What would a methane product standard look like? 
CATF believes that the Commission should adopt a  mandatory methane 
emissions performance standard effective on all gas sold in the Single Market 
from January 2025.  The upstream oil and gas industry has indicated that it can 
achieve a methane intensity of 0.2% by 2025. We consider this an appropriate 
minimum standard that the Commission should adopt, at least initially, along with 
commensurate midstream and downstream limits . To ensure compliance by 
2025, the EU should adapt this performance standard as soon as possible as a 
condition of market access under the Gas Package.  
The methane product standard should cover all gas and gas-derived hydrogen 
exported into and traded within the EU.  It should apply to upstream, midstream 
and downstream methane emissions. CATF suggests a methane emissions limit 
applying to importers, energy wholesalers, and retail suppliers. From 1 January 
2025, they should be able to demonstrate  compliance of the ir purchased gas 
with the methane product standard. 
As available technologies are developing fast, the methane standards should be 
regularly updated to ensure that the ambition of the performance standards 
matches the existing technologies. 
Five foundational mechanisms would enable the implementation of a methane 
performance standard:   
1) An EC-administered body working in coordination with ACER and the NRAs 
and acting as a European Methane Emissions Observatory . It should  be 
integrated in the existing EU Energy Markets Observatory administered by DG 
ENER and mandated to collect, audit and make public measurement data 
available through IMEO and other public and private methane emissions 
data providers;  
2) A clear mandate enabling both A CER and the NRAs to act as well as 
adequate resources to execute on their function;  
3) A mandatory methane procurement standard requiring EU shippers and gas 
buyers to purchase 100% of their  fossil gas volumes  from producers that are 
shown to be emitting no more than 0.2% methane as of 1 January 2025;   
4) A legal base enabling NRAs, national and regional governments to administer 
proportional and dissuasive penalties such as taxes, levies or tradeable permits 
on all non -compliant gas transactions, including through pipeline, LNG, long -
term contract or spot.  
 
5) A coordination between the methane strategy, including any methane 
performance standard, and the hydrogen strategy to ensure that hydrogen 
produced from methane, either domestic ally or imported, should not be 
considered “low -carbon hydrogen” unless upstream methane emissions are 
addressed and meet any standards or requirements that will be issued via the 
methane strategy.  
Carbon capture and storage 
According to nearly all modelling scenarios, reaching climate neutrality by 2050 
and decarbonising the economy will require the development and deployment at 
scale of carbon capture, removal, and storage technologies. CO 2 infrastructure 
will therefore be required  for large-scale decarbonisation. The revision of the 
Gas Package is an opportunity to review the conditions for access to the natural 
gas transmission networks  and expand relevant elements of the existing gas 
regulatory framework to facilitate the deployment of CO2 infrastructure for carbon 
capture and storage.  
To match the EU climate ambition, the development of the CO2 infrastructure 
should take place in parallel with the development of the hydrogen one. To 
facilitate the fast uptake of carbon capture and storage technologies, the 
Commission should consider a change in the scope of the Directive to expand 
the transportation of gas to CO2 in order to allow transmission system operators 
(TSO) to transport the CO2. Since storage is not available in all Member States, 
cross-border transport of CO 2 will be needed  and i t is possible that gas 
infrastructure companies will play a role in providing CO2 transportation services. 
To do this effectively, such companies should be able to offer CO 2 transport 
services to the market, based on a clear legal basis and this may require an 
amendment to the Gas Directive. The legal mandate of the National Regulatory 
Authorities (NRAs) should also be expanded to cover CO2 transport. 
To ensure a level playing field, a regulated approach to CO2 transport would also 
be needed to ensure  transparency and competition , based on  flexibility, non-
discriminatory third-party access, and regulated tariffs.

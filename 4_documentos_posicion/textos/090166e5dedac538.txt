Equinor welcomes the opportunity to participate in the consultation to revise the Gas Directive and Gas 
Regulation in the context of the EU’s climate change mitigation ambitions. To drastically cut GHG 
emissions in line with the 2030 and 2050 targets, an enabling legislative framework for low carbon and 
renewable gases is fundamental, in particular to decarbonise the hard-to-abate sectors. Fair 
competition between effective, efficient and affordable low-carbon energy technologies needs to be 
maintained in the EU’s new decarbonisation legislation. We note and welcome the recognition of this 
principle made in the Inception Impact Assessment published in March 2021.  
In response to the consultation on the Hydrogen and gas Market Decarbonisation Package, we call on 
the EU Commission to establish a policy framework that 
• recognises that establishing an open and fully-functioning liquid decarbonised gas market 
will take time;  
• is inclusive for all decarbonisation solutions irrespective of origin and technology, and 
incentivises their deployment based on efficiency and GHG emissions reduction potential; 
• includes a clear and comprehensive terminology for renewable and low carbon gases to be 
used across all legislative proposals; 
• establishes a comprehensive, manageable, EU-wide cross-sector certification scheme under 
one primary legislative proposal; and 
• enables all infrastructure development, repurposing and retrofitting alternatives to drive 
decarbonisation, supply and demand build, and ultimately market liquidity. 
 
We further detail these points below within the framework and structure of the consultation. 
I.  General questions on revising the Gas Directive and Gas Regulation 
In addition to carbon pricing measures under the EU ETS, the EU’s 2030 legislative framework will 
include more regulatory measures such as setting targets and quota for clean energy. We acknowledge 
the rationale of such an approach as it can help achieve the deployment of clean energy in the absence 
of a sufficiently high carbon price. However, we regret that the EU’s clean energy targets are not 
open to non-renewable low carbon technologies. As a result, such technologies as carbon capture 
and storage (CCS) and low-carbon hydrogen made from natural gas are not incentivised sufficiently to 
trigger the required emissions reductions in the hard-to-decarbonise sectors. We believe that together 
with a reinforced EU ETS, the Decarbonisation Package can and should include measures open to 
all low-carbon technologies irrespective of origin and based on their efficiency and life-cycle 
GHG emissions reduction potential. Such measures are needed to create initial market demand for 
low-carbon products in the industrial, energy storage and transport applications. 
As outlined in the 2030 Climate Target Plan Impact Assessment, natural gas will continue to play a key 
role in EU energy mix well into the 2030s. However, we would like to stress that in order to enable 
scalable decarbonisation of the gas use thereafter, an appropriate legislative framework is needed 
already today. If gaseous fuels were to maintain a share of over 300 Mtoe in final energy use by 2050 – 
as suggested in the Impact Assessment – the realistic potential for biomethane and renewable 
hydrogen will prove insufficient to decarbonise a considerable part of gaseous energy use. Due to their 
scalability potential and relative affordability, both CCS and low-carbon hydrogen from natural gas are 
needed in addition. To demonstrate their scalability and decarbonisation potential, these technologies 
must be introduced in industrial scale projects within this decade. In Equinor’s view, this can only be 
achieved by establishing frameworks allowing all low-carbon and renewable technologies to contribute

An ambitious 2030 and 2050 decarbonisation agenda implies that the EU has little time to realise 
effective decarbonisation under a limited set of technological solutions. We therefore recommend that 
clean energy targets and quotas be open to both renewable and low-carbon solutions and focus 
on GHG emissions reduction. Such a framework inclusive of all decarbonisation solutions would not 
necessitate technology-specific targets. 
However, if technology-specific targets that are already in place are to be continued or set for 
renewable gases and/or renewable electrolyser capacity, then to secure a level playing field reciprocal 
targets should be set for low-carbon gases to help deliver on the decarbonisation targets. Failing to do 
this would not provide sufficient investment signals needed to deploy non-renewable decarbonisation 
solutions necessary to set the EU on the climate neutrality pathway by 2050. 
II. Consumer’s choice and renewable and low-carbon gases 
Affordability is key: policies should aim to deliver GHG emissions reduction at the lowest possible cost. 
The “Hydrogen 4 EU” study1, conducted by IFP Énergies Nouvelles (IFPEN) , SINTEF and 
Deloitte, finds that “the contribution of hydrogen to decarbonizing the EU’s energy system can exceed 
EU projections, with demand potentially exceeding 100 million tonnes of H2 by 2050. It also stresses 
the essential role [that] low-carbon hydrogen (which can be produced from natural gas in combination 
with CCS, or methane pyrolysis) can play in supporting the deployment of renewable hydrogen and the 
integration of higher volumes of renewable energy overall. 
In both scenarios, renewable hydrogen production scales up rapidly after 2030. However, a more 
diverse technology mix enables a lower-cost (€1 trillion less from now until 2050) and a more effective 
pathway to EU climate neutrality by 2050.” Enabling all GHG emissions reduction technologies to 
compete and supporting the deployment of the most emissions-efficient by setting a framework to 
incentivise the market/consumers to select the best solution will enable such substantial savings.  
Demand-side policy mechanisms are best suited to enable member states to opt for the most cost-
efficient technologies to ensure scale-up.  Such mechanisms include demand-side targets, which 
already apply to renewable energy but should be expanded to include low-carbon solutions with 
efficient life-cycle GHG emissions savings. Such mechanisms should also include demand-side funding 
opportunities to enable the transition to low-carbon solutions in specific sectors.  
As consumer choices are the most impactful drivers, we underline the need for consumer-side targets 
and not quotas and/or targets imposed on producers, as consumer-side targets would lead to the most 
cost-efficient market solutions for decarbonisation. Such demand-side policy mechanisms should 
incentivise the consumption of carbon neutral gases irrespective of their production technology. The 
industrial sector, in particular, would need appropriate funding opportunities in the form of carbon 
contracts for difference or other, in addition to the funding available under the Innovation Fund. The 
Decarbonisation Package should guide EU industrial decarbonisation policy and support schemes to 
encourage member states to make the best use of the funding available through the sale of EU ETS 
allowances. Cost-efficient and affordable industrial decarbonisation solutions are key to scaling up the 
use of renewable and low-carbon solutions and to preserve and create jobs in Europe’s industrial 
sectors.   
A consumer-side funding approach would be the most favourable for preserving a technology-open 
approach to hydrogen market development. This would also be coherent with consumer-side targets. 
To ensure affordability, policies should be balanced in sharing the transition costs and protect the 
European consumers from unaffordable transition policies as much as possible. This implies that the

IV. Hydrogen infrastructure and a hydrogen market 
Carbon capture and storage: development of a European CO2 network 
The consultation does not cover CCS or CO2 network development, while large-scale CCS deployment 
will prove important for decarbonising the EU’s hard-to-abate sectors. Furthermore, development of a 
European CCS network should be considered from an integrated system point of view and in line with 
power and gas network decarbonisation. A number of scalable European CCS projects of common 
interest under the fourth PCI list attest the relevance of the CCS technology in industrial applications as 
well as the need to consider CO2 network development for the production of low-carbon hydrogen in a 
more structured and integrated European energy infrastructure planning framework.  
Principles for regulation of infrastructure developments  
Enabling the gradual development of the hydrogen market and infrastructure should guide future 
legislation of hydrogen and gas networks. The Third Energy package can be a model for a hydrogen 
market, but the low level of market maturity should be recognised, and regulation should be 
implemented in sync with stages of market maturity rather than fully from the beginning. While this 
implies a fit-for-purpose regulatory approach, visibility, predictability and stability are required. 
In this context, it is essential that firm regulatory principles be established early on (ex. ownership 
unbundling, 3rd party access, transparency, non-discrimination), that a regulatory roadmap be 
developed from the outset and for authorities to have a collaborative and closely involved approach. 
Building on the achievements of the Internal Gas Market, the regulatory framework for decarbonisation 
of the gas sector should enable all infrastructure alternatives: 
• building new dedicated hydrogen pipelines, 
• repurposing existing gas networks to 100% hydrogen, and 
• blending hydrogen in natural gas networks. 
 
Selecting the appropriate alternative should be based on their decarbonisation and cost efficiency merit 
and best fit, on a case-by-case basis, without jeopardising the security of supply of the natural gas 
network and the liquidity of the natural gas market. 
V. Access of renewable and low carbon gases to the existing methane gas networks and 
markets, including LNG terminals and gas storages 
Questions 68-69 (re)introduce the idea of an inter-TSO compensation mechanism allowing for the 
abolishment of IP tariffs to eliminate the so-called ‘pancaking’ effect. This was already discussed and 
dismissed in 2018 as a result of the Quo Vadis study by REKK & EY, where the potential redistribution 
effects did not justify the risks and complexity of setting up an inter-TSO compensation mechanism. It 
showed that the NWE market is already largely integrated and that it could be more beneficial to foster 
regional mergers. We strongly suggest continuing to focus on implementation of the 3rd Energy 
Package instead.  
IPs provide important investment signals for the gradual retrofitting of the existing natural gas grid into a 
dedicated hydrogen backbone, where the system is under-utilised based on natural gas transportation 
alone (e.g. the Dutch Lo-Cal gas pipelines). 
As highlighted in the ESI and Hydrogen Strategies, the nascent and still-yet-to be created hydrogen 
market is expected to first develop around pockets of industrial clusters and local TSOs will most likely 
develop hydrogen backbones. Without IPs, nominal and/or physical congestion signals will not be 
apparent, potentially undermining needed investment signals.

Definitions and classification of renewable & low carbon gases 
Colour-coding hydrogen and other renewable and low-carbon gases will not suffice for standardisation 
and use of such gases in the networks. We strongly recommend that the revision of the gas package 
includes a clear and comprehensive terminology for renewable and low carbon gases based on 
scientific characteristics and life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions to be used across all legislative 
proposals and ensure legal consistency between the 12 legislative packages currently under review. 
Guarantees of Origin (GO) & certification 
The issue of GOs was addressed multiple times during the Madrid Forum, but it is still unclear whether 
the intention is to set up one set of GOs, including full GHG Life Cycle Analysis, for renewable & low 
carbon gasses. We are concerned with the possible split of GO/certification coverage in various 
directives/regulations leading to potential gaps between the legislative files. 
A comprehensive, manageable, EU-wide cross-sector certification scheme needs to be established to 
evaluate and value standardised full life cycle GHG emissions intensity of all renewable and low-carbon 
energy carriers consolidated under one primary legislative proposal. The EU should also expand its 
certification scheme to address imports of hydrogen, alternative energy carriers and derived products to 
avoid unintended GHG emissions at locations where the energy is produced. 
Ensuring security of supply & demand 
The consultation includes questions related to information on the right to switch supplier, and 
specifically mentions “Abolish special treatment of fossil methane long-term contracts e.g. abolish 
derogations for take-or-pay clauses”. The market for low-carbon gases is at the very beginning of its 
development. With no market depth or liquidity, suppliers need security of demand and off-takers need 
security of supply. This cannot be achieved without certain volume constraint conditions in bilateral 
contracts placing obligations on the supplier with respect to availability of gas and the off-taker with 
respect to off-take commitments, or by establishing other fall-back or safeguard mechanisms. Imposing 
the end of volume commitments in bilateral contracts between producers, wholesalers, retailers and 
large industrial companies would undermine this objective of security of supply and demand. 
Blending of hydrogen  
The consultation appears to underestimate the complexity associated with blending of hydrogen in 
natural gas streams. There are potential challenges related to gas mix quality and stability, especially 
for cross-border flows and trade during the build-up phase as well as in the long-term, as the gaseous 
fuels mix evolves and natural gas imports continue.  
Developing value chains to convert the energy and feedstock in industrial clusters to hydrogen will 
provide substantial rapid emission reduction gains. Solutions for industrial clusters can serve as a 
backbone for further infrastructure development, that can reduce the ensuing costs of supplying low-
carbon gases to other sectors and more distributed consumers in transport, heating and power. 
Building on the achievements of the Internal Gas Market, the regulatory framework for decarbonisation 
of the gas sector should enable all infrastructure alternatives: 
• building new dedicated hydrogen pipelines, 
• repurposing existing gas networks to 100% hydrogen, and 
• blending hydrogen in natural gas networks. 
Selecting the appropriate alternative should be based on their decarbonisation and cost efficiency merit 
and best fit, on a case-by-case basis, without jeopardising the security of supply of the natural gas 
network and the liquidity of the natural gas market.

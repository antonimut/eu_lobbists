Question 33 :  What regulatory model at EU level do you consider suitable to foster the 
emergence of a well-functioning and competitive hydrogen market and hydrogen infrastructure? 
We need regulation to ensure “competition in the market”.  
A common approach is needed in which an EU legislative framework outlining key regulatory 
principles (such as neutrality of network operation, third party access, cost reflective and market 
compatible network tariffs, treatment of private networks) are set as networks can represent 
natural monopolies.  
The rules could be developed in a stepwise-but-coordinated approach. The need for detailed rules 
and their practicalities are indeed difficult to foresee in case of a nascent activity; not all options 
nor situations could be foreseen in advance. 
High-level regulatory principles as well as a minimum set of key technical rules are nevertheless 
needed at EU level from the start.  
Member States could develop more detailed technical rules if needed, in order to quick start the 
emergence of supply and demand for hydrogen, which are essential for the emergence of well-
functioning and competitive H2 markets. 
More detailed EU -wide technical rules  could then be developed progressively, building on the 
initial EU regulatory principles and on the best practices of Member States w.r.t. the detailed 
technical rules. 
When developing their detailed technical rules, Member States should however pay attention “a 
priori” to this consolidating phase in order to avoid difficult-to-reconciliate regulatory divergences 
between Member States and the creation of investment uncertainty. 
The underlying principle is that some regulatory flexibility is needed to kick-start the development 
of the European hydrogen market and infrastructure.

Question 42:  Should (regulated) network operators (e.g. gas, electricity or hydrogen 
TSOs/DSOs) have a role in Power-to-gas installations (i.e. electrolysers)? 
The key principle is that n etwork operators should not own, develop, manage or operate 
power-to-gas installations.  
Investment and management of power-to-gas installations should primarily be market-based and 
open to competition among market players. Investment by regulated entities could discourage 
investments by market participants and create competition distortions.  To avoid conflicts of 
interest and market foreclosure, system operators should be precluded from investing in and 
running power-to-gas installations except in clearly defined circumstances (time, scope, etc.). This 
requirement is similar to the case of electricity storages in the Electricity Directive (Directive EU 
2019/944). 
 
The development of market-based projects related to energy grid operations (e.g. grid connection 
and grid services, like balancing provision)  requires that s ystem operators are making explicit 
their needs in terms of flexibility procurement to the market players. This transparency on network 
needs is a key requirement to foster the development of power-to-gas installations, but also of 
electricity/energy storages, demand response and gas-to-power installations (i.e. power plants). 
 
By way of derogation, Member States may allow network operators to own, develop, manage or 
operate Power-to-gas installations where all of the following conditions are fulfilled: 
- other parties, following an open, transparent and non-discriminatory tendering procedure 
that is subject to review and approval by the regulatory authority, have not been awarded 
a right to own, develop, manage or operate such fac ilities, or could not deliver those 
services at a reasonable cost and in a timely manner; 
- such facilities are necessary for the system operators to fulfil their obligations [under the 
Electricity or Gas Directive] for the efficient, reliable and secure operation of their energy 
system and the facilities are not used to buy or sell commodities (electricity, gases, 
hydrogen) in the energy markets; 
- such facilities are strictly limited in scope and scale to the needs r elated to the se 
obligations and these needs have been made explicit at an earlier stage to market parties 
in the European and national integrated infrastructure planning exercises; 
- the regulatory authority has assessed the necessity of such a derogation and has carried 
out an assessment of the tendering procedure, including the conditions of the tendering 
procedure, and has granted its approval; 
- such facilities are not accounted for in the regulated asset basis of network operators; 
The regulatory authorities shall perform, at regular intervals or at least every [5] years, a review 
on the existing power-to-gas installations having initially obtained such a derogation and launch 
a public consultation in order to assess the potential availability and interest of market participants 
in investing in such facilities.  
Where the public consultation, as assessed by the regulatory authority, indicates that third parties 
are able to own, develop, operate or manage such facilities in a cost -effective manner, the 
regulatory authority shall ensure that the network operators' activities in this regard are phased 
out within [18] months.

As part of the conditions of that procedure, regulatory authorities may allow the network operators 
to receive reasonable compensation, in particular to recover the residual value of their investment 
in the power-to-gas facilities, provided that the network operators justify that this value is based 
on market-conform assumptions. 
 
Question 44 : Today’s rules for gas network tariffs (see Art. 13 of the Gas Directive) seek to avoid 
cross-subsidies between network users but also to provide incentives for investments. In an 
emerging hydrogen market, the transported hydrogen volumes as well as the customer base 
might be low initially.   This could lead in certain cases to high initial hydrogen network tariffs for 
early users of a hydrogen network. 
These questions on the network tariffs for  the future hydrogen network and for  the methane 
network are difficult to answer at this stage and deserve a more detailed discussion. 
First, the l ack of visibility on the proposed market design for hydrogen and on expected 
supply/demand for this commodity (e.g. industrial clusters vs decentralized production/demand)  
implies that comments/opinions on cross -subsidies for infrastructure would be mainly 
based on principles, not on the actual regulatory needs no r on visibility on the cost 
implications. Consequently,  this l ack of visibility of an y cross-subsidy principle (as of today 
undesigned) does not allow ENGIE to commit to an answer. 
Second, the transition to a net -zero economy by 2050 will entail importan t costs for the energy 
sector and for all the energy consumers (households, industries, transport, building, etc.).  
Already today, we see that the cost of decarbonization policies are raising concerns for some 
consumers, esp. more vulnerable ones. Consequently, the transition costs should be closely 
kept under control by using all possible synergies between energy carriers  (electricity, 
natural gas, renewable and decarbonized gases, heat , etc. ). This is exactly the reason why 
ENGIE believes that integrated planning exercises will have some added value, see our answers 
to the questions in Section III. Consumers should be well aware of the cos ts induced by their 
choices (e.g. impact of electric heat pumps on the electricity capacity needs, both in terms of 
network and of generation for handling peak/stress events). 
This also means that all possible synergies between gaseous carriers (CH 4 / H2) should be 
investigated further. Ultimately, the skills and competencies required by network operators to 
handle these gases are similar, (part of) the existing infrastructure (CH4) could be repurposed for 
creating (part of) the newly required infrastructure (CH4 and H2), the further achievement of the 
net-zero target could  make some infrastructures progressively redundant, etc. By exploiting 
synergies between gaseous carriers and allowing existing gas TSO/DSO to handle H 2 networks 
as well, one also minimizes the risk of “stranded jobs” by ensuring a proper transition of the work 
force. 
In the interest of the cost for consumers, some flexibility needs to be built in compared to the 
main regulatory principles.  Regarding possible cross -subsidies for the infrastructure, one 
should leverage on the situation of a phase -out of the old infrastructure and the phase -in of the 
new infrastructure, wh ich will be needed to accommodate the different needs of various 
renewable and low-carbon gases (incl. biomethane and hydrogen) for the consumers. Assuming 
that cross-subsidies between the various legs of the infrastructure would exist, they should take 
place - in any case - under regulatory supervision, both for hydrogen and the other (renewable) 
gases. This supervision will ensure that the infrastructures needed are developed and managed 
in a cost-efficient way, and are exploiting the available synergies between energy carriers. Cross-
subsidies should probably only be allowed under a certain geographical perimeter (e.g. not across 
borders).

Third, independently of cross -subsidies for the infrastructure, support for hydrogen is 
necessary. Financial support will be needed to retrofit and/or build H 2 transmission, distribution 
and storage. Support will be needed to foster the development of renewable and low -carbon H2 
production facilities, with particular attention given/paid to renewable H 2. Of course, any sort of 
support for hydrogen should be transparent, enable the development of H 2 (supply, demand, 
infrastructure) and of the hydrogen economy, and not unduly burden other energy vectors (like 
natural gas). As an attention point, the cost of decarbonizing a given energy vector should not be 
transferred to another energy vector that has to decarbonize as well. 
 
 
Question 60. Hydrogen may be transported via pipelines into the EU, but also via non -network 
based transport options. In case you expect non-network based imports from outside the EU, in 
which way do you expect hydrogen to be carried into the EU? 
Hydrogen imports may mostly occur by ships.  
Although trucks are one of the main modes of transport today, they are used on short distances 
and to end-points (such as refueling stations) . Transport by trucks does not seem very efficient 
as a long -distance import pathway.  ENGIE therefore expects non -network based imports 
from outside the EU mainly by ships. 
The form of imports will depend on the actual development of the different elements for the various 
carriers (liquified hydrogen, ammonia, LOHC) in the value chain, both outs ide of EU and in the 
EU. If only one answer had to be provided regarding transport by ships, ENGIE would mention 
the imports via liquefied hydrogen. 
ENGIE would also like to stress th at the consultation could have mentioned more openly 
other energy carriers like e -methane/synthetic methane , BioLNG  or e -methanol. For 
instance, synthetic methane could be handled and treated somehow as methane/natural gas  in 
the current infrastructure. It would have been a good opportunity to highlight such solutions that 
are based on existing infrastructures.

About Vopak  
 
Royal Vopak is the world’s leading independent tank storage company. We store vital products 
with care. With over 400 years of history and a focus on sustainability, we ensure safe, clean 
and efficient storage and handling of bulk liquid products and gases  for our customers. By 
doing so, we enable the delivery of products that are vital to our economy and daily lives, 
ranging from chemicals, oils, gases and LNG to biofuels and vegoils. We are determined to 
develop key infrastructure solutions for the world’ s changing energy systems, while 
simultaneously investing in digitalization and innovation. Vopak is investing in developing 
infrastructure solutions for new energies and feedstocks to transport and store hydrogen, 
CO2 and green ammonia, as well as flow batteries. Vopak is listed on the Euronext Amsterdam 
and is headquartered in Rotterdam, the Netherlands. Its facilities, projects and feasibility 
studies in several EU countries, including the Netherlands, Belgium, Germany, Spain and 
Portugal. For more information, please visit www.vopak.com. 
 
Additional Remarks  
 
We would like to comment on some of the questions in the consultation and argue on some 
answers we have provided:  
 
● Question 1:  
Vopak believes all of these gaseous fuels will play a role towards the 2030 decarbonisation 
and will be needed; the company is therefore actively pursuing new infrastructure 
solutions to facilitate the new supply chains, with a particular focus on hydrogen. 
Regarding hydrogen, both renewable and low-carbon hydrogen will be needed to achieve 
the 2030 objectives and the EU should provide for a favourable environment for both 
types. In addition, the EU should promote both local production of hydrogen (low-carbon 
and renewable) and imports from within and fro m outside the EU, to ensure security of 
supply from diverse sources, as local production in demand centers will not be able to 
meet demand. Vopak’s studies indicate that various technologies will develop in parallel, 
serving various end -markets, to transport and store hydrogen, including through liquid 
hydrogen carriers (LOHCs), conversion to ammonia, and liquefaction. We believe that 
hydrogen will ultimately develop into a global traded energy commodity.  We also see 
options to make current supply chains more sustainable. An example is that Gate terminal 
in Rotterdam, a Gasunie-Vopak joint venture, has just been certified for bio-LNG.

● Question 8:  
From Vopak perspective, current skills, knowledge and expertise are key for safe, clean 
and efficient storage and handling of products. While additional training might be needed 
to handle specific new products, we expect that terminals and ports already ha ve much 
of the required skills and competences, including in the area of safety (both occupational 
safety and process safety). Additional jobs will be created and skills developed in the 
construction phases of new terminals and installation and in the repu rposing of existing 
terminals and installations, as well as in relation to new technologies, like hydrogenation 
and dehydrogenation plants. 
 
● Question 9:  
Installation and infrastructure operating on fossil gas can be retrofitted and reused for 
hydrogen. This is shown for example by projects by gas network companies to retrofit and 
reuse gas pipelines to transport hydrogen or a gas -hydrogen blend and the H -vision 
project that includes adapting installations like boilers in refineries to enable use of 
hydrogen. Moreover, as the transition to low -carbon and renewable fuels is not 
happening overnight, companies have enough time to adapt.  
Crucial is clarity on regulatory measures like carbon pricing and a comprehensive vision 
of environmental and carbon targets, including clear timelines. 
 
● Question 31:  
 
A robust certificates/guarantees of origin framework for renewable and low -carbon 
hydrogen is needed (REDII revision) to allow efficient trade across borders and sectors 
and for proving reliable sustainability standards (climate value) of hydrogen. Certification 
is key to justify a higher price for low -carbon and renewable hydrogen and renewable 
hydrogen. As long as there is not one European certification framework, customers will 
have no incentive to pay a higher price for renewable or low -carbon than for grey 
hydrogen. Unclarity also remains with regard to taxation, especially for countries outside 
the EU. When hydrogen is carried as ammonia or LOHC, it is sometimes considered as 
different substances which lead to ambiguity and uncertainty. There are some artificial 
barriers on tax and customs and thus a need for a clear taxation policy. This should be 
taken into account in the ongoing revision of the Energy Taxation piece of legislation. 
 
 
● Question 57:  
Do you see the need to develop larger-scale, dedicated hydrogen storage facilities in the 
EU in light of the increased use of hydrogen in the EU? 
o Yes       
 
Vopak sees a clear need for hydrogen storage, but the question on what types of storage 
are considered here and what is considered “larger scale”  should be raised. Tank storage 
in ports will be needed to facilitate exports and imports, while large -scale storage in 
caverns might be needed to provide security of hydro gen supply in view of weather and 
seasonal effects. Also, storage of LOHCs, ammonia or liquefied hydrogen must be done 
in dedicated tanks with different specifications for each, while gaseous hydrogen can only 
be stored in pressurized bullets or caverns.

● Question 60:  
Hydrogen may be transported via pipelines into the EU, but also via non -network based 
transport options. In case you expect non -network based imports from outside the EU, in 
which way do you expect hydrogen to be carried into the EU? 
 
o Shipped into the EU as liquefied hydrogen. 
 
o Shipped into the EU as ammonia. 
 
o Shipped into the EU on the basis of Liquid Organic Hydrogen Carriers (‘LOHCs’). 
 
o Transported into the EU via trucks. 
 
Vopak would like to tick the 3 first boxes. Indeed, Vopak believes that all three routes, being 
LOHC, ammonia and Liquified Hydrogen (LH2) need to develop before 2030 and will develop 
to transport hydrogen into the EU. We believe LOHC and ammonia will develop first, then LH2 
towards 2030.  
 
● Question 61: 
Do you see a need to prepare EU LNG terminals to receive liquefied hydrogen?    
 
o Yes, today's import terminals can play an important role in supplying the EU. 
o No, imports will become important but large-scale LNG terminals will not be relevant.  
 
Vopak answered “no” as we consider that LNG terminals are not suitable for receiving and 
storing liquified hydrogen, unless tanks and other infrastructure are demolish ed and built 
anew, given the important temperature difference between both LNG and LH2. This will at 
least require significant investments. At the same time, knowledge and expertise in handling 
LNG as well as terminal location and other infrastructure su ch as jetties will be relevant for 
the deployment of LH2.  
We would also like to acknowledge that LNG terminals  can be repurposed for receiving and 
storing ammonia, one of the hydrogen carriers. LNG terminals can also provide space for CO2 
infrastructure (liquefaction, compression…). Finally, LNG could be decarbonized and 
transformed into hydrogen when it comes out of the tank (before injection into the gas 
pipeline network), although this is an expensive solution given the costs in the entire chain.

● Question 72: 
 Which renewable and low -carbon gases, in your view, can be imported via LNG 
terminals?  
 
LNG terminals can be used for ammonia storage. They cannot easily be reused for LH2, 
unless tanks and other infrastructure are refurbished demolished and/or built anew. To 
our knowledge, the nickel-steel alloy (tanks, pipelines, pumps) is unsuitable for Liquefied 
Hydrogen.  LNG terminals can also provide space for CO2 infrastructure (liquefaction, 
compression…). 
 
● Question 73: 
- Require LNG terminals and other gas depressurising sites to provide waste heat/cold 
to nearby heat/cold consumers.  
 
Vopak would like to ask what are the practicalities of formalizing this requirement ?  Use of 
waste heat and/or cold is commonly included in business plans whenever this makes 
business sense, as this is seen as an opportunity rather than an obligation.  
 
● Question 92: 
 How do you see the security of supply challenge in the context of the decarbonisation of the 
supply of gases in the EU in line with the climate-neutrality objectives? 
o Security of supply will not be an issue when renewable and low-carbon gases will 
be used in the EU. 
o Security of gas supply will still be an important challenge that needs to be taken 
into account in the context of increased use of renewable and low-carbon gases in 
the EU. 
o New security issues should be taken into account. 
 
Vopak believes that security of gas supply is an important challenge that needs to be taken 
into account. Therefore, we would have selected the second and third options but have ticked 
the last box as only one can be selected.

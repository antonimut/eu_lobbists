EU Data Act Inception Impact Assessment: Vodafone Feedback  
Overview  
Vodafone welcomes the publication of the Inception Impact Assessment on the EU Data Act an d strongly 
support the key objectives of this file to harness the power of data to achieve the objectives of the European 
Green Deal, EU Recovery Plan and Digital Decade.  If we get this right, enhanced sharing and reuse of data 
could unlock significant economic and societal benefits for Europe. Vodafone h as previously 
commissioned Deloitte to model the economic benefits of B2B data sharing in a number of industry sectors, 
finding that the sharing of machine-generated non-personal data would add €1.4tn in economic value to 
the EU economy by the year 20271. This potential will be further enhanced by 5G and narrow-band IoT. 
Realising this value depends, in large part, on the successful sharing of data  between organisations. 
Sharing is vital, as there may be a wide range of potential uses for the data throughout its lifecycle, and 
many of those uses may not take place within the organisation that holds the data or may require different 
skills. Data can be shared with customers and suppliers throughout the supply chain (vertical data sharing); 
with peers to create larger data sets including more variation (horizontal data sharing); and outside the 
sector with stakeholders who can make use of the data in new settings (external data sharing).  
The potential of data sharing can only be realised when organisations have the freedom to innovate, when 
they respect privacy concerns and find the right balance between what is technically possible, what is 
legally allowed and what is socially acceptable.  
 We provide our initial views on the policy options outlined in the IIA below and will elaborate on these views 
in our response to the public consultation on the Data Act.  
Business to Government Data Sharing  
Timely and quality data insights have the power to transform government decision making, optimising 
outcomes for citizens and delivering better value for taxpayers. This fact has been acutely evident 
throughout the Covid crisis, where we have supported governmen ts with improved insights on the 
movements of people in affected areas  allowing appropriate health policy responses . The issues around 
B2G data sharing have been addressed comprehensively by the B2G data sharing expert group, who 
reported to the Commission  earlier 20202. We would wholeheartedly agree with their conclusion that: 
“Requiring access to certain datasets in specific situations does not necessarily imply that the data 
should be shared free of charge . Companies incur costs to make data available, including time and 
resources for preparing the data, adapting it to the specific request and building the infrastructure 
necessary for the transmission of such data (e.g. APIs). For B2G data sharing to become sustainable, these 
initiatives should be mutually beneficial.” 
So, while Vodafone is strongly in favour of increased sharing of reuse of data, both across the private sector 
and between business and public administrations, we think it is best is to focus on governance model and 
right incentives. In so doing we do not conceive of data as a public asset, or something which should be 
held in the public interest or common good, as to do so fails to recognise the sustained and significant 
investment that is required over the long term to develop, maintain and refine big data analytics 
techniques. We would therefore discourage the Commission from considering the ‘high intensity’ policy 
intervention described in the IIA which could create a legal right for public sector access to privately held 
data, in favour of making use of data analytics in a fair and responsible and human-cantered manner,

Business to Business Data Sharing  
We see a number of barriers to data sharing  between business (B2B); Commercial barriers (the concern 
that firms will have a limited incentive to share data when they might not appropriate the gains themselves); 
Security barriers (the concern that sharing will create vulnerabilities to intentional or inadvertent security 
breaches.) and also legal barriers (concerns, particularly in the healthcare sector, that a range of existing 
rules may not allow data sharing.) 
To address these barriers we support the development of clear regulatory principles for the circumstances 
in which data sharing shou ld be encouraged, the development of targeted policy measures  that can 
facilitate data sharing and reflect those principles (particularly accreditation to ease sharing between 
organisations without an established commercial relationship and regulatory guid ance) and also the 
promotion of appropriate data sharing models across sectors,  e.g. the extended vehicle and neutral 
server concepts that are being developed in the automotive sector could be used in other sectors with OEM 
manufacturers (e.g. agricultural  equipment). Above all the regulatory framework must bring about 
market conditions that allow for effective monetization of data assets , to ensure that those who 
invest in the underlying technology and analytics capabilities are able to generate a fair ret urn on those 
investments.   
On the policy options outlined by the Commission, we would be predisposed to support transparency 
obligations for manufacturers of connected devices on rights to access and use non -personal/industrial 
data, but would however require more details on the suggested ‘modalities’ for access to data, stipulating 
how parties agree to access data based on FRAND terms. As a general comment we also look forward to the 
Commission’s review of the Database Directive , that should remove any un necessary barriers to data 
sharing while respected at all times legitimate intellectual property rights over such data.  
The European Cloud Market 
Vodafone supports the overarching aim of the Commission , to create an open and competitive 
European cloud ecosystem, ensuring diversity and resilience; however, we are pragmatic about how to 
best achieve this. Today the reality is that European providers occupy only a very small percentage of the 
market for cloud services in Europe (the four leading providers will account for over 80% global revenue in 
2021). This has real world consequences: an investment gap of 11bn EUR  per annum per EU/US-China 
and low uptake in Europe.  
Measures to build EU cloud champions are important but also face significant challenges and  will take 
time, given the pace and scale of innovation from non -European cloud vendors. Instead we would urge 
policy makers to focus on the legal frameworks that can be put in place to encourage and incentivise 
enhanced switching and portability between c loud service providers  in full respect of EU data 
acquis, including binding obligations on dominant cloud vendors (often referred to as ‘Hyperscalers’) to 
support service interoperability in Europe (for e.g. via Gaia-X or the EU Cloud Rulebook).  
On the policy options outlined in the IIA Vodafone strongly supports measures to promote service 
interoperability and data portability (building on those already in place or underway via the Free Flow of 
Data Regulation and Digital Markets Act) including possible new binding legal for cloud computing service 
providers to offer data and application portability and other distinct conditions of contractual, technical and 
economic nature.

International Data Transfer  
While Vodafone recognises the concerns in t he IIA around government access to personal and non-
personal data of companies established in the Union, we urge the Commission to proceed cautiously in this 
area. We are concerned that the requirements described here for CSPs (for example having in place legal, 
technical and organisational measures  to prevent the transfer of or access to non-personal data of EU 
companies to third countries’ governmental authorities ) could result in de facto data localisation, to the 
detriment of the EU’s digital ambitions and EU privacy standards leadership.  
We should instead focus on strengthening EU GDPR “soft power” and export the EU model of data 
privacy regulation.  
The costs of data localisation are well understood we fear Europe cannot remain competitive i f 
localisation of data becomes a legal obligation, as this would cut us off from the globally 
interconnected digital supply chains that underpin the digital economy. We are also concerned about 
the potential signal this development sends to other countries around the world who may be considering 
instituting data localisation measures and will be emboldened if Europe is seen to be charting this course. 
Fragmentation of the Internet in this manner will not only hamper the competitiveness of European 
business domestically but could also prevent us from being able to access and compete in markets outside 
of Europe.  
As such we would encourage the Commission to continue work at the multilateral level to reach a global 
agreement on cross -border data flows that res olves conflicts of jurisdiction  and defend/use GDPR legal 
mechanisms for data transfers. We also note the publication of the revised SCCs as a key development in 
this regard that ensure that an adequate level of protection should travel with that data wherever it is stored 
and processed to ensure that the data subject’s rights are not undermined . Combined with strong  single 
market rules protecting EU citizen’s privacy and data protection rights  (GDPR, ePR, NIS Directive) we are 
confident that the EU’s policy objectives can be met without recourse to measures outlined in the IIA that 
could result in forced data localisation.

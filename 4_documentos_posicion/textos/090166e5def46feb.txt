ZDH Feedback on the Inception Impact Assessment 
ZDH welcomes the possibility to provide 
feedback on the inception impact assess-
ment regarding the Data Act and amended 
rules on the legal protection of databases . 
Access to data is a key prerequisite for fair 
competition in the European single market.  
 
We would therefore like to share some obser-
vations and comments on the problems the 
initiative aims to tackle and offer some solu-
tions on how to address these challenges ac-
cordingly. 
 
A. General observations 
 
1) The inception impact assessment com-
prehensively pinpoints the core chal-
lenges for SMEs in acquiring data access 
and using data in business-to-business 
situations (B2B) on page 2 (i,  ii, iii) and 
rightly focuses on the GDPR which plays 
a key role in data portability for data sub-
jects. 
 
2) We believe that under the current regime 
of the GDPR, article 20 is not fit for pur-
pose. Although it gives the data subject 
important control rights over his or her 
data, there is in practice no technical pos-
sibility for the customer to transfer the 
data directly and in real time to the ser-
vice provider (e.g., for maintenance and 
repair services). 
 
3) SMEs largely depend on IoT platforms for 
granting access to processed data which 
is relevant for maintenance and repair 
services in downstream markets and of-
ten this access to data is simply refused 
due to conflicting interests. The decision 
of the European Commission to leave it 
so far up to the industry to develop in-
teroperability formats that enable cus-
tomer and thus personal data portability, 
has not sho wn any improvements for 
SMEs.  
 
4) Processed data is mixed data that com-
bines technical data with the data of the 
customer and has thus been processed 
into information data, i.e., metadata. 
Since downstream markets are always 
characterized by B2C relationships, they 
inevitably always include personal di-
mensions of data. Only this processed 
data can provide the skilled crafts with 
the relevant information required for their 
activity in downstream markets. 
 
5) In the field of IoT platforms and its subse-
quent application in the smart home area, 
investments are often laid out for 20 to 30 
years. Being unable to grant access to 
their data, customers are therefore 
trapped in a system for 20 years due to 
the lock-in effects. A good practice for 
data sharing in this regard is the Metering 
Point Operation Act in Germany. It gives 
the (private) electricity end customer the 
3 
right to decide which of his (potential) ser-
vice providers he wants to give access to 
the smart metering point data as an inter-
face. 
 
6) It is therefore crucial to design market 
conditions in such a way that customers 
can make their own free decisions as to 
whom they give access to their data. All 
companies along the value chain should 
have equal access to data which is rele-
vant for the development of new services 
for the customer. This means that all 
companies who have the consent from 
the user of a device/machine should be 
able to analyse the data and thus acquire 
relevant information to make service of-
ferings to the user. The users of the re-
spective devices therefore need to be 
technically able to give their consent to all 
companies they want to. For functioning 
conditions, it is also necessary that the 
service providers have direct communi-
cation access to their (potential) custom-
ers via the corresponding smart devices / 
facilities (display). This is also not en-
sured under the current legal situation. 
 
7) Data access should be “fair by design”. 
Data solutions should therefore be de-
vice inherent to allow for real time access 
to the data generated by the device and 
provide for a customer related communi-
cation interface. 
 
8) It is of vital importance that all legislative 
and non -legislative Commission 
initiatives on data access and d ata flow 
are consistent with each other, do not 
contradict each other and build upon an-
other (i.e. data strategy, DMA/ DSA; Data 
governance act etc.). The overarching 
goal should be to counter the market im-
balances and to consider access to data 
as paramount for competition across 
markets. Fair access to data is a prereq-
uisite to achieve fair market conditions on 
digital and non-digital markets in Europe. 
The Data Act therefore needs to build on 
the current discussions on the DMA 
when it comes to the importance of gate-
keepers withholding data in niche mar-
kets.  
 
B. Objectives and Envisaged Policy Op-
tions  
 
Regarding the envisaged policy options, we 
ask for clear rules and a clear legal frame-
work as part of the Data Act which allow for 
fairness in B2B relations especially with re-
gard to their consequences for the B2C sec-
tor and avoid unfair conditions for access to 
and use of data. Such rules should cover ac-
cess and use rights of competition relevant 
data in all sectors and B2B relations and be 
in line with the requirements of the GDPR.  
 
General fairness obligations could be com-
plemented by model contract ter ms recom-
mended by the Commission. But in view of 
legal certainty, it would be best to lay down 
clear data access and use rights, potentially 
on the basis of fair, reasonable, 
4 
proportionate, transparent and non-discrimi-
natory terms for non -personal data. Es pe-
cially the access to data generated by objects 
connected to the IoT must be regulated. Sec-
tor specific principles for access to data, 
could be envisaged to take into account spe-
cific characteristics of the relevant sector. If 
such an approach is taken it  should be en-
sured that the interests of both data holders 
and data users are taken into account, e.g., 
when SMEs use IoT objects or provide ser-
vices in secondary markets for maintenance 
and repair services.  
 
In order to address the concerns regarding 
article 20 of the GDPR, manufacturers of 
smart home appliances should be obliged to 
create technical interfaces that allow for real-
time portability of the data these devices col-
lect during their use. 
 
The core question the Data Act needs to an-
swer is how to guarantee access to data -
driven downstream markets that are digitally 
controlled by manufacturers. "Data", inter-
faces, services, software are used by vehicle 
or smart home manufacturers, platform oper-
ator or IT companies to establish factual con-
trol over f urther economic activity. Markets 
that used to be independent of one another 
are increasingly being linked and made de-
pendent on one another.  
 
A regulatory approach must thus answer 
those questions about distribution conflicts 
and not only focus on the co mplexities of 
ownership rights of mixed datasets.  The 
exclusion of certain companies from down-
stream markets as a result of corporate strat-
egy leads to foreclosure. Foreclosure pre-
vents competition,  thus innovation is im-
paired, as well as fair distribution of opportu-
nities for companies of all kinds.  
 
Therefore, we conclude that the proposed 
policy options 1) (“Specific transparency ob-
ligations for manufacturers of connected ob-
jects on rights to access and use of non-per-
sonal data in professional use for the benefit 
of users of such objects”) and 2) (“a B2B fair-
ness test to avoid unilaterally imposed unfair 
conditions for access to and use of data”) do 
not go far enough to solve the issues of fair-
ness and access issues in the digital econ-
omy. 
 
Finally, we believe that in addition to the Data 
Act the importance of data in the context of 
abuse control should also be codified in Eu-
ropean competition law as it has recently 
been done in the amended German compe-
tition law (GWB). 
 
C. Preliminary Assessment of Economic 
Impacts and Impacts on Innovation 
 
Innovative and research -intensive SMEs 
strengthen the innovative capacity of the in-
dustry, but only innovative and qualified 
crafts are a guarantee for the development of 
industrial autonomy in Europe. They are the 
ones which enable true innovation for con-
sumers because they do not build in stand-
ardised products but customi sed solutions 
5 
and bundle services out of a variety of prod-
ucts, giving the consumer real choice and 
custom-made products. The innovative po-
tential of the skilled crafts sector, which is 
also important for large companies, results 
from the fact that it invol ves actual practical 
testing and optimi sation. With open data 
standards skilled craft businesses can offer a 
much more individual ised service than just 
the standard product made available by the 
manufacturer. At the moment, the smart 
home sector is heavily dominated by a few 
players. Other manufacturers, so called “hid-
den champions” are excluded from the mar-
ket. 
 
A craftsman who has to install the product 
contractually offered by the industrial partner 
(even if he realises that the customer has dif-
ferent wishes, or another product would bet-
ter meet the customer's needs) is stripped 
from his entrepreneurial freedom. He is con-
tractually bound to install precisely this one 
product as he has not concluded a work con-
tract, but a purchase contract. This, conse-
quently, means that the customer is paying 
for this standardi sed product. According to 
our estimates, the added value benefits of 
open data formats would lead to a 30 -40% 
cheaper price offering in e.g., smart home 
devices if the customer did not have to use 
the standard product but could receive a cus-
tom-fit product and service from the crafts-
man.

Mastercard response to the Inception Impact Assessment concerning the Data Act 
 
Mastercard welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on the Inception Impact Assessment (IIA) of the European 
Commission’s future Data Act (including the review of the Directive 96/9/EC on the legal protection of databases) . 
Mastercard is supportive of the development of a human -centric data ecosystem which will encourage public entities 
and business to share and use data in a fair and transparent way. This is fully in line with our Data Responsibility 
Imperative that guides all our data practices across the organization globally.  
 
Mastercard is playing a key role in advancing data science for social good. We are  partnering with private sector 
entities, NGOs, and governments on projects that seek to advance inclusive growth and financial inclusion – including 
with Harvard University, London School of Economics, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Stanford University, 
McGill University, IMF, World Bank, and numerous states and cities. For example, Mastercard’s Center for In clusive 
Growth excels at using insights and expertise to help social, civic and government organizations use data driven insights 
to make a positive impact.  
 
We agree with the aims identified in the European Data Strategy, including creating a digital economy that places the 
interests of the individual first, in accordance with European values, fundamental rights and rules. Based on the  IIA, 
we understand that Data Act will help meet these objectives by put ting measures in place t o ensure more public and 
private actors can benefit from the use of data , including through fair allocation of economic value among actors of 
the data economy. If done well, the Data Act could improve trust in data sharing and strengthen the use of data-driven 
insights for public good and social impact.  
 
In order to achieve these very worthy goals, we recommend addressing a few key elements within the future rules, 
including: 
1. Allowing time for innovation  
2. Addressing fairness in data sharing   
3. Recognizing the value of data insights to advance social good 
4. Considering issues associated with mandatory data sharing    
5. Exploring alternative tools to improve access to data and fairness in data sharing   
 
We look forward to continuing to partner with the European Commission to shape the upcoming Data Act and create 
an innovative data-sharing ecosystem fully aligned with European values and fundamental rights.    
 
 
 
1. Allowing time for innovation  
Innovative data new services are developing at a rapid pace and new forms of collaboration are emerging . We are only 
beginning to scratch the surface of how data can be used , combined, and understood to deliver transformational change 
benefiting public authorities, businesses, communities and individuals.  
Better understanding existing data sharing. While we appreciate the European Commission’s interest in ensuring the 
greater availability of data, we believe that if not done carefully, regulating a market that is still in its infancy could impede 
nascent data sharing models. This goes both from the commercial side, but also from the public sector and civil society. To 
better understand its current workings and failures, we would suggest conducting a comprehensive qualitative study into 
how companies and governments are using a nd sharing data, generating insights, and working together to leverage the 
power of data. This study should also look into which collaborative models are most appropriate for different use cases. 
Such a study would complement the Commission’s most recent market data study report on the topic (Shaping Europe’s 
digital future).

2. Addressing fairness in data sharing   
Mastercard supports the Commission’s desire to ensure that more individuals, businesses and communities are able to 
benefit from the transformational power of data. In order to achieve this goal, we believe certain underlying obstacles that 
lead to information inequality need to first be addressed. These obstacles include lack of data analytics skills, lack of access 
to the right technological tools and lack of capital needed to run complex data -driven projects. Indeed, it has been our 
experience that when it comes to  who is able to reap the benefits that Big Data has to offer, “fairness” in the legal or 
contractual sense rarely plays a key role.  
 
Reframing what qualifies as fair. While we acknowledge there may be imbalances in the access to and use of data where 
a party dominates a particular data space, as long as the imbalances do not lead to the abuse of the dominant position 
that violates competition law, mandating companies to  provide access to data could be considered an inappropriate level 
of intervention. 
 
Safeguards for data sharing. Fairness should be considered from the perspective of data holders sharing information with 
other businesses and public authorities. In this respect , lessons should be learn t from the experience under the Payment 
Services Directive 2 (PSD2), where the lack of reciprocity has created an imbalance in the level playing field to the detriment 
of incumbent players. The Data Act should also take into account the advice from the High-Level Expert Group Report on 
Business-to-Government Data Sharing to ensure no harm is done, notably: 
 
‘Business-to-government data collaborations must ensure that legitimate private-sector interests, notably commercially 
sensitive information such as trade secrets, are respected. They should allow private companies or civil -society organisations 
to continue to be able to use and monetise the private -sector data in question as well as derived insights to their benefit. 
Private companies and civil-society organizations should not be held liable for the quality of the data in question or its use by 
pubic authorities for public-interest purposes. Public-sector bodies may not use pr ivate-sector data for commercial purposes 
or to compete commercially with a company that has similar offerings …’ [Towards a European strategy on  business-to-
government data sharing for the public interest, p. 82]  
 
Fair compensation. The Data Act should in clude safeguards to balance the impact on the company that is mandated to 
disclose the data. For example, public need purposes must not be used to overrule existing contracts for the same data, 
which would undermine payments under existing contracts. To this point, we would hope that the Data Act will reflect the 
High-Level Expert Group Report on Business-to-Government Data Sharing position on compensation: 
 
‘Business-to-government data -collaboration agreements should seek to be mutually beneficial, while acknowledging the 
public-interest goal by giving the public -sector body preferential treatment. This should be reflected in the level of 
compensation agreed, the level of which should be determined taking into account the other principles...’ [Towards a European 
strategy on business-to-government data sharing for the public interest, p. 83] 
 
Clarifying the role of contracts. We acknowledge the importance of having the proper legal agreements in place to govern 
any data sharing initiatives. Model data contracts may facilitate some forms of data sharing, especially for more straight 
forward data sharing constructs involving entities that do not have capacity to envisage all the legal safeguards.  However, 
flexibility to compete could be curtailed if companies are forced to accept contractual standards or concepts of fairness 
that disregard the nature of the data they hold or the business model they operate.  
 
Standardizing certain contractual details is likely to benefit those actors who already have capability to work with data , 
but are unable to reap the benefits of their data sharing collaborations due to the dominant negotiation power of their 
data sharing partners . However, in our view  the number of parties that would stand to benefit from the focus on unfair 
contractual terms would be fairly limited  because the most beneficial data collaborations are not limited to j ust sharing 
data but encompass working together intensively towards a common goal .

3. Recognizing the value of data insights to advance social good  
Partnerships are key enablers for success. Mastercard has a wealth of expertise to share when it comes to working with 
partners to solve for a lack of technology, skills or vision when it comes to how to  leverage their data. We have a track 
record of partnering on data -driven inclusive growth projects that help advance positive social impact  and reduc e 
information inequality. In our view the most successful projects – whether partnering with public authorities, civil society, 
or another business – are not predicated on  equal footing on data access but on leveraging data to produce and deliver 
data  insights in a content and format that adds value for the intended aim.   
 
During the global pandemic, Mastercard partnered with policy makers at national and regional level to better understand 
the economic impact of COVID-19 and contribute to the effort to track and shape policy response measures.  Pursuing this 
objective, Mastercard developed  and shared reports using anonymized and aggregated transactions to display indexed, 
near-real time trends in spend activity in specific industries, such as eating p laces, drug store chains  and grocery stores 
during lockdowns and other pandemic containment measures. Such projects demonstrate the fundamental role that data 
insights and B2G data sharing can have in helping us to overcome societal challenges.  This was ba sed on voluntary 
collaboration, which proves that certain challenging circumstances, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, still do not necessitate 
mandatory data sharing regimes.  
 
4. Considering issues associated with mandatory data sharing    
Mastercard believes that the IIA is currently missing comprehensive alignment with the High-Level Expert Group Report on 
Business-to-Government Data Sharing  in particular the “Principles on business-to-government data sharing for the public 
interest” and would recommend that the Data Act better aligns with these Principles. Without replicating these Principles, 
we would like to provide the following considerations that build on and supplement them:  
  
Avoiding conflicts with the GDPR. The IIA introduces data sharing  concepts derive d from an individuals’ right to the 
protection of personal data as enshrined in the GDPR.  While it becomes difficult to distinguish between personal and non-
personal data when they are in the same pool, simply replicating personal data concepts to non-personal data could dilute 
the unique protection afforded to personal data. On the other hand, considerations similar to those found within the GDPR 
could be beneficial , but are currently missing. For example, a much more pragmatic approach  would be to include a 
consideration for legitimate business interest which must be balanced with the rights of others (including individuals). 
 
Ensuring appropriate transparency.  Transparency is critical to allow companies and the public in general to ensure that 
data is only used for the intended public interest need and not for any other purpose. It can also be used to check whether 
this data is monetized. Questions also exist in respect of transparency towards individuals by companies in respect of their 
data collection: Privacy notices would need to include the appropriate disclosures about data recipients ; or would public 
authorities be required to disclose to citizens the possibility of such data sharing as default , as any company could be 
mandated to share data?  
 
Ensuring proportionality in the use of private sector data. If data sharing for certain purposes would be mandated, such 
purposes should be proportionate and justified. “Public interest need” should be narrowly defined to avoid a risk that public 
authorities’ demand for data is used for other purposes and leads to disproportionate requests for data that do not match 
the actu al and proven needs  (fishing expeditions and providing for potential future needs that may not realize) . These 
purposes should be decided in a harmonized way across the EU.  
 
 
Ensuring data  privacy and  security. Any obligation to share data with public recipients, or mandating B2B data 
access would have to require data recipients to implement and demonstrate appropriate privacy and security standards 
and to make data sharing conditional on meeting these standards. Absent appropriate safeguards, mandating the sharing 
of data could pose significant risks to individuals and businesses, through data breaches, regulatory investigations and legal 
proceedings. Not being able to require this from the data recipient, would thus undermine the basic protections in the 
GDPR, jeopardize trust and negatively impact society as a whole.

Applying data minimization. Mandating the sharing of data could run counter to the GDPR principle of data minimization 
and data use limitation. If the same purpose (i.e. addressing public interest need) could be achieved by sharing data insights, 
using information already available to public authorities or leveraging other less intrusive solutions, mandating the sharing 
of raw data would not be necessary . The Data Act would have to clarify that if companies are mandated to share data, 
they should be able to anonymize or pseudonymize the data and in a collaborative manner decide on the data sets to be 
shared, the data use rights, time limitations, and the format that apply to them.     
 
Upholding data quality. Data-driven c ompanies invest in sophisticated tools to use and interpret data , and build 
professional multidisciplinary knowledge  hubs, including with the help of trained data scientists, data analysts, data 
engineers, business strategists, data security professionals and lawyers. While s ome bigger municipalities or tech savvy 
governments have excellent capabilities to work with and interpret data, that’s not the case for all other public or private 
entities.  
 
If data is shared with entities that lack the right tools, skills  or knowledge to appropriately interpret and find patterns in 
the data, that data may be interpreted wrongly. This could lead to inaccuracy of results or unintended bias baked into the 
analysis. Some companies could even be blamed for not having supplied sufficient or appropriate information, when in fact 
the analysis of the data by the recipient would be at fault. Our experience is that depending on the level of data maturity, 
different level s of assistance are needed. Even sharing data insights often requires additional services that help public 
authorities understand how to read the insights. An obligation to share raw data would be counterproductive . Therefore, 
a form of quality requiremen t or data recipient ’s responsibility requirement should be included in  any data sharing 
framework.  
 
Avoiding stifling innovation. Mandatory data sharing may remove incentives for companies to invest in innovation. Some 
examples of the impact  of mandatory data sharing can be found in the financial sector. PSD2 has mandated financial 
institutions (ASPSPs) to open up access to regulated APIs. While this has benefited consumers and overall competitiveness 
in the financial services sector, as well as created new business models and business opportunities, it has also led to an 
imbalance in the Open Banking ecosystem. ASPSPs have had to ma ke significant investments in their infrastructure and 
interfaces to comply with PSD2 without return on this investment. This has made certain ASPSPs reluctant to invest 
further in their APIs to ensure high quality for data that is exposed. There is a less on to be learnt here for any data sharing 
corridor (B2B or B2G) : achieving a fair and balanced exchange of value is critical to a healthy, vibrant and innovative 
ecosystem. 
 
Mitigating the risk of reducing competition . Mandatory data sharing may  inadvertently reduce competition in markets, 
through enabling competitors to standardi ze pricing, content, terms and conditions, etc. Many companies use algorithmic 
models to help determine features and pricing of products or services. These models rely on market data which is generally 
not universally accessible. If data (such as IoT usage, consumer preferences, supply chain availability) becomes more easily 
accessible, it is likely there will be a convergence of pricing and product/service features, as these pricing and feature models 
all try to optimi ze the product mix. Ultimately this could lead to price increas es as models react to optimize profit. At 
present, outside of the big technology platforms, the current randomness of data accessibility is stimu lating innovation 
and multiple niche offerings. 
 
Clarifying structures for data sharing. The IIA looks at facilitating data sharing through data intermediaries. It should be 
clarified if these intermediaries are the same as those proposed in the Data Governance Act  (DGA). The future Data Act 
should avoid creating overlapping or conflicting structures that are already being constructed under the DGA. 
 
5. Exploring alternative tools to improve access to data and fairness in data sharing  
Bringing the tools to the data. Importantly, the sharing of data is not always the most effective, proportionate or efficient 
way of achieving a particular aim. Non-data sharing options may often be better suited to the issue at hand, maximizing 
the benefits while minimizing negative impact for individuals and businesses. For example:  
 
• Options, such as the sharing of technology, algorithms (“bring the algorithm to the data”), allowing access to the 
data to make computations on -premise – without data being shared – e.g. via segregated sandbox; restrictions 
to extract a copy of the data, etc., should be explored and encouraged. A specific emphasis could also be given to

techniques like homomorphic encryption and secure multi -party computation which allow insights/value to be 
extracted from the data, while avoiding key risks in terms of privacy, security, IP rights etc. 
 
• There should be preference for keeping the data in its original place, especially if multiple public authorities require 
access to the same data. A business should be able to determine whether access is provisioned for work on the 
data in-situ or whether to generate copies of it. A proliferation of copies of datasets would increase risks around 
privacy, commercial confidence or intellectual property, for which business has a duty of care. 
 
• Providing access to dynamic data (that changes in real time) is better achieved through bringing the tools to the 
data, rather than push ing the data to another location. For example, d uring the COVID-19 pandemic, not only 
were static datasets in demand that showed change over a long period (such as from one year to the next), but 
also datasets that changed on a daily or even hourly basis.  
 
Addressing lack of skills, tools and knowledge.  Mandating the sharing of data with public authorities  is unlikely  to 
empower the se authorities  to develop their own data processing capabilities , as in order to do so, many need to first 
develop the skills needed to adequately organize, assess, leverage and protect the data they already have. The Commission 
should develop a framework for a data skills and technology program targeted at the public sector and SMEs  to improve 
capabilities and strengthen their use of data . This should be supported by  dedicated EU funding and national matching 
schemes to dramatically grow Data Scien ce capacity  in public authorities  and SMEs . The forthcoming Data Act could 
stand to create lasting change within the data sharing ecosystem  by building the talent needed from within. In addition,  
the Commission and/or Member States could develop a repository of best practices to highlight innovation in building data 
knowledge capacities across various sectors and provide a platform to recognize successful initiatives. 
 
For instance, Mastercard currently runs a worldwide Girls4Tech™ program, inspiring girls to build technology skills, which is 
well aligned with the aims and objectives of EU Code Week and other initiatives supported by the European Commission. 
With our employees serving as role models and mentors, we developed a signature STEM curriculum based on global 
science and math standards that showcases our payments technology —algorithms, encryption, fraud detection, data 
analysis, digital convergence and the power of our network 1. We've reached more than one million girls in 31 countries  
(including in Europe) and we aim to reach five million by 2025.  
 
Improving information and collaboration.  Companies should also be incentivized to make high -level data analysis more 
available, easily searchable  and expand the scope of public data sharing. For example, M astercard shares value derived 
from data, including publishing white papers and documents that provide a picture of t rends and patterns or consumer 
behavior. Voluntary data insights sharing could be enhanced through  the creation of data sharing hubs, where use cases 
on a particular societal problem, sector or region could be promoted and future partners, research questio ns and data 
collaboratives identified. These hubs could share knowledge about what projects are possible, indicating what kind of data 
insights were involved in past projects, how outcomes can be effectively leveraged, what decisions can be  taken as a result 
and whether the outcomes will be as expected by the project design.  
 
In conclusion 
Trying to explain how best to unlock the power of data for all  – and why mandating raw data sharing  would not be the 
best way to achieve these goals – it may be useful to think about the analogy of cooking.  
 
No one can deny that there are some cooks out there (possibly your mother or the chef at a renowned multi-Michelin starred 
restaurant) that are capable of doing incredible things in the kitchen. Achieving these extreme levels of deliciousness 
requires a combination of things. Certainly, the ingredients matter, but so do the  recipes used, the tools available in the 
kitchen and the skills of the cook in question. Surely, we have all been in the situat ion where despite your best efforts and 
deliciously fresh fruit, veg or other artisan products, for whatever reason the meal you intended to “wow” ended up resulting 
in more “ow!”

To bring this analogy home to the world of data sharing, we must imagine the ingredients as the raw data, the recipe could 
be considered the data analysis technology or approach deployed (think of it like the  “how to use the data ingredients 
instructions”), whereas the professional, public authority or civil society organization  is the cook: they must know what to 
do with the data, how to use the tools available to them.  Assuming that the a dish will taste and look the same because 
the same ingredients are used, even though they would be prepared respective by  a Michelin-starred chef and a university 
student attempting to cook for herself  for the first time , would be foolish. Likewise, assuming that everyone needs a 
Michelin-star meal every time they eat would also be inappropriate. Just as raw data alone does not necessarily resu lt in 
transformational data insights, eggs, sugar, cream and salt are not going to turn into Crème Brûlée unless the proper skill, 
tools and recipe are leveraged. 
 
Some organizations will require Crème Brûlée and they’ll need to ensure that in addition to the data needed to get there, 
the skills and tools are available as well – if not in house, than through the partner with whom the data sharing is taking 
place. Other organizations may not need Crème Brûlée, they may be satisfied with a take-away meal at times, or a fruit 
salad, the recipe of which can be easily shared and replicated and – as long as the quality of the ingredients is good – will 
result in a perfect dessert. 
 
Taking this cooking analogy one step further, no matter who is cooking and what the ingredients are, if certain precautions 
are not taken, there is a risk of food poisoning. On the topic of risk, we believe the Data Act needs to avoid a preference for 
the sharing of  raw data . When raw data is shared  without proper safeguards , the value brought by the data sharing 
initiative could be jeopardized, causing harm and finally create distrust and unfairness for citizens and companies alike.

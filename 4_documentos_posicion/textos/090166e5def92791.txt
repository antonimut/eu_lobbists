Introduction  
The European Banking Federation (EBF) welcomes the opportunity to comment on the  
European Commission’s Inception Impact Assessment (IIA) on the Data Act and continues 
to support its ambition to build a Single Market for Data in Europe.  
Making more data available and improving the way in which data is accessed and used is 
essential for tackling a wide array of challenges. The Data Act is an opportunity to take a 
significant step forward in doing so and we would like to stress the need for more ambition, 
particularly when it comes to increased access to data across different sectors. Therefore, 
while we welcome the policy option to improve technical standards for portability of data 
generated by individuals, this should not be limited for personal data from IoT 
devices, as viewed by most EBF members.  The Data Act should take a cross-sectoral 
approach. Otherwise, the Data Act  risks not only missing the potential that an 
enhanced data portability right for other data sets from across sectors can bring, 
but it also  risks to create further asymmetries in the current data sharing 
ecosystem, particularly when it comes to interoperability and technical

A. CONTEXT, PROBLEM DEFINITION AND SUBSIDIARITY CHECK  
 
I. Context 
The IIA mentions that the horizontal legislative initiative “would complement the proposal 
for a Regulation on European Data Governance, already adopted by the Commissio n” 
(p.1). However, we would also recommend alignment with: 
i. The Digital Markets Act1, which enhances the data portability right for end users 
and extends this right to business users for data generated through the activities 
of a business user. 
ii. Developments at the sectoral level  e.g., the Revised Payment Services 
Directive (PSD2)2 and work done by the banking sector in this regard such as 
developing interfaces that allow data access.   
Regarding the following  in the Context explanation : “It may be complemented by 
initiatives for the individual sectoral data spaces or for data access and use in specific 
sectors” (p.1), we would like to once more underline the importance of taking a cross 
sectoral approach to avoid heightening existing asymmetries in data access. Sectoral data 
spaces should include not only the data generated within that specific sector, but the data 
needed to provide services  in that specific sector. It would also be helpful to 
understand the initiatives for the sectoral data spaces and the links with the Data 
Act, as the sectoral data spaces are meant to be understood “as a concept.”  
Finally, w e fully support the need to align an y actions with the General Data 
Protection Regulation ( GDPR). Any initiatives around data access or data  sharing of 
personal data must always be done on behalf of and be authorized by the 
customer.    
 
II. Problem the initiative aims to tackle 
 
a. Use of privately held data by the public sector  
The banking sector is already sharing a considerable amount of data with authorities and 
the experience of the financial sector can be taken as a reference for other sectors. There 
are also ongoing pilots in the RegTech/SupTech space. We would also like to  underline 
that fulfilling the current extensive reporting requirements in the banking sector incurs 
significant cost and any extension would further increase these and should be avoided.  
 
b. Data access and use in business-to-business situations 
The IIA men tions a “fairness test”  to avoid  unilaterally impose unfair conditions for 
access to and use of data in B2B situations. This proposal raises many questions, including 
who would perform this test,  can the results be challenged, what would be the

• Data portability for personal data from IoT devices 
We welcome the IIA’s recognition  that, in absence of an obligation to put in p lace 
technical interfaces for automated data exchange, including in real time, it can 
make it hard to offer certain services that require real time data flows. This is not 
only a disadvantage fo r IoT devices for personal use. An enhanced data portability right 
can offer opportunities across sectors. Please see Annex 1 for potential use cases.  
More broadly, the data portability right, as it currently stands under Art. 20 GDPR, focuses 
on ‘switching’ and ‘transferring’ the data from one service provider to another service 
provider. It does not factor in the non -rivalrous characteristic of data, which 
permits that the data can be used by the original data controller and the receiving 
company as well. In other words, giving the opportunity for the user to grant a third - 
party access to their data, with their consent, for a new service, for example , which will 
not interfere in the capacity of the current data holder to keep on providing its 
services based on the same data . This is a crucial point as the non -rivalrous 
characteristic of data means that the same data can generate value simultaneously in 
different sectors or even in different providers within the same sector. However, in all 
cases, it should ultimately be the customer who is in control of the data use.  
 
III.  Basis for intervention (legal basis and subsidiarity check) 
It is important that, in all the areas to be covered by the Data Act, the investments and 
added value made by companies when it comes to the data they hold is considered.  
Sharing of derived and inferred data should therefore, in members view, not be 
made mandatory or included in an enhanced data portability right under the Data 
Act. This type of data constitutes a crucial strategic and economic asset and is a strong 
element of competitiveness for companies. This is also in line with the Article 29 Working 
Party Guidelines on portability3, which note that the data concerned in the right is the data 
provided by the data subject, which they qualify as: 
• “Data actively and knowingly provided by the data subject (for example, mailing 
address, username, age, etc.) 
• Observed data provided by the data subject by virtue of the use of the service or 
the device. They may for example include a person’s search history, traffic data 
and location data. It may also include other raw data such as the heartbeat tracked 
by a wearable device.” 
The right on data portability should exclude inferred and derived data , the 
Guidelines also note4.  
 
B. OBJECTIVES AND POLICY OPTIONS  
 
I.  Objectives 
We support the objective to improve  technical standards for portability of data 
generated by individuals  to enable users to play a more active role in the data 
economy, taking advantage of the potential opportunities which can be offered (please 
see Annex 1 for potential opportunities). This enhanced data portability right must give

users the tools to decide when and with whom to share their data in a trusted 
and secure way; it must respect fundamental values and key principles 
underlying the regulatory framework which currently governs the protection and 
security of personal data . Leveraging on the portability right in the GDPR will help 
ensure this purpose.  
Improving technical standards for data portability is also an opportunity to improve 
data sharing across sectors – not just for IoT devices for personal use. The narrow 
scope of the objective risks to miss the point by:  
• Reducing customer choice; 
• Creating an unlevel playing field by proceeding only with enhanced data 
portability for these specific data sets; 
• Risking other sectors proceeding with diverging interoperability formats, 
creating more barriers for data sharing in the future. 
The Data Act should therefore open up this possibility for all sectors , with the data 
sets that should fall in the scope as a minimum from:  
• Telecommunication providers; 
• Utilities; 
• E-commerce marketplaces; 
• Mobility service providers. 
Enhanced data portability from certain digital platform providers (those designated as 
gatekeepers) is already included in the DMA proposal.   
 
II.  Policy Options 
 
We strongly support the approach of user centricity that the GDPR establishes 
and believe it should also be kept in the Data Act . This is the same approach taken 
in the DMA, where the user (both individuals and business users) will have the opportunity 
to effectively port/grant access to their data, while maintaining individual control over it.  
The IIA’s proposal to contribute to portability of data generated by individuals  through 
providing for technical specifications to help individuals take advantage of the portability 
right is supported by members, together with the proposal to mandate companie s selling 
smart home appliances, wearables , and home assistants to have in place technical 
interfaces that allow real time portability of the data these devices collect during their use. 
However, as mentioned above, the scope  for individual’s data  should not be limited to 
personal data from IoT devices, as viewed by most EBF members. The scope for this user-
centric data sharing should also be extended to companies’ data. This would complement 
the current proposal by extending the model proposed based on providers -driven data 
sharing to also include user driven data sharing. This will provide the opportunity to 
add a user-centric data sharing model, instead of data s hared by companies at the back 
of the consumer/business user that has generated such data.  
A cross-sectorial point of view is also very important, and the user-centric approach 
can contribute to realising cross -sectoral data sharing  since the boundaries 
across sectors have been blurred . This becomes clear when seeing how much the 
Green Data Space or the Health Data Space are naturally leveraging data from different 
contexts (i.e. mobility data). The focus becomes which data are needed/useful to 
provide better products/services to users in a given sector  rather than which 
data are generated in the specific sector.

Regarding the elements to consider for the technical specifications , we emphasize 
the need to look at access and transfer mechanisms, security, standardisation, and 
liability/redress. Each are examined in turn below.  
 
a) Access and transfer mechanism 
We would like to stress once more that access and or transfer data must always be done 
on behalf of and be authorised by the customer, and in line with the GDPR. 
Standard dedicated interfaces (i.e., APIs) with real-time direct data access 
between the data controller and the receiving company/user that can take place on a one-
off or recurrent basis are needed. It should be possible for users to initiate the access 
and transfer request through the interface of the receiving company, which 
would connect with the data controller for authenticating the user and executing 
the transfer.  
We would also encourage to take into account requirements specific to d ifferent sectors, 
such as the banking sector in order to reflect the reality that banks have already 
successfully developed interfaces allowing data access according to PSD2 that are meeting 
customer needs  in terms of security and convenience and continue engaging in 
collaborations across the ecosystem economy. This should be leveraged to the benefit of 
an efficient development of the data sharing ecosystem. Coherence with existing initiatives 
must be ensured and interoperability across standards is crucial.  
 
b) Security  
Appropriate requirements are needed to protect users from unauthorized data sharing. As 
in PSD2, authentication requirements and secure communication must therefore 
be part of the policy option. Security levels should be proportionate with the sensitivity 
of the information exchanged and equivalent to that required in the interface of the data 
controller where the user can access this information to. Authentication of the user should 
be similar in the direct and indirect request channels5.   
With regards to the customer interaction, it is important to strike the right balance between 
security and convenience. Transparency is therefore also an important element and is 
needed in terms of operationalizing data sharing. 
 
c) Standardisation 
Data f ormats, exchange protocols, security (authentication requirements and 
secure communication), and API models should be standardised , taking into 
account existing mechanisms and processes for standardisation across sectors, with the 
involvement of industry t o develop the technical standards. This would also take into 
account the broader initiatives that have been developed over the recent years at industry 
level, both at European and global level, (ISO standards, API technology, etc.) across 
different sectors (e.g. the financial sector).  
Also, while we recognize that it is largely in the competitive space, standardized and 
recognizable interfaces may be helpful for users and support building trust in data sharing.  
As interoperability is key, the Data Act sho uld mandate the development of technical 
standards that ensure interoperability between different technological tools (e.g. APIs), 
with industry involvement, based on international standards.  This includes finding a 
common approach in defining technical requirements for portability from a cross-

d) Liability/redress 
A proportionate approach could require firms wishing to access data on behalf of users to 
use appropriate security certificates and include a framework for resolving questions of 
liability and redress. 
Once data is transferred, the responsibility should be on the receiving company. The 
company sending the data should only be liable for the sharing process (e.g ., 
authentication, transmission). The user would have to provide consent, and then the 
responsibility would fall on the data receiver. Any further use of the data while being 
accessed (and for the data that based on legal obligations may have to be retaine d after 
such access has been revoked) shall be used within the possibilities provided in the GDPR.  
In addition to the technical elements mentioned above, we would like to emphasize the 
importance of aligning the proposed ac tions with other initiatives in the EU policy 
landscape on data to ensure consistency and give legal certainty for individuals and firms. 
 
Conclusion 
While the II is a good starting point when it comes to enhanced data portability for 
individuals, a broader scope is needed, one that goes  beyond personal data from IoT 
devices to all sectors  and holds user -centric data sharing at the core . The Data Act is a 
key instrument to build the Single Market for Data in Europe, it should not create an 
unlevel playing field to the detriment of consumers and firms.

ANNEX 1 – Potential Use Cases for Cross-Sectoral Data Sharing  
When looking at different scenarios and combinations of data, it is important to note that 
these are potential opportunities which could be leveraged in practice , in this case, 
by the banking sector. These must be handled carefully, in line with relevant regulation, 
such as the Ge neral Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). In this regard, any initiative 
will need to be carefully assessed in such a way that risks and opportunities are 
carefully balanced while respecting the privacy rights of individuals.  Some of the 
use cases are more prone to pose privacy risks than others, it is essential that each of 
these, prior to be put in practice once possible legal or other types of obstacles are 
removed, are subject to the scrutiny of proper data protection risk assessments. 
•  Green financing and sustainable investment opportunities   
The financial sector has a key role to play in the transition to a more sustainable economy 
including by ensuring that financing is able to flow towards sustainable investments and 
by supporting customers in their own decision making as they make their businesses and 
house-holds greener. Access, for instance, to data related to the purchase of organic 
products, services, consumption, etc. related to energy efficiency, food safety, was te 
management and climate change, would allow financial institutions to provide better 
advice on sustainable investments, offering tailor-made proposals on financial instruments 
related to the Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) sector. The tailor -made 
proposal can be sent to the targeted customers (e.g. retail customers, existing investors) 
who have provided access to such data to their banks, who are bound to use such data in 
line with GDPR. 
Two examples are carbon footprint calculations and sustai nability 
recommendations and loans for renewable energy installation. 
a) Carbon footprint calculations and sustainability recommendations:  Prices and 
quantities of utilities and goods consumed such as petrol, transport services, 
electricity, or water would al low a more accurate calculation of an individual’s 
environmental footprint, allowing comparisons regarding their efficiency and 
recommendations for more sustainable and/or cheaper suppliers. Any access to 
such information requires an explanation of which d ata are being accessed, for 
which purposes and how it will be further used. The consumer could receive an 
enhanced user experience through personalization and relevant information 
provision. Providing broader choices, recommendations and more information c an 
help consumers take action to reduce their environmental impact.  There are also 
benefits for the wider market and economy through the increased competition due 
to more comparison and active recommendations of alternative utilities providers. 
It also helps to increase sustainability by promoting individual action to reduce the 
environmental footprint. 
b) Loans for energy installation: By allowing consumers or firms to share information 
about their energy use and details regarding their property, more provid ers could 
build specifications to facilitate green loans for renewable energy installation.  A 
customer would share with the bank information held by their energy provider on 
their energy consumption and data on their property from the national cadastre. 
Such access and further use of data would take place in line with GDPR.  Potential 
benefits for the consumer would include the ability to receive advice directly from 
their bank on whether it is in their interest to make an investment in renewable

energy installation and how this could be financed. By easing the information and 
administrative constrains on individuals, much wider installation of independent 
renewable energy could be achieved, supporting the economy -wide energy 
transition. 
 
• Additional tools for decision making on personal finances 
Improved insight into spending habits – on behalf of and authorised by the customer- can 
allow the development of services that support users in improving their grip on their 
finances via personalised advice. Timely an d relevant financial advice supports financial 
inclusion by helping to overcome traditional information asymmetries and helps address 
challenges around financial education. To be able to provide these advisory services, data 
is needed from non-financial sector players. 
For example, access to customer purchase logs and wish lists in a defined e -commerce 
marketplace prior to the purchase being made could help to improve personalized financial 
advice. The customer could provide access to this data to their ban k or other financial 
advice providers when shopping online, after being made aware of such information will 
be used and what to expect from such use.  
This could help the customer in assessing whether they can afford a certain item given 
their current fina ncial situation and future financial needs. The customer could even be 
provided with the option to find alternative, cheaper, or greener suppliers for a given 
product. The result is a tool which helps to manage customer finances in an anticipated 
way, not only at an ex-port manner where a purchase has been made. Given the sensitive 
nature of the data, this use case should only be carried out once a proper data protection 
impact assessment has been conducted and the risks have been adequately mitigated.

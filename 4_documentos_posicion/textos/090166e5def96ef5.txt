European Commission Inception Impact Assessment consultation  
Distance Marketing of Financial Services Directive (DMFSD) 
 
Eurofinas is the voice of specialised consumer credit providers across Europe. As a Federation, Eurofinas 
brings together associations representing finance houses, specialised banks, captive finance companies 
of vehicles and equipment manufacturers, and universal banks. We welcome the opportunity to respond 
to the European Commission’s public consultation on the inception impact assessment on the Distance 
marketing of financial services Directive.  
 
Since the Directive’s adoption, a number of important developments have taken place with, not least, new 
market entrants, the digitalisation of retail financial services  in addition to evolving expectations  of 
consumers with regards to expedience, convenience and added value. Overall, the goals of the framework, 
i.e. ensuring a common framework for the distance marketing of financial services and a high level of 
consumer protection, still remain relevant and the framework has been able to preserve its relevance.  
 
This being said , the framework dating back to 2002 has also been superseded by both horizontal and 
product-specific legislation, e.g. the Consumer Credit and Mortgage Credit Directives and the General 
Data Protection Regulation. In light of this, we believe careful assessment and caution are required before 
any decision on whether or not to review the DM FSD, whilst not losing sight of the principles of better 
regulation and proportionality.  
 
With regards to the issues identified in the IIA, Eurofinas would like to stress the following:   
 
- Reduced relevance and added value of the Directive 
In response to the Inception Impact Assessment on the Review of the Directive on Distance Marketing of 
Consumer Financial Services (2002/65/EC) , Eurofinas would like  to note that most of the issues raised 
are already being addressed in the C CD and MCD review processes, as well as other complementary 
legislation and initiatives.  
 
In line with the established principles of the Better Regulation agenda, it would be prudent to first await the 
outcomes thereof prior to in parallel establishing a new set of competing requirements through a revised 
DMFSD.

- Pre-contractual information requirements not adapted to digital environment 
With the provision of large amount of information, the risk of information overload is substantial. This is 
further amplified by the duplication of information at the various stages, i.e. at the advertising, pre -
contractual information and contractual stages , and through overlapping frameworks where product -
specific legislation is calling for further and more prescriptive information to be provided.  
 
The issues are further accentuated through the increased prevalence of digital distribution channels, not 
least the use of mobile devices. It has become increasingly difficult to ensure that the consumer is able to 
make an informed choice and, at the same time, to provide for a customer -focused, seamless journey. 
The consumer may often be overwhelmed by the onerous amount of information, which, while prescribed, 
is often viewed as complex and technical, and overwhelming to overview on mobile devices. 
 
There is a need to better adapt the information requirements to the digital channels, to simplify and to 
streamline the information relevant to the different stages of the process as well as to the complexity and 
value of the financial service on offer . This would help consumers to make informed comparisons and 
decisions, and, at the same time, meet consumers’ demand for a smooth and sea mless experience. 
However, relevant adaptations should be made in the product -specific directives. Indeed, the DMFSD’s 
main value builds on its horizontal and technologically neutral approach , which ensures a strong  
adaptability to new products and/or technologies. 
 
- Right of withdrawal not fully effective 
It is essential to also continuously ensure that consumers benefit from having access to relevant 
information at the appropriate time, to allow them to make informed decisions and exercise their various 
rights, via both on- and offline channels. The right should be at least as easy to exercise as the entry into 
of lending agreement, e.g. if a credit can be agreed online, the right of withdrawal should be able to 
exercise with the same ease. In this context, and as shown in the Commission’s evaluation report , the 
current right of withdrawal under the CCD, providing for a 14 -day withdrawal period, has been proven to 
be effective.  
 
The Commission’s New Consumer Agenda stresses the need for a comprehensive and holistic approach 
to be taken with regards the Euro pean consumer protection framework, thereby ensuring an equal level 
of protection across the Member States as well as across sectors. 
 
The issues related to the right of withdrawal  under the DMFSD raise no specific concerns. The potential 
issues mentioned would be better addressed in product-specific legislation and its enforcement. 
 
- Practices creating issues for consumers purchasing financial services at a distance that 
are not covered by the Directive such as non-transparent personalisation of offers, display 
of the offers in a way that exploits consumer behaviour (e.g. pre-ticked boxes), emphasising 
benefits over cost. 
The issues outlined above are already subject to a myriad of national and European legislative initiatives, 
not in the least the Unfair Commercial Pra ctices Directive, Consumer Rights Directive, as well as 
Consumer Credit Directive and Mortgage Credit Directive. Moreover, personalisation and targeting  are 
already today subject to the relevant provisions of the GDPR  as well as the ePrivacy Directive currently 
under review. As such, little benefit would be gained by pursuing this as well in a revised DMFSD providing 
for additional provisions on the same subject-matter.

- Cross border transactions 
As identified by the Commission previously, the level of pure cross -border transactions, involving a 
financial service provider  in one Member State and a customer in another one, has not substantially 
increased. The reasons behind this are multi-faceted: low consumer demand due to language barriers and 
a general home bias, allied to already good access to strong and competitive local markets.  
 
From a business point of view, the provision of credit in a cross -border setting may often be viewed as 
complex from an operational and legal point of view. The need to take into account deviating national legal 
frameworks following on the Rome I Regulation when/if marketing to another Member State, requires 
substantial resources in the form of legal expertise as well as other relevant functions with country-specific 
knowledge.  
 
Enabling the digital journey may play a role in increasing these activities. However, many of the limiting 
factors still remain outside the remit of the financial services frameworks, for example, diverging national 
consumer protection frameworks as well as important differences in contract, taxation as well as recovery 
and insolvency law. There is also a general lack of convergence concerning remote identification, 
electronic signatures and KY C processes, etc. Concrete obstacles persist for providers in enabling 
effective and / or compliant tools to enable fully digital solutions in relation to, for example, verification of 
identities or the signing of credit agreements. This is the case for bo th domestic and cross -border 
transactions. It is unlikely that the supply and demand for cross -border transactions would substantially 
increase without addressing many of these issues which are in fact outside of the scope of the DMFSD  
and further subject to on-going initiatives.  
 
- Objectives and Policy options 
 
The IIA rightly points that since 2002, product-specific as well as horizontal legislation has been introduced, 
thereby reducing the importance and relevance of the Directive. As most of the issues addressed either 
fall directly within the scope of the oth er legislation, or, alternatively, cannot be addressed by applying 
modifications only to the European regulatory framework without addressing national legislative provisions 
across Europe, As such, it would be unwise to move forward with a full revision of  the DMFSD prior to 
having completed the  review processes of the CCD and MCD  as well as announced and expected 
regulatory next steps in relation to AI and open finance initiatives.

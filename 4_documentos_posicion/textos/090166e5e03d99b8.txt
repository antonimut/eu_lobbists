  France	Assos	Santé	expectations	and	views	on	the	future	of	the	Product	Liability	Directive	(85/374/EEC).	  Healthcare	products	are	expected	to	cure	people.	However,	they	might	be	harmful,	even	in	the	absence	of	misuse.	Should	they,	in	these	rare	cases,	engage	the	responsibility	of	their	producers?		Healthcare	 products’	 adverse	 effects	 or	 reactions	 are	 secondary	 effects	 that,	 contrary	 to	 the	primary	 expected	 beneficial	 effects,	are	 unwanted	 and	 can	 be	 very	 harmful	 for	 the	 patient.	Severe	adverse	effects	may	even	cause	the	death	or	the	permanent	disability	of	the	patient.	They	can	however	be	accepted	by	the	regulators	when	the	expected	benefits	are	considered	superior	to	the	anticipated	risks,	and	especially	when	the	occurrence	of	the	adverse	effect	remains	very	rare.	It	has	to	be	noted	that	there	is	no	way	for	a	patient	to	prevent	the	occurrence	of	a	severe	adverse	event.	Therefore,	the	question	is:	“Who	should	be	liable	for	this	risk?”		- The	 patient,	 who	 is	 supposed	 to	 be	 fully	 informed,	 when	 the	 risk	 is	 mentioned	 on	 the	leaflet?	- The	producer,	who	is	in	the	position	to	generate	a	profit	from	its	activity?	- The	 society	 that	 benefits	 as	 a	 whole	 from	 the	 progress	 brought	 by	the	healthcare	product?		Creating	many	hurdles	for	victims	of	adverse	events	seeking	compensation,	the	Product	Liability	Directive	has	de	facto	left	the	patient	with	the	full	burden	of	the	risk.		Is	this	a	socially	and	politically	acceptable	choice?		This	situation	is	both	unfair	for	the	victims	of	the	adverse	events	and	highly	counter-productive	from	a	public	health	perspective.	On	one	hand,	the	victims	are	the	ultimate	and	weakest	link	of	the	 pharmaceutical	 product	 life	 cycle.	 On	 the	 other	 hand,	 this	de	 facto	 exemption	 of	responsibility	 is	 a	 strong	 deterrent	 for	companies	 to	 invest	 in	 the	 prevention,	 mitigation	 and	management	 of	 adverse	 effects.	 While	 pharmaceutical	 companies	spend	every	y e a r 	b i l l i o n s 	o f 	dollars	 in	 R&D,	most	o f 	it	 is	 invested	 to	 demonstrate	 their	 products'	b e n e f i t s	a n d 	v e r y 	l i t t l e 	i s 	done	 to	 improve	 the	 drugs’	 safety	 profiles.	 For	 instance,	research	 on	the	 Stevens-Johnson	Syndrome	receives	 scarce	 funding	with	 no	 significant	 industry	 involvement,	 while	 it	 is	a	life-threatening	condition	resulting	 from	 a	 rare	adverse	 reaction	 to	several	 drugs,	 including	antibiotics,	anti-inflammatory	treatments,	antiepileptic	drugs,	antiviral	drugs,	and	many	others.	For	all	those	reasons,	the	main	objective	of	France	Assos	Santé	is	to	make	sure	that	producers	of	healthcare	products	for	human	use	will	be	liable	for	the	damages	caused	by	their	products	and	that	victims	can	be	fairly	compensated	for	the	damages	they	suffer.

Product:		Should	the	EU’s	product	liability	legislation	and	its	strict	liability	regime	cover	all	tangible	or	 non-tangible	 items	 (including	 software)	 causing	 damage? The	central	question	for	France	Assos	Santé	concerning	the	scope	of	the	Directive	could	rather	be	 formulated	 as	 follows:	“Does	 the	 current	 EU	 product	 liability	 legislation	 effectively	protect	 the	 interests	 of	 patients	 and	 consumers	 with	 respect	 to	healthcare	products	 for	human	use?”.		It	 is	 necessary	 to	 guarantee	 that	 patients	 and	 consumers	 can	 effectively	 claim	 compensation	from	the	producers	for	damages	caused	by	healthcare	products,	which	is	not	the	case	with	the	current	 legislation. It	 is	now	s o	difficult	 for	 victims	 to	 obtain	 redress	 for	 damages	 caused	 by	healthcare	products	based	 on	 the	 Directive	 that	 some	 of	 them	 try	 to	 get	 compensation	 based	 on	 other	 liability	regimes. As	 a	 result,	victims	 are	 more	likely	to	obtain	reparation	when	the	defective	product	is	received	in	the	hospital,	which	creates	an	unequal	protection	of	patients	and	consumers	contrary	to	the	objectives	of	the	Directive. Defect:		Do	you	think	that	it	would	be	necessary	to	maintain	the	notion	of	defect	in	the	Directive?	If	not,	how	would	you	conceive	it	differently?		Article	 6(1)	 of	 the	 Directive	 specifies	 that	 a	 product	 is	 defective	 when	 it	 does	 not	 provide	 the	safety	a	person	is	entitled	to	expect,	taking	all	circumstances	into	account.	When	a	person	takes	a	 medicinal	product,	 he/she	 expects	 his/her	 state	 of	 health	 to	 benefit	 from	 it,	 which	 includes	remaining	free	of	severe	and	rare	adverse	events,	shall	they	be	listed	in	the	patients’	leaflet	or	not.		When	the	damage	was	statistically	foreseeable,	though	unavoidable	–	as	in	the	case	of	identified	possible	 adverse	 events	 of	 medicines	–	t h e 	p r o ducer	 should	 be	 held	 liable	 even	though	 he	 has	taken	 all	 possible	 precautions	 to	 avoid	 the	 damage.	 A	 warning	 alerting	 consumers	 of	unavoidable	damages	that	might	be	caused	by	the	product,	like	serious	but	rare	adverse	events	mentioned	in	patient’s	leaflets,	should	not	exempt	the	producer	from	liability.		Moreover,	for	victims	of	adverse	events,	it	is	almost	impossible	to	demonstrate	both	the	defect	and	the	causal	relationship	between	defect	and	damage:	- either	 the	 risk	 is	 known	 (based	 on	s c i e n t i f i c 	s t u d i e s 	o r 	p h a r m a c o v i g i l a n c e	d a t a)	 and		mentioned	in	the	leaflet,	so	the	product	will	not	be	considered	as	defective.

Exemptions	to	liability:		 Should	the	development-risk	clause	be	maintained?		No.	It	should	be	noted	that	it’s	the	only	part	of	the	directive	that	countries	can	opt	out,	even	if	few	countries	did. Most	 of	 these	 risks	 can	 only	 be	 evidenced	 in	 phase	 IV	clinical	 trials	 over	 large	 populations	 or	 through	 pharmacovigilance	 signals	 and	 real	 life	 data,	after	the	product	has	started	to	be	marketed	and	used	by	patients. Therefore,	 the	 discrepancy	 between	 the	 legal	 protection	 granted	 to	participants	in	clinical	trials	and	the	lack	of	protection	resulting	from	the	current	application	of	the	 product	 liability	 directive	 to	 all	 other	 patients	 is	 not	 understandable,	 all	 the	 more	 so	 as	innovative	drugs	are	usually	sold	at	incredibly	high	prices. Timelines:		 Are	you	of	the	opinion	that	the	current	timelines	for	liability	claims	are	appropriate?		Article	10(1)	of	the	Directive,	which	introduces	a	limitation	period	of	3	years	to	proceedings	for	the	 recovery	 of	 damages,	 makes	 any	 attempt	 by	 victims	 of	 pharmaceutical	 products	 to	 get	redress	 for	 the	 adverse	 events	 they	 experienced	 practically	 impossible.	Both	 the	 short	 time	period	–	3	years	–	and	the	starting	point	of	the	limitation	period	–	‘The	limitation	period	begins	to	 run	 from	 the	 day	 on	 which	 the	 plaintiff	 became	 aware,	 or	 should	reasonably	 have	 become	aware,	of	the	damage,	the	defect	and	the	identity	of	the	producer’	–	are	considerable	obstacles.	As	a	reference,	medical	liability	actions	engaging	the	responsibility	of	 healthcare	 professionals	or	 healthcare	institutions	 in	 France	 are	 time-barred	 10	 years	 after	the	 consolidation	 of	 the	health	state	of	the	victim	(Article	1142-28	of	the	French	Public	Health	Code).	In	case	of	late/misdiagnosis	–	very	frequent	when	dealing	with	rare	syndromes	resulting	from	medicines’	 side	 effects	–	o r 	e v o l v i n g 	p e r s o n a l 	i n j u r i e s , 	t h e 	v i c t i m s ’ 	l e g a l 	a c t i o n s 	b a s e d 	o n 	t h e	Directive	will	systematically	be	time-barred. As	regards	the	extinction	of	rights,	Article	11	of	the	Directive	reads:	‘The	rights	conferred	upon	the	injured	person	pursuant	to	the	Directive	extinguish	upon	the	expiry	of	a	period	of	10	years	from	 the	 date	 on	which	 the	 producer	 put	 into	 circulation	 the	 actual	 product	 which	 caused	 the	damage,	 unless	 the	 injured	 person	 has	 in	 the	 meantime	 instituted	 proceedings	 against	 the	producer’.	First,	it	is	difficult	for	victims	of	adverse	events	to	determine	the	date	on	which	the	producer	put	the	pharmaceutical	product	into	circulation,	as	they	often	don’t	keep	track	of	the	medicines	they	take.	 It	 would	 thus	 be	 very	 helpful	 to	 reverse	 the	 burden	 of	 proof,	 asking	 the	 producer	 to	establish	the	date	of	release	of	the	actual	product.	Second,	 10	 years	 is	 a	 very	 short	 time-period	 when	 victims	 need	 to	 establish	 the	 causal	relationship	between	the	pharmaceutical	product	they	took	and	the	side	effect	they	experienced.	For	 example,	 several	 victims	 of	 Lyell’s	 syndrome	 caused	 by	Tetrazepam	 had	 to	wait	 over	 10	years	 before	 this	 risk	 was	 recognised	by	 the	 French	 Medicines	 Agency,	which	withdrew	 the	product	from	the	market	in	July	2013	because	of	a	late	negative	benefit	/	risk	assessment5.	There	 should	thus	be	 just	 one	 limitation	 period	 lasting	 minimum	 10	 years.	It	s h o u l d 	start	running	from	the	day	on	which	the	plaintiff	became	aware,	or	should	reasonably	have	become	aware	of	the	damage,	the	defect	and	the	identity	of	the	producer.	What	 about	 cases	 where	 the	 damage	 occurs	 within	 the	 first	 ten	 years	 of	 placing	 the	product	on	the	market,	but	effects	only	become	apparent	later?		This	 situation	 is	 indeed	 frequent	 with	t e r a t o g e n i c 	e f f e c t s 	of	 medicines	 taken	 by	 pregnant.	The	effects	 on	 the	 children’s	 health	 are	 often	 evidenced	 more	 than	t e n 	y e a r s 	a f t e r 	t h e 	i n t a k e 	o f 	medicine	by	the	mother	(see	Depakine	for	instance).		The	only	starting	point	of	the	timeline	for	liability	claims	should	be	the	consolidation	of	the	state	of	 health	 of	 the	 victim	 (late/misdiagnosis	 are	 frequent	 when	 dealing	 with	 rare	 syndromes	resulting	from	medicines’	side	effects).

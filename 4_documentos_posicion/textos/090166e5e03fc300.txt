I. REVISION OF THE PRODUCT LIABILITY 
DIRECTIVE 85/374/EEC 
Fit-for-purpose 
ACEA believes that the European legal framework for product liability based on the 
Product Liability Directive (the Directive) is generally fit for purpose. The Directive 
provides legal certainty for consumers and allows for their effective compensation 
where required. It also respects development processes and innovation, and thus 
provides a well‐balanced liability system. Regarding the next generation of automated 
vehicles, ACEA considers the current liability regime also as generally sufficient and 
balanced. 
ACEA believes that some careful and limited adaptation of the scope of the Directive 
may nevertheless be beneficial.  
 
A. ADAPTATION OF THE DIRECTIVE TO THE 
DIGITAL AGE AND CIRCULAR ECONOMY 
Intangible product  
ACEA believes that the definition of “products” as provided in Article 3 of the Directive 
should be adapted to ensure that it covers damages caused by digital contents and 
software. We believe that developers, manufacturers and providers of digital content, 
software, applications, and algorithms that are embedded in the product and that serve 
to use the  product as it was intended should be held liable for damages caused by 
defects in these intangible products. This reflects the reality of today’s interconnected 
products the safe functioning of which can be affected by such intangible elements. 
Changes to the product 
The Directive should cover defects resulting from changes to products after they have 
been put into circulation. Companies that remanufacture or refurbish products should 
be held liable for da mages caused by defects  resulting from these changes. To the 
extent that the remanufacture or refurbishment results in a substantial modification of 
the initial product, they should be considered producers for the purpose of this Directive 
and be subject to all obligations it imposes on producers.  
Similarly, developers, manufacturers and providers of digital content, software, 
applications, and algorithms that are installed in a product after it has been put on the 
market and that serve to use the  product as it was intended should be held liable for

damages caused by defects in these intangible products. This also follows from the 
inclusion of intangible products in the scope of the Directive as suggested above. 
Cybersecurity 
ACEA does not believe that the strict liability rules provided under the Directive are 
adapted to tackle cybersecurity risks. We believe addressing critical cybersecurity 
issues is crucial and should be a fully integrated part of the development process of a 
product. Products should be designed so that they are protected against all credible 
and reasonably foreseeable threats that might occur over a reasonable timespan. 
Nevertheless, no system can be 100% secure; despite a producer’s best efforts, every 
system has a vulnerability, and the evolving cyberthreat landscape means that every 
mitigation measure put in place by a producer will have a shelf life.  
Producers of products with cybersecurity vulnerabilities should therefore not  be held 
strictly liable for damages caused by cyber-attacks. Their liability should be limited to 
cybersecurity issues that were foreseen or reasonably foreseeable, but for which no 
adequate mitigation measure was implemented, or for which the mitigation measure 
implemented was defective.  
Range of damages 
ACEA does not believe that the range of damages covered under the Directive needs 
to be extended to cover non-material damages. The automobile industry believes that 
compensation for such damages can already be sought by injured parties und er the 
Directive (e.g., psychological harm) or under other EU instruments (e.g. GDPR). 
Environmental damages appear particularly unfit to be included in the scope of the 
Directive since they relate to harm done to society rather than to an individual. 
 
B. EVIDENCE AND PROCEDURAL ASPECTS OF 
THE DIRECTIVE 
Burden of proof 
ACEA does not believe that rules of the Directive relating to evidence require revision. 
The information, technical or otherwise, to which an injured party may have access to 
support their claim is a matter of national procedural law, and it is up to national courts 
to decide what information the defendant will be ordered to disclose to the plaintiff.  
Similarly, what a court may or may not infer from existing case law and previous claims 
for damages caused by products in the same production line is a matter of national 
procedural law. It should further be noted that in cases where a multitude of consumers 
suffer damages caused by defects in the same product, consumers may already rely

on Directiv e (EU) 2020/1828 on representative actions for the protection of the 
collective interests of consumers to bring a collective claim for compensation against 
the producer of that product, based on the provision of the Product Liability Directive. 
Reversing the burden of proof 
ACEA strongly cautions against reversing the burden of proof in the Product Liability 
Directive. The principle that a plaintiff must prove their claim is common to all Member 
States and exceptions to that principle, whether at national or European level, are few 
and far between. Such a reversal would have severe and far -reaching consequences 
for all industries in the EU, as it would create an assumption that all damages suffered 
due to the use of a product was caused by a defect in that product. This could quickly 
turn into an assumption that all products put on the market can be assumed to be 
defective, until proven otherwise. This would severely impact consumer trust. It would 
also very likely lead to a dramatic surge in the number of la wsuits brought against 
producers in the EU, many of which will likely be frivolous, which would not only 
financially burden the industry, but also courts in all Member States. 
 
II. LIABILITY FOR ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE  
ACEA does not believe that the Directive  needs to be amended to cover products 
equipped with Artificial Intelligence (AI). Pursuant to article 1 of the Directive, the 
producer is liable for damage caused by a defect in his product. The Directive further 
provides that a product is considered defe ctive when it does not provide the safety 
which a person is entitled to expect, “taking all circumstances into account”. As such, 
the Directive covers damages resulting from defective products irrespective of whether 
this product is equipped with AI technology. 
ACEA therefore stresses that the need for revisions to the current EU legislative 
framework for liability to cover the risks caused by certain AI applications, must be 
clearly demonstrated and any potential gaps in the current application EU legislative 
framework clearly established.  
Furthermore, as the Directive is a horizontal regulation covering all sectors, any 
assessment of the need for revision must meet certain criteria. It must be cross ‐ 
sectorial, as it cannot be undertaken in a way that tackles a single aspect of one 
specific sector; it must be conducted in an open form so as to involve the ov erall 
stakeholder community; and it must take into consideration interdependencies with 
other legal areas, including data protection law.

Harmonisation 
ACEA does not believe that the Member States’ approach to extracontractual liability 
for AI has created any legal uncertainty or legal fragmentation. Manufacturers do not 
believe that the current situation warrants any broad harmonisation of liability for AI at 
EU level. Nevertheless, the industry believes that the emergence of such issues should 
be avoided, and that this would be best achieved through the adoption of non-binding 
initiatives.  
Guidance 
European institutions should issue recommendations  and guidance  with a view to 
ensuring that Member States put in place clear rules regarding extra -contractual 
liability applicable to damages caused by AI, and to avoid any wide and irresolvable 
discrepancies between the rules applicable in various European jurisdictions, thus 
averting the emergence of legal fragmentation or legal uncertainty. 
ACEA therefore beli eves that the adoption of such non -binding initiatives should be 
given priority , and that any legally binding initiative should only be envisaged after 
careful consideration, should such  non-binding measures prove ineffective in 
preventing the emergence of legal fragmentation and legal uncertainty.

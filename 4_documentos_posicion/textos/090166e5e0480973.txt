 
  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
BSA Comments on the Initial Impact Assessment on  
adapting liability rules to the digital age 
 
July 2021 
 
 
 
BSA |The Software Alliance 1 welcomes the opportunity to provide input into the European 
Commission’s Initial Impact Assessment  (IIA) on adapting liability rules to the digital age and 
circular economy.  
 
BSA is a strong proponent of the broad objectives the Commission articulates in the IIA — to 
promote the smooth functioning of the internal market, and to protect consumers against harm 
and ensure they can be remediated where harm occurs. We also recognize that the P roduct 
Liability Directive (PLD) is three decades old, and that stepping back to consider the need for its 
reform is warranted. That said, for now, we support the Commission’s Baseline Scenario (i.e. no 
immediate reforms to the existing regime) rather than any of the proposed options set out in the 
IIA.  
 
There are two important reasons to pause before re -opening and revising the PLD in the 
immediate term: 
 
• First, it remains unclear what problem the reforms are intended to fix. T he PLD has 
worked well in a wide variety of contexts for over 30 years . The PLD sets out clear, w ell 
understood and time -tested rules that apply across a wide ran ge of products, including 
those with embedded software . The Directive is complemented by national civil liability 
frameworks that reflect Member State legal traditions and principles, including 
evidentiary rules, damages regimes, and contract and tort law . Before upending this 
longstanding system, it is important to clearly identify the problems that need to be 
solved. At present, however, many of the harms identified in the IIA are unsubstantiated 
and speculative. For example, the IIA speculates that t he s pecific characteristics of AI

“could make it difficult and costly” in future for injured parties to prove defect or fault. 
The IIA engages in similar speculation about other types of software. But the IIA fails to 
cite any concrete evidence, at either EU o r Member State level, that consumers, on the 
rare occasions when they are harmed by these technologies, cannot obtain compensation 
for those harms. The IIA (Option 1(b)) also suggests that the PLD could be extended to 
encompass immaterial harms, including privacy-related harms, without explaining why 
this might be necessary—in particular given the GDPR and the robust protections for data 
subjects that it provides. Rushing to change liability rules to address hypothetical concerns 
risks chilling innovation into socially beneficial (and even safety -enhancing) AI, which 
would leave consumers worse off in the longer term . We encourage the Commission to 
do more to identify any actual weaknesses in the current system, and only then to develop 
reforms that are targeted at fixing those issues. 
 
• It also remains unclear how any new liability regime will intersect with the proposed AI 
Act. Product safety and product liability are complementary regimes designed to pursue 
complementary policy goals: to encourage the development of safe products, and to 
ensure that consumers have recourse when things go wrong . Product safety regimes 
achieve the first goal by requiring producers to take steps to ensure that the products they 
market are safe; liability rules achieve the second goal by allowing consumer to recover 
for the harms caused by defective products. The two regimes thus go hand -in-hand, and 
must be fully aligned to ensure they achieve their goals in a sensible manner and that they 
work both for business and for consumers. In the case of AI, however, the EU is currently 
considering a new safety framework, in the form of the AI Act. It is cle ar that the AI Act 
will directly impact the EU’s liability regime. For example, a number of the Commission’s 
concerns center on the perceived lack of transparency in relation to AI —and the IIA 
proposes changes to the burden of proof rules in the PLD to address these concerns. But 
the AI Act includes several provisions that will impose transparency requirements on 
providers of AI —requirements that may alleviate the concerns identified by the 
Commission and therefore cut against any proposal to change burdens  of proof. This is 
just one example of how the AI Act will be highly relevant in determining what changes, if 
any, may be needed to the PLD. In other examples, the IIA proposes that, in reforming the 
PLD, “the strictly liable person could be identified by reference to the ‘user’ as defined in 
the proposed AI Act” (Option 1(c)) and also that it may be appropriate to reverse the 
burden of proof in cases of “non -compliance with AI -specific obligations” in the AI Act  
(Option 2.2(b)). Whether these and similar reforms make sense, however, will turn on 
how “user” is ultimately defined, and on the obligations that sit on users, under the final 
AI Act. Any changes to the liability framework—whether in the form of a Recommendation 
to Member States (per Option 1(c)(1)) or actual changes to the EU legislative regime —
must await the conclusion of the AI Act’s legislative process in order to ensure the two 
regimes are appropriately aligned.  
 
If the Commission nonetheless chooses to proceed, we encourage it to follow the p rinciples 
below: 
 
• Focus on consumer harm. The reforms proposed in Options 1 and 2 in the IIA suggest that 
the focus of any legislative changes will be on consumer harms, and that reforms will not 
extend to business harms. We agree with this proposed scoping. Companies have well -
established practices for allocating liability for potential harms through contract, as 
appropriate to the risk, context, and applicable laws. Businesses are thus better served by

having contractual flexibility to negotiate commercial terms with software / AI developers 
and other partners in the supply chain that are calibrated to the risks for the particular 
project and the roles of the parties. The PLD and national liability regimes are focused on 
compensating consumer (not business) harms for this reason . Any new liability rules (or 
clarification of existing rules as they apply to software / AI)  should similarly focus on 
consumer harm.  
 
• Recognize the difference be tween physical products and software. Each of the 
Commission’s proposed reforms in Option 1  would extend the  existing liability regime 
beyond physical products, to encompass software (including, but not limited to, AI). In 
contrast to physical products, wh ich are generally used for a foreseeable range of 
purposes, however, software often can be deployed in an almost unlimited number of 
ways across a wide range of devices  for many different purposes ; further, software’s 
functioning often depends heavily on the person using  the service and the context in 
which it is used . The PLD recognizes this distinction . It imposes no -fault liability on 
defective products. Where software is embedded in a product before it is put into 
circulation, the PLD provid es a remedy. But it does not extend liability to stand -alone 
software that a user subsequently downloads onto a device . Other EU laws also 
recognize the distinction between products and services, e.g. the recently adopted Digital 
Content Directive versus the Sales of Goods Directive. We thus encourage the Commission 
to keep the focus of the PLD on products. To the extent the Commission believes there is 
a need for a liability framework for software, we encourage the Commission to identify 
real and demonstrable gaps in the existing protections available to consumers (including 
via consumer protection laws, cybersecurity rules, the recently adopted Digital Content 
Directive, and various national rules) and to target any changes narrowly and precisely.  
 
• Ensure any new rules are technology neutral. The IIA proposes a range of specific reforms 
to the EU liability regime that would target AI systems and the products that integrate 
them (see Options 1(c), 2.1(c), 2.2(b)- (d)). Products and services shouldn’t be subj ect to 
unique or heightened liability rules simply because they integrate AI . That approach will 
deter providers from offering AI-driven products that may in fact make consumers safer, 
out of fear that doing so could expose them to increased liability. Further, because AI is 
deployed in a virtually limitless set of scenarios — from AI-powered tangible products and 
fully autonomous systems to AI software that merely assists or informs human action and 
decision-making, and including systems that evolve through machine learning and / or can 
be personalized — a one-sized-fits-all liability regime (as proposed in Option 1(c)(iv) of the 
IIA) would be all but impossible to implement. In effect, such a regime would impose the 
same liability rules on both driverless cars and, for example, AI systems that retailers use 
to predict what color of paint a particular customer might prefer.  
 
• Impose liability on the party best able to mitigate risk. As the proposed AI Act recognizes, 
many AI systems are deve loped by multiple entities working in collaboration, and they 
have the potential to operate in a wide range of different settings, with the involvement 
of a number of actors . Some parties create “enabling technologies” for AI —  such as 
database hosting capabilities, template tools and AI models. Other parties may customize 
these tools for specific use cases. And these parties may be separate from the party that 
actually deploys the AI in the real world. A key goal of any reform to the EU product liability 
regime should be to ensure that liability for harm falls on the entity best positioned to 
4 
 
foresee and mitigate risk — generally the user, as suggested in IIA Option 1(c). This would 
be similar to the GDPR’s concept of controller.  Article 29 Working Party gu idance on 
controllers and processors (WP 169) describes this party as the “determining body” that 
decides the “how” and the “why” of a processing operation. Applying this concept in the 
context of AI, the “AI controller” will generally be the deployer of an AI system (e.g., a 
vehicle manufacturer that integrates an AI -driven language recognition system into an 
automobile, or a bank that uses an AI tool to score consumers for loans).  
 
• Apply strict liability only in narrow circumstances, to the highest risk AI. The type of strict 
liability seemingly contemplated in Option 1(c)  — i.e. liability that is imposed regardless 
of the producer’s  fault or whether a product is defective —  is generally reserved for 
extreme circumstances involving abnormally dangerous activities that present substantial 
risks to others. In effect, the “condition” of allowing this substantial risk is that the party 
imposing it must take responsibility for harms that result from it, irrespective of any fault 
or product defect. Strict liability should only be imposed on AI systems in similarly narrow 
circumstances. Given the current state of AI development and deployment, subjecting 
today’s AI systems to strict liability of the sort contemplated in IIA Option 1(c) is 
unwarranted. When considering future AI -driven products, strict liability would be 
appropriate only when a product operates in public spaces and poses a substantial risk of 
physical harm to “innocent bystanders” ( i.e. individuals that did not willingly engage with 
the AI enabled product in some way).  
 
BSA would be happy to discuss these issues further with the European Commission or other 
interested parties.

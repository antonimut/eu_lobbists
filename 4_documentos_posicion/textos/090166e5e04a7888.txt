Microsoft Comments on the Inception Impact Assessment  
“Adapting liability rules to the digital age and circular economy” 
 
Microsoft appreciates  this opportunity to offer  feedback on the Commission’s Inception Impact 
Assessment on Adapting Liability Rules to the Digital Age and Circular Economy (“IIA”), in particular its 
proposals on new liability rules for artificial intelligence (“AI”) and software.  
 
At Microsoft, we use the power of AI in many of our products and services, and we offer a range of AI 
models, platforms, and services that our cust omers integrate into t heir own systems and  services. 
Through this work, we’ve seen first -hand the  many ways in which  AI can  power innovative n ew 
products and  make existing products better , more accurate  and more accessible . AI will also be 
instrumental in helping solve the worl d’s greatest challenges. Our AI for Good initiative showcases a 
few ways in which organizations are using AI  to benefit humankind , from tackling environmental 
degradation and improving healt hcare to promoting humanitarian  action and protecting cultural 
heritage. 
 
We also recognize that AI, like any powerful technology, can present risks and where amongst the first 
major technology companies to adopt Responsible AI Principles. We operationalize these principles 
through our Office of Responsible AI (ORA), our  AI, Ethics, and Effects in Engineering and Research 
(Aether) Committee, and our Responsible AI Strategy in Engineering (RAISE).  
 
Against that ba ckdrop, we welcome the Commission’s  thoughts on w hether to change the EU’s 
product liability rules to take account of AI, or to expan d those rules to apply to softw are. Given the 
Commission’s proposed AI Act, this is a timely discussion. The following comments first set out several 
principles that we believe should guide any reform of the EU’s existing product liability rules, and then 
respond to specific policy options set out in the IIA. 
 
I. Guiding Principles 
 
• Maintain a technology-neutral approach 
 
We urge the Commission to maintain a technology-neutral approach to liability , as  set out in the 
Product Liability Directive (“PLD”). Producers should not be subject to heightened liability rules simply 
because their products include an AI component. The term “ AI” encompasses a broad range of 
technologies that can take i nnumerable forms and be integrated into  products and services in even 
more ways. Also, the AI component of a product might have no influence on safety risks (e.g., AI 
integrated into a vehicle’s entertainment system).  It make s little sense to apply a single set of 
heightened liability rules to offerings as diverse as  an AI-powered braking system , a “smart” 
thermostat, and an app for arranging photos on a phone. Such an approach elevates form (whether a 
product falls within a legal def inition of AI) over substance (whether  the use of AI in a part icular 
product creates unique health or safety concerns) and could deter producers from offering AI-driven 
products to EU consumers, even if those products are safer than their non-AI counterparts.  
 
We encourage, the Commission to maintain the technology-neutral liability approach set out in the 
PLD that leaves it to the courts, in individual cases, to take account of any unique health or safety risks 
that a specific product may present, just as courts do today for other types of technologies (electrical 
products, those using an internal combustion engine, etc.).

• Pursue an evidence-based reform  
 
The current EU product liability regime has served consumers well for over 30 years. Over this period, 
laptops, tablets, smartphones, and other devices offering a ccess to digital content and services have 
become ubiquitous, yet there is no indication the existing product liability regime prevents consumers 
from recovering for injury or other harms caused by such products. Although the IIA points to studies 
that have identified where gaps in liability protection for consumers might arise, there is no tangible 
indication that systemic gaps in protection  exist in practice . The IIA also contends the proposed 
changes are needed due to the “specific characteristics” of AI  (“autonomous behavior, continuous 
adaptation, limited predictability and opacity”) (IIA, pp. 2 -3); however, these characteristics are not 
unique to AI-equipped products, the operational characteristics of many traditional products being 
opaque to consumers. Moreover, the identified characteristics of AI products do not apply as a group 
to many AI systems (e.g., many AI systems do not act autonomously). 
 
We urge the Commission, as a first step, to compile real-world examples of harm to consumers caused 
by malfunctioning AI-powered products or services, and  to determine whether the consumers were 
denied recovery  for those harms, before it adopts major changes to the existing product  liability 
regime. Objective data should provide a clear basis to evaluate whether changes are needed, and the 
appropriate scope of those changes. 
 
• Ensure the rules create the right incentives  
 
A successful product liability regime ensures consumers receive compensat ion for injury or harm 
caused by products, in a way that appropria tely balances incentives for producers. The existing EU 
product liability regime works well because it  incentivizes producers to offer safe products  without 
imposing liability for wholly unforeseeable harms , or where the risk, considering other relevant 
factors, is acceptable and consistent with a high level of protection for user safety and health . 
Substantial modification of these long standing, carefully reasoned standards may deter producers 
from bringing innovative new products and services to market.  
 
Changes to the EU’s product liability rules  that expressly target AI-powered products and services  
could create such d isincentives and consequently deter producers from offering new, AI -powered 
products and services that may be safer and more effective than their non-AI counterparts—a result 
that leave s consumers worse off in the long run , and affects the competitiveness  of the EU in AI 
development Although strict liability regimes benefit consumers by making it easier to obtain 
compensation for injuries, they also can make it harder for economic operators to manage or mitigate 
their potential liability. We encourage policymakers to bear in mind these trade-offs that strict liability 
involves and to strive for outcomes that appropriately balance societal costs and benefits.  
 
• Align the liability framework with the regulatory regime  
 
Any change to the E U’s product liability framework should align with  and complement  the EU’s 
broader consumer p rotection and  product safety regime s. For instance, many software -powered 
devices are already regulated under various sectoral rules, such as the Medical Devices Regulation. 
Similarly, the Digital Content Directive already extends EU consumer protection rules to various types 
of software. The Commission’s proposed AI Act, once adopted, will also impose a comprehensive EU-
wide safety regime on AI systems.  
 
The Commission should allow those rules to take effect before deciding whether amendment of the 
EU’s liability regime is also needed. If the Commission does move ahead with changes before then, it 
will be important to ensure  alignment between all these regimes. For instance, any AI system that

II. Responses to IIA’s proposed policy options 
 
• Maintain focus of PLD framework on products  
 
Option 1.a of  the IIA would extend  the PLD’s str ict liability rules to cover “intangible products (e.g. , 
digital content/software)” (IIA, p. 4). This fails to recognize fundamental differences between products 
and services. Whereas ta ngible products typically have a discrete set of uses, a nd therefore a  
foreseeable range of risks, the digital services —standing alone–that are the focus of the  IIA may be 
deployed in an almost unlimited number of scenarios and for a wide range of uses. Ho w services 
function, and the risks they present, will therefore often depend on how the user deploys the service. 
By way of example, it is far easier to foresee the potential uses and risks of a tangible product such as 
hedge clippers than, for instance, a speech recognition system, which can be used in an almost infinite 
range of scenarios.  
 
It is virtually impossible, as a practical matter, for service providers to anticipate every conceivable use 
of the service, or its associated risks. Targeting the PLD’s strict liability regime at software and services 
could deter suppliers from offering them on the EU market, or could lead to increased prices for EU 
consumers. To avoid this outcome, we urge the Commission to maintain the PLD’s current focus on 
tangible products (which is sufficiently flexible so that it may include products that include embedded 
software) and avoid extending the Directive’s strict liability regime to either stand-alone software or 
to services. 
 
• Limit liability to physical / material damage to consumers  
 
Option 1.b of the IIA proposes to “extend the rang e of damages for which compensation can be 
claimed under the [PLD] to non -material damage s (e .g., data loss, privacy infringements or 
environmental damage)” (IIA., p. 4). We urge the Commission not to adopt this proposal. First, existing 
EU legislation alr eady provides redress mechanisms for consumers for many types of non -material 
damages. For example, Article 79(1) of the GDPR gives data subjects the right to an effective judicial 
remedy for any infringement of their rights under the Regulation, and Artic le 82(1) gives  them the 
right to obtain compensation for both material and non -material d amage. Providing a separate, 
potentially overlapping basis for compensation will cause confusion, could lead to forum shopping, 
and could lead to double claims for a single harm. Second, the potential financial liability associated 
with non -material damages is less predictable, and thus more difficult for providers to accurately 
foresee and adequately address, than is the case with material damages.  
 
• Impose liability on the entity in the best position to foresee and mitigate risk  
 
Option 1.c of  the IIA proposes to impose  strict liability on the “provider” of AI -based services, the 
“operators/users” of such services, or both (IIA, p. 4). For the reasons noted above, w e believe that 
imposing any form of  new liability rules , including strict liability,  on products or services  merely 
because they incorporate AI is not needed. 
 
If the Commission nonetheless moves forward on these proposals, we believe these rules will be most 
effective if they impose liability on the entity that is in the best position to foresee and mitigate risk. 
In many cases, this will be the entity that  controls how and whether the fin al product or service  
incorporating the AI features is deployed or otherwise put into service (e.g., a retailer that deploys a

facial recognition system; a bank that uses an AI tool to generate consumer credit ratings). This entity 
can then contractually allocate indemnification for any defects in the system’s components to relevant 
suppliers, through indemnity cla uses and the like. This is a common practice for other co mplex 
products that involve multiple  suppliers and ensures that parties address financial responsibility for 
any harms based on the unique att ributes and char acteristics of the product or service at  issue. 
 
• Reserve strict liability for AI systems presenting high risks of sever harm.  
 
For the reasons set out above, the need to impose  strict liability on  products or  services that 
incorporate AI (Option 1.c), or to alter the burdens of proof in cases involving such products or services 
(Option 2.2) is not evident.  
 
If the Commission nonetheless moves forward, we urge it to proceed incrementally and focus in the 
first instance on AI systems presenting unique particularly severe risks. For instance, the Commission 
might consider limiting strict liability to some specifically defined AI systems within the “high risk” AI 
systems under the proposed AI Act (so long as compl iance with its requirements, as well as other 
recognized defenses to strict liability, such as contributory negligence, product misuse or assumption 
of risk would constitute a defense).  
 
Imposing strict liability on all products and services that incorporate AI —irrespective of whether the 
product or services p resents a unique and severe risk of harm, or whether the AI com ponent is a 
contributing factor to that risk—makes little sense from a policy perspective.

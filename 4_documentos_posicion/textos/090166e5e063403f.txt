EHTEL has interacted with its membership, and with a series of projects in which it and its constituency participates, to offer a set of responses to a selected number of questions posed by the European Commission’s open consultation on the European Health Data Space (EHDS). The specific questions covered by EHTEL are the eight questions: 2, 3, 4, 10, 12, 13, 23, and 26. The association’s position is classified into four parts (Parts 1, 2, 3, and 4), which follow.  Each set of responses contains a background description of EHTEL’s understanding of the situation, supported by reference to articles and White Papers or position papers. The three projects on which EHTEL draws chiefly are InteropEHRate,1 mHealthHub,2 and vCare3.  Most positions expressed relate to concrete and practical organisational and implementation aspects of the EHDS. Part 1 - Access to and exchange of data for healthcare With its open public consultation on the European Health Data Space, the European Commission is asking questions on the aim and objectives of a European framework for the access and exchange of personal health data (Q2), on rights in relation to electronic access and sharing of health data (Q3), and on technical ways to enforce these rights (Q4).   EHTEL’s position in relation to questions 2, 3, and 4 of the consultation  Background4 Access and exchange of personal health data should draw on a citizen-centred approach to health data exchange which would be complementary to the existing healthcare-centred approach developed by the eHealth Digital Service Infrastructure (eHDSI).  Through secure health data sharing tools, citizens would be empowered to access their own health data from mobile devices equipped with smart electronic health records apps. These tools provide privacy and security in health data transactions in an easy-to-use mode. They have the potential to expand the use of related digital health products and services that complement personal health records, by supporting new hybrid models of care that combine face-to-face and virtual care.  Ultimately, such hybrid care can improve all the dimensions of quality of care: access, effectiveness, efficiency, patient experience, safety, and equity. Part 2: Access to and use of personal data for research With its open public consultation on the European Health Data Space, the European Commission is asking questions on the mechanisms, processes/elements, and incentives which would be the most appropriate to facilitate secondary use of health data (Q10, Q12 and Q13).   EHTEL position in relation to Q10, Q12 and Q13 of the consultation  Background  The relevant regulatory agencies in Europe, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the USA, the Federal Drugs Administration (FDA) have already published guidance for the use of real-world health data analytics. The FDA defines real-world evidence (RWE) as: “Healthcare information derived from multiple sources outside of typical clinical research settings, including electronic medical records (EMRs), claims and billing data, product and disease registries, and data gathered by personal devices and health applications.” It acknowledges that such data sets can “effectively complement the knowledge gained from ‘traditional’ clinical trials, whose well-known limitations make it difficult to generalize findings to larger, more inclusive populations of patients, providers, and healthcare delivery systems or settings reflective of actual use in practice.”11 In 2020, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) published draft guidelines on registry-based studies. The guidelines make the case for optimising the use of registry-based studies as a source of real-world evidence.12  Meanwhile, the pharmaceutical industry has made rapid recent progress in the use of real-world evidence.13 Thanks to various partnerships with the pharmaceutical industry, real-world data analytics and evidence is increasingly based on data located in electronic medical records and registries.  Decentralised clinical trials work with real-world health data and tend to be conducted locally in a patient’s community, rather than in a traditional research institution: they are often alternatively called “direct-to-participant” trials or “virtual studies”. 14 They combine a mix of approaches to patient involvement in data gathering. (They may, for example, be based on the collection of real-world data.) Relevant data can be collected direct from patients who are motivated to offer their own data for research purposes, e.g., through the use of hand-held devices that are connected to their electronic medical records. Part 3: Digital health services and products With its open public consultation on the European Health Data Space, the European Commission is asking a question about how to foster the uptake of digital health products and services at national and EU levels (Q23).   EHTEL position in relation to Q23 of the consultation  Background  Digital health solutions are developed everyday worldwide. For public authorities, however, identifying sound solutions adapted to the specific use cases and the context of intervention is not a trivial issue. Efficient, constantly updated and independent evaluation is thus of critical importance.  Many other potential considerations are at stake, and cover regulations and standards especially with regard to the International Organization for Standardization’s ISO 82304-2.19 Several new and upcoming topics are outlined that should be considered, e.g., especially the public publication of reimbursement criteria for digital solutions.   In answer to question 23, EHTEL and its consistency consider that:  - An operational initiative supported by a European network of relevant authorities in the Member States should develop a European framework for assessment of digital health products and the reimbursement of their use in e.g., a hybrid model of care.20 This framework should address the integration of the data produced by these services and products in electronic medical records/electronic health records, by referring to interoperability standards and adequately documented good practices.  - An efficient and meaningful process of evaluation of digital solutions requires intervention on at least two levels:  o Evaluation of generic essential criteria, such as Clinical/Professional Assurance, Data & Privacy and Usability & Accessibility, referring either to the available state-of-the-art or existing mature standards. The experience and know-know (including in the making of and validation of assessment algorithms) – accumulated by the best private and public initiatives currently used and supported by the Member States – should be made available to the widest possible audience through an appropriate public-private partnership.21 This form of evaluation could ultimately be harmonised and automated to a large extent.  o Advanced assessment based around a set of criteria. With the limited interoperability and very different needs in each (Member State or regional) health system, more work needs to be done at Member State level on a range of topics.  Part 4: Artificial intelligence in healthcare With its open public consultation on the European Health Data Space, the European Commission is asking a question about measures in relation to the data sets required for the development and testing of Artificial Intelligence (AI) (Q26).   EHTEL position in relation to Q26 of the consultation  Background  Measures to facilitate the access to GDPR-compliant health data are certainly more than welcome. Yet, they will not entirely resolve the challenge of not having adequate and sufficient data to train algorithms (i.e., in circumstances if or when health data cannot be ‘mapped’ against the precise environment and/or services to be tackled by the services envisioned).  Particularly in the healthcare domain, many data are very context-specific. Having access to meaningful data to train algorithms is therefore not a trivial issue. Many innovative projects do not have access to a sufficient volume of quality data that is specific or pertinent (i.e., that takes into consideration criteria like context, service, gender, or age).  Given the nature of algorithms, one needs to be aware of certain of their limits. (Even for a simple medical recommendation, a proposed AI-based decision will not necessarily always be optimal, since an algorithm will not be in the position to take the same informed risks that a human being would.) This has important consequences for the types of services to be supported and the human-machine interactions needed.   In answer to question 26,  EHTEL and its constituency believe that: - One needs to develop pro-active strategies to the collection of data which could support the initial training of algorithms, bearing in mind the main services to be developed.  - Digital solutions that are totally under patients’ control (e.g., mobile devices) provide an important new opportunity to collect different types of data, and need therefore to be promoted. Recent experience in European projects, such as Captain26 or vCare, demonstrates that citizens and patients are often ready to accept to spend time and energy to provide the data needed to train algorithms (even over a substantial period of time) provided they, as citizens/patients, are correctly informed and supported.  EHTEL and its constituency offer a set of solutions  with regard to directions to be taken, as examples of pro-active strategies (having upcoming European programmes/projects in focus) : - Projects supported by the European Commission should be stimulated to invest sufficient time and resources in collecting the data they need before putting their algorithms to the test.   26 The Captain project approach involved the collection of data for 9 whole months in order to be able to train the algorithms.

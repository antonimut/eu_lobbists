International Diabetes Federation European Region position on the European 
Commission’s proposal for a European Health Data Space 
26 July 2021 
Background 
The European Health Data Space (EHDS) is a  Commission priority that aims to leverage the potential of digital 
health to provide high -quality healthcare and reduce inequalities. It seeks to promote better exchange and 
access to different types of health data (electronic health records, genomics data , data from patient registries 
etc.), to support healthcare delivery (primary use of data) and health research/health policy making (secondary 
use of data). A legislative proposal is expected to be adopted in Q4 2021. 
The Commission proposed that the EHDS be built on three main pillars: 
• A strong system of data governance and rules for data exchange 
• Data quality 
• Strong infrastructure and interoperability 
The EHDS will not be built from scratch. The proposal will be based upon a number of existing frameworks such 
as the Cross-Border Healthcare Directive, GDPR, Data Governance Act and the AI Regulation. 
 
Relevance of the EHDS initiative to the diabetes community 
 
Today, digitalisation is transforming diabetes care, probably more rapidly than any other therapeutic area. It is 
critical to ensure that the ever -increasing quantity of health  data (including data collected though diabetes 
devices, apps, etc.) and the adoption of new digital solutions be leveraged not only to improve health outcomes 
for people living with diabetes  (PwD) but also to inform policy makers  of the actions required to improve 
diabetes management and prevention, to allow for more research and innovation, and ultimately to build more 
resilient healthcare systems. 
 
The COVID -19 pandemic has exposed the fault lines in our current healthcare systems and has had a 
disproportionate effect on people living with pre-existing health conditions. Moreover, not only can diabetes 
lead to severe cases of COVID -19 but there is al so emerging evidence that COVID -19 can trigger new cases of 
diabetes.  
 
In this context, it is crucial to have in place a knowledge ecosystem which allows for: 
 
• better understanding of the interconnections between various health conditions and the impact and 
effectiveness of existing preventative strategies and treatments, resulting in fewer comorbidities and 
addressing the needs of vulnerable population groups; 
• the development of precision, evidence -based, personalised care, resulting in a truly person-centred 
approach and better health outcomes; 
• reduced inefficiencies and healthcare waste (administrative, operational, and clinical) and the delivery 
of more benefits to PwD at a lower cost to society; 
• improved research and innovation, notably through the secondary use of data;  
• data to be translated into actionable action, to inform decision-making and to design policy responses 
both at national and trans-national level; 
• easier and more comprehensive access by PwD to their own data /comparative health data, allowing 
for meaningful engagement with their healthcare team, and more active self-care; and 
• coordinated action and joint responses to future health crisis.

Small data sets and clinical trials have proved insufficient to maintain robust and agile ecosystems that can 
support the resilience of health systems and contribute meaningfully to improved health outcomes for patients. 
People living with diabetes and other conditions will benefit from massive data spaces that are interoperable 
and can be scaled up to  genuinely respond to the unmet needs of patients  and improve understanding of 
conditions.  
 
IDFE position related to the stated objectives/implementation of a EHDS 
 
1. Harmonised, Europe-wide data frameworks have the potential to bring about significant improvements 
in the prevention, management and care of diabetes and other non-communicable diseases as well as a 
reduction in health inequalities. 
 
The COVID-19 pandemic has brought into sharp relief the different levels of digitalisation and preparedness to 
increased digitalisation of healthcare systems across different EU Member States, and the substantial 
inequalities in health outcomes within and between Member States this brings.  
 
It has also highlighted the need for more responsive, advanced and innovative healthcare systems and outlined 
how the lack of standardised data collection and data sharing across Member States hinders the development 
of a collective and coordinated response across the Union in a time of crisis. In non-crisis times, it also represents 
a major barrier to the development of more resilient healt h systems and to the implementation of effective 
policies to tackle non-communicable diseases.  
 
To make sure that the EHDS framework achieves its objectives, data needs to be collected across the Union 
around a commonly -agreed set of indicators, including  patient-reported outcome and experience measures. 
These are essential to support a move towards more innovative, patient-centred, value -based healthcare 
systems, which would allow for improved resource allocation, faster and easier identification of high -value 
interventions and reduced expenditure, and ultimately improved health outcomes and well-being for healthcare 
system users. IDF Europe calls for this to be taken into account in any European Health Data Space framework. 
 
Building a European Diabetes Information System can take place through a stepwise approach, leveraging the 
pre-existing national and regional registers in place across many European countries, for example, in the case of 
diabetes, the European Best Information th rough Regional Outcomes in Diabetes (EUBIROD), a specialised 
network progressively built by a stream of EU-funded projects. Such registers can also further be integrated into 
more general data registers, registers focusing on other conditions etc. 
To do so would require the: 
• Creation of an information gateway that would enable diverse stakeholders e.g., epidemiologists, 
statisticians, clinicians and policy makers to access better data for research and decision -making, in 
strict collaboration with local data custodians. 
• Deployment of a solution that would be owned by a community and does not require a specific central 
organisation to maintain each node. Individual care providers and data custodians would be able to use 
it independently regardless of nationa l and international developments. An open -source license will 
facilitate this process.  
• Set up of EU web portals for large scale quality of care evaluation, while enabling automated data 
capturing of Patient Reported Outcomes Measures (PROMs) for person centred care. The architecture 
would allow standardised data to be shared with any third -party app, thereby supporting 
interoperability and not constraining users to one proprietary application. This would enhance the 
choices for PwD and people living with other conditions and open opportunities for additional data. 
• The sharing of best practices and a progressive expansion of the coverage of individual diseases and 
indicators for all Member States, scaling up the intervention through dissemination and technol ogy 
transfer across Europe. 
• The rollout of a privacy -enhanced real world data infrastructure, through the progressive installation 
of the same statistical software, transmitting finely tuned micro-aggregate data towards the European 
Health Data Space, for further use and repeated aggregation.

2. The EHDS regulation must have a clear governance framework built on transparent foundations that fully 
protect citizens’ data in accordance with GDPR rules  and promotes inclusiveness. Data protection and 
ethical and responsible use of data must be non-negotiable notions.  
 
It is essential that a strong, transparent governance approach is promoted, which includes all relevant 
stakeholders. The initiative’s governance model and its deployment must guarantee that all data will be secure 
and that it will preserve the r ights of the individual, including the right not to share data, without any penalty 
attached. Special attention must be given to ensuring the ethical use of the data as well as the modalities of this 
usage (how, by whom, for what purposes, with what implications), determining its ownership (notably, but not 
exclusively, in the context of user -generated data through C ontinuous Glucose Monitors (CGMs) and other 
monitoring devices) and clearly defining rules concerning the data sharing consent process. This is a pre-requisite 
to building the trust necessary for PwD and other stakeholders to share their data.  Additionally, it is essential 
that the required conditions are met for all citizens to be included in the data gathering and research efforts , 
taking into account potential data gaps and biases, to guarantee that all citizens, irrespective of their ethnicity 
for example, benefit from the research conducted, and ultimately, potentially better treatments/health 
outcomes. 
 
 
3. PwD must be included and treated as equal stakeholders (no tokenism) in the design of the EHDS as they 
are best placed to determine which care and outcomes matter most to them , how they wish to gain 
access to their own and others’ data and how they wish their data to be used.  
 
In-depth and meaningful engagement with PwD must be fully integrated in the design of any future solutions  
and in decisions surrounding data sharing and data accessibility. The creation of digital platforms where PwD 
can access their own data for research and educational purposes or cross reference it with anonymous data of 
other PwD to gather comparable insights, should be implemented as part of the framework.  
 
 
4. Tackling the digital divide and digital literacy needs of PwD, healthcare professionals and regulators is a 
pre-requisite to guaranteeing equal access to health and a reduction in health inequalities within and 
among Member States 
 
PwD can only meaningfully be brought on board and share their data in a secure way if they have adequate 
education and information. Moreover, reducing inequalities can only be achieved if digital health is accessible 
to all. EHDS must work in concertation with other initiatives to ensure that all EU citizens have the means and 
knowledge to avail themselves of the digital health revolution through improved digital literacy and access to 
digital tools. As importantly, this means ensuring that people have a full understanding of what data sharing and 
informed consent mean, for example for participation in research projects. Other than addressing the social 
determinants of health, this also means tackling, often unconscious, biases within healthcare  systems, which 
may lead to some groups of users not being offered the use of some digital tools, and educating healthcare 
professionals in the benefits of such tools and how best to engage with users, and promote uptake. It also means 
ensuring that all relevant stakeholders are involved in the preparation and training of people around the topic 
of data sharing and informed consent, for example in the case of people living with diabetes, national and local 
diabetes associations.

5. The increased use of digital tools and systems as well as artificial intelligence (AI) for the management 
of people living with diabetes and other conditions must not supersede the role played by face -to-face 
interactions with the healthcare team, and/or the need for human oversight to remain pre-eminent. 
 
Healthcare systems across Europe rapidly adapted to the constraints resulting from the pandemic and 
successfully deployed a range of remote interventions in place of the traditional face-to-face consultations. Best 
practices developed during the pandemic m ust be leveraged and further integrated into existing care set -ups 
for more resilient healthcare systems. Nevertheless, in doing so, it is crucial to remember that digitalisation and 
artificial intelligence are merely tools – albeit ones that have the potential to generate cutting-edge advances in 
support of better/faster diagnosis, epidemiological analyses, self-management of one’s condition, evaluation of 
the effectiveness of health interventions, identification of health patterns etc. As such, they should not be used 
to replace traditional interactions, but rather to support and strengthen them  – for example, in the case of 
diabetes in creating the frame for a discussion around data patterns generated through CGMs and potential 
identification of new treat ment solutions/treatment adaptation. Diabetes often imposes a significant 
psychological burden on the person living with it, while its management can also be hugely complex and cover 
not just the condition itself, but an array of complications and co-morbidities. Therefore, regular conversations 
and interactions with a healthcare professional remain essential and must be guaranteed , even in a highly 
digitalised environment.  
 
Beyond the relationship aspect, the use of AI must also be framed in a way which g uarantees a high level of 
human oversight and inclusiveness, with healthcare professionals being firmly acknowledged as the ultimate 
decision-makers (in collaboration with PwD) regarding treatment decisions over any recommendations derived 
from AI systems.

 
 
INTA  
 
Comments on a possible EU system for non-agricultural geographical indications 
 
June 2021 
 
The International Trademark Association ( INTA) through its Geographical Indications (GIs) 
Committee has been closely monitoring the initiatives and debates at the European Union 
level about creating a system of protection for GIs for non-agricultural products to supplement 
the existing protection systems for geographical indications for agricultural prod ucts and 
foodstuffs (Regulation 1151/2012), wines (Regulation 1308/2013), aromatized wines 
(Regulation 251/2014) and spirits (Regulation 110/2008). INTA has contributed to this debate 
by providing, in October 2014, comments on the European Commission's public consultation 
on the Green Paper on geographical indications for non -agricultural products (Making the 
most out of Europe's traditional know -how: a possible extension of geographical indication 
protection of the European Union to non-agricultural products (COM (2014) 469 final)). 
 
INTA is committed to continuing to provide its insights as an organization that brings together 
the knowledge and expertise of brand  owners and professionals,  from more than 187 
countries of the world, including 1351 in the European Union and the UK, dedicated to 
supporting trademarks and related intellectual property (IP)  rights, including GIs, to foster 
consumer trust, economic growth, and innovation.  INTA’s membership includes several GI 
associations.  
 
I. General Comments 
 
INTA supports the protection of GIs  in accordance with its Board Resolution on ‘Protection of 
Geographical Indications’. INTA’s position is grounded in a desire for good govern ance and 
regulation, recognizing the principle of “first in time, first in right,” which in turn is based on the 
well-established principles of territoriality, exclusivity, priority, and good faith when resolving 
conflicts between geographical indications and trademarks, including collective and certification 
marks. INTA has responded where it can to the questions in the consultation document but wished 
to add this broader statement that we hope is useful to the EU in their deliberations. 
 
We wish to stress , as a preliminary comment, that INTA does not advocate for expansion or 
contraction of GIs. We wish to focus on the quality of the systems that register them, so that they 
provide a clear and effective governance. INTA considers that the harmonization of protection for 
all GIs would be positive insofar as it would serve to increase legal certainty, and provided it does 
not involve disruption of existing legal rights, including trademarks.  
 
In this context, INTA recommends that any regulation of geographic al indications to have  
effective and transparent mechanisms for addressing:  
 
(i) applications for geographical indications, applications for amendments, and applications for 
cancellation; and

(ii) opposition, cancellation, and notification procedures that recognize pre-existing trademark 
rights with which geographical indications might conflict or cause confusion. 
 
INTA note s that an additional  EU protection regime for non -agricultural GIs has the 
potential to create  many conflicts between newly created non-agricultural GIs  and the 
numerous pre-existing trademark rights protected in the Single Market  and pre-existing 
generic terms unless careful safeguards are introduced. Such safeguards should aim to 
ensure a fair and transparent system, with maximum legal certainty on the scope of 
protection of the newly created Gis for a minimum disruption of pre-existing rights.  
 
An excessive scope of protection could unduly prejudice the existing rights of trademark holders, 
and limit the freedom to use generic terms. The GI protection regime in the European Union needs 
to be fair and effectvie. 
 
If the EU recogn izes a wider range of GIs then  INTA recommends careful consideration of 
how existing rights can  coexist with new rights in balance with other intellectual property 
rights, including trademarks, on the basis of well -established intellectual property principles 
of priority, exclusivity, good faith and territoriality and based on the principle of ‘first in time, 
first in right’ that govern the relationship between IP rights, and further noting GI rights should 
not be granted for generic terms. 
 
II. Specific comments 
 
To ensure a balanced system for the harmonized EU GI protection for non -agricultural 
products, there should be clearly defined criteria for protection, transparent procedures, 
adequate boundaries between geographical indications and trademarks, to balance p ublic 
and private interests, and due respect for prior rights. Accordingly, any new system should 
reflect the following: 
 
• Avoid negative effects: The harmonized EU protection system for non-agricultural GIs 
should be aimed at avoiding negative effects on the internal market, including in its 
interaction with IP rights granted through the trademark system. 
 
• Scope of Protection: 
  
o The scope of protection for geographical indications should be tailored to prevent a 
misleading use but look to avoid any increase in potential conflicts. Further, the scope 
of protection for GIs in the EU should also be tailored to prevent the impairment of 
the right to use generic terms. 
 
o In designing the scope of protection, it is important to bear in mind that an excessive 
scope of protection, e.g., going beyond consumer deception and including unclear 
and/or not clearly defined concepts, could unduly prejudice existing rights of 
trademark holders, create obstacles for traders in the development and innovation of 
new brands and limit the freedom to use generic terms. On the other hand, greater 
clarity on the terms of protection would increase legal certainty and predictability. Any 
new system should avoid triggering misconceptions about protection to individual 
components or derivatives of geographical indications.  
 
3 
 
• Registration: The registration should require a formal procedure that ensures the 
fulfilment of the requirements for protection and transparency and provides clarity about 
the scope of protection of the terms to be protected and allows interested parties to voice 
their concerns in the context of the procedure.  
 
• Examination:  
 
o Examination should ensure compliance with the requirement of the link between the 
product and the location; with product specifications, and require evidence relating to 
the link to the territory. Particular care should be applied for non -geographical terms 
to verify that the term at issue has acquired a geographic association.  
 
o Examination should include a review t o ensure that protection of the GI would not 
result in a conflict with any prior trademarks and that protection of the GI would not 
impair the free use of any generic terms. 
 
o The compliance with the protection requirements should not only be a matter for 
examination in the country of origin but also subject to full scrutiny by the competent 
EU agency or body, and the facts be made accessible to the public. The examination 
should take into consideration market realities, such as indicated geographical area, 
including the production of a certain good in third countries  and how that product is 
generally identified , as well as quality and reputation. This should be open to 
challenges by any interested party. 
 
• Non-EU GIs: Protection should be open to non-agricultural GIs of third countries without 
discrimination, and with direct access to the registration system by interested private 
parties.  
 
• Safeguards & Resolution of conflicts:  
 
o Appropriate safeguards should be included to avoid likelihood of confusion  with 
existing trademarks, to ensure that conflicts between trademarks and non-agricultural 
GIs will be resolved in line with the principles of priority, exclusivity, good faith and 
territoriality, and that GIs should not be registered for generic terms in the European 
Union. 
 
o Such safeguards should also include issues pertaining to the suitability of the non-
agricultural GI. 
 
• Priority: 
  
o Existing trademark rights should be duly respected and serve as grounds for an 
opposition against the registration of a later non-agricultural GI irrespective of whether 
the prior trademark rights exist in the European Union as a whole or only in an 
individual EU Member State , and without a requirement for reputation, renown, or 
length of use of the trademark to serve as a ground for an opposition.   
 
 
4 
o On the other hand, the registration of a non-agricultural GI should prevent registration 
of a trademark which was applied for subsequently, if this would conflict with the 
protection granted to the non-agricultural GI. 
 
o Existing generic terms should be duly respected and serve as grounds for an 
opposition against the registration of a non-agricultural GI irrespective of whether the 
generic term is in use in the European Union as a whole or only in an individual E U 
Member State. As a matter of example, while “Bohemian Crystal” could be registered, 
“Crystal” on its own could not. We are not saying that there is a ban on the use of 
“Crystal” per se. 
 
o The protection under any new system should not prejudice prior rights with an earlier 
priority, and in particular, the right to maintain the registration, use and renew a prior 
trademark should be guaranteed even in case that the non-agricultural GI  is 
registered despite an opposition (including the possibility to file new trademark 
applications including the previously protected mark in order to allow for 
modernizations and updates of existing marks). 
 
o All interested parties should be able to voice their concerns and obtain protection for 
their prior rights, preferably through objections filed directly with the European 
Commission and not through the authorities of EU Member States. 
 
• Cancellation & Amendment : Appropriate cancellation and amendment procedures, 
open to any party with a legitimate interest, including the group or entity on behalf of 
which the non-agricultural GI has been registered, should be in place to enable the 
amendment or cancellation of terms that were registered in spite of not fulfilling the 
protection requirements or which are infringing prior rights, impair the use of generic 
terms, as well as those terms for which protection is no longer justified due to change d 
factual circumstances (e.g. absence of presence on the market over a prolonged period 
of time, economic need, legal justification).

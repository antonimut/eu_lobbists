VÖWG - Feedback on the public consultation on UWWTD 
Vienna 07.07.2021  
EU transparency register number: 16441688074-96 
With the Green Deal, the European Commission has for the first time adopted a holistic approach to 
tackling climate and environmental challenges. The water sector plays a vital role in ensuring and 
improving the quality of European waters and public health, environmental safety and other services 
of general interest. The revision of the EU Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive (UWWTD) will have 
to address future challenges such as climate change, wastewater reduction, micropollutants, 
digitalization, fosteri ng nature -based solutions as well as encouraging circularity of resources. 
Therefore, it is highly anticipated, also with regard to closely linking the revision of the UWWTD with 
the Green Deal and the "zero pollutant target" it provides for. Coherence with the EU Water 
Framework Directive and its daughter directives is required.  
While expected measures and newly developed treatment methods might have a positive impact on 
water conservation, energy efficiency can be compromised as a result. Consideration o f new 
parameters and thresholds to be met as part of the UWWTD review, particularly with respect to trace 
elements (micropollutants) and nutrients, must take this relationship into account to promote, rather 
than hinder, funding for the transition to achieving these environmental goals. In the UWWTD-Fitness 
Check, the Commission recognized the interlink between climate change and environmental 
protection goals and indicated that the primary goal of wastewater treatment remains environmental 
protection. It must adhere to this principle in the revision as well. 
The Austrian public enterprises, and particularly the water sector, are fully aware of their 
responsibility, not only towards health and sustainability aspects, but also towards economic efficiency 
towards the fee -paying citizens. In wastewater treatment, the primary goal must remain to reduce 
water pollution from wastewater discharges as much as possible. Municipal companies are already 
testing and using new technologies to save or even generate energy.  One example is heat recovery 
from wastewater. However, additional legal requirements for wastewater treatment, such as the 
introduction of more advanced treatment stages and stricter parameters, are challenging and cost 
intensive. Nevertheless, these efforts are part of the transition to achieving environmental goals and 
must therefore be based on a reliable financing framework. Therefore, thresholds linked to the energy 
efficiency of plants must not limit the financing options for achieving environmental goals. 
Preventing pollution at source 
We strongly agree with the principles enshrined in the zero-pollution action plan: 
• the precautionary principle, 
• the principle that preventive action must be taken 
• the principal that environmental damage must be rectified at source 
• the polluter pays principle 
• the extended producer responsibility scheme (EPR) 
From a municipal wastewater industries perspective, the introduction of an EPR scheme must form a 
central component of the revised directive. At the same time, the life cycle concept should not be lost 
sight of: a holistic producer responsibility goes far beyond the financing of "end of pipe" measures. 
Pollution prevention at source must take precedence over other  reduction measures and their 
financing. In case of imported goods, a scheme must be implemented to ensure the internalization of

the external costs. By applying the EPR for imported goods it could be ensured that manufacturing 
countries follow European environmental requirements. In addition, a ban of all non-essential uses of 
substances of emerging concern (e.g. PFAS) should be considered to limit the environmental impact of 
industry and agriculture. 
Furthermore, a clear regulation must be included in the  directive to prohibit the discharge of 
substances via indirect discharges into the sewer system and thus into wastewater treatment plants if 
these substances have an inhibitory effect on biological purification processes or even lead to process 
disruption. In case of violation, the polluter would have to be sanctioned, which goes beyond a mere 
"damage control in the wastewater treatment plant" and considers the major consequential damages 
in the water body that occur afterwards. 
In short: A coherent regulatory framework with clear instruments, covering all persistent, mobile, toxic 
(PMT) and very persistent, very mobile ( vPvM) substances needs to be in place to prevent and limit 
the emission of these substances to the water cycle to protect the water resour ces. Prerequisite for 
an efficient and effective solution is coherence with other sectoral EU legislation (WFD, EQS, DWD, 
REACH etc.). 
Additional treatment steps for micropollutants 
With new, upcoming substances of emerging concern, wastewater treatment plants will likely have to 
face new challenges. The decision of stepping up wastewater treatment efforts with enhanced 
treatment technologies shall be based on case-by-case substance risk assessment and environmental 
impact assessment. Furthermore, a definition of new "sensitive areas" should be chosen to make the 
high financial resources involved as effective as possible. 
Selected WWTPs in Austria, Switzerland and Germany have started to work with additional treatments 
with the purpose of addressing only one  specific category of micropollutants: pharmaceuticals for 
human use. Even with state-of-the-art treatment technologies, WWTPs are not capable of removing 
all unwanted substances due to intrinsic limitations of treatment technologies. There can never be a 
single, off -the-shelf-solution for removing all micropollutants. If an additional treatment is 
implemented, the costs for these additional measures could, for example, be covered by solidarity -
based financing based on the Swiss model. 
The sustainable way f orward is a risk -based approach with a full environmental impact assessment 
with a risk assessment for present micropollutants, considering specific parameters  (number of 
connections, quality of receiving waters, etc.), to develop advanced treatment method s as a 
complementary tool to control at source measures. 
Resource protection 
To ensure the supply of high -quality drinking water to citizens, the protection of resources from 
pollution is paramount, as recognized in the WFD, whose objectives should be mainstreamed in 
UWWTD. The upcoming revision of UWWTD should also be coherent with other sectoral policies and 
legislation (WFD, DWD, GWD, EQSD, soil strategy) as healthy soils are not only essential to meet 
climate and biodiversity goals under the Europea n Green Deal, but also to protect groundwater 
(drinking water resources) from any form of pollution through its filter function.

Circular Economy 
The Austrian public enterprises support the EU circular economy ambitions and EU Green Deal 
strategies, but under no circumstances can they be allowed to contaminate drinking water resources 
(surface and groundwater). That said, if there are new regulations regarding phosphorus recovery for 
treatment plants > 20.000 PE through thermal treatment, there must be an opportunity for agricultural 
application of sewage sludge for smaller communities. In addition, considering   that next to valuable 
nutrients (phosphorous and nitrogen), sewage sludge can contain micropollutants, such as 
pharmaceuticals, PFAS, microplastic , pathogens etc. – which might end up in the environment if 
sewage sludge is used as fertilizer in agriculture – a regulatory framework is needed (similar to the 
recent EU Water Reuse Directive) to support sustainable and resilient sludge management, 
incorporating a broader scope for risk assessment of potential impacts on water resources and human 
health and strict sludge quality control. A general prohibition of agricultural use of sewage sludge is 
hindering in achieving true circular economy. 
Combined sewer overflows and surface runoff from settlement areas 
Austria has a high degree of connection to the public sewer system. The ÖWAV rule sheets 19 and 35, 
which are applied nationally, have created a state of the art accepted by experts. The implementation 
of such rules should primarily be based on a secure data basis for combined sewer overflows and water 
quality. Preference should always be given to the use of existing infrastructure over the construction 
of new facilities. 
A perpetuation of climate extremes such as heat, drought and heavy rain will have a lasting impact on 
all areas of action in municipal water management, including drainage. The infrastructure of 
wastewater disposal must therefore be designed to be future -proof and still be kept affordab le. The 
climate robustness of wastewater disposal must be examined, and adaptation measures must be 
supported with subsidies (possibly comparable to the swiss model). 
One-sided measures that only involve the wastewater disposal company do not go far enough. Green 
and blue measures must take a central position in line with the new EU strategy for adaptation to 
climate change. Especially in urban settlement areas, integrated "rainwater management plans" 
should be developed jointly by all parties involved (spatial planning, zoning, green space, traffic areas, 
sewage system, etc.), establishing a holistic, water -sensitive way of thinking and approaching urban 
development: water areas, greening on roofs and facades, and multifunctional land use along the lines 
of a "sponge city" (e.g. the climate concept for the City of Vienna) are an essential component of 
climate adaptation. 
Energy consumption, renewable energy production and greenhouse gas emissions 
Many Austrian wastewater treatment plants already operate in a very energy -efficient manner. For 
this reason, new absolute energy reduction targets should be rejected. Instead, activities and 
corresponding incentives should be set via energy audits, also within the framework of benchmarking. 
To securely record greenhouse gas emissions, a reliable data basis must first be established in order 
that the effectiveness of contingent measures can be assessed at all.  Regarding the "Fit for 55" 
package, coherence between new requirements during the revision of the Energy Efficiency Directive 
(EED) and considerations in the context of the revision of the UWWTD is particularly important. The 
EU Commission's proposal for the revision of the EED of 14 July 2021 foresees that in future the average 
annual energy consumption will determine whether a company is obliged to carry out an energy 
management system or energy audit, and no longer whether a company is classified as a no n-SME 
according to EU Recommendation 2003/361/EC. In the revision of the EED, it will have to be examined

Monitoring and reporting 
Compared to other European countries, Austrian wastewater treatment plant operators are alr eady 
subject to very comprehensive monitoring and a correspondingly extensive reporting system. Stricter 
measures across the board on the issues of nutrient-sensitive areas, nutrient removal, small settlement 
areas, and small sewage treatment plants are no t effective. More far -reaching measures should be 
introduced if there is a proven need. 
Systematic monitoring of SARS-CoV-2 in wastewater 
The Austrian wastewater industry is open to the monitoring of (new) pathogens in wastewater by 
health authorities, but  clearly rejects the EU Commission's proposal to introduce a Europe -wide 
obligation for wastewater monitoring for SARS -CoV-2 and its variants in the EU Urban Wastewater 
Treatment Directive. The investigation is a task within the framework of infection prot ection and 
pandemic control. Any regulations would therefore have to be discussed elsewhere. In any case, these 
measures will have to be financed by the relevant authorities responsible for health & disease control 
and must therefore be clearly regulated.

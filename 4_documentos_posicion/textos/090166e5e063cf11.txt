 
 
Xylem Position on the public consultation on the revision of the Urban Waste 
Water Treatment Directive 
 
Xylem welcomes Commission’s effort to revise the Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive (UWWTD). 
Access to clean water and safe sanitation is fundamental to building a more sustainable world. As a leading 
technology and solutions provider for the wastewater sector  dedicated to protecting, optimizing and 
managing water, sustainability is at the core of who we are and what we do. A revised Directive could drive 
more sustainable decision-making by WWTP operators, thereby improving environmental performance and 
building resilience to emerging challenges. 
Xylem’s key recommendation is that the revised Directive should include policy measures to 
modernise Europe’s wastewater infrastructure  and to achieve greenhouse gas emissions 
reductions and energy efficiency gains, while simultaneously ensuring  adequate protection of the 
environment and human health . We would like to stress that this can be achieved by integrating 
digital solutions (software & digital technologies) into WWTP operations without replacing existing 
hardware and equipment, thereby reducing the need for costly infrastructure investments. 
On this basis, Xylem would like to make the following recommendations for the UWWTD revision: 
1. Capitalise on energy efficiency potential and reduce greenhouse gas emissions using 
digital tools 
Xylem welcomes the Commission’s recognition of the energy efficiency potential of Europe’s 
wastewater sector. With a view to achieve the ambitious target to reach climate neutrality by 2050, it is 
important t o capitalise on this potent ial and to move  to an energy  neutral wastewater sector .  The 
Commission should introduce adequate energy audit and transparency requirements as well as energy 
use reduction targets  - ensuring consistency with the EU’s broader energy efficiency objectives, 
including load-based parameters of energy consumption. Addressing water leakages is an important 
element concerning energy efficiency, as leakage requires energy input for additional pumping to keep 
the flow performance of the system.  
In wastewater treatment, Xylem would like to underline that existing digital solutions applied to this and 
other processes can achieve significant energy savings with limited investment needs  (no civil 
engineering or hardware costs) within a short period of time . As an example, a WWTP in Cuxhaven, 
Germany, with 400,000 residents achieved a 26% reduction in aeration energy usage, corresponding 
to 1.1. million kWh annual or 485 metric tons of CO2-equivalent, just by using digital solutions.1 Across 
Europe, the implementation of similar real-time decision support solutions to wastewater treatment can 
further reduce energy use and GHG emission by 0.3 million metric tons CO2-equivalent.  
Beyond energy savings and energy efficiency, requirements for the reduction of NOx emissions from 
anaerobic digestion would contribute significantly to climate change mitigation.  
To enable future energy neutrality, it is also necessary to exploit energy embedded in water and 
wastewater flows, e.g. through reuse of the energy of the organic matter, capturing potential energy of

2. EU-harmonised measures regarding stormwater overflows & urban run-off  
Given the increased pressures on wastewater infrastructure from increasing urban development and 
intensity of rainfall events, a n EU-wide harmonised approach to stormwater overflows wou ld bring 
significant environmental benefits. This should include requirements for separation and balancing of 
flows in new or upgraded infrastructure, as well as mandatory assessment of stormwater overflows at 
national level. Based on these assessments, Me mber States should be required to set mitigation and 
management plans for stormwater overflows, including frequency targets and coliform limits, and 
promoting digital management solutions that can significantly reduce overflow events and limit the 
investment into new civil infrastructure. 
Conventional approaches to address Combined Sewage Overflows (CSOs) through construction of 
new civil infrastructure are very capital intensive , as illustrated by the  Thames Tideway Tunnel in 
London ( £3.8bn)3.  However, modern digital solutions can be used as alternative and significantly 
reduce the capital requirements for CSO elimination.   
As an example, the WWTP in South Bend, Indiana, US reduced CSO events by more than 70 percent 
within a short time period by implementing digital management solutions. 4  This resulted in a drop of 
over 50% in pathogen E. coli concentration in to the Saint Joseph River  and allowed capital works 
savings of approximately €420 million.  
Recommendations: 
• Set EU-harmonised measures regarding stormwater overflows & urban run -off in a 
revised UWWTD, including: 
o Requirements for elimination of overflows by balancing of flows in modernized 
wastewater infrastructure

3. Contaminants of Emerging Concern should incrementally be included in the scope  
In view of  the EU ’s Zero Pollution ambition, Contaminants of Emerging Concern (CECs) , including 
pharmaceuticals, endocrine disruptors and microplastics, should be included within the scope of the 
Directive to ensure adequate treatment before wastewater is discharged into the environment. In 
addition to protecting the environment, expansion  of treatment requirements for CECs could support 
the increased reuse of sewage sludge. At a minimum, CECs should be included in treatment 
requirements for large agglomerations, where there is a risk that they are present in certain 
concentrations.  
It is important to note that the perceived trade -off between enhanced water quality/contamination 
removal on the one hand and energy efficiency and GHG reductions on the other, can be overcome by 
employing modern digital solutions - with limited investment needs.  
An example of cost -effective management of additional CEC treatment in  wastewater can be seen in 
Switzerland, where some  50% of effluent will be treated with 80% removal of micropollutants 5. By 
combining additional revenues from tariffs with subsidies from the national budget, charges are capped 
at €8 per year/inhabitant to cover the total cost of €1 bn.6  
Recommendations:  
• Incrementally include treatment requirements for Contaminants of Emerging Concern 
within the scope of a revised UWWTD, at a minimum for large agglomerations. 
4. Incentives and requirements for resource recovery & reuse  
The revised Directive should ensure a closer alignment between EU water legislation and the EU’s 
Circular Economy objectives through the promotion of resource recovery, and non-potable and potable 
water reuse from wastewater. To achieve this, the Commission should construct an enabling framework 
which provides certainty of the quality of recovered materials, and consider further harmonised 
standards for  recovery and reuse of raw materials from sludge. Recovery and reuse could also be 
further stimulated through the introduction of conditionality clauses for EU funds for wastewater 
treatment projects. 
Recommendations:  
• Harmonise standards for recovery and reuse of raw materials from sludge

5. Small agglomerations and the right to Access to Sanitation 
The new Article 13 on access  to water in the recently agreed recast of the Drinking Water Directive 
constitutes an important step towards fulfilling the objective of the Right2Water initiative and UN 
Sustainable Development Goal 6 on access to clean water and sanitation. Xylem calls on the 
Commission to mirror this provision in a revised UWWTD, ensuring access to sanitation for all in 
Europe.  
The Commission could consider EU -wide objectives for connection rates. Where centralised 
wastewater management is not feasible, Member States c ould be required to prepare decentralised 
wastewater management strategies to mitigate environmental impacts. Meanwhile, the Commission 
should ensure adequate standards at agglomerations of all sizes, by encompassing small 
agglomerations in the scope of the revised UWWTD.   
Recommendations:  
• Explicitly enshrine the right to sanitation in EU law 
• Introduce an EU-wide connection rate target 
• Require decentralised wastewater management plans where applicable  
• Include small agglomerations in the scope of the revised Directive 
6. Monitoring obligations and surveillance of pathogens in wastewater 
In light of the Commission’s intention to explore additional monitoring obligations and modernisation of 
reporting requirements, Xylem would like to highlight the importance of supporting the uptake of 
innovative monitoring technologies including IoT solutions for alarm management and real -time 
decision support systems, which is not currently encouraged by the current Directive 7. Revising the 
monitoring methodology through digitization could encourage data access, traceability and compliance 
to Utilities. Extending monitoring obligations to effluent and environmental waters could also contribute 
to pinpointing sources of pollution. Digital monitoring and surveillance tools would also enable Member 
States to improve their resilience to future pandemics throu gh early detection of pathogens in 
wastewater. To this end, the Commission should consider the option of introducing a requirement for 
national plans for using wastewater surveillance as a pandemic control measures, as well as the 
formulation of a watchlist of critical pathogens for large agglomerations. 
Recommendations:  
• Revise the monitoring methodology in light of the opportunities brought by digital 
solutions 
• Extend monitoring obligations to effluent and environmental waters 
• Formulate a watchlist of critical pathogens for large agglomerations, and facilitate 
national pathogen monitoring plans

  EVALUATION OF THE URBAN WASTE WATER TREATMENT DIRECTIVE Kemira Comment Paper  Kemira was pleased to see that the European Commission has recognized the same achievements and shortcomings as Kemira in the Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive’s evaluation. Kemira agrees that the directive has been successful in improving the condition of EU water bodies. However, full implementation of the directive is needed in every member state. Technological, cost-effective solutions exist to reach much more ambitious targets for water treatment. Kemira strongly believes that the benefits of further EU level action to protect European water bodies would outweigh the costs and also serve in meeting the EU’s climate and circular economy goals, Thus, the Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive should be opened for a review without further delay.  Kemira’s observations on the evaluation report:   1. Full implementation 2. Emission limit values 3. Digitalization 4. Emerging pollutants, such as pharmaceuticals and microplastics 5. Remaining loads of pollution: Storm-water overflows 6. Efficient and circular water treatment  1. Full implementation • Implementation of the UWWTD varies substantially across Europe. In some countries water treatment is much more ambitious and efficient than the directive requires, while in others there are significant implementation gaps. • In Kemira’s view, the implementation gaps are in many cases caused by inefficient treatment methods rather than limited treatment capacity. By combining chemical and biological treatment in an optimal way, many wastewater treatment plants would be able to treat more water more efficiently without the need for major capital investment.  2. Emission limit values • Urban waste water remains one of the main point sources of phosphorus emissions. The limit values of different pollutants in the current directive are not strict enough, as the EEA report on the state of European waters illustrates.  • Emission limit values BOD, COD, TSS and especially phosphorus in water discharges should be tightened.

• The current limit of 2 mg of phosphorus (P) per liter of water (population centers of 10,000–100,000 people) and 1 mg of phosphorus per liter of water (population centers over 100,000 people) should at minimum be halved. • For example, nearly all wastewater treatment plants in Sweden and Finland achieve a maximum level of 0.5 mg P/l.  3. Digitalization  • The UWWTD evaluation report notes that advances in monitoring methods will allow more efficient and accurate monitoring of both existing and emerging pollutants.  • Kemira agrees with the Commission that automated monitoring can improve the efficiency and accuracy of water treatment and limit the opportunities for occasional and deliberate non-compliance. Thus, not only provisions on the frequency of sampling at waste water treatment plants should be reviewed but also the way of reporting compliance performance, which will further support the enforcement of existing regulation. • This improved visibility over key process parameters can be achieved with existing technology and without substantially increasing the cost of treatment.  • Digitalization also enables further analysis of treatment processes, optimization of chemical dosing, and predictive process operation, which leads to higher overall treatment efficiency within the boundaries of existing infrastructure.  4. Emerging pollutants, such as pharmaceuticals and microplastics • The scientific community, policy makers and general public see the growing evidence of contaminants of emerging concern, including pharmaceuticals and microplastics, in water bodies as an increasingly important issue. Additional treatment requirements targeting such pollutants should be included in the directive, as the current directive does not cover them directly. i. Pharmaceuticals in wastewaters can not be managed solely by source-control, since almost every human being can be an emitter. Waste water treatment plants, as the end-of-pipe measure, need to upgrade their technology to protect the quality of surface and ground waters. ii. Microplastics can, to a large extent, be removed by using similar technologies as when removing other particles.  • Switzerland has a functioning regulatory and finance model for tackling micropollutants: 75% of the investment in upgraded treatment technology is paid from the national budget, and the municipalities fund a portion of this (9 CHP/person/year). Municipalities with already upgraded WWTPs are exempted. The rest of the investment and operation costs are covered directly by the municipalities. • An EU-wide, unified target substance list (such as e.g. the Swiss-list) would foster new technology innovation and improve micropollutants removal even further.  5. Remaining loads of pollution: Storm-water overflows    • The directive is one of the main factors to ensuring that the EU’s bathing water sites are safe. However, storm-water overflows are referred to only in a footnote.  • As the Commission evaluation report states, combined sewage overflows and urban runoff are sizable sources of remaining pollution loads.

• Climate change is increasing the scale and frequency of extreme weather conditions and flooding caused by storms. Subsequently, the treatment of storm-water overflows is becoming increasingly important.  • Pollution from storm-water overflows must be considered in the revision of the directive, including requirements for limiting the overflows and safe disinfection of the discharges.  • Cost-effective solutions for safe disinfection of overflows exist, and they should be made mandatory at EU level.   6. Efficient and circular water treatment  • Waste water treatment requires energy (0,8% of all energy consumed in Europe). However, the UWWTD contains only limited provisions on reuse and recovery options for waste water, and these have never been strictly enforced at the EU-level.  • Chemical treatment can save up to 50% of energy compared to solely biological treatment. As an added benefit, more biogas can be produced during the chemical treatment process. • Recasts of the waste water and sludge regulations offer the opportunity to convert today’s mainly linear waste treatment into a much more circular model, with the recovery of nutrients, energy and water. To accelerate this process, the EU should propose clear guidance and set harmonised standards for waste water and sludge reuse.  • The EU is highly dependent on phosphorus imports from third countries. At the same time, phosphorus removal from waste water is not nearly as efficient as it could be. Further incentives for using recovered and recycled phosphorus should be put in place.

 
 
 
          Position Paper 
 
 
 
The discharge of treated and untreated urban waste water  is one of the pressures in water bodies that 
are used as resources for drinking water production. Many pollutants that can be found in urban waste 
water effluent are related to our daily lives, such as nutrients , pathogenic microorganisms, detergents, 
endocrine disruptors and pharmaceutical residues. Households are a major source of these substances. 
In addition to this, also industry is discharging its fluid waste streams in urban sewer systems. 
Unfortunately, w aste water treatment plants do certainly not remove all of these pollutants . The 
treatment efficiency depends on the intrinsic properties of the pollutant and the installed treatment.  
 
Waterbodies are therefore influenced by discharges from waste water treatment plants. Wastewater 
percentages in the larger European rivers vary but may well be in the range of 5 to 60 percent. Many 
Dutch drinking water operators are depending on surface water from rivers as source for their drinking 
water production. This is why the revision of the Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive (UWWTD) is 
also relevant for the protection of drinking water  resources. The revision provides the legislative 
framework and incentives to improve the licensing of drinking water relevant pollutants in sewer systems 
and their treatment at waste water treatment plants , and so to further reduce undesirable impact on 
drinking water resources.  
 
The European Commission is now preparing a legislative revision proposal of the UWWTD. Given the 
relevance of this Directive, Vewin (the association of Dutch drinking water suppliers ), expresses in this 
document its opinion on the way forward to protect the sources of our drinking water.  
 
Improvement suggestions for the revision of the UWWTD with relevance to drinking water: 
 
Sensitive zones  
Improve the connection between the UWWTD and the WFD . Stricter requirements are needed for 
drinking water protection zones / receiving waters that act as drinking water resources. Consider drinking 
water-catchment areas as “vulnerable area” and include an assessment at the intake for drinking water 
(river water or riverbank filtrate), when assessing industrial emissions  or emissions from waste water 
treatment plants.  
► Stricter requirements are needed drinking water resources 
 
Pollutants of emerging concerns 
Aim for a targeted approach of contaminants of emerging concern (CEC’s) for vulnerable areas like  
drinking water supply zones, in combination with thresholds for relevant proxy substances to accelerate 
treatment. Obligation to upgrade relevant (selected) treatment plants to deal with CEC’s, especially 
those that discharge into vulnerable areas (drinking water supply zones). This concerns also for 
substances where households are a major source of these substances. 
► Improve regulation and treatment plants to deal with CEC’s 
 
Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)  
Introduction of EPR could contribute to the polluter pays principle. In addition, a  better track and trace 
system for polluting substances is required. It is necessary to know how these substances are processed 
and eventually end up in the environment (sewer systems) and drinking water resources. Product 
Passports could be a beneficial tool for this. 
► Implement Extended Producer Responsibility for polluting substances (at least SVHC)  
 
Industrial discharges (IED) 
Strict licensing should apply to both indirect discharges into wastewater systems (UWWTD) and direct 
discharges to  open water (IED).  Chemical substances cause increasingly risks for drinking water 
resources. Therefore the new UWWTD should also contribute to reduce the release of chemical 
substance in the environment. Moreover, s tricter permitting procedures (REACH) and research on 
Persistent Mobile and Toxic (PMT) substances and substances of Very High Concern (SVHC) are key.

The SVHC label in the regional/national licensing process should be used to minimize the emission of 
these substances as much as possible with the most stringent emission thresholds and meet acceptable 
ecological and human quality standards. For the sub -category substances PFAS, a ban should apply 
on the use all PFAS and on the discharge of PFAS into sewer systems.  
► Restrict emissions of Industrial Waste Waters for PMT / SVHC substances  
► A complete ban on the use and discharge of PFAS is essential  
 
 
Monitoring 
The U WWTD m onitoring requirements should certainly be expanded and optimised, with particular 
reference to Persistent Mobile and Toxic (PMT) substances and SVHC not remov able in the treatment 
process impacting drinking water treatment.  Better coordination of the UWWTD monitoring parameters 
with the WFD and EQS and the drinking water directive is essential including consideration of the watch 
lists. Monitoring must be continually adjusted to new findings and changes in water quality.   
► Improve monitoring for PMT / SVHC substances and relevant WFD parameters 
 
Reporting 
Discharges from waste water treatment plants larger than 10.000 pe should also be registered in the 
European Pollutant Release and Transfer Register (E -PRTR) for industrial discharges.  Preferred 
reporting frequency would be once a year.  
► Improve reporting of WWTP’s  
 
Information to the public 
Increased transparency would provide a better insight and understanding of the location, amount and 
quality of WWTP  discharges, especially in relation to the WFD and article 9 of the new DWD (EU) 
2020/2184: Risk assessment and risk management of the catchment areas for abstraction points of 
water intended for human consumption drinking water resources. 
► Increase transparency on location, amount and quality of WWTP discharges  
 
Innovation / Adaptation to technological progress & BREFs 
We encourage the growing legislative ambition of  to foster  continuous improvement in treatment 
technology. We believe that the best way to boost inno vation is through distinct quality requirements, 
making sure that the goals formulated are compatible with ecology and drinking water requirements. 
The instrument of Best Available Techniques Reference Documents (BREFs), can help to describe and 
regulate a minimum treatment level for waste water treatment plants. Moreover, BREFs could also be a 
beneficial instrument for pre-treatment installations for industrial discharges into the sewer system. 
► Stimulate innovation through distinct quality requirements and use the system of BREFs to ensure 
the progress

ÖVGW Feedback on the public consultation on UWWTD 
 
Wastewater services play a vital role in ensuring and improving good quality of European 
surface waters and public health and environmental safety.  
We welcome the revision of the UWWTD in order to help addressing future challenges, such as 
climate change, wastewater reduction, micropollutants, digitalisation and fostering nature-based 
solutions and encouraging circularity of resources.  
Preventing pollution at source – for a toxic free environment 
We agree with the zero pollution action plan, based on the art.191.2 TFEU and the principles 
enshrined in it: the precautionary principle, the principle that preventive action should be  taken 
and that environmental damage should, as a priority, be rectified at source, and the principle 
that the polluter should pay. Additionally, the Extended Producer Responsibility Scheme shall 
help contribute to allocate the costs of necessary additional treatment steps.  
Especially for PFAS, a ban of all non-essential uses is considered a step forward to limit the 
emission of micropollutants.  
A coherent regulatory framework with clear instruments covering all persistent, mobile, toxic 
(PMT) and very persistent, very mobile (vPvM) substances needs to be in place to prevent and 
limit the emission of these substances to the water cycle to protect drinking water resources. 
Prerequisite for an efficient and effective solution is coherence with other sectoral  EU legislation 
(IED, WFD, EQS, DWD, REACH, etc.). 
Top priority - Drinking water resource protection 
To ensure the supply of high quality drinking water to citizens, the protection of water resources 
against pollution is paramount, as recognised in the WFD, whose objectives should be 
mainstreamed in UWWTD and other sectoral policies. 
Due to multiple pathways, the water resources protection is only effective if preventive action is 
taken and the source-control approach is implemented: the latter is also instrumental to achieve 
a truly circular economy. 
We support EU circular economy ambition and EU Green Deal strategies, BUT not at the costs 
of elevated risks for drinking water resources (surface and groundwater) through waste water 
effluent and sewage sludge deposition on agricultural land.  
Beside valuable nutrients (phosphorous and nitrogen), sewage sludge also often contains 
micropollutants, such as pharmaceuticals, PFAS, microplastic, pathogens – which might end up 
in the environment if sewage sludge is used as fertilizer in agriculture. A regulatory framework is 
needed (similar to EU Water Reuse Directive) to support sustainable and resilient sludge 
management, incorporating a broader scope for risk assessment of potential impacts on water 
resources and human health and strict sludge quality control. 
Additional treatment steps for micropollutants  
Often, wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) function as pathways for substances of emerging 
concern, entering the water cycle through wastewater effluent in receiving waters.   
The decision of stepping up wastewater treatment efforts with enhanced treatment technologies 
shall be based on case-by-case substance risk assessment and environmental impact 
assessment.

Selected WWTPs in Switzerland and Germany have started to explore this way forward with 
extra treatment with the purpose of addressing only one specific category of micropollutants: 
pharmaceuticals for human use. BUT even with state of the art treatment technologies, WWTP s 
are not capable of removing all unwanted substances, due to intrinsic limitations of treatment 
technologies. There is no one off-the-shelf-solution for removing all micropollutants.  
BETTER WAY forward is a full environmental impact assessment with a risk assessment of 
present micropollutants considering specific setting (number of connections, quality of receiving 
waters, etc.) to develop advanced treatment steps as a complementary tool to control at source 
measures.  
Protecting the valuable functions of European soils 
The upcoming revision of UWWTD should also be coherent with other sectoral policies and 
legislation (WFD, DWD, GWD, EQSD, soil strategy) as healthy soils are not only essential to 
meet climate and biodiversity goals under the European Green Deal, but also to protect 
groundwater (drinking water resources) from any form of pollution through its filtration function.

Sewage pre-concentration for maximum recovery
and reuse at decentralized level
V. Diamantis, W. Verstraete, A. Eftaxias, B. Bundervoet, V. Siegfried,
P. Melidis and A. Aivasidis
ABSTRACT
Pre-concentration of municipal wastewater by chemically enhanced primary treatment (CEPT) was
studied under controlled laboratory conditions. Both iron and aluminium-based coagulants were
examined at gradually increasing concentrations (0.23, 0.35, 0.70 and 1.05 mmol/L). The CEPT sludge
generated from different coagulation experiments was digested in batch anaerobic reactors, while
the supernatant was tested in a dead-end micro ﬁltration setup. The results of the study show that
biogas yield was dramatically decreased (from 0.40 to 0.10 m
3/kg chemical oxygen demand of
inﬂuent) with increasing coagulant dose. In contrast, supernatant ﬁlterability was improved. Based on
the laboratory results, a conceptual design was produced for a community of 2000 inhabitant
equivalents (IE), using CEPT technology (at low coagulant dose) with anaerobic digestion of the
concentrates. According to this, the capital and operational costs were 0.11 and 0.09 €/m
3,
respectively. The biogas generated is used for digester heating and the overall process is energy self-
sufﬁcient. At a small-scale and in private applications, CEPT technology is preferably operated at
higher coagulant dose, followed by membrane ﬁltration for water reuse. Accordingly, sewage
puriﬁcation and reuse is possible without implementing aerobic biological processes.

INTRODUCTION
Sustainability in sewage treatment is of major importance
both for environmental protection and resource recovery.
Municipal sewage constitutes a valuable source of water,
energy and nutrients that can be valorized or reused
(Verstraete et al. ). In most cases water reuse technol-
ogies incorporate aerobic biological processes (extended
aeration), followed by advanced membrane ﬁltration.
Depending on the membrane process (e.g. ultra ﬁltration
(UF), reverse osmosis (RO)) the permeate is suitable for
uses from irrigation to indirect potable reuse ( Dewettinck
et al. ). The treatment train requires, however, a signi ﬁ-
cant energy input (up to 1 kWh/m
3 for the activated sludge
þ1 kWh/m3 for UF/RO) ( van Houtte & Verbauwhede
). The potential for energy recovery depends on excess
aerobic sludge digestibility. However, when the solids re-
tention time (SRT) in the activated sludge process is
increased, there is a decrease in both the amount of excess
sludge and its methane production potential ( Bolzonella
et al . ).
One option to decrease energy consumption for waste-
water puri ﬁcation is to implement an up ﬂow anaerobic
sludge blanket (UASB) reactor for sewage pre-treatment, fol-
lowed by a low-energy input aerobic process, such as a
trickling ﬁlter, a rotating biological contactor or a con-
structed wetland ( Khan et al. ). In this case, the energy
required for wastewater processing is lower by 90%, com-
pared to the conventional activated sludge approach. The
quantity of biogas recovered by the UASB process is, how-
ever, limited to 60 –80% of the theoretical values, due to
methane solubility and loss with the anaerobic ef ﬂuent
(Seghezzo et al. ). Moreover, the anaerobic degradation
of organic matter proceeds simultaneously with the
reduction of sulphates to sulphides, which renders the
anaerobic ef ﬂuent highly odourous ( Subtil et al . ). This

decentralized level, close to densely populated areas.
Another option to maximize energy (methane) recovery
from sewage is to pre-concentrate and digest the organics
present therein. The remaining wastewater (major stream)
is further upgraded by a low-energy input aerobic process
(Diamantis et al. ), direct membrane ﬁltration
(Verstraete et al. ) or even disposed of to non-sensitive
environments ( Odegaard ). Sewage pre-concentration
is possible with the so-called chemically enhanced primary
treatment (CEPT) process ( Harleman & Murcott ).
The CEPT technology is a high-rate and compact process,
which incorporates a hydraulic retention time (HRT) of
20–40 min for the coagulation/ ﬂocculation step and 1 –3h
for the subsequent clari ﬁcation ( Tchobanoglous et al.
). In existing sewage treatment plants, CEPT can be
easily incorporated in overall plant design ( Keplinger et al.
). Moreover, by dosing coagulant salts (e.g. iron chlor-
ide) in raw municipal wastewater, the sulphides present in
sewage are precipitated as metal sulphides, therefore
odour control is achieved ( Nielsen et al. ).
In this study, a CEPT process was studied under con-
trolled laboratory conditions, with original municipal
wastewater. The effect of coagulant type and dose was exam-
ined in relation to CEPT supernatant quality and ﬁlterability.
Moreover, CEPT sludge digestibility was evaluated in batch
anaerobic reactors. Based on the laboratory results, a
conceptual design was produced for a decentralized appli-
cation of 2000 inhabitant equivalents (IE) and the overall
costs were calculated.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Coagulation studies
Raw municipal wastewater was sampled daily from the
wastewater treatment plant of the city of Gent (Belgium).
Fifty litres of wastewater was used for each coagulation
experiment. CEPT was performed using iron- or aluminium-
based coagulants [FeCl
3 and Al2(SO4)3]. The coagulant dose
varied from 0.23, 0.35, 0.70 to 1.05 mmol/L. Accordingly,
the metal ion to P ratio varied from 1.2, 1.8, 3.7 to 5.5.
The required quantity of coagulant was added into the
wastewater vessel (50 L) and rapid mixing (100 rpm) was
applied for 20 min. A polymeric ﬂocculant (Polymer
1100P) was dosed into the vessel at 0.2 mg/L and slow
mixing (at 50 rpm) was performed for a further 20 min.
When the ﬂocculation process was completed, the mixture
was left to clarify inside the vessel for 1 h, after which
40 L of supernatant were removed using a pump. The
remaining sludge (10 L) was placed into Imhoff cones for
additional thickening for 2 h. The supernatant from the
Imhoff cones was rejected and the sludge samples were
used for the anaerobic digestion experiments. Chemical
analysis of the supernatant and the sludge were performed
for pH, chemical oxygen demand (COD), PO
4-P and
NH4-N according to Standard Methods for the Examination
of Water and Wastewater ().
Filterability of the CEPT supernatant
Supernatant ﬁlterability from different coagulation exper-
iments was examined in a dead-end micro ﬁltration setup
(Figure 1 ). A ﬂat-sheet micro ﬁltration membrane, with
pore size of 0.45 μm and surface area 12.56 cm 2, was used
for each trial. The trans-membrane pressure (TMP) was
maintained constant at 0.6 bar using a vacuum pump.
Wastewater was added into the micro ﬁltration device in
50 mL portions, while the vacuum pump was in operation.
Upon addition, the time required for complete water in ﬁl-
tration was recorded. A second wastewater portion was
added and the procedure was repeated a total of ﬁve times.
Digestibility of CEPT sludge
The sludge obtained from different coagulation experiments
was digested in batch anaerobic reactors (operational volume
¼ 1 L). The digesters were installed and operated under
mesophilic conditions (35
W
C), and inoculated with 300 mL
of anaerobic biomass and 300 mL of CEPT sludge. Metha-
nostim liquid (Avecom, Belgium) was added to ensure no
limitation of trace elements occurred. Two reactors were

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Effect of CEPT on supernatant quality
Figure 2 shows the effect of coagulant type and dose on
supernatant COD and phosphorus (PO 4-P) concentrations.
Both iron- and aluminium-based coagulants were effective
in decreasing wastewater COD by 50 –60% at doses
/C21 0.35 mmol/L. Simultaneously a signi ﬁcant part of the sol-
uble COD (20 –25%) was removed and this was attributed to
the adsorption of organics onto metal hydroxide precipitates
(Yan et al. ). For low coagulant dosing ( <0.35 mmol/L),
residual phosphorus concentration was equal to 1 –2 mg/L.
Ammonia (NH 4-N) concentration was not affected by the
CEPT process which is bene ﬁcial in case of wastewater
reuse for land irrigation/fertilization (see Figure S1,
Electronic Supplementary Material, available online at
http://www.iwaponline.com/wst/067/639.pdf).
Effect of CEPT on sludge digestibility
Figure 3 shows average values of the quantity of CEPT
sludge generated as a function of the coagulant dose.
Indeed, sludge production increased linearly from 1.5 to
3% of wastewater volume treated, with increasing coagulant
dose, and this was attributed to the formation of metal
precipitates ( Tchobanoglous et al. ).
Figure 4 shows the cumulative biogas production per kg
inﬂuent COD (CODin) during anaerobic digestion of differ-
ent CEPT sludges. From the results presented it is evident
that biogas production ceased within 10 d of residence
time. This is closely related to the fact that COD of the con-
centrates was in the medium range [6 ( ±1) g/L], and
optimum conditions for the anaerobic digestion were main-
tained during the trials (high activity seed sludge, optimum
temperature (35
W
C) and trace elements supplementation).
However, increasing the coagulant dose from 0.23 to
1.05 mmol/L resulted in a dramatic decrease in biogas
yield, from 0.41 to 0.16 and 0.39 to 0.06 m
3/kg CODin for
aluminium- and iron-based coagulants, respectively. The
biogas generated at high coagulant dose was not considered
of interest for practical applications. On the other hand,
lower coagulant doses (e.g. <0.35 mmol/L) generated

Membrane ﬁlterability of the CEPT ef ﬂuent
Figure 5 shows the effect of increasing coagulant dose on ﬁl-
terability of the CEPT supernatant (for aluminium-based
coagulant). The data presented show the time required for
complete in ﬁltration of 50 mL wastewater portions, in a
dead-end micro ﬁltration setup. Increasing the coagulant
dose was bene ﬁcial to achieve rapid water in ﬁltration (or
high ﬂuxes). Accordingly, membrane fouling was controlled.
Increasing the coagulant dose >1.05 mmol/L did not result
in further improvement of supernatant ﬁlterability.

CEPT system
Taking as an example a community of 2000 IE, the waste-
water ﬂow rate is 500 m 3/d ( ∼20 m3/h). The required
volume for the coagulation tank is equal to 15 m 3 (design
HRT ¼ 40 min), and the next-step clari ﬁer is 60 m 3
(design HRT ¼ 3 h), having a surface area of 20 m 2
(design surface loading rate ¼ 1.0 m3/(m2h)). The capital
costs (CAPEX) of the CEPT process are in the order of

Anaerobic digestion
The CEPT sludge generated at the designed coagulant dose
corresponds to 10 m 3/d ( ∼2% of the incoming wastewater
ﬂow) and the COD concentration is equal to 6 g/L. The
CEPT sludge is further stabilized and valorized using a con-
tinuous stirred anaerobic digester. The required volume for
the digester is equal to 100 m 3 (design HRT ¼ 10 d, organic
loading rate ¼ 0.6 kg/(m3d)). The CAPEX of the latter is in
the order of (100 m 3) × (1,000 € /m3) ¼ 100,000 € (Ubaydul-
laev ), which includes: digestion tank (concrete with
thermal insulation), sludge pre- and post-thickening tanks,
biogas handling equipment and boiler, heat exchanger,
pumps, pipes, valves, process monitoring equipment, engin-
eering and design. Post thickening tank (10 m
3 volume) and
sludge dewatering bags ﬁlters entail an additional cost of
40,000 € . Considering 15 years as the life time of the plant
and 6% interest, the annual CAPEX is 14,000 € /yr or
0.08 € /m3 wastewater treated.
The biogas recovered by the digester is (10 m 3/d) ×
(6 kg/m3) × (0.35 m3/kg CODin) ¼ 21 m3/d. Generally,
such biogas production is considered low for electricity gen-
eration, but it is suf ﬁcient to maintain the digester
temperature at mesophilic conditions (see Calculations 1,
Electronic Supplementary Material, available online at
http://www.iwaponline.com/wst/067/639.pdf). Biogas gen-
eration can be further increased if high-strength sewage is
available, or by co-digestion with organic-rich substrates
and wastes ( Athanasoulia et al. ). Indeed, the OPEX
of the anaerobic digester are negligible ( <0.01 € /m
3).
A motor of 0.7 kW suf ﬁces for digester mixing ( Tchobano-
glous et al. ) which entails an electricity cost of
(0.7 kW) × (24 h/d) × (365 d/yr) × (0.12 € /kWh) ¼ 740 € /yr
or 0.004 € /m3. An equal amount (740 € /yr) is required
annually for trace element supplementation (nickel, cobalt,
molybdenum) at dose 40 mg/m
3.
Concluding, implementation of a CEPT process fol-
lowed by anaerobic digestion of the concentrates for a
decentralized setting of 2000 IE costs ∼0.20 € /m
3 waste-
water treated and the process is energy neutral.
Recommendations for small-scale applications
At small-scale and private applications (e.g. from 20 to 200
IE), it is desirable to operate the CEPT process at higher
coagulant dose (e.g. 1 mmol/L). Accordingly, the CEPT
supernatant remains of high quality and post-membrane ﬁl-
tration is ef ﬁcient. Thus, irrigation water (rich in nitrogen)
can be recovered as the ﬁrst product of the process, without
implementing aerobic biological processes. The overall
system requires minimum reactor volumes (total HRT
∼3 h). For a 200 IE facility (wastewater ﬂow rate 50 m
3/d),
the quantity of sludge generated is approximately 2 m 3/d
(4% of the incoming wastewater ﬂow) and is considered of
limited digestibility. This is important for medium-term sto-
rage of the concentrates, since diffuse methane emissions
can be minimized ( Diaz-Valbuena et al. ).
CONCLUSIONS
– CEPT of sewage can ef ﬁciently decrease wastewater COD
by 50 –60% and phosphorus by >90% at low HRT (3 h)
and cost (0.10 € /m3).
– Increasing the coagulant dose is bene ﬁcial to remove the
majority of the organics and phosphates, and generate a
supernatant of good ﬁlterability. Moreover, CEPT sludge
of limited digestibility is produced, which is important
for medium-term storage at small-scale and private decen-
tralized applications.
– At lower coagulant dose, an organic-rich sludge can be
harvested which is suitable for a waste-to-energy strategy.
This is important for larger-scale facilities. Accordingly, it

6.40.1 Introduction 
The main goals of water sanitation systems are to protect human health and the environment and to recover valuable resources from 
used water (e.g., water for reuse, energy, and nutrients) [1, 2]. 
Activated sludge is nowadays routinely used for biological treatment of municipal effluents. For example, in Greece, more than 
85% of the installed treatment plants consist of conventional activated sludge (CAS) [3]. The process enables organic matter 
oxidation to CO2 and excess biomass, ammonia oxidation to nitrate (which is sometimes reduced to nitrogen gas), and (partial) 
phosphorus incorporation to biomass. Several pharmaceuticals and trace organic chemicals may be degraded, while others remain 
unaffected [4]. Metal removal is mainly performed by adsorption and complexation with the biomass and ranges from 50 to 98% 
depending on initial metal concentration [5]. Therefore, the treated water is usually disposed to the environment. 
Regions with arid and semiarid climatic conditions and limited access to freshwater supplies have recognized the value of these 
discharges in advance. Conventional processes such as the activated sludge, coupled with advanced treatment schemes (micro­
filtration (MF)/ultrafiltration (UF) and reverse osmosis), enable recovery of high-quality water suitable for reuse. At the centralized

Water District; NEWater, Singapore; West Basin, California; and Oostduinkerke, Belgium) [6]. 
The CAS process traditionally receives wastewaters that are pretreated by primary sedimentation [7]. Primary sedimentation is 
most efficient in removing settleable solids, whereas the biological processes are essential for removing soluble, colloidal, and 
particulate (suspended) organic substances. For applications such as treating wastewater from smaller-sized communities, primary 
treatment is often not used as more emphasis is placed on simpler and less-operator-intensive treatment methods [7]. 
Nitrogen removal is often required before discharging treated wastewater to sensitive water bodies (to prevent eutrophication). 
All of the biological nitrogen removal processes include an aerobic zone in which biological nitrification occurs. Some anoxic 
volume or time must also be included to provide biological denitrification to complete the objective of total nitrogen removal by 
both ammonia oxidation and nitrate and nitrite reduction to nitrogen gas. In this case, the primary clarification step is commonly 
omitted since nitrate reduction requires an electron donor, which can be supplied in the form of influent wastewater biochemical 
oxygen demand (BOD). 
Therefore, the activated sludge process is nowadays designed without separating the solid fraction, at least to a major extent. In 
this article, CAS systems with and without anaerobic digestion (AD) are analyzed and discussed in relation to electricity consump­
tion, greenhouse gas emissions, and maximum energy recovery. Novel approaches, implementing an enhanced separation of the 
solid and liquid fraction upfront, are conceived and proposed for sustainable wastewater treatment. 
6.40.2 Actual Situation in Wastewater Treatment Plants 
6.40.2.1 Electricity Requirements 
Electricity is generated not only by centralized facilities in most cases employing hard coal, lignite, oil, and natural gas, but also from 
nontraditional sources (nuclear, biomass, hydro, and wind). The greenhouse gas emissions related to electricity production are in 
the order of 0.34, 0.57, and 0.50 kg CO2 kWh−1 for EU27, USA, and worldwide, respectively [8]. In the present work, a value of 
0.50 kg CO2 kWh−1 was used for the calculations. 
At municipal level, the electricity consumption of a wastewater treatment plant is comparable to other consumers (schools, 
streets, etc.). A study by Power Application Group Inc. on behalf of Ontario’s Independent Electricity System Operator revealed that 
445 municipalities of Ontario spent around $680 million annually for electricity and they consumed 6.6 billion kWh yr−1 
(on average ~ 570 kWh per inhabitant−1 per year−1). The average consumption of electricity for wastewater treatment represented 
16% of the total electricity consumption and it was comparable to other uses such as street lighting (14%), water treatment and 
pumping (17%), housing (16%), and arena (11%). 
For a conventional aerobic wastewater treatment plant (i.e., activated sludge), aeration accounts for 60–70% of the total 
electricity requirements followed by wastewater pumping [9]. The oxygen requirements for the biodegradation of carbonaceous 
material (BOD) is in the order of 0.90–1.30 kg O2 kg−1 BOD removed for sludge retention times (SRTs) of 5–20 days. One 
inhabitant equivalent (IE) rejects ~ 60 g BOD per day or 21.9 kg BOD yr−1. With an overall efficiency of the activated sludge process 
of 90–95%, that is, 20 kg BOD removed IE−1 yr−1, the oxygen requirements correspond to 18–26 kg O2 IE−1 yr−1. Ammonia 
oxidation to nitrate requires stoichiometrically about 4.3 kg O2 per kg N. With 13 g total nitrogen (TN) rejected per IE and day and 
with 90% nitrification efficiency extra ~ 18 kg O2 IE−1 yr−1 are required. 
Oxygen can be supplied by means of air bubbles introduced to the water to create gas–water interfaces. There are several types of 
aeration systems used for wastewater treatment. The most frequently used are the diffused-air systems and mechanical aeration. 
Considering a conversion factor of 1.0 kg O2 kWh−1, the emissions related to the provision of aeration for BOD removal are 9–13 kg 
CO2 IE−1 yr−1. In case that nitrification is also included, this accounts to 18–22 kg CO2 IE−1 yr−1.

Novel treatment schemes implementing the membrane bioreactor (MBR) technology require additional energy for membrane 
fouling control and this is usually accomplished by excess pumping (to increase cross-flow velocity), aeration (to produce air slugs 
and air scouring), and the operation of rotational or vibratory equipment. It has been often reported that the electricity consump­
tion is a factor of 2 or more compared to CAS [10]. 
Finally, major savings are possible by appropriate control of water aeration through dissolved oxygen (DO) measurements and 
by minimizing flow short-circuit in aerated channel reactors [11]. 
6.40.2.2 Excess Sludge Production 
The activated sludge process is usually designed toward minimization of excess sludge production. It is well known that increasing 
SRT and hence decreasing sludge loading rate can decrease sludge production in aerobic wastewater treatment processes. 
Additionally, by increasing the biomass concentration it would theoretically be possible to reach a situation in which the amount 
of energy provided to the microorganisms equals the maintenance demand. This is the concept of extended aeration, which 
combines high mixed liquor suspended solids (MLSS) with high SRT. It is impossible, however, to increase the sludge concentration 
significantly in the conventional activated sludge process by means of sedimentation. Accordingly, the MBR provides an interesting 
technology. Innocenti et al.[ 12] reported a decrease in excess sludge by 75–84% by implementing the MBR at SRT > 100 days. In a 
similar study, the optimum SRT for a MBR was reported to be ~80 days [13]. 
6.40.2.2.1 Primary sludge 
One IE rejects annually (0.065 kg d−1)*(365 days) = 23.7 kg suspended solids (SS) consisting of the settleable (~ 18.2 kg IE−1 yr−1)a nd  
the colloidal fraction (5.5 kg IE−1 yr−1). The degree of SS removal in the primary clarifier is, among others, a function of the hydraulic 
retention time (HRT) and for typical design values (HRT = 1–2 h), the removal of the settleable fraction is ~80%, corresponding to 
60% total SS removal. Therefore, approximately 14.2 kg SS IE−1 yr−1 are separated in the primary sludge, with a volatile solids content 
of 9.2 kg volatile suspended solids (VSS) IE−1 yr−1. The respective removal of BOD by the primary clarifier is ~25%. 
6.40.2.2.2 Secondary sludge 
In Table 2, the VSS yield per kilogram BOD removed as function of the SRT is given for pretreated (clarified) and raw municipal

factor of 1.5 is possible by the CAS process at low SRT [9]. 
6.40.2.3 Energy Recovery from Methane 
The organic matter (expressed as chemical oxygen demand (COD)) released annually by a population equivalent is approximately 
(0.120 kg d−1)*(365 days) = 43.8 kg COD, which corresponds to ~ 16.4 kg Total Organic Carbon (TOC) IE−1 yr−1. The theoretical 
chemical energy content is therefore equal to 177 kWh IE−1 yr−1 considering that 1 kg TOC is equal to 10.8 kWh. This energy content 
is, however, accessible after separation, drying, and incineration of the organics. 
Digesting all the readily degradable COD present in domestic sewage (0.090 kg COD IE−1 d−1 or 32.8 COD kg IE−1 yr−1) may 
result in 32.8*0.32 = 10.5 m3 CH4 IE−1 yr−1. The corresponding energy content is equal to ~30 kWh-el IE−1 yr−1. Separating and 
digesting only the solid fraction (0.045 kg VSS IE−1 d−1 or 16.4 kg VSS IE−1 yr−1), one can generate 16.4*0.5 = 8.2 m3 biogas IE−1 yr−1 
(having 65% methane) or 5.3 m3 CH4 IE−1 yr−1. The corresponding energy content is equal to 16 kWh-el IE−1 yr−1. It is therefore 
evident that approximately half of the wastewater degradable COD is related to particles.

At short sludge age, a significant part of the organic load is removed from the liquid stream mainly by adsorption and rapidly 
introduced into the sludge stream. In this case, almost complete utilization of biodegradable material is possible, but since the

should be dry (less than 10% moisture content), which requires large amounts of energy and may be considered unsustainable in 
most cases. The technology of solar sludge drying is a zero energy input process and is especially suitable for Mediterranean 
conditions [15].

lower than the energy recovered by sludge digestion. Operational data from the wastewater treatment plant ‘Strass’ in Austria verify 
this trend, that is, it is possible to render the CAS process self-sufficient, in terms of energy requirements, by implementing a high-
rate biosorption process at low SRT, AD of the sludge, and oxygen-limited autotrophic nitrification/denitirification (OLAND) 
treatment (see also Section 6.40.3.3.2) of the digestate [9]. 
6.40.2.4 Methane Emissions from Sludge Disposal 
Methane is considered the most important greenhouse gas (GHG) emitted from wastewater management. Most small- and 
medium-sized aerobic treatment plants provide excess sludge storage that combines primary and secondary sludge. The material 
is dewatered by means of filter presses and is often disposed to a lagoon or landfill. In this case, anaerobic degradation occurs [16] 
and can result in a potential release to the environment of 2.4–7.1 m3 CH4 IE−1 yr−1 (see Table 3) or 1.63–4.83 kg CH4 IE−1 yr−1 
considering a density of methane equal to 0.68 kg m−3 (at 1.013 bar and 15 oC). The global warming potential (GWP) for methane 
is 25 compared to CO2, thus, the GHG emissions are 40–120 kg CO2 IE−1 yr−1. This potential value may be lower in practice due to 
environmental factors influencing CH4 production (temperature, pH, nutrient limitations, biological inhibition, physicochemical 
interactions, etc.) and the extent of decomposition and carbon sequestration in the disposal site or landfill. 
In large-scale facilities where the excess sludge is digested in a technical way, the above-mentioned emissions are avoided since 
the biogas is collected and burned or reused. Indeed, it is possible to avoid 3.6–10.7 kg CO2 IE−1 yr−1 by replacing conventional 
fossil fuels (see Table 3). 
6.40.2.5 Nitrous Oxide Emissions from Waste Biodegradation 
Since 1 kg N2O has a GWP of 310 kg CO2 on a time horizon of 100 years [17], N2O emissions during biological nitrogen removal 
can weigh heavily on the overall CO2 footprint of a wastewater treatment plant. The production of N2O can be related to nitritation, 
that is, ammonia oxidation to nitrite, or to denitrification. For both processes, N2O emissions are stimulated by rapidly changing 
process conditions and by high nitrite levels. Furthermore, high DO levels and low COD/N ratios enhance denitrification emissions, 
whereas high ammonium concentrations (also simultaneously with nitrite) and specific (about 1 mg O2 l−1) or changing (low to 
high) DO levels increase nitritation emissions [18]. Given the highly dynamic nature of N2O emissions, accurate quantifications can 
only be obtained from grab samples taken at high frequency or from continuous online measurements [18]. Only some reports exist 
using this approach on full-scale wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), displaying a wide range of N2O emissions: from 0.01% to

6.40.3 Sustainability in Wastewater Treatment 
6.40.3.1 Maximum Separation of the Solid and Liquid Fraction 
There are several advantages to be considered for a chemically enhanced primary treatment (CEPT) step: 
• Simple and rapid process start-up. Immediate operation after short- or long-term pauses. 
• The addition of chemicals (coagulants and flocculants) requires simple dosage systems. 
• Minimum energy requirements and no energy for water aeration. 
•	 Minimum diffuse CO2 emissions. Wastewater organics are concentrated rather than degraded. It is possible to concentrate up to 
50% of the soluble COD through adsorption into sludge flocs and precipitates formed. 
•	 The hydraulic retention time required is significantly lower compared to conventional biological processes, thus the overall 
footprint of the plant is minimum. 
• Effluent quality in terms of organic contaminants is similar or even higher than secondary standards. 
• Nitrogen is recovered in the primary effluent and can be reused at local level (for gardening, irrigation, etc.). 
• Complete removal of phosphorous is possible by coagulant use. The P can be subsequently recovered from the digested sludge. 
• Removal of heavy metals from water is possible by the use of coagulants (iron and aluminum). 
• It is possible to upgrade the effluent bya low-energy bioprocess (e.g., trickling filter (TF)) ensuringresidual BOD removalandnitrification. 
Indeed, maximum separation of the solid fraction is possible by implementing a CEPT step. This includes a rapid mixing tank where 
a coagulant is dosed, a slow-mixing flocculation process, and a final clarifier. Odegaard [21] reported that more than 35 CEPT plants 
in Norway were consistently operating at removal efficiency 75% for COD, 90% for P, and 90% for SS. 
The energy requirements for a typical coagulation process results from the operation of rapid and slow mixers, having an energy 
input of 0.010 and 0.003 kWh m−3 of water treated or 0.64 and 0.20 kWh IE−1 yr−1, respectively. Accordingly, the CO2 emissions are 
in the order of 0.42 kg CO2 IE−1 yr−1. This is especially favorable in terms of GHG accounting. However, aluminum sulfate 
production requires large amounts of energy, in the order of 10 000 kWh ton−1 of produced aluminum [22]. The respective values 
for ferric chloride and organic polymer production are 350 and 12 500 kWh ton−1 [22]. Considering a conversion factor of 0.50 kg 
CO2 kWh−1 of energy input [8], this results in 5.000, 0.175, and 6.250 kg CO2 kg−1 of aluminum, ferric chloride, and polymer, 
respectively. A coagulant dose of 80 mg l−1 (as alum) results in an emission factor of 0.400 kg CO2 m−3 of water treated or 25.6 kg 
CO2 IE−1 yr−1. For ferric chloride, the latter value is ~1.1 kg CO2 IE−1 yr−1, dosing at 100 mg l−1. The flocculant is usually dosed at a 
maximum level of 3–4m g  l−1 and represents an emission factor of 1.2–1.6 kg CO2 IE−1 yr−1. Therefore, adding extra 2.5 kg CO2 
IE−1 yr−1 from the use of chemicals (iron and flocculants), a maximum of 3.0 kg CO2 IE−1 yr−1 can be assumed for the CEPT process. 
The amount of concentrated sludge produced by CEPT is 1.5–2.0 times higher compared to conventional settling processes 
because of high SS removal efficiency and the coagulant precipitates formed (e.g., aluminum or iron hydroxide, calcium sulfate for 
alum treatment, etc.). Also, a significant part (up to 50%) of the soluble organic matter is absorbed by the flocs and removed from 
the liquid phase. Anaerobic digestion of the concentrated organics can result in biogas production in the order of 70–80% of the 
theoretical methane potential (10.5 m3 CH4 IE−1 yr−1). However, AD of the chemically enhanced sludge can potentially decrease 
biogas production, for the same amount of volatile solids fed, by a factor of 10–15% [23]. This is due to low levels of bio-available P 
and trace elements, which may hinder the ability of microorganisms to metabolize organic substrates. Indeed, the decrease in biogas 
productivity depends strongly on the coagulant dose and it is especially severe when more than 100 mg l−1 (iron or alum) are added 
during wastewater treatment [24]. 
Optimization of the CEPT process is possible by implementing a dissolved air flotation (DAF) step. The DAF offers significant 
advantages over conventional sedimentation processes. It is usually designed for overflow rates of up to 15 m h−1 and provides 
increased separation efficiency, since smaller particles can be removed. The corresponding footprint can therefore be decreased by a 
factor of 5–10. The COD removal in the order of 70–90%, with effluent COD concentrations being lower than 100 mg l−1, are 
obtained from practical scale modules designed for flow rates between 1 and 40 m3 h−1 [21]. Comparably with the sedimentation 
process, coagulation also plays an important role in flotation systems. When alum was used as the coagulant, on average 99% 
removal of P and higher than 85% removal of SS was observed. Finally, it is possible to remove several pharmaceuticals and 
personal care products [25, 26]. 
The energy required for DAF includes water recirculation and air compression and approximates 0.02–0.04 kWh m−3 or 
1.28–2.56 kWh IE−1 yr−1. Therefore, the GHG emissions may be 0.6–1.3 kg CO2 IE−1 yr−1. Adding an extra 2.5 kg CO2 IE−1 yr−1 
for use of chemicals (iron and polymer) the overall GHG emission is at maximum 3.8 kg CO2 IE−1 yr−1. 
The DAF systems have been reported to produce highly concentrated sludge [27]. In this case, the dry solids concentration 
achieved using a cationic polyelectrolyte was approximately 5%. This is especially attractive since small volumes of relatively 
concentrated sludge are collected. Digesting the latter may recover up to 70–80% of the wastewater energy content in accordance 
with the amount of COD separated by the process.

6.40.3.2 Primary Membrane Filtration 
Instead of implementing CEPT to separate the organics present in sewage, the use of membranes offers an interesting alternative. 
The MF/UF membranes operated directly on sewage achieved permeate COD between 10 and 80 mg l−1 with complete rejection of 
the SS [35, 36]. Pathogen rejection was also high [35]. Although, increasing membrane pore size may result in bacteria break­
through, the formation of cake layer provides additional filtration and enhances bacteria removal [36]. Finally, the rejection of 
nutrients during primary membrane filtration is limited [35, 37]. For decentralized applications, this is important, since essential 
macro- and micronutrients can be recovered and consequently reused at local level. 
Although membrane systems show superior performance in terms of effluent (permeate) quality, their use in sewage 
concentration is limited to pilot testing due to poor hydraulic performance which is attributed to membrane fouling. If the 
steady-state permeate flux for a given wastewater type and process conditions approaches extremely low values, it is uneconomical 
(in terms of both capital and operational expenses) to proceed in full-scale application. 
A shear-enhanced cross-flow UF was employed for sewage concentration and water recovery [38]. The permeate stream was 
transparent and characterized by low COD concentration (< 50 mg l−1), while the major part of the nutrients were captured 
(permeate total Kjeldahl nitrogen (TKN) = 20–25 mg l−1 and total phosphorus (TP) = 6–8m g  l−1). Sewage concentration by a factor 
of 10 was possible at steady-state fluxes of 65 l m−2 h−1 and transmembrane pressure of 3 bar. However, electricity consumption 
from the pilot-scale module was significant (5–10 kWh m−3) and the GHG emissions from fossil energy use may sum up to 
160–320 kg CO2 IE−1 yr−1. Therefore, new high-flux membranes and efficient fouling control and mitigation technologies need to 
be developed. This is especially important if direct applications on sewage are envisaged. 
Finally, it is possible to upgrade the CEPT or secondary effluent by MF/UF at overall energy input equal or less than 0.5 kWh m−3 
[39], which corresponds to 15 kg CO2 IE−1 yr−1. 
6.40.3.3 Ammonia Removal 
6.40.3.3.1 Nitrification 
By separating the solids and the liquid fraction up front, a primary treated water low in SS and organics is recovered. The use of 
coagulants can additionally decrease the P and heavy metals content to a major extent. Thus, ammonia nitrogen (residual 50–70%) 
constitutes a major pollutant if disposal rather than recovery is the objective. For effluent upgrade, low-rate trickling filters consist of 
a promising technology. The principal advantages over the CAS process include: 
• Lower energy requirements. 
• Simple operation (sludge recirculation, mixed liquor control, and sludge wasting are not required). 
• No problems of bulking sludge in the final clarifier. 
• Better sludge thickening properties. 
• Less equipment maintenance needs. 
• Better recovery from shock toxic loads. 
• Wastewater flow approaches a plug-flow hydraulic regime, which is favorable for wastewater treatment kinetics (first order). 
•	 The use of advanced primary effluent can reduce the loading of the filter (in terms of both suspended and colloidal organics); thus 
fouling of the packing may be controlled. 
Both BOD removal and nitrification can be accomplished in rock or plastic packing TFs operated at low organic loadings. In most 
low-rate filters, only the top 0.6–1.2 m of the filter packing will have appreciable biological slime [7]. As a result, the lower portions 
of the filter may be populated by autotrophic nitrifying bacteria, which oxidize ammonia nitrogen to nitrite and nitrate. If the 
nitrifying population is sufficiently well established, and if climatic conditions and wastewater characteristics are favorable, a 
well-operated low-rate filter can provide good BOD removal and a highly nitrified effluent [28]. 
The power required for a TF is 0.002–0.004 kW m−3 reactor volume for low-rate filters and up to 0.006–0.010 kW m−3 for 
high-rate applications [7]. The filters are usually designed according to the hydraulic or organic loading rate. The organic 
loading rate in terms of COD is 0.15–0.50 kg m−3 d−1 for low-rate filters and 1–4k g  m−3 d−1 for high-rate ones. For a COD 
concentration of the advanced primary treated water equal to 150 mg l−1, the hydraulic retention time for a trickling filter will 
never be higher than 1 day. Thus, the energy required per cubic meter of water treated is at maximum 0.05–0.10 kWh m−3 
water treated or 3–6 kWh IE−1 yr−1. 
Solids production will depend on the wastewater characteristics and the TF loading. At lower organic loading rates, the biomass 
has a longer SRT, and, as a result, less biomass is produced. Sludge production in this case may be 0.010–0.015 kg VSS IE−1 d−1, 
which corresponds to 3.6–5.5 kg VSS IE−1 yr−1. Digesting this fraction can result in 1.8–2.7 m3 biogas IE−1 yr−1 or 1.2–1.7 m3 CH4 
IE−1 yr−1, having an energy content 3.6–5.1 kWh-el IE−1 yr−1. Overall, the process can be self-sufficient and sustainable in terms of 
energy requirements and GHG emissions, respectively.

Stoichiometrically, OLAND requires only 1.8 kg O2 kg−1 N, which is around 60% lower than nitrification requirement of 4.3 kg 
O2 kg−1 N[ 32]. On the one hand, in aerated tank systems, this implies that OLAND consumes only ~ 8 kWh IE−1 yr−1, compared to 
~ 18 kWh IE−1 yr−1 for nitrification. Such reactor configurations include sequencing batch reactors, upflow reactors, and moving bed 
reactors [32]. On the other hand, for systems relying on passive aeration, the energy consumption will be lower than the 
conventional process. A typical example of such technology is a rotating biological contactor (RBC) (e.g., [30, 32]). As indicated 
above, the oxygen cost in aerated tank systems is generally around 1 kg O2 kWh−1. In comparison, this can be up to 2 kg O2 kWh−1 
for conventional disc RBCs (equal to 4 kWh IE−1 yr−1), and even up to 5 kg O2 kWh−1 for advanced RBCs with carrier material 
present in rotating cages (equal to <2 kWh IE−1 yr−1)[ 33].

[31], sludge production from OLAND is lower compared to nitrification. Overall, OLAND produces less than 0.10 kg VSS kg−1 N 
[34], which corresponds to ~0.5 kg IE−1 yr−1. This is especially favorable toward minimizing excess sludge production.

6.40.5 Conclusions 
The following conclusions were arrived at: 
1. The CAS process designed at high SRT results in significant GHG emissions due to high electricity requirements for water 
aeration. At short SRT it is possible to harvest the majority of the organics with minimum energy input, while reusing the biogas 
produced through anaerobic sludge digestion can result in energy self-sufficiency. 
2. Anaerobic digestion of the concentrates and sludge generated during municipal wastewater treatment is essential for carbon 
economy. Disposal of these materials to the landfill can contribute to the release of 40–100 kg CO2 IE−1 yr−1 due to diffuse 
methane emissions. 
3. Implementation of a CEPT step enables maximum separation of the organics, P, and heavy metals from the water. Use of 
coagulants/flocculants produced in the biorefinery market is of significant importance. The primary treated water contains some 
50–70% of the initial nitrogen load and can be reused at local level for gardening/irrigation. 
4. In cases that the primary treated water is designated for disposal to sensitive water bodies, the TF technology is especially 
interesting for effluent nitrification and residual BOD elimination. As an alternative for waters with a low COD/N, OLAND can 
achieve nitrogen removal at lower energy consumption and sludge production compared to nitrification. 
5. The possibility to combine CEPT with subsequent direct low-pressure membrane filtration and the development of high-flux 
membranes open perspectives for combining maximum recovery of nutrients and water without imposing a considerable energy 
input.

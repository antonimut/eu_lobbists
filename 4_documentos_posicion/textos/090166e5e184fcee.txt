Overview of FEP’s position on the review of the Database Directive 
(September 2021) 
 
 
The Federation of European Publishers would like to complement its response to the online 
questionnaire on the Data Act and the protection of databases, in order to further develop its 
analysis of the issues raised. FEP members rely on the  exclusive right that is the  database 
right, as a complement to Copyright and to propose specific services related these 
databases.  
 
Publishers databases include different types of data and require significant investments to 
be created, curated, enriched and maintained. For instance, legal databases may comprise 
both copyright protected works,  raw data (not protected by copyright ) and machine 
generated data, integrated in a consistent structure. However, the fact that raw data are not 
copyright-protected does not mean that  the publisher did not need to make  heavy 
investments to obtain,  structure, verify, elaborate,  organise these elements  and integrate 
them with other database contents , particularly in terms of high value -added manpower, 
sophisticated computer equipment and creation of algorithms that will process these data. 
 
The sui generis right is the essential legal basis to both offer licences to third party and prevent 
third party infringements. In addition, contractual clauses and technical measures can provide 
additional and complementary protection, but the sui generis right remains the guaranteed 
protection on which all publishers can rely , regardless of their size or technical capabilities , 
as it  legally allows publishers to freely decide whether or not they give access to their 
databases. Besides, in the Member States where legal protections complementary to the sui 
generis right exist, such as the notion of parasitism , it  proved difficult to use due to the 
complexities related to the demonstration of the existence of a damage, and could therefore 
not replace the protection offered by the sui generis right.  
 
At the same time, publishers use third party databases  on a daily basis for the purpose of 
their business and to create their own databases, which they do in full respect of IP and 
contractual rules. 
 
Database rights protect the investments, bring legal certainty and achieve a good balance 
between the rights and the interests of the right-holders and the users, in particular thanks 
to the new mandatory copyright exceptions foreseen by Article 3 to 6 of the 2019 Copyright 
Directive and the restrictions concerning public sector bodies stipulated by the 2019 Open 
Data Directive. Therefore, the sui generis right provided by the Database Directive does not 
need to be reviewed, especially on the question of access and sharing of data. There is a lack 
of evidence that this right might cause  any significant difficulties and, at the same time, it 
forms the legal and legitimate basis for the business model of many publishers providing 
valuable databases to their clients.

Practically speaking, this right does not often lead to litigation: it is clear, deters misuses and 
allows rightholders to know all the extractions/reproductions of their databases by 
incentivising licences . Moreover, where conflicts arise, they are usually solved  without 
requiring the involvement of  courts, especially given the risks it could represent for trade 
secrets. In fact, it is only recently that technologies have allowed recurrent mass extractions 
which ensure the continuous  update of the database and thus maintain or increase the 
value of the data, but also make unauthorised access to databases  easier. In this context, 
reliable legal protections like the sui generis right are more necessary than ever for databases 
producers. 
 
Today, legal publishers for instance are developing second-generation databases, including 
search tools and decision -making assistance solutions based on artificial intelligence (AI).  
These databases analyze cases and contracts, and create links between court cases, laws, 
comments, analysis and other relevant elements. Such links help detect trends among cases 
and contracts which can contribute  to predicting possible outcomes for futur e court 
decisions, or identify risks in contracts. This kind of decision -making assistance tool, such as 
CaseLawAnalytics of Dalloz  is typically based on machine -generated data. In fact, the sui 
generis right has recently and increasingly become more essential due to the development of 
AI. It is therefore essential that the sui generis right covers these databases, to make sure that 
the investments of the creators of such databases are protected an d give opportunity for 
relevant third parties to benefit from these innovative databases through licences.  
 
It is fundamental that the European Commission preserves the sui generis right granted to 
database producers.  This regime does not prevent free flo w of data, but on the contrary 
contributes to creating growth by encouraging investments in quality data, which are 
necessary for very promising sectors, such as AI and assistance to decision -making. The 
absence of this right would need to be compensated by additional technical measures, to the 
detriment of investments on services themselves and could lead to a chilling effect that would 
ultimately reduce the offer for users. Moreover, far fewer actors would be incentivized to 
create and maintain meaningful  and costly databases, thus making other actors relying on 
such databases lag behind, both in terms of innovation and economic growth. This would 
therefore be completely at odds with the emergence of a data-driven economy.  
 
The 1996 Directive does not need to be reviewed as machine generated data are already 
covered by the scope of the sui generis right, when they require investments to precisely 
process data. Further limitation to the database right, whether through compulsory 
licences, new exceptions or other measures  would be disproportionate and create 
significant and unjustified economic harm to database creators. 
 
As a conclusion, the Database Directive has proven to be an effective and essential tool for 
database creators and has generated little case  law, showing the clarity and fitness for 
purpose of the current legal framework, especially on its most essential elements. Re-opening 
the Directive would lead to uncertainty for database creators, threaten their business 
model, their investments in innovative services and  high-value added manpower , and 
ultimately hurt the ability for European actors to rely on European databases.

Detailed answers to some questions of the public consultation 
 
 
Intellectual Property Rights - General questions 
 
In your view, how are intellectual property (IP) rights (including the sui generis  database 
right) and trade secrets relevant for business-to-business sharing of data? 
 
Publishers rely on IP to protect their databases, which req uire significant investments to be 
created, curated and enriched and may include both copyright protected works and other data 
(including machine-generated data). For instance, legal databases may combine copyright -
protected works, raw and machine generated data, each requiring specific investments and 
preprocessing (anonymisation of public sector information for example) . IP protection 
provides the clear and secure framework  necessary both to conclude licences with relevant 
actors and to deter bad actors from attempting to misappropriate or misuse the databases.  
 
 
“Control over the accessibility and use of data should not be realised through the  
establishment of additional layers of exclusive, proprietary rights”. To what extent   do 
you agree with this statement? 
 
Publishers strongly disagree with this statement.  The sui generis right is, as other copyright, 
an exclusive right that allows rightholder not only to give access to their content but also to 
refuse it, which is an essential component of the business model of database creators.  Legal 
publishers for insta nce are developing second -generation databases, including search tools 
and decision -making assistance solutions based on artificial intelligence (AI). Legal Tech is 
precisely based on the combination of Big Data and Artificial intelligence. In fact, the su i 
generis right has recently and increasingly become more essential in innovative database 
creation due to the development of AI.  
 
The selection (or creation), gathering, elaboration, verification, curation, enrichment, 
organization, optimization for discoverability (enabling new ways to explore data, to allow a 
new retrieval), maintenance and securisation of such data in databases eligible to IP 
protection requires significant resources and investments. Such investments may finance high-
value added manpow er, sophisticated computer equipment and the creation of new 
algorithms to process the data or the production of systems to create new data. They must be 
protected in order to incentivise such efforts. In addition, some of these data may themselves 
be subject to exclusive and proprietary rights (copyright-protected works for instance), which 
justifies the additional protection of such databases to prevent misuses.

In the absence of adequate protection, far fewer actors would be incentivised to create and 
maintain meaningful and costly databases, thus making other actors relying on such 
databases lag behind, both in terms of innovation and economic growth. They would not be 
in a position to fully achieve the potential of the Big Data economy that the EU is calling for. 
 
 
Questions on the Database Directive 
 
Please select what describes you best (creator or user of databases) 
 
Publishers are both users and creators of databases, containing mixed type of data. For the 
purpose of the services they can provide to their customers, publishers create databases that 
can contain copyright -protected works, raw data, machine generated dat a (for instance to 
link different pieces of data/works together and provide enriched data) or other types of data. 
For instance, legal publishers’ databases feature copyrighted works such as author 
commentaries to legislation, original tables, flow  charts and videos, integrated with other 
information sources and data (legislation, caselaw, etc.), including machine generated data, 
in a consistent, original and indivisible way. 
 
At the same time, publishers use third party databases (bibliographic databases, public sector 
databases, etc.) on a daily basis for the purpose of their business and to create their own 
databases, which they do in full respect of IP and contractual rules. For instance, Scopus is an 
abstract and citations database produced by Elsevier since 2004, which uses data in scientific 
publishers’ databases, on the basis of contractual agreements.  
 
 
In your view, how does the Database Directive apply to machine generated data (in  
particular data generated by sensor-equipped objects connected to the Internet-of- things 
objects)? 
 
Publishers consider that the sui generis right may apply to databases containing machine 
generated data. Databases containing machine generated data require much more effort and 
investment to be created and maintained and will likely offer the most valuable kind of service 
to third -party users. For instance, legal publishers offer databases that analyse cases and 
contracts, and link them with existing court cases, laws, analysis and other relevant elements. 
The trends detected through these  links can contribute to predicting possible outcomes for 
future court decisions or underline risks in contracts. These decision-making assistance tools, 
like Dalloz’s  CaseLawAnalytics (demo: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J14n8qW-VAc) 
usually rely on machine-generated data. It is essential that these databases  are covered by 
the sui generis right , to protect the investments of the creators of such databases and allow 
third parties to obtain licences and benefit from these innovative databases.

According to your experience, which of these statements are relevant to your     
activity / protection of your data? 
Publishers use the sui generis right to regulate contractual relationships with clients and to 
fight against infringements, but also use a combination of other contractual and technical 
means. 
To protect their database and regulate access to it, publishers rely on the sui generis right as 
the es sential legal basis to both offer licences to third part ies or prevent third party 
infringements. Contractual clauses and technical measures can provide complementary 
protection, but the sui generis right is the only protection on which all publishers can rely, 
regardless of their size or technical capabilities (which can prevent them from deploying 
technical measures or enforcing contracts). The protection provided by the sui generis right 
furthermore allows flexibility in the access methods to data, in line with different users’ needs 
and technical capabilities. For example, the most common method to access the data of the 
Italian Books in print is via File Transfer Protocol (FtP) service: data are distributed via file and 
usages are regulat ed via licensing agreement. In this context, the sui generis right ensures 
protection against unauthorized data sharing or distribution. Without the sui generis right, 
the Books in Print would be forced to put in place more restrictive access methods and further 
technical measures to control every single aspect of the use of the data, with a higher degree 
of technical complexity and higher costs not affordable for all data users.  
 
To what extent do you agree that there is a need to review the sui generis 
protection for databases provided by the Database Directive, in particular as  
regards the access and sharing of data. 
Publishers strongly disagree with this statement. The sui generis right provided by the 
Database Directive does not need to be re viewed, in particular as regards the  access and 
sharing of data. It is the legal basis on which the business model of many publishers rel ies to 
create valuable databases and  there is a lack of evidence that this right might cause any  
significant difficulties. Database rights already achieve a good balance between the rights and 
the interests of the right -holders and the users, recently updated with new mandatory 
copyright exceptions from the 2019 Copyright Directive and restrictions concerning public 
sector bodies from the 2019 Open Data Directive. Database producers can agree on uses via 
contracts, but they still need a legal framework in cases of infringements, not only to act as a 
deterrent, but also to incentivise  them when it comes to making investments. Thus, a review 
would threaten the investments of publishers and their ability to recoup their costs in creating 
and maintaining these databases. The current sui generis right achieve s its dual purpose of 
protecting database creators while providing a framework for legal access to databases, 
notably through tailored licences, which publishers offer with a wide range of options

depending on the quantity and richness of data requested as well as for the usage purposes  
(e-commerce, consultation, research etc.). 
 
Do you think that it is necessary to clarify the scope of sui generis right provided by   the 
Database Directive in particular in relation to the status of machine generated data? 
 
The scope of the sui generis right does not require any clarification, including with regards to 
machine-generated data, as shown by the lack of significant caselaw or dispute on the matter. 
Machine generated data are already covered by the scope of the sui generis right, when they 
require investments to precisely process data. Such investments may finance high -value 
elements such as specialised  manpower, sophisticated computer or new algorithms. AI and 
machine-related data (automatic translations and summaries, Legal tech and prediction of 
court decisions) requires significant investments, which need to be protected so as to foster 
innovation. The absence of their protection would be completely at odds with the emergence 
of a data-driven economy. 
 
 
Do you think that the Database Directive should provide specific access rules to  ensure 
access to data and prohibit companies from preventing access and extraction through 
contractual and technical measures? 
 
Publishers strongly disagree with this statement. Such an approach would undermine the 
fundamental nature of the sui generis right as an exclusive right, which allows a rightholder 
to freely decide to prohibit access to his content. It is essential that database creators‘ freedom 
of contract remains fully intact, as well as their ability to protect their database from 
infringements through contractual clauses or technical measures. The existing, and recently 
updated, limitations and exceptions to d atabase rights already provide a good balance 
between the rights and the interests of the right -holders and the users. Further limitation to 
it, whether through compulsory licences, new exceptions or other measures  would be 
disproportionate and create significant and unjustified economic harm to database creators.

ZAW position on the European Commission's proposal for a 
Consumer Credit Directive 
 
1. Fundamental remarks 
 
• ZAW generally supports the aim of the Consumer Credit Directive to provide 
consumers with credit-relevant information that enables them to make an 
informed credit decision. At the same time, it is clear that compulsory complex 
financial information provided in a (too) limited space in visual or audiovisual 
media by the advertising companies is counterproductive. The longer and more 
complex the compulsory disclosures, the less likely consumers are to pay attention 
to and understand them. This means that the actual goal - increased transparency 
for potential credit users - is not achieved. 
 
• The consultation of all stakeholders and member states has clearly shown that the 
information consumers receive in advertising needs to be streamlined. The revision 
of the Directive therefore has the explicit aim of "avoiding information overload 
and unnecessary burden" - but the proposals clearly fall short. 
 
• The "standard information in advertising" provided for in Article 8 of the proposed 
Directive - which revises and replaces Article 4 of the Directive - is still clearly too 
far-reaching and thus counterproductive: these compulsory information 
requirements for media advertising must be effectively reduced and streamlined. 
 
• The rules on mandatory information do not make any practicable differentiation 
between the individual advertising media, and the proposed exceptions are 
insufficient. 
 
• As proposed, the mandatory information continues to disproportionately curtail 
the legitimate communication interests of the advertising industry and damages 
the advertising funding necessary for the existence of free and independent media 
without any added informational value for consumers.

2. In detail 
 
a. Article 7 (General principles for marketing and advertising) 
According to the explanatory memorandum of the proposed directive, Article 7 
introduces general principles for marketing and advertising. The current Consumer 
Credit Directive does not have a comparable provision. However, it is not clear 
what added value or function these general principles are supposed to have. From 
our point of view, it is a mere clarification, as the content of the provisions does 
not go beyond what would be required anyway under the UCP Directive, namely 
that commercial communication (about credit agreements) must not be 
misleading. We therefore propose to include a recital with the same content 
instead of Article 7. However, if a different meaning is to be attached to the 
proposal, this would have to be expressed clearly (or more clearly) - at least in the 
explanatory memorandum. 
b. Article 8 (Standard information to be included in advertising) 
The imposition of credit-relevant mandatory information for media advertising, e.g. 
for advertisements in newspapers and magazines or TV and radio spots, meets 
with fundamental concerns of the ZAW. Media advertising serves to make a 
(credit) offer known and, above all, interesting in the first place. Only afterwards do 
customers seek further - contract-deciding - and naturally more complex 
information. The ZAW had already criticised the over-bureaucratisation in the form 
of the "standard information in advertising" in the past, as it disproportionately 
restricts the advertising presentation of credit agreement products in practice: This 
applies in particular to the requirement, which also applies under the new proposal 
for a directive, to already provide for significant parts of the pre-contractual 
information (regulated in Article 10 of the proposal for a directive) - which are 
intended for the information of consumers before or at the conclusion of the 
contract - as mandatory information in the advertising for credit agreement 
products. It should be noted that the information requirements apply to every 
consumer credit up to a credit amount of EUR 100,000 (the "de minimis threshold" 
of EUR 200 does not apply anymore), although customers are granted a fourteen-
day reflection period after conclusion of the contract during which they can 
revoke the agreement (Article 26 of the proposed directive).

Another shortcoming, which the ZAW had always pointed out, concerns the fact 
that no practicable differentiation between the individual advertising media was 
made in the Directive. The proposal for a Directive takes a step in the right 
direction with the new Article 8 (2) sentence 2, according to which in special and 
justified cases, if the medium used for the transmission of the standard information 
does not allow for the visual presentation of the information, certain mandatory 
information is omitted - however, the amendments are clearly too short-sighted: 
The exceptions only concern one form of advertising and only a small and in the 
end not very significant part of the "standard information", namely (1) in the case of 
credit in the form of deferred payment for certain goods or services, the cash 
payment price and the amount of any down payments and (2) where applicable, 
the total amount to be paid by the consumer as well as the amount of the 
instalments. On the other hand, the following shall be indicated unchanged: Fixed 
or variable borrowing rates, together with details of any charges payable by the 
consumer and included in the total cost of the credit, the total amount of credit, 
the annual percentage rate of charge up to and including the duration of the credit 
agreement. 
This means that, for example, the APR must continue to be communicated in in-
store radio in retail shops using a representative example, just as it is in advertising 
flyers of credit institutions, although the advertising media differ significantly from 
each other. 
This is all the more surprising as the extensive stakeholder consultation revealed 
that respondents from all stakeholders and Member States agree that the 
information consumers receive in advertising needs to be streamlined. Consumer 
associations even speak of an existing "information overload".  Thus, the proposed 
amendments in Article 8 (new) of the Directive also pursue the explicit goal of 
"avoiding information overload and unnecessary effort".  This can hardly be 
achieved by waiving one or two mandatory information items of an otherwise 
unchanged catalogue of mandatory information only for a small fraction of media 
advertising in a specific sector, namely primarily radio advertising. Rather, the 
exemption should be extended to other forms of advertising and - wherever 
reasonable and appropriate - also apply to the other elements of the catalogue of

mandatory information. In any case, the permissibility of a "media break" should be 
stipulated in justified cases, i.e. the possibility of a reference to an internet address 
where the mandatory information can be accessed, which should also have a 
positive effect on the level of information in the consumer's decision-making 
process. 
Finally, it should be remembered that compulsory information in media advertising 
always represents a curtailment of the legitimate communication interests of the 
advertising industry, which has serious consequences for the media: State-
imposed compulsory information reduces the space that can be freely created in 
an advertising measure. Advertising thus becomes more expensive for companies, 
as they pay the same price for less freely designable space. This form of advertising 
thus loses its attractiveness for companies, which results in financial losses for the 
media, which depend on refinancing through advertising. As a result, this also 
indirectly affects editorial coverage of financial and consumer topics, which 
contributes significantly to educating and forming consumers' opinions on these 
topics and enabling them to make informed decisions. 
3. Conclusion 
More information does not automatically mean more consumer protection. The 
revised mandatory information requirements continue to disproportionately 
restrict the legitimate communication interests of the advertising industry and 
damage the financing of advertising, which is essential for the existence of free 
and independent media, without adding any informational value for consumers.  
Article 8 should therefore be fundamentally revised once again; in particular, the 
catalogue of mandatory information requirements must be sensibly shortened and 
noticeably streamlined. In addition, a practicable differentiation between the 
individual advertising media must be made.

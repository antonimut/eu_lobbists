Introduction/Executive Summary 
Our digital and physical lives are increasingly linked to the apps, services, and devices we use on a daily 
basis to access a rich set of experiences. This digital transfo rmation, accelerated by  the effect of the  
COVID-19 pandemic and the consequent lockdowns, allows us to interact with hundreds of companies, 
websites, and thousands of other users in the ways that were previously unimaginable. However, 
growing digitalizatio n has al so made digital infrastructures and citizens more exposed to breaches, 
which affects our social, professional, and financial lives.  The pandemic and its inherent effect on the 
growth of our digital workforce, demonstrates the value of secure, remote identification for all to access 
public and private services. 
Microsoft believes that every person has a right to an electronic identity (eID)  that they own and control- 
one that securely stores elements of their identity and preserves p rivacy. We are committe d to 
supporting this effort as part of our ‘Tech Fit 4 Europe’ initiative to help Europe realize its eID and tech-
related aspirations.  We therefore  support the European Commission’s v ision outlined in the 
Communication “Shaping Europe’s Digital Future” that a universally accepted, public eID is necessary 
for citizens to have access to their data and securely use digital products and services.1 In the 2021 
Communication “2030 Digital Compass: the European way for the Digital Decade”, the Commission sets 
the objective that, by 2030, the Union and its citizens should benefit from a wide deployment of a 
trusted, user -controlled identity , allowi ng each user to c ontrol their own online interactions and 
presence – with 100% online provision of key public services and 80% of citizens  using a digital ID 
solution. 
Microsoft supports the European Commission’s ambition to improve the security a nd trust in our 
industry, welcomes the proposal to amend the 2014 E -IDAS regulation (910/2014) , and would like to 
take this opportunity to share our views  in addition to our earlier input in 2020. Our additional 
recommendations are focused on the following four points. 
1. Trust: At Microsoft, we have elected to f ocus on developing tools and services based on open 
standards and, where appropriate, open -source technologies that support the establishment and 
maintenance of the trust framework. We believe that secure and autonomous identity cannot be 
achieved or provided without this common trust framework. We have seen positive reception by 
private enterprises and many nation states, federal, and provincial agencies.

Trust Framework 
Today, tools and technology that finally offer a common framework of trust have begun to emerge. This 
framework can enable the issuance of verifiable credentials by mobile applications, browsers, and other 
online services directly to individuals in a w ay that the individuals autonomously control. Microsoft 
encourages the Commission to use this proposal to support, protect, and establish this new approach 
as the standard for delivering trustworthy identity solutions in the future. 
Microsoft’s mission is to empower every person and every organization on the planet to achieve more. 
Microsoft believes that individuals should truly own their digital identities. And  through ownership, 
individuals should have the freedom to determine which organizations verify, authenticate, and use their 
identities and associated data —other than their own governments as required by law. Therefore, to 
enable this type of individual ow nership, the next generation of E -IDAS should emphasize the 
importance of “autonomous control” of digital identities. And like individuals, organizations—public or 
private—should have the same ability to decide how to process and verify the identities of  the other 
organizations (and employees that represent them) with whom they partner.  
At Microsoft, we have elected to focus on developing tools and services b ased on open standards and 
where appropriate open-source technologies that support the establi shment and maintenance of the 
trust framework. We believe that  secure and autonomous  identity cannot be achieved or provided 
without this common trust framework. We have seen positive reception by private enterprises and many 
nation states, federal, and provincial agencies. We are encouraged by this new way of thinking to 
facilitate exchange of identity attributes around the world.  
Microsoft agrees with the Commission’s call for “[a] more harmonized approach to digital identification 
[to] reduce the risks and costs of the current fragmentation due to the use of divergent national 
solutions [that] will strengthen the Single Market by allowing citize ns, other residents as defined by 
national law and bu sinesses to identify online in a convenient and uniform way across the Union.” 2 
Microsoft also agrees with the Commission that “[e]veryone should be able to securel y access public 
and private services relying on an im proved ecosystem for trust servi ces and on verified proofs of 
identity and attestations of attributes . . . everywhere in the Union.”3  
The Commission’s call for “a common technical architect ure and reference framework and common 
standards to b e developed in collaboration wi th the Member States” is an essential step toward true

ownership of digital identity. However , addition to these requirements, a “trust framework” must be 
decoupled from a technical implementation.  
True ownership and autonomy of digital identities requires a trust framework—namely, a set of broadly 
accepted digital identity standards and governance rules for identity establishment, issuance, and 
verification. The trust f ramework can facilitate the aims of EIDAS  2.0 to supp ort interoperability and 
portability between the technology solutions or services, whether private or public.  
 
Microsoft recommends that the Commission use this opportunity, the  revision of eIDAS Regulation, to 
champion such a trust framework for digital identity. By doing so, the Co mmission can better achieve 
its core goals in addition to allowing more opportunity for new innovators to compete with established 
industry participants and provide best -in-class experiences for end -users and organizations. For  
example, such a technology -agnostic approach would enable an EU citizen to access services 
throughout the Union and around the world, confident that when they present attributes from their 
digital iden tity—e.g., vaccination status or age —that those att ributes will be accepted as val id. In 
contrast, without the abstraction of a trust framework, a verifying organization could use a different 
identity solution. This would result in the need of the EU citiz ens to carry separate and proprietary 
supporting digital identities—exasperating businesses in the EU that are simply trying to accept digital 
identities from anyone from around the world —negating any benefits of portable, digital identity 
solutions. Therefore, an approach that mandates the use of a specif ic technical implementation would 
only reinforce the fragmentation of digital identity schemes that currently exist today, the very outcome 
the Commission seeks to avoid with eIDAS 2.0. 
To conclude, Microsoft believes that decoupling the trust framework from the technical implementation 
can help EU citizens and businesses alike meet the core aims of the GDPR. Current data protection 
regulations assume that credentialing, authentication, a nd verification are  done by third -party 
custodians of an individual’s personal information. Through this decoupling, individuals will have 
tangible control over their own identities, which opens significant and meaningful opportunities to 
enhance privacy and security protections. For example, using cryptography, individuals can tender proof 
of certain f acts about themselves (e.g., their age, employment status, citizenship, etc.) without 
exchanging any of the underlying personal data. The underlying technology offers the irrefutable proof 
of the claims presented by the individual—vouched for by the trusted authenticators. Indeed, by putting 
individuals in direct control of their own data, an E -IDAS 2.0 mandate that includes a trust framework 
for identity can reduce the need for overly restrictive regulation of data transfers, data col lection, and 
data use. 
Interoperability  
The proposal also requires providers of web browsers to facilitate the use of qualified certificates for 
website authentication. On this topic, Microsoft has identified the following recommendations:  
1. Maintain the a bility for our Certificate Consumer root store to independently determine if a 
Trusted Service Provider (TSP) should be a trusted member of our Trusted Root Program which 
establishes the websites, code, emails, and documents trusted by Microsoft.  
2. Ensure TSP ’s meet CA/Browser Forum baseline requirements and are audited against these 
industry standards by established audit schemes (e.g., WebTrust or ETSI) on an annual basis and 
that our Trusted Root Program r etains the ability to remove trust when  TSPs no longer align 
with our interests or are compliant with our program requirements. 
In general, Microsoft is concerned that, as proposed, the use of Attribute Certificates to communicate 
QWACs (“ac-QWACs”) will encounter significant  interoperability concerns with existing software and

Harmonization & Inclusivity 
The proposal rightfully refers to the revised NIS Directive (NIS2) and Cybersecurity Act (CSA) on several 
occasions. We propose to further clarify the roles and coordination betw een the (national) competent 
authorities responsible for compliance of NIS2 and the revised E-IDAS. Regarding the references made 
to the Cybersecurity Act a nd certifications, we invite the Commission to clarify its intentions regarding 
future certifications under the CSA, as the current Union Rolling Work Programme has not identified  a 
scheme for trust services to be developed by ENISA.  
Lastly, as this concerns technical specifications, complex technology, and mandates the Commission to 
use level 2 EU regulation (Implementing and Delegate Acts), Microsoft recommends to define the 
intention to structurally cooperate with private actors in shaping and implementing these policies.

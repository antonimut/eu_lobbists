Onfido response to the European Commission’s proposal introducing 
a trusted and secure European Identity 
 
Introduction 
Onfido is a global remote identity verification provider that partners with 1,600 organisations 
worldwide, including many EU fintechs such as Adyen, Bunq, Getaround, Nickel, Revolut, Wise 
and Monese.  
 
Our leading biometric and AI technology enables clients to p rove that their customers are 
who they claim to be, enabling them to comply with regulatory obligations including AML and 
KYC requirements in the EU. Onfido works by scanning a user’s photo ID to detect whether 
it’s genuine and compares this to a selfie or  a short video taken by the user to check facial 
biometrics match those on the photo ID and ensure the person is the live and genuine owner. 
We use a hybrid model that combines machine learning with human expert oversight, which 
delivers best-in-class speed, consistency and accuracy.   
 
Started just nine years ago, we have scaled rapidly and now employ over 100 people across 
the EU and more than 450 worldwide, with offices in Paris, Lisbon, London and Berlin as well 
as the US, Singapore and India. We are co ntinuing to invest and grow our EU presence to 
meet increasing customer demand in both the fintech space and other verticals.  
 
We are thought leaders in AI bias, ethics, fraud, security and privacy. We were winners of the 
2020 CogX Award  for “Best Innovation in Algorithmic Bias Mitigation” and “Outstanding 
Leader in Accessibility” and were “Highly Commended” in the SC Europe A wards 2020 for 
“Best Use of Machine Learning”. We work with Interpol to develop leading practice in fraud 
prevention and publish a widely-acclaimed annual Fraud Report considering the state of the 
market. Onfido is a founding member of the Better Identity Coalition in the US and a board 
member of the FIDO alliance that is dedicated to best -in-class global authentication 
standards. 
 
We embrace the opportunity to share our expertise and experience  to help develop 
regulatory reforms to foster innovation, acces sibility and financial inclusion for all across 
national borders.  
 
The eID proposal provides an opportunity to put in place a comprehensive, unified, highly 
robust, standardised, and certifiable framework which promotes user convenience, 
inclusiveness, and innovation. 
 
We consider that such a framework, which is driven by strong collaboration with industry and 
has innovation at its heart, is key to the future success of the eIDAS regime. However, to make

Qualified trust services 
 
Option 1: Identify remote identity verification and remote identity authentication as qualified 
trust services 
The inclusion of electronic archiving services, electronic ledgers and the management of 
remote electronic signature and seal creation devices as qualified trust services is a positive 
first step in expanding the current list and thus facilitating greater innovation. Nevertheless, 
we believe that the list should be expand ed further, establishing both remote identity 
verification services and remote identity authentication services as qualified trust services. 
This would allow private sector providers of these services to operate across the EU without 
facing the current fragmented regulatory framework. 
 
Remote identity verification and remote identity authentication checks (e.g. biometrics + ID 
doc + database checks) are comprehensive methods of establishing trust, which establish that 
the user requesting the service: 
 
● is tr uly there without the need of a witness (with further advances, this will also 
capture if the person is being forced); 
● is a genuine user with non-fraudulent documents; and 
● is not a politically exposed/sanctioned individual. 
 
These are key factors in facilitating compliance with “know your customer” and associated 
AML regulatory requirements. Furthermore, remote identity verification and remote identity 
authentication has the flexibility to add additional safeguards that will further improve 
confidence that trust is being established with the correct individual, allowing these methods 
to adapt to stay ahead of bad actors. 
 
Therefore, adding remote identity identification and remote identity authentication to the 
list of Qualified Trust Services would enable public and private entities in the EU an additional 
high-quality method to establish qualified trust with individuals, which achieves a high 
assurance about the individual in a secure, reliable, convenient and fast manner.  
 
Remote identity verification is a key enabler for the financial services and tech ecosystems in 
Europe. It is a critical and foundational piece of the infrastructure required to complete the 
EU’s vision of a Digital Single Market and Capital Markets Union. By verifying that a customer

is who they say they are online as part of the onboarding process, European businesses from

Examples for the use of remote identity verification include opening bank accounts, buying

The pandemic in particular has created further urgency for the proper regulation of such 
services. For instance, obliged entities under EU AML rules have a set of customer due 
diligence responsibilities to uphold. Without a proper framework on th e use of remote 
identity verification and authentication solutions, they were unable to do so as efficiently as 
they should during the pandemic. The lack of an EU -level certification of such services also 
hindered the ability of obliged entities to use ser vices across EU borders, given the 
fragmented rules and oversight over such services between Member States. This problem has 
already been identified by the EU in both the Commission’s ROFIEG report and the eID and 
KYC Expert Group report, where it was recognised that national regulatory bodies across the 
EU have different standards with regards to the compli ance of technical solutions to digital 
identity verification.  
 
Although the new European electronic IDs and the Digital Identity Wallets may play a 
significant role in alleviating current concerns, the purpose of the new framework is not to 
replace physical ID cards completely, but rather give the option for citizens to use a digital ID 
instead of a physical one. Therefore, physical IDs and the relevant onboarding processes for 
citizens that opt to keep them will remain highly relevant for the foreseeable future. The 
proposal should therefore take this into account and thus make sure to also improve the 
legislative framework for those that opt to keep their physical IDs. More specifically, such 
citizens should be able to use remote identity verification sol utions alongside their physical 
ID to verify their identity in non-face-to-face settings.  
 
Option 2: Mutual recognition of Article 24.1(d) “equivalent assurance” 
Another option to achieve the same goal would be to establish mutual recognition of 
confirmed equivalent assurance under Article 24.1(d). This Article currently sets out that: 
 
“The information referred to in the first subparagraph shall be verified by the qualified 
trust service provider either directly or by relying on a third party in accordanc e with 
national law:

(d) by using other identification methods recognised at national level which provide 
equivalent assurance in terms of reliability to physical presence. The equivalent 
assurance shall be confirmed by a conformity assessment body.”  
 
Although relevant ETSI standards (ETSI TS 119 461) do provide guidance on the requirements 
which need to be demonstrated by Identity Proofing Ser vice Providers (IPSPs) to provide for 
equivalent assurance, which can be confirmed by conformity assessment bodies, potentially 
different interpretations of said standards could lead to a fragmented approach to the ability 
of IPSPs to provide their services.  
 
A mutual recognition regime for equivalent assurance should therefore be introduced. Such 
a regime should explicitly allow for IPSPs to passport this equivalent assurance. This would 
allow IPSPs to provide their services across the EU without having t o engage a conformity 
assessment body in each Member State and undergo multiple conformity assessments which 
is highly inefficient and adds no value. Such a regime should make it clear that Member States 
must allow such passporting in order to give the ind ustry the necessary certainty and 
confidence that this one -stop shop approach will be accepted and indeed welcomed in all 
countries.  
 
Option 3: IPSP criteria to be uniform across the EU through clear reference to ETSI standards 
for identity proofing in the eID Regulation 
If remote identity verification and authentication providers are not recognised as qualified 
trust service providers and remain categorised as identity proofing service providers, a more 
uniform implementation of standards required by such providers is necessary across the EU. 
A formal reference to the recent standards published by ETSI policy requirements for identity 
proofing ( ETSI TS 119 461 ) should be included in the Regulation to harmonise the 
interpretation of these rules across borders, and therefore facilitate the use of remote 
identity proofing services. 
 
This can be achieved through the revision of Article 24.1 of the eIDAS Regulation (910/2014), 
adding a new subparagraph (d) as set out below: 
 
“by means of remote identity proofing in accordance with sections 9.2.2 and 9.2.3 of 
ETSI Technical Specification on policy requirements for identity proofing (ETSI TS 119 
461); or”

Levels of Assurance certification for trust service providers and identity proofing service 
providers 
It is critical that the European framework for trust service providers promotes investment in 
innovative solutions. To that end we consider that it s hould promote the use of industry -
driven standards which are flexible and outcomes-based.  
 
To achieve this, the new eID Regulation should establish a Levels of Assurance framework for 
all qualified trust services and identity proofing services including t hose that are not 
necessarily a part of a  national electronic identification (eID) framework. Such a framework 
would be a voluntary code of conduct for businesses. Businesses that choose to uphold the 
highest standards would be able to be certified as serv ice providers with a ‘high’ Level of 
Assurance. The regime would effectively increase transparency for the users of such services, 
funnel demand to providers with the highest levels of assurance and therefore increase the 
incentive to raise standards generally across the industry.  
 
 
Electronic Identification and European Digital Identity Wallets 
 
The Commission has worked hard to create the new eID framework. We believe that for the 
framework to be effective it must as a minimum: 
- be easy to use for citizens of all ages as well as for businesses; and 
- provide for security and appropriate protection against misuse of data 
 
Usability 
Given the voluntary nature of the proposed tools, citizens will only opt to pick up digital 
identity wallets if they are as easy to use, and to encourage mass adoption they will need to 
be easier to use than physical IDs. The user experience (UX) therefore needs to be at the heart 
of the thinking when developing national European Digital Identity Wallets.  
 
Under the proposed rules the acceptance of wallets will be mandatory for a large array of 
businesses for identification and authentication purposes. Such businesses would have a 
disproportionate increase in administrative costs if they suddenly had to acc ept 27 different 
wallets for such purposes. In essence, significant divergence between different national 
wallets will also be detrimental on the usability of wallets for business purposes. That is why 
we are encouraged by the fact that Member States will spend the next 12 months formulating 
this common technical architecture for European Digital Identity Wallets. It will be critical for 
this architecture to facilitate the maximum level of harmonisation across national wallets by 
setting standards that are common across countries and that incentivise a healthy range of 
different industry providers to engage in the process

Security 
We consider that it is important to recognise that biometric authentication provides a very 
high degree of security. It should therefore be considered the starting point for discussions 
on security for the proposals and governments should have to include a biometric 
authentication mechanism in their wallets. Such an authentication mechanism would make 
digital IDs significantly mor e secure when compared to existing physical ones and would 
therefore incentivise citizens to transition to the new ‘trusted and secure European identity’ 
at a much faster pace.  
 
This can be achieved through the broadening of the current Levels of Assuranc e for 
authentication in electronic identification means. The regime currently requires ‘dynamic 
authentication’, (which under the Commission’s guidance is implemented by an 
authentication factor (e.g. a one time key from a device) or by an authentication mechanism 
(e.g. dynamic challenge in a challenge -response authentication)) for the authentication to 
have a ‘high’ level of assurance. This should be relaxed to allow for other secure 
authentication methods such as biometric authentication. This would be in  line with the 
Commission’s aim for technological neutrality, which the current regime is too prescriptive to 
support. 
 
Separately, this could be achieved through the technical architecture for European Digital 
Identity Wallets that will be drafted by Memb er States over the next year. The technical 
architecture should clearly include the need for a form of biometric authentication whenever 
wallet holders choose to use their wallets.

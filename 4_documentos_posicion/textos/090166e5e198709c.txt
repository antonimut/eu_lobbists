Feedback on the Commission’s EU Digital Identity Proposal  
We welcome the opportunity to provide feedback on the Proposal for a Regulation of the European 
Parliament and of the Council amending Regulation (EU) No 910/2014 regarding the establishment 
of a framework for a European digital identity (“EU Digital Identity Proposal”). We understand 
that the EU Digital Identity Proposal follows on from the European Council Conclusions which 
called “for the development of an EU-wide framework for secure public electronic identification 
(e-ID), including interoperable digital signatures, to provide people with control over their online 
identity and data as well as to enable access to public, private and cross-border digital services”.  1
Security, privacy and user experience are of utmost importance for any initiative to digitise identity 
credentials, and it is imperative to protect an individual’s identity to ensure broad adoption and 
usage of digital identities. Apple has designed a technical architecture whereby users can easily 
add, view and manage their identity cards. The Apple Wallet application takes full advantage of the 
privacy and security built into iPhone and Apple Watch. Users’ identity data is encrypted and 
protected against tampering and theft. Biometric authentication using Face ID and Touch ID 
ensures that only the person who added the ID to their device can view or present their ID or 
driver’s license in the Apple Wallet application.  
The European Institutions are in a unique position to provide leadership across Member States and 
to develop a framework based on common international standards and approaches for digital 
identities, avoiding a fragmented country-by-country approach which fails to account for the 
mobility of European users and cross-border service offerings. We therefore support the 
development of an EU-wide framework for digital identities and the objective of providing EU 
citizens with control over their online identity and data. We further welcome the stated goal of 
developing a harmonised approach for a digital identity usable across the EU to reduce 
fragmentation from divergent national solutions. We strongly believe that the European Institutions 
should work towards the adoption of common international standards for digital identities to meet 
this objective.  
We see several opportunities for improvement in the context of the existing EU Digital Identity 
Proposal. In this document we have laid out our key observations which we are happy to elaborate 
upon further during technical and implementation discussions (e.g., as part of the development of 
the Toolbox) in cooperation with the relevant European Institutions, and which we strongly believe 
the EU Digital Identity Framework should take into account. These observations concern several 
essential topics, including:
•data protection and data minimisation requirements;
•EU Digital Wallet acceptance requirements for private parties, including eligibility criteria; 
•IP rights holder consultation and engagement; and
•security certiﬁcation requirements and requirements for qualiﬁed certiﬁcates for website 
authentication.
It is critical, in our view, that the EU Digital Identity Framework does not undermine security and 
privacy for users and does not create avenues for malicious actors to target or compromise 
sensitive consumer data, posing significant risk of harm to users.

The Commission recommends that Member States develop a Toolbox for a coordinated approach 
towards a “EU Digital Identity Framework" and that this Toolbox will include a comprehensive 
technical architecture and reference framework, a set of common standards and technical

We support the objective of ensuring a common approach and technical architecture for EU Digital 
Identity Wallets, and we urge the relevant European Institutions to push for the adoption of 
international standards, specifically, ISO 18013-5 (Mobile driving licence or “mDL”) and 23220 
(eID) (in the following referred to as “ISO Standards”) as common standards for the EU Digital 
Identity Framework and Toolbox. There are significant important benefits that derive from an 
adoption of the ISO Standards as common standards for the EU Digital Identity Framework and 
Toolbox. These include the following: 
•Standardisation and acceptance: Adopting the ISO Standards will greatly facilitate the 
establishment of a standardised identity payload for relying parties to process. This is critical to 
achieve the stated objective of broad acceptance of digital identities by users and relying 
parties across Member States. The adoption of the ISO Standards would ensure ease of 
integration for digital relying parties via a single integration (based on a common API standard) 
to enable the acceptance of digital identities issued by different Member States. ISO Standards 
also support multiple methods for physical acceptance (including NFC and QR Code for secure 
device engagement and BLE, NFC and Wifi Aware for data retrieval).  
•Identity proof and authentication: The ISO Standards can also ensure that the EU Digital 
Identity Framework meets sufficient levels of assurance to authenticate identities, enabling 
proof of a real identity and proof of a citizen’s ownership of that real identity.  
•Governance: The ISO Standards also support a certification process to authenticate relying 
parties, which is key to ensure and enhance trust in the EU Digital Identity Framework. The ISO 
Standards further provide a process to manage and distribute Member State keys to relying 
parties for the authentication of identity credentials.  
•Other related identity attributes: The EU Digital Identity Proposal seeks to establish an 
infrastructure that enables the sharing of attributes and documents related to an individual’s 
identity (e.g., work status, diploma, etc.). It is possible to leverage existing ISO specifications 
for additional document types to support this vision. In addition, the ISO Standards support 
future customer experiences and the European Institutions can leverage the ISO 
standardisation work that has already been undertaken to meet the vision and requirements for 
the EU Digital Identity Framework.  
2.Data protection and data minimisation  
As would be expected, the Commission is rightly placing data protection at the core of the EU 
Digital Identity Framework to ensure the offering of user-centric, secure and privacy friendly digital 
identity solutions. 
We support the Commission’s data protection objectives, and strongly believe that the use and 
processing of personal data must be underpinned by strong privacy and security protections. In 
order to meet this objective, we believe that it is critical that the EU Digital Identity Framework (i) 
does not cause additional identity usage data to be seen, accessible or collected by Member 
States or other authorities that would not be currently collected in a non-digital context, and (ii) no 
mandated intermediary must be interposed between the data being shared directly from the 
citizen’s electronic device to the relevant relying party.  
We would welcome clarification from the relevant European Institutions regarding the 
Commission’s vision for how the protection of identity data, including from third-party access, will 
be ensured, and how compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (“GDPR”) will be 
achieved. In order to foster and maintain user trust, it is necessary, in our view, that the EU Digital 
Identity Framework ensures that third-party access to data is not mandated, that it is minimised

from a technical perspective, and that — unless there is a compelling reason otherwise — identity 
data is only shared directly between the user’s electronic device and the relevant relying party. 
Data must also not be tracked across relying parties. This should be underpinned by strong guard 
rails around data minimisation, requiring only the collection of the absolute minimum amount of 
identity data that is needed in the specific instance to deliver an individual element of the identity 
service. For example, if a particular service requires the relying party to verify that a user is over 
the age of 18, only that piece of identity data should be shared, rather than any additional identity 
elements such as the user’s date of birth. We believe that an adoption of the ISO Standards will 
help to achieve this objective, and that it is critical for the relevant European Institutions to ensure 
that the European Digital Identity Framework sets out minimum criteria to meet the requirements 
for data protection mandated by the GDPR.  
3.Interplay with the Digital Markets Act  
The Commission has proposed that the EU Digital Identity Framework should build on the Digital 
Markets Act to ensure that business users and providers of ancillary services have access to 
certain hardware and software features, and interoperate with them through the EU Digital Identity 
Wallets or Member States’ notified electronic identification means.  
The Digital Markets Act Proposal is still being reviewed by the co-legislators, and has yet to be 
adopted, interpreted or applied by the European Institutions and courts. Against this background, 
the EU Digital Identity Proposal should not anticipate or prejudge the interpretation and application 
of draft provisions in the Digital Markets Act Proposal.  
In any event, we believe that any draft proposals for access in the EU Digital Identity Framework to 
hardware or software of private companies should be analysed carefully in close consultation with 
all relevant stakeholders, to ensure that proposed measures are proportionate, protect intellectual 
property rights, and do not detrimentally affect privacy and security features or reduce competition 
or innovation between different technical architectures.   
4.Exclusion of user confirmation or simple log-in mechanisms 
The eIDAS Regulation introduced the mutual recognition of electronic IDs and electronic trust 
services (including e-signatures, electronic seals, electronic time stamps and website 
authentication). The eIDAS Regulation does not apply to user confirmation or log-in mechanisms 
(such as Facebook’s or Google’s account log-in, or Sign in with Apple) which are currently used to 
access private online services. The Commission rightly points out that these user confirmation or 
log-in mechanisms are self-asserted and do not offer a link to trusted and secure government 
electronic IDs.  2
The currently proposed definitions of “identification” and “authentication” refer to the electronic 
identification of a natural or legal person, or the origin and integrity of data in electronic form (see 
Article 3(2) and (5) of the eIDAS Regulation), meaning that user confirmation or log-in mechanisms 
for access to private online services are out of scope of the eIDAS Regulation.   
However, in light of the expansive objectives of the EU Digital Identity Proposal, and the aim that 
EU Digital Identity Wallets be used digitally with very large online platforms, the current definitions 
of “identification” and “authentication” could also be interpreted as covering user confirmations or 
log-ins to private services (e.g., social networks and e-commerce services more generally). This 
would appear to go beyond the objectives of the European Council Conclusions, and would 
introduce a significant burden on private organisations which do not offer mechanisms that link to

5.Optional acceptance of EU Digital Identity Wallets and eligibility criteria 
The Commission is proposing that private relying parties providing services in numerous sectors 
(e.g., transport, energy, banking and financial services, social security, health, drinking water, 
postal services, digital infrastructure, education or telecommunications) should be obliged to 
accept the use of EU Digital Identity Wallets for the provision of services where strong user 
authentication for online identification is required by national or European Union law or by 
contractual obligation.  
While we support the wider usage and acceptance of EU Digital Identity Wallets, we believe the 
Commission’s proposal imposes significant costs and efforts on private parties, by obliging 
organisations operating in the EU to integrate with and provide ongoing support for EU Digital 
Identity Wallets across all Member States. Furthermore, the EU Digital Identity Proposal, as 
currently envisaged, will create a guaranteed, mandatory cohort of both public and private relying 
parties that are mandated to support EU Digital Identity Wallets offered by Member States as well 
as Member State-sponsored digital identity providers. This will disadvantage companies (including 
start-ups and small developers) offering alternative digital identity solutions, distort competition 
and stifle innovation, in potential conflict with EU competition policy.  3
We strongly believe that acceptance by private relying parties should be optional, and that the 
European Institutions should encourage Member States to offer digital solutions that are attractive 
and compelling enough for users to adopt them. We are also concerned that the Commission will 
have the discretion to extend the list of private online service providers that could be mandated to 
accept the use of EU Digital Identity Wallets in the future, thereby creating significant uncertainty 
across the entire industry.  
We would therefore welcome changes to the EU Digital Identity Proposal to state that private 
relying parties (including online platforms, for the reasons set out in Section 4 above) are not under 
an obligation to accept EU Digital Identity Wallets, and that such acceptance is instead made 
optional.

credentials. As stated above, we strongly believe that the EU Digital Identity Framework should be 
based on the ISO Standards.

7.Security Certification  
We welcome the approach taken by the Commission to ensure that conformity of the EU Digital 
Identity Wallets with the requirements under the eIDAS Regulation should be certified by 
accredited public or private sector bodies. We believe that an approach whereby individual 
Member States designate their own public or private sector certification bodies is likely to result in 
fragmentation if they use divergent standards or security evaluation assurance levels.  
In order to ensure a high level of harmonisation, certification should be based on commonly agreed 
standards and mutual acceptance. As such, we would suggest to the European Institutions to 
recommend the adoption of Common Criteria certification as a basis for security certification of EU 
Digital Identity Wallets. In order to ensure that the security evaluation is proportionate in the 
context of the solution, the European Institutions should not prescribe security evaluation 
assurance levels, but should instead recommend high level security standards in the context of the 
EU Digital Identity Proposal. 
8.Requirements for qualified certificates for website authentication  
The Commission has proposed that Qualified Website Authentication Certificates (root 
certificates) must be automatically recognised by web-browsers (which are also required to ensure 
support and interoperability with such root certificates). This approach creates significant security 
risks to users for the following reasons:  
•Each Member State will adjudicate the standard and establish its own qualified certificate 
authority, introducing fragmentation and many points of potential failure. 
•Browsers will lack transparency into what standard a certificate authority has met and will lack 
recourse if a Trust Service Provider (“TSP”) is found to be acting maliciously. This would set a 
problematic global precedent and eliminates the ability of browsers to push back on attacks on 
the integrity of Transport Layer Security (“TLS”).  
•The proposed changes to the eIDAS Regulation are silent on whether any corresponding 
standards will be created to meet baseline requirements, or whether baseline requirements will 
be maintained and improved. 
•Users do not interact with Extended Validation (“EV”) TLS certificates which is why browsers 
generally moved the EV information out of the address bar (although users can still click on the 
lock icon to see it). Qualified Website Authentication Certificates do not appear to have any 
additional benefit to users over EV TLS certificates. Additionally, there are many examples 
showing that the organisational identity information provided in a certificate may be 
misleading. 
In addition, from a product and user safety perspective, the following considerations should be 
taken into account: 
•The creation of user displays on devices as small as a smartwatch is technically challenging. 
Imposing additional constraints, without data-backed justification for doing so, is detrimental 
to producers’ ability to innovate and improve. 
5
•The best protections for users derive from highlighting danger, rather than safety. Users 
presume safety, and therefore indicating safety does not statistically change users’ decision-
making. Highlighting instead those sites which are not secure fosters the objective to ensure 
effective user protection. 
We strongly believe that the current proposals will not only undermine the security of browsers, 
but are in fact contrary to how root programs operate, and will create a problematic global 
precedent that eliminates the ability of browsers to repel malicious attacks. Consequently, we 
would welcome it if the European Institutions considered withdrawing these proposals for 
automatic recognition of and interoperability with Qualified Website Authentication Certificates.  
Conclusion  
The European Institutions have an opportunity to deliver on innovation and broad user adoption of 
electronic IDs and EU Digital Identity Wallets, including on a cross-border basis. In our view, the 
success of these projects will derive from (i) the reliance on international technical standards 
which are interoperable and facilitate integration, (ii) a strong focus on security, data protection 
and user experience as well as (iii) effective and collaborative engagement with industry 
stakeholders.  
We look forward to continuing our engagement with the European Commission, the European 
Parliament and the Member States in this context, including regarding the development of the 
Toolbox.

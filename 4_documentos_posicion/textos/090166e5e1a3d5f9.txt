ASSURALIA’s additional comments on 
the EC consultation on the 
 
DATA ACT 
 
 
 
This paper provides additional comments by the Belgian Insurance Market on the European 
Commission’s public consultation on the upcoming legislative initiative on the Data Act.  
 
I. Business-to-government data sharing for the public interest 
 
Belgian insurance companies share data with the government for different purposes, e.g., 
proof of insurance with police services, ‘Ultimate beneficial owners ’ with the tax 
administration, employee’s pension benefits with the pension’s administration.  
 
There are some technical difficulties related to the lack of interoperability, data quality, and 
access to data on a structured format.  
 
On requests for access to data, there are some legal barriers. For instance, insurers are not 
allowed to use the single national identification number database (numéro de registre 
national) to identify their client (identity and address), while this could eliminate the risk of 
confusion between two people who have the same surname and the same date of birth, 
notably in the context of preventing money-laundering. Also, a recent decision of the Belgian 
national data protection authority (23 June 2020) prohibits insurers access to the vehicle’s 
technical information database (Banque carrefour des vehicles), while this information allows 
for the correct identification of the vehicle and therefore prevents fra ud and potential 
overestimation of the premium and therefore contributes to the general consumer protection 
objective. 
 
When drafting a business to government data sharing potential legislation, the main first step 
will be to clearly delimit public interest: what is the goal to reach the societal objective? 
 
Any potential compulsory data sharing initiative must be very clearly framed i.e. what data 
(e.g. only aggregated data, no proprietary data generated, analysed and interpreted, 
internally modelled, enriched) to be shared by whom, with whom and for what purpose. 
 
Insurance companies’ data is an extremely valuable asset, in particular when enriched. 
Therefore, data sharing cannot simply become the rule, it must be assessed case by case, on 
a voluntary basis.

Insurance companies are currently subject to extensive reporting obligations. Additional 
obligations, if not proportionate and legitimate, could lead to the disclosure of core business 
strategies and discourage investments in innovation. 
 
This being said, i nsurer’s societal role being paramount, the sector agrees that, in case of 
extreme conditions (e.g., floods), no information sharing can be denied. We also understand 
that only a global data sharing by the industry can be efficient for some public policies, such 
as preventive ones, while only partial data from only a few companies willing to share their 
data on a voluntary basis would not be efficient.  
 
Sharing data at market price seems a fair solution since data itself is very costly for the 
insurance companies (they have to be enriched and secured). Moreover, on an IT perspective, 
data sharing infrastructures are very time spending to implement and to manage. Therefore, 
it is paramount to, at least, ensure a fair allocation of costs between parties. 
Companies’ strategies and data policies can greatly vary. A one -size-fits-all approach is 
therefore unlikely to capture all nuances. A combination of incentives has the highest chance 
of success. 
Having said that, public authorities should be able to obtain health data or insurance claims 
data at a preferential rate for prevention purposes.  
 
While specific rules on proportionality would only be necessary for mandatory B2G data 
sharing, they would then play an important role in ensuring that companies are not 
overburdened by data access requests. 
Questions remain on whether these datasets will be made publicly availab le to market 
participants. B2G data sharing must not alter the competitive landscape or provide additional 
benefits to certain players by forcing incumbent companies to share proprietary information 
accumulated over decades. 
Another important element to consider is reciprocity. 
 
Finally, it is important to ensure consistency between the upcoming legislation on the Data 
Act and existing legislation.  
 
 
II. Business-to-business data sharing 
 
The insurance industry shares data intra -industry (e.g. for the purpose of reinsurance or 
industry-wide statistics , between companies  for instance individual claim history when 
customers switch insurance company , with brokers) , with external stakeholders across all 
business lines (e.g. loss data with surveyors, repair centres, health professionals, government 
agencies; employees benefits with employers ), with third -parties in the context of 
partnerships for innovative services (e .g. with car manufacturer or tech companies for 
insurance telematics, ) or training of algorithms (e.g. fraud, predictive maintenance) and with 
the national insurance association. 
 
Some insurance companies are developing for instance telematic motor insurance which offer 
consumers the possibility to customise their insurance policy according to their driving habits. 
“Pay as you drive”: discount on the premium, depending on how you drive, and insights in 
driving behaviour to help to improve over time. “Pay w hen you drive”: insurance payment 
based on the mileage actually driven. It strongly benefits consumers by rewarding low -risk 
drivers with lower premiums, it improves road safety and allow inclusion of young drivers . 
 
 
 
 
 
Partnerships with other sectors  also exist,  such as, in Belgium , AssurPharma which is a 
partnership between insurers and pharmacists to facilitate the (additional) compensation of 
medication by private medical insurers (certificate sent digitally and securely from the 
pharmacy to the in surer, no need to send paper by the post, which accelerates the 
compensation).  
An example within the Belgian insurance sector  is the project Proof of insurance which is a n 
ongoing sectoral initiative to fully digitalise the motor liability insurance certificate (a database 
for each vehicle insurer’s identity, no need to have the paper certificate in the vehicle  which 
facilitates the proof of insurance by the driver and control by the public authorities  and 
therefore e nhances road safety). Claims database ( Databank schadegevallen)  is another 
recent sectoral initiative to fight organized fraud (a database for all motor claims, identification 
of fraudulent claims by flagging unusual patterns that a human might miss, reduction of huge 
costs related to fraud leads to reduction of premiums). 
 
Data exchanges with other companies also aim to simplify processes for customers by asking 
them less information for claims management for instance. 
 
Data is also exchanged for risk modelling. 
 
This being said, personal data is shared only with the c ustomer consent and only on an 
aggregated way. 
 
More B2B data sharing has the potential to improve  insurers’ understanding of risks  (better 
products for emerging risks), improve costumer experience, as well as support fraud detection 
activities (e.g. by training more efficiently AI algorithms). 
 
Nevertheless, insurers face multiple challenges when requesting access to other companies’ 
data. The most common hurdles are legal (GDPR, e-Privacy, SII non-insurance activities) and 
competition (level playing field, market power of BigTech) . There are also concerns on how 
the data will be used (for instance, will it impact my credit score). Also important are technical 
difficulties (interoperability, formats, standardisation) as difficulties on how you draft written 
and readable data accessible for other parties. 
 
The data act should strengthen the framework  conditions for data partnerships  but should 
ensure that companies have enough contractual flexibility. 
Benefits of data sharing could be that data monopoly would be broken.  
The main risk is that wrong interpretation of data could lead to B2B discrimination of 
companies as wrong assumptions can be made with incomplete data. 
 
 
III. Tools for data sharing: smart contracts 
 
Some of our members report that they are using smart contracts. 
 
Smart contract technology can be an effective tool , although it is not the only solution to  
ensure the sharing of data generated by IoT. Any chosen tool should be technological-neutral. 
When implementing smart contract solutions, it  is important to provide for an owner of the 
grid so that liability issues can be addressed.

Despite significant efforts to modernise privacy rules, the GDPR a nd the guidelines adopted 
by the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) are not entirely innovation-friendly or fit for a 
digital age. For instance, the use of blockchain technology in insurance could be jeopardised 
due to potential incompatibilities with the GDPR. 
 
Data standardization and the technical interoperability of different blockchain technologies 
are key to support the use of smart contracts. Given the early stage of developments, it is 
difficult to advocate for certain standards. This being said,  we are of the view that industry 
bodies are best placed to take the lead on standardization, if need be. 
 
 
IV. Clarifying rights on non -personal Internet -of-Things data 
stemming from professional use 
 
Insurers offer services/products based on objects connecting to the IoT. 
 
As a main principle, we believe that individuals should be in control of their data. For example, 
if a customer decides that an insurance undertaking may access their driving data, the vehicle 
manufacturer should grant access on reasonable terms. 
 
The after -market for cars is a considerable economic factor with providers from different 
industries competing to offer products and services . Therefore, the lack of non-personal IoT 
data sharing may prevent the industry to develop innovative insurance products/services (e.g. 
predictive maintenance of smart factories). Furthermore, manufacturers could develop 
applications linked to insurance. 
 
 
V. Improving portability for business users of cloud services 
 
The Belgian Insurance sector considers that there is a need to establish a right to portability 
for business users of cloud computing services in EU legislation. 
 
Negotiation of contracts with cloud providers sometimes is difficult and very time -consuming 
but too detailed requirements could be burdensome to negotiate and apply in practice. 
 
It is important that any approach taken envisages appropriate consultation with stakeholders.

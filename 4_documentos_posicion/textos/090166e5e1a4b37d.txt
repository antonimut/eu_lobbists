THE EUROPEAN FARMING COMMUNITY’S PERSPECTIVE ON THE 
DATA GOVERNANCE ACT 
The use of new digital technologies in the agricultural sector is becoming increasingly important 
and affects all players. In order to reach the full potential of data use and to generate value, more 
data needs to be made available, shared with confidence and be technically fit for re-use. Trusted 
and reliable analytics and artificial intelligence — key ingredients for transparent, evidence-
based policy-making— require findable, accessible, interoperable, secure and high-quality data.  
However, the agricultural and agri-food sector in Europe is characterised by a very large number 
of small and medium-sized enterprises (there are over 10 million farms in Europe), the 
majority of which do not store the data themselves but entrust it to their providers 
and suppliers1. 
The Data Governance Act is generally in line with the farming community’s expectations. Even 
though the communication mentions that it was built on the experience of the code of conduct, 
farmers’ consent is not the focal point of the Data Governance Act.  
The digital identity of farms and consent are essential to be able to create a climate of trust and 
transparency among suppliers through to consumers. Accountability, responsibility, and 
consistency of consent protocols are additional pillars that must be considered. There is an 
imbalance of power between the different players involved in digital agriculture. The expertise 
that major players and companies have acquired is disproportionate to the expertise that a 
farmer is able to develop on their farm. This implies major knowledge and power asymmetries. 
The farmer's expression of consent to the use of data from their farm, taking into account the 
type of data, the purposes, the actors involved in the process, the portability of the data, and the 
possibility to revoke their consent at a later stage, is an essential right for farmers. It will help to 
abate their fears and reluctance to share their farm data. In addition, allowing farmers to 
associate the features of data-driven agriculture with positive impacts that benefit their 
enterprise is key. The signatories of the European code of conduct on agricultural data sharing, 
who are the main organisations representing our sector, agree on these principles. Priority 
should be given to a consent model among the stakeholders, one that is driven and coordinated 
by the farming community at EU level and farm associations (MS Level). 
Without this prerequisite, which is mandatory to create a climate of trust, we fear that this will 
lead to distrust among farmers on the subject and a refusal to share data. It will induce a 
valuation to the detriment of or without return for the farmers, at a time when they are widely 
recognised to be experiencing income problems.  
The farming community supports the introduction of a clause making the informed and explicit 
consent of the holders of non-personal data mandatory before any data exchange. Data 
generated and collected on-farm during farming operations should be attributed to the farmer. 
This ‘data sovereign farmer’ should control access and use of all data and particular data relating 
to their farming operation through an accessible and easy-to-use consent mechanism. This 
builds on the European code of conduct on data sharing. The tools to manage and monitor 
farmer consent should swiftly be made available. 
Data-altruism is an interesting concept for valuing data to everyone’s benefit; we welcome its 
implementation in this text. On the other hand, it raises the question of properly defining the 
concept of altruism in matters relating to data. The protection of people and private and 
professional property must remain a priority as certain experiences have shown the difficulty of

intervening afterwards. The public sector also holds vast amounts of data that cannot be made 
available as open data because they include information on individuals or company information 
(e.g. health data, information on financial systems). 
Previous experiences have shown the damage caused by opening certain public databases. 
Several anti-farming groups have misused this data on farms and sometimes seriously damaged 
the farms targeted. The challenge lies in finding a way to extract knowledge from the data, while 
fully preserving privacy or other rights that may be attached to the data. 
It is essential to take these possibilities into account in the regulation that is under discussion. In 
addition to carefully analysing the data collection purposes, which must not harm the persons 
concerned by this data, altruistic users of data must be considered fully responsible and 
accountable for the use they make of this data and assume responsibility of any damage to 
property and people (e.g. business losses, reputational damage to the individual and their farm) 
likely to result from their actions. If farmers can be penalised for matters related to recording 
and managing data then so should those entities governing any open data space. 
Farming practices that are legal and compliant run the risk of being exploited by anti-agriculture 
activists whose political agenda is to distort the public perception of farming. The risks of 
sharing data should be explicitly detailed in any consent process that involves opting in to data 
being shared and exchanged on-farm. 
The current wording of the draft regulation defines and only deals with "data sharing" and 
makes no reference to "data exchange". Such an omission would effectively exclude a large 
number of private sector companies from the scope of the future regulation. The term "data 
sharing" is generally associated with open sharing of data, often as part of initiatives for the 
common good, while the term "data exchange" is generally associated with the exchange of 
commercial data, especially between organisations.  
In Europe, the term "data exchange" is also increasingly used at policy and regulatory level to 
refer to data exchange processes and the markets in which they take place, as is the case for 
some adopted directives/European recommendations (e.g. exchange of electronic medical 
records) as well as for projects funded by Europe under the H2020 program (e.g. CUREX, 
Infinitech, CyberSec4Europe, to name a few). 
As mentioned previously, data from farms is not stored on-farm. Data exchange, which allows 
data to be transmitted from farms to their partners, is therefore mainly used in the agricultural 
sector, given the fact that the ten million farms must exchange and forward their data to 
upstream partners (agri-suppliers, cooperatives, traders, advisers, etc.) to benefit from 
innovative services; or to the supply chains and end consumers for traceability purposes.  
Finally, it is important to stress the central role that farmer associations and agri-cooperatives 
play in data collection, brokerage, training, awareness and governance, as well their potential 
role as ‘data sharing intermediaries’ if farmer members so wish. 
This regulation should make it possible to secure farms and restore a balance between farmers 
(data transmitters) and their partners (data users), while controlling the use of their data, in 
particular by multinationals. The agricultural sector is one of the strategic sectors to ensure the 
food security of Europeans. It is therefore necessary to ensure that farmers benefit from a range 
of digital services that specify whether the sovereignty of their data is guaranteed or not. Some 
key principles: 
• Operational criteria: data must be stored and processed in Europe. The operator must 
always know where their data is (transparency) and the data must be subject to our 
regulations.  
• Legal criteria: the contract with the operator must fall under Member State or European 
law.  
• Regulatory criteria: the cloud provider must comply with the GDPR and the data must 
not be subject to extra-European regulations (immune to the Cloud Act) 
• We support the need to introduce the definition of "sovereignty".

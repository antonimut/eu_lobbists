TELEFÓNICA COMMENTS ON EUROPEAN COMMISSION’s  
PUBLIC CONSULTATION ON THE DATA ACT 
 
1 September 2021 
 
 
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
Telefónica welcomes the opportunity to comment on the different measures currently 
being considered by the European Commission with a view to the forthcoming legislative 
proposal for a Data Act. In addition to the response to the public consultation 
questionnaire, Telefónica wishes to stress the comments on the EC’s Inception Impact 
Assessment we submitted last June.  
 
- Telefónica supports the overall objective of the creation of a European model 
fostering data sharing and re-use across sectors and Member States. In order to 
succeed in this, any future legislative framework should focus on clarifying how 
data sharing, data pooling and data re-use can take place as all relevant actors 
(Public Administration, businesses and individuals) need to understand how data 
can be voluntarily shared whilst upholding rights at stake (personal data, IPRs, 
trade secrets…). 
 
- Anonymization should be one of the pillars of any data sharing in case of data 
originating from individuals. Anonymization will ensure data sharing is done in 
full compliance with data protection rules. In case of sharing personal data, GDPR 
applies.  
 
- Telefónica understands the need to promote greater data availability and reduce 
the existing technical, legal and economic barriers to data exchange. The success 
of a European data space will depend on creating an entire ecosystem that 
encourages the voluntary exchange of data, from the public and private sectors. 
It is not about imposing additional obligations, but about creating a sustainable 
ecosystem in which all parties will win.

1.- B2G DATA SHARING 
Telefónica supports the overall objective of building a flexible framework for improved 
access to and use of data, making data sharing a reality for B2G and B2B in the interest 
of EU citizens. Currently, with some positive exceptions, little sustainable B2G data 
sharing is taking place. This has been clearly manifested during COVID-19 pandemic, 
where companies have been requested, in a crisis mode, to share data with Public 
Administrations. Telefónica has engaged actively with Public bodies at local, national 
and EU level to provide anonymised aggregated mobility data to help in the fight against 
the spread of covid-19 in full compliance with EU legislation.  
But this example cannot be extrapolated as the way ahead to ensure that sustainable 
B2G data sharing models generalise. Due to the urgency of the situation, companies 
have shared data pro-bono, but this is not sustainable in the long term, as companies 
have heavily invested in data analytics and need to see their investment preserved. 
Moreover, the pro-bono aspect of the initiative has prevented companies from adapting 
to the specific needs of the European Commission and companies have shared data as 
they had it available. This has created a significant burden on the teams of the Joint 
Research Centre of the European Commission to make all data interoperable and has 
led to a suboptimal solution because due to the needed common dominator NUTS1 
granularity in the data was lost. 
Instead of ad-hoc urgent responses for crisis situations, Public Administrations should 
understand that long term public policies based on data insights might be an important 
factor for reducing public expenditure and freeing up budget for a sustainable data-
sharing model, benefitting the public sector (less budget), citizens (less taxes) and 
companies (fair compensation for collecting, anonymising, processing and analysing the 
data). If Public Administrations really wish to perform evidence-based policy making, 
they will need to rely on best quality data driven services. 
 
1 NUTS or Nomenclature of Territorial Units for Statistics developed by Eurostat.

Telefónica agrees that we need to define at EU level the necessary basis to facilitate 
B2G data sharing. This basis should not be through imposing mandatory B2G data 
sharing, but on the contrary working in order to incentivise voluntary data sharing 
schemes through: 
- reduction of transaction costs and perceived risks for data suppliers 
- tax incentives 
- fostering standardisation of contractual provisions and interoperability of data 
formats 
- launching pilot projects that would allow businesses to give valuable feedback 
on established principles to assess their robustness in practice and consequently 
lead to market evidence as a basis for future considerations. 
 
For the EU to build a strong data economy and society, at large, EU cannot rely on 
mandatory obligations for companies to share data without a fair compensation. 
Where is then the incentive for companies to invest in robust data business, if they face 
the threat that these investments will not be rewarded?  
In order to have data available, a flexible, future-proof regulatory privacy framework 
that preserve privacy and, at the same time, foster innovation, is crucial. Neither data 
economy nor data sharing will emerge if no data is available due to rigid and no future 
proof privacy rules. Finding the right balance is of essence. 
In parallel with the Data Act, there is the Data Governance Act (DGA). The DGA sets the 
“conditions” (not obligations) for re-use of public data by private companies. If DGA 
builds on the principle of “voluntary data sharing”, for public data (which has been 
created with public investment), even more the same principle should underline any 
B2G data sharing in the future Data Act. 
If the future Data Act imposes B2G data sharing obligations without fair compensation, 
these obligations -complemented with the provisions of the DGA- will trigger the sharing 
of the data (initially provided by private companies to Public Administrations) to other 
businesses (G2B data sharing). Thus, any possible business model sustainable in the long 
term will disappear as companies will not have any chances to see their investments 
rewarded by future demand of their services, by public or private entities.  
Instead of maintaining voluntary schemes where potential customers of data driven 
services negotiate a data sharing agreement under commercial conditions, companies 
will be imposed a requirement to offer the same services to public and private entities.

Despite the principle of data use limitation, by which governments are not allowed to 
share business data onwards if not defined in advance in an agreement, the risk remains

combine it with other data sources and share it further with third parties. The question 
then arises if there is still room for B2B data sharing if companies would be obliged to 
share data with Governments and then in turn Governments can share data with

2.- B2B DATA SHARING 
B2B data sharing and data pooling are nascent markets that will continue to grow as 
companies from different sectors are steadily moving towards data-driven models, and 
clear business opportunities for sharing data between companies will continue to 
appear. Therefore, it is of utmost importance to preserve contractual freedom as a 
guiding principle, or otherwise innovation on big data will suffer dramatically. 
In addition, B2B voluntary data sharing should be extremely respectful of privacy and 
data protection rules in case individuals are the origin of the data (as opposed to data 
generated from machine sensors). During the past few years, Telefónica has heavily 
invested in an Anonymisation solution, building a Big Data platform to create 
anonymised, aggregated insights in a privacy-preserving way.  
Competition rules need to be updated in order to facilitate those cooperation 
agreements among competitors aiming at B2B data sharing and data pooling. Indeed, 
fostering such agreements among competitors will help companies offer innovative 
digital services, contribute to the development of AI and enable European Data spaces. 
In addition, B2B data sharing and data pooling agreements will allow stakeholders to 
compete with non-European ones within the digital world, as well as to resolve 
structural competition problems in digital markets. Therefore, Telefónica welcomes the 
ongoing EC work towards the revision of the Block Exemption Regulations for horizontal 
cooperation agreements and the Horizontal Guidelines.  
  
5 
 
 
 
In this line, Telefónica is calling for a new safe harbour for data sharing and data pooling  
agreements through a new Block Exemption Regulation, providing stakeholders with full 
legal certainty to ensure that such kind of agreements, under certain conditions, comply 
with Article 101.3. TFEU. Telefónica also believes data sharing and data pooling 
agreements should be considered pro-competitive in the Horizontal Guidelines. 
B2B data sharing and data pooling are aimed at launching innovative services and 
resolve market failures in digital markets. Taking into account that digital markets are 
very fast-moving and dynamic and companies are the most interested in accessing, re-
using and sharing data, no mandatary provisions for a “fairness test” are necessary as 
they would only create hurdles for such cooperation. In the same vein, no mandatory 
provisions on data access and use for non-personal data are necessary. Imposing new 
rights and obligations to data access and use without fair compensation will not bring 
the right conditions to incentivize companies to engage actively in more data sharing. 
On the contrary, companies obliged to share data will stop investing in such data and 
those companies with a “right” to access other businesses’ data will obtain less quality 
data driven services.    
Mandatory access to data provisions are proposed within the DMA especially in 
situations where this would open-up secondary markets for complementary services. 
Indeed, the DMA already tackles the concern of absence of (or reduced access to) some 
key inputs in the digital economy, such as data contemplating for those companies that 
qualify as online gatekeepers an obligation to provide business users, or third parties 
authorised by a business user, free of charge, with effective, high-quality, continuous 
and real-time access and use of aggregated or non-aggregated data, that is provided for 
or generated in the context of the use of the relevant core platform services by those 
business users and the end users engaging with the products or services provided by 
those business users.   Any obligation on data sharing should be confined to online 
gatekeepers that benefit from access to vast amounts of data that they collect while 
providing the core platform services as well as other digital services as recognized in the 
DMA.

3.- DATA PORTABILITY   
Telefónica agrees that the right to data portability as defined by Article 20 GDPR is a key 
element to provide the proper balance between the Fundamental Right to data 
protection and innovation across the digital value chain. Telefónica’s data strategy aims 
to “unlock the value of peoples’ data for them and for society as a whole” by providing 
users with transparent tools to easily manage their data. In this context, Telefónica is 
working on innovative ways to allow customers to share their data with partners. Data 
portability is at the heart of any broader data sharing policy.  
However, it is of outmost importance to avoid any overlaps between different set of 
rules, GDPR, DMA, DGA, Data Act. This can be very counterproductive as a company or 
an individual will have very different legal instruments to understand how to exercise 
the right to data portability.  
GDPR has established the basic principles of the right to data portability in the context 
of personal data. It is not a question of applying it mutatis mutandi to non-personal data.  
Data connections are an essential differentiating factor in the market and companies 
will find a way to allow customers to port the personal data that is most relevant to 
them, in the format that makes more sense in light of the technological developments. 
However, technical standards for data portability might be fundamentally different if we 
refer to health data or to electricity consumption.    
In addition, EU policy makers should consider the risk that given the digital capacity of 
tech giants, they are likely to faster obtain benefits from reusing data than any other EU 
industry. This might be the case of ported data based on the data portability right as well 
as in the larger B2B context. EU companies can become net donors of data which will 
ultimately benefit the same tech giants.

4.- COMPETITIVE CLOUD MARKETS 
Telefónica believes that a true European single market for data will only see the light if 
European secure and sustainable infrastructures can support it. The growth of 
connected devices, evolving into smart connected systems, will generate an exponential 
increase in data management requirements that a more traditional centralised cloud 
will not be able to manage. A key competitive advantage will be edge networks. There 
is an opportunity for telcos to solve that problem by developing the vision of the 
network as a platform, while overcoming challenges like industry fragmentation and 
data sovereignty.  
Cloud, as well as edge computing, will be a major element of the future connectivity 
architecture (5G and beyond). A new processing layer is emerging, one that will redefine 
not only telecoms but also the IT sector in the future. Edge computing is becoming an 
industry strategic point, where collective effort could be beneficial. Edge computing is a 
sound European alternative to the current of offer of the US hyperscalers. It will be of 
utmost importance to enable a distributed cloud-edge infrastructure, potentially by 
federating/ interconnecting operators’ resources and homogenising APIs, catalogues 
and tasks. 
Having said that, Telefónica questions which is the added value of a binding obligation 
for cloud service providers to offer data portability. Such right is already enshrined in 
GDPR, and the DMA is also proposing portability and interoperability requirements on 
big platforms, including cloud service providers acting as gatekeepers, for their business 
users. Overlapping provisions will not provide more legal certainty, but on the contrary 
will bring confusion for companies and individuals.

5.- SAFEGUARDS FOR NON-PERSONAL DATA IN THE INTERNATIONAL CONTEXT 
Basically, European Commission (EC) is considering to expand the requirements for the 
cross border transfer of personal data outside the EU to non-personal data. EC is 
concerned that non-personal data, which might be of high value for companies, might 
be misused if they are not protected under the same conditions as in the EU.  
 
While we agree with the basic principle that the protection of data shall travel with the 
data, which so far it has been very focused on personal data, it seems wrong to try to 
extend the rules applying to personal data to non-personal data.  
 
Rules on trade secrets, Intellectual Property Rights already protect non-personal data. 
Make a parallelism between rules that were created having in mind the specificities of 
personal data (data portability, cross-border data transfers…) and rules applying to non-
personal data might not be the right way to ensure better protection of EU industrial 
data.  
 
Main problem is not the lack of a protection mechanism for non-personal data (which is 
currently protected by trade secrets, by IPRs or sui generis right for databases…), but 
the lack of enforcement of that system of protection. This will not be solved by 
prohibiting non-personal data from traveling outside the EU, but by reinforcing the 
enforcement of the protection mechanisms that are already currently available to 
European organisations.

CONCLUDING REMARKS  
 
Voluntary Data Sharing based on contractual freedom should be maintained as guiding 
principle. B2B data sharing is a nascent market that will continue to grow as companies 
from all sectors are steadily moving towards data driven models. The same applies for 
B2G data sharing as Public Administrations will understand that using privately held data 
could reduce public expenditure and free up budget for a sustainable data sharing 
model, benefitting the public sector (less budget), citizens (less taxes) and companies 
providing data (fair compensation). This is the virtuous cercle that needs to be created 
and not only in times of crisis, but with the aim to develop better evidence-based public 
policies in the long term.  
 
What is necessary is to set the right environment that stimulates data sharing (such as 
tax incentives) while minimising the associated risk and providing legal certainty, 
commercial drive and transparency rights. Any regulatory action at this stage is 
premature as the market is still developing. Regulatory sandboxes for exploration or 
training programmes for public administrations are more adequate approaches than 
imposing mandatory data sharing on private companies with regard to the data they 
generate. Otherwise, innovation on big data will suffer dramatically as European 
companies will see their investment in data penalised. 
 
As a true believer of data sharing, Telefónica takes its responsibility as relevant player: 
our support from day 1 to COVID-19 spread models by sharing anonymous mobility 
matrixes with Health Authorities, has been instrumental to correlate mobility patterns 
with pandemic evolution. Telefónica believes this is just the tip of a massive iceberg 
which is still to be capitalized. We cannot be reactive to these emergencies but proactive 
in all the areas where wellbeing of our citizens can be impacted, either to make it to 
grow or to protect it from threats. Investments are needed in this area and need to be 
structural, considering that best effort is not an option if we want to capitalize this 
opportunity for European citizens, probably one of the biggest we have ever faced. 
 
Inclusiveness in reaping the benefits of digitalisation for Public Administration, 
businesses and citizens should be the guiding principle for a European approach to data 
while, at the same, framing a smart model that creates the ecosystem and legal certainty 
that the Data Strategy needs to thrive, without being perceived as a barrier that 
prevents innovation and progress, but, on the contrary, generating a friendly research 
and technological development environment committed to securing both innovation 
and individuals’ and businesses’ rights.

EU Data Act Public Consultation: Vodafone Group Comments   
 
I. Business-to-government data sharing for the public interest 
 
B2G Data Sharing is currently functioning well  
Cooperation between MNOs and the public sector is broadly well-functioning, with operators able to 
supply governments and public bodies with timely data insights to help them improve their decision 
making and delivery of public services. To accelerate and deep en this cooperation we look to the 
creation of sustainable agreements, based on adequate compensation for the investments made by 
operators in the creation of data insights.  
 
In our experience one factor that does hold back the full potential of B2G data sharing is a general lack 
expertise and capacity necessary to structure, manipulate and create insights from raw data, and 
associated security controls. This demand side failure means in many cases, the full potential of data 
help by network operators is simply not being tapped. To resolve this, we call for a strategy to improve 
data literacy and data skills across the public sector, as well as an awareness raising campaign across 
those departments of the full potential of data insights in their given area, be it transport, healthcare, 
agriculture or municipal planning.  
 
It is thus not a lack of willingness on the side of private sector to enter into cooperat ion based on 
voluntary agreements that has held back the full potential of B2G data sharing to date, but rather 
approaches to data sharing that do not fully allow EU operators to invest in and market data analytics 
services. Our fear is that if EU operator s are not afforded sufficient room to innovate in this space, 
government’s will be forced to outsource data analytics tasks, and this could lead to a further 
entrenching of the dominance of a small number of non-European data and cloud giants. 
 
Public Interest Data Access Right  
Vodafone strongly rejects the logic of a data access right or mandate based on diffuse and ill -defined 
notions of the ‘public interest’. Data can be generated by public or private entities, however the notion 
that data arises naturally from a community and that the value of social resources of community non-
personal data should primarily accrue to it, is fundamentally inaccurate. Telecommunications 
metadata exists as a by -product/externality of our core service offering: connectivity . Its existence is 
rendered possible only through the provision of a communications service and the investments made 
in the underlying infrastructure. Further big data analytics techniques can be applied to develop 
insights that have economic and societal value, however the raw data itself is a proprietary asset, and 
not something belonging to the community at large. It should be added that there exists an important 
difference between data sharing and data services. In our experience public authorities do not benefit 
from the supply of raw, unstructured data , which they in many case do not have the capacity or 
expertise to structure and produce meaningful insights. What is more valuable is access to data 
services, where the data itself has already been structured and analysed to produce meaningful 
insights that can inform public policy and decision making.  
 
With regards to IoT data, to promote innovation, participants in the IoT value chain should reasonably 
endeavour to share non -personal, machine -generated data on a fair, r easonable and non -
discriminatory (FRND) basis, taking full account of any related security, privacy, competition law or 
confidentiality consideration.

However, such expropriation of privately held assets must take place “under the conditions provided

B2G data sharing arrangement that takes place in the EU. 
 
Considering the need for a sustainable cooperation on both sides (private and public), sharing of data 
at preferential or no costs would disincentivize private actors to provide such data to public entities, as 
generation of data insights is only possible with considerable effort, e.g. costs, staffing, data 
optimization. MNOs see this as a nascent market and one where we can scale rapidly and provide 
impactful, societally beneficial services, but only if we are able to secure fair return on investments. If 
this doesn’t happen, our fear is that government’s will need to outsource to the dominant tech 
platforms for the generation of data insights and the opportunity for European firms to be competitive 
in this market will be lost.  
 
These issues have been addressed comprehensively by the B2G data sharing expert group, who 
reported to the Commission earlier in the year. We would wholeheartedly agree with their conclusion 
that: “Requiring access to certai n datasets in specific situations does not necessarily imply that the 
data should be shared free of charge. Companies incur costs to make data available, including time 
and resources for preparing the data, adapting it to the specific request and building the infrastructure 
necessary for the transmission of such data (e.g. APIs). For B2G data sharing to become sustainable, 
these initiatives should be mutually beneficial”. 
 
In very exceptional circumstances, mandatory access to data could be justified, for ex ample where 
there is a national emergency or immediate threat to life.  In such circumstances access to data should 
be limited to the minimum necessary to address health/emergency needs and should be deleted 
when no longer needed. Costs should be covered a nd access should be restricted where possible to 
insights, rather than raw data.  Any mandatory requirements should not undermine the incentives to 
invest in data analytics capacity, and should not lose sight of the importance of stimulating demand 
for suc h data. For example, governments must be equipped with the skills and capabilities to 
meaningfully analyse data driven insights and use these to inform policy making and spending 
decisions. This can be done by ensuring the requisite data skills and access to digital tools and 
platforms exist across government, at both the central (state) and local (city/regional) level. 
 
Above all the regulatory framework must bring about market conditions that allow for effective 
monetization of data assets, to ensure that  those who invest in the underlying technology and 
analytics capabilities are able to generate a fair return on those investments. 
 
II. Business to Business Data Sharing  
 
Data Services under development  
Vodafone has developed a data analytics platform with customers spanning retail, real estate, logistics, 
public sector and insurance. The data analytics platform creates valuable insights from aggregate and 
anonymised network data. The solution works by using Big Data and by analysing anonymized 
telecommunications data based on geolocation, being GDPR  and ePrivacy  compliant. It provides 
location intelligence to discover where and when things happen, patterns of movement and aggregate 
mobility, resulting in insight -led decision -making and greater efficiency for businesses of all sizes, 
industries and sectors.  
 
Benefits of Data Sharing for Telecoms Sector  
According to a recent ERT study, the most significant benefits from data shari ng are developing and

The ERT finds that B2B data sharing will be a tremendous driver of innovation, business creation, 
productivity and economic growth, and is set to grow significantly in future. But while some pioneering 
companies are already experimenting with industrial data sharing, B2B data sharing has yet to achieve 
true scale in Europe. 
 
Vodafone has previously commissioned Deloitte to model the economic benefits of B2B data sharing 
in a number of industry sectors (agriculture, automotive, smart, cities, manufacturing, healthcare) 
finding that the sharing of machine-generated non-personal data would add €1.4tn in economic value 
to the EU economy by the year 2027 . This potential will be further enhanced by 5G and narrow-band 
IoT. Realising this value depends, in large part, on the successful sharing of data between 
organisations. Sharing is vital, as there may be a wide range of potential uses for the data throughout 
its lifecycle, and many of those uses may not take place within the organisation that holds the data or 
may require different skills. Data can be shared with customers and su ppliers throughout the supply 
chain (vertical data sharing); with peers to create larger data sets including more variation (horizontal 
data sharing); and outside the sector with stakeholders who can make use of the data in new settings 
(external data sharing). 
 
For mobile operators, we expect that data sharing will benefit us in a number of ways: data can be used 
to refine and optimise our core connectivity services (for example data from digital platforms on 
latency and jitter that lets us know where we need to focus to manage congestion on our networks). 
Data can also provide us with valuable insights into our consumers, allowing us to serve them with 
more relevant offers and reduce churn.  
 
Perhaps the most significant opportunity for network operators though is in the field of industrial IoT 
and 5G services, where we are leading the development of advanced digital solutions for businesses 
to run their operations more efficiently. Here data generated in industrial suppl y chains (for example 
those flowing from IoT sensors on a production line or across a logistics supply chain) can be collected 
and analysed to spot patterns and identify areas for possible efficiency gains and rationalisation. 
Europe is particularly well placed to lead here, given its strong manufacturing base 
 
III. Improving Portability for Business Users of Cloud Services  
 
Vodafone supports the overarching aim of the Commission, to create an open and competitive 
European cloud ecosystem, ensuring diversity a nd resilience; however, we are pragmatic about how 
to best achieve this. Today the reality is that European providers occupy only a very small percentage 
of the market for cloud services in Europe (the four leading providers will account for over 80% global 
revenue in 2021). This has real world consequences: an investment gap of 11bn EUR per annum per 
EU/US-China and low uptake in Europe.  
 
Measures to build EU cloud champions are important but also face significant challenges and will take 
time, given the pace and scale of innovation from non-European cloud vendors. Instead we would urge 
policy makers to focus on the legal frameworks that can be put in place to encourage and incentivise 
enhanced switching and portability between cloud service providers, including binding obligations on 
dominant cloud vendors (often referred to as ‘Hyperscalers’) to support service interoperability in 
Europe (for e.g. via Gaia-X or the EU Cloud Rulebook).

In this context Vodafone strongly supports the European Commission’s proposals in this area to drive

Wider Context: EU’s Industrial Strategy  
Vodafone is committed to helping the EU deliver on its twin ambitions around digital and green 
transformation. Cloud technology and increasingly edge technology will be a key strategic enabler for 
this transition, ensuring resilient, scalable and energy efficient processing capacity where and when it 
is needed.  
 
We support the concept of Open Strategic Autonomy as applied to the cloud computing market. This 
means that we can utilise best in class technology from across the globe, while investing in our  own 
capabilities and leveraging public funds where there is an overriding public need or to correct market 
failure, for example where there are insufficient means for customers to switch freely between cloud 
service providers. Vodafone is involved in a number of these initiatives and is committed their success.  
 
IV. Safeguards for non-personal data in international contexts  
Vodafone continues to navigate the fallout to the Schrems II ruling, where the ECJ invalidated Privacy 
Shield as a legal tool for legitimising transfers of European personal data to the US because of the 
latter’s “extensive” national security regime. In doing so, we make continued use of alternative transfer 
mechanisms to underpin cross border data transfer to third countries that do not have an Adequacy 
Decision in place with the EU, including Standard Contractual Clauses.  
We strongly welcome the updated SCCs published by the European Commission, and associated 
guidance from the European Data Protection Board on supplementary measures in providing certainty 
and predictability for businesses in the legal and organisational measures they need to put in place to 
ensure that EU citizen’s data is sufficiently protected when processed outside of the EU.  
From a policy perspective the SCC’s are very encouraging, as they signal the clear intent from the 
Commission to facilitate cross border data flows, rather than following a rigid interpretation of Schrems 
II that would look to prevent data transfer to third countries. We understand the Commission will follow 
up later in the year with a set of common questions on the SCCs being used for data transfers outside 
the bloc. We look forward to reviewing these questions as a practical aid in our ongoing compliance 
work to ensure that international data transfers are in line with Schrems II.  
Ultimately in a data-driven economy, cross border transfers underpin all industries. We all rely on data 
flows to enable enterprise and consumer services, supplier support, data centres and back -
office/support functions. Data localisation will adversely affect the resilience, reliability and security 
of global supply chains with additional impacts to the global digital agenda across all industries.  
 
This is truly a cross -industry topic that not only impacts companies operationally but also their 
strategic development of data driven technology. We remain united by our vision and commitment for 
a strong and competitive Europe and we fear Europe cannot remain competitive if localisation of data 
becomes a widespread practice, as this would cut us off from the globally interconnected digital 
supply chains and expertise that underpin the digital economy.

EU Regulatory Solutions to third-country access to data  
Vodafone strongly supports activities at the international level, via the G7, OECD and EU-US Trade and 
Tech Council to agree on a multilateral framework for government data access reques ts, that 
reconciles legitimate national security activities of governments with the privacy and data protection 
rights of citizens. Ideally negotiators will be able to agree on a high level set of rules, governance 
principles and baseline standards for the  protection of personal data as it flows between the EU and 
US that can form the basis of a durable legal framework replacing the makeshift agreements of recent 
years.  
 
This will of course require a level of common understanding on levels of access permit ted by 
governments to fulfil their national security obligations, appropriate checks and balances and rights 
to redress for citizens. Vodafone is actively participating in discussions towards this goal, including 
providing input to the OECD high-level principles and safeguards for government access to personal 
data held by the private sector, to be published later in the year. 
 
We reject the other regulatory options included in t he public consultation (for example: introducing 
an obligation f or data processing service providers to put in  place specified legal, technical and 
organisational measures to prevent the transfer to or access of foreign authorities to the data they 
store or process) on the basis that these would be extremely burdensome for European business and 
tend towards EU data protectionism rather than focusing on EU data protection.

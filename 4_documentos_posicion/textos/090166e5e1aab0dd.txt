INTRODUCTION 
C4C welcomes the opportunity to contribute to the European Commission’s public consultation on the Data 
Act. For further questions on our submission, please do not hesitate to contact Caroline De Cock, C4C 
Coordinator, at cdc@n-square.eu. 
ABOUT C4C 
C4C is a broad-based coalition that seeks an informed debate on how copyright can more effectively promote 
innovation, access, and creativity. C4C brings together libraries, scientific and research institutions, digital 
rights groups, technology businesses, and educational and cultural heritage institutions that share a common 
view on copyright. Find a detailed overview of us and our members here.

VII. Intellectual Property Rights – Protection of Databases 
Intellectual Property Rights - General questions 
Question 2: “Control over the accessibility and use of data should not  be realised through the 
establishment of additional layers of exclusive, proprietary rights ”. To what extent do you agree 
with this statement? 
We strongly agree that no additional layers of exclusive, proprietary rights should be established. 
The creation of such additional exclusive, proprietary rights prove to be detrimental, as evi denced by the 
negative effect s flowing out of the adoption of the Database Directive’s  sui generi s right. Therefore, we 
strongly oppose the creation of specific data access regimes or any broadening of the scope of the sui generis 
right. 
The Database Direct ive has illustrated that once new rights are attributed they are almost impossible to 
repeal, even if they end-up falling short of their objective.  
We consider that additional layers of protection of information create disproportionate burdens for those 
willing to utilise this information, as it requires more efforts from them to negotiate licences or clear rights, 
and thus unnecessarily hinders access and increases costs. Moreover, such additional restrictions often do 
not generate any benefits, quite the contrary. 
Dr Annemarie Beunen pointed out that the that the sui generis right is arguably over-strong and discriminates 
against both competitors and non -profit users such as researchers, educational institutions, libraries, 
museums and archives” and adds that it “fails to balance the commercial interests of database producers 
against the public interests of society at large, such as the free flow of information” (pp. 270-1) [1]. 
This is usually the case when new rights are granted, namely: they benefit the bigger players who are already 
active in the market. BEUC, the European consumer organisation, made this point in its response to the EC’s 
first public consultation back in 2006, as it explained that this Directive also impacts consumers because: “it 
increases the barriers to entry for potential competitors in the database market, thus raising costs and stifling 
innovation to the detriment of, amongst others, the consumer.”

Questions on the Database Directive 
Question 9: To what extent do you agree that there is a need to review the sui generis protection 
for databases provided by the Database Directive, in particular as regards the access and sharing of 
data. 
C4C considers that no review can fix the inherent  issues of the sui generis right: its withdrawal is the only 
option. The additional restrictions on the use of data stemming from this protection have not generated any 
benefits, quite the contrary it has created additional barriers as explained in our responses to the questions 
above. However, should this be deemed not a feasible option to pursue, we suggest introducing constitutive 
formalities for database producers in order to benefit from the sui generis protection. This could be achieved, 
for example, through a mandatory registration requirement for those databases that a database producer 
wants to protect, instead of automatically granting protection. Dev Gangjee, Associate Professor of 
Intellectual Property Law at Oxford, explains that “constitutive f ormalities act as an entry level barrier into 
copyright protection, by requiring positive acts on the part of those seeking protection” (p. 226) [1], therefore 
this would help re-tip the balance in favour of the public. Such an electronic register could be facilitated by 
the European Union Intellectual Property Office, as they have experience with the unitary trademark and 
design registries.

Question 11: In your opinion, how should the new scope of the sui generis right be defined? 
C4C suggests to withdraw the sui generis right. If this is not the path chosen by the EC, there is no need to 
clarify the scope of the sui generis right, as the CJEU has already clarified its scope and scale. There should 
certainly not be an extension of the sui generis right to machine generated data.

Question 16: In 2018, the Commission published an Evaluation of Directive 96/9/EC on the legal 
protection of databases , which was preceded by a public consultation. The Evaluation Report 
pointed out several legal uncertainties related to the Database Directive that may prevent the 
Directive from operating efficiently. Please indicate which of the following elements of the Database 
Directive could be reviewed: 
Definition of a database : The definition of a database in the Directive is too broad. Professor Tatiana 
Synodinοu notes [1] that the Court of Just ice of the European Union (CJEU) “has continually and clearly 
adopted a wide and teleological definition of the concept of ‘database’.” 
Notion of substantial investment in a database: The Database Directive does not treat all database producers 
equally. Dr Annemarie Beunen describes [2] the fact that scientists and universities are put at a disadvantage 
as producers of databases, compared to those merely compiling existing scientific data into a database. This 
is because the cost of the creation of the data is not taken into account as part of the substantial investment 
that grants the sui generis protection. 
Mireille van Eechoud and Brenda van der Wal, from the Institute for Information Law (IViR) of the University 
of Amsterdam, Netherlands, have pointed out [3] that the ‘substantial investment’ criteria raises a lot of 
uncertainty in relation to the application of the sui generis protection to public sector databases (p. 26). 
Exceptions to the sui generis right: The exceptions in the Database Directive are not mandatory and Member 
States have implemented them according to their own interpretation of the D irective. Moreover, the sui 
generis protection adds an additional layer of confusion by confining the exceptions to the ‘lawful users of 
database’, but the Directive does not define the term - see remarks below. The Database Directive has thus 
failed to br ing legal certainty for lawful users and to achieve harmonisation. One minor upside to the 
Directive: it did introduce guarantees that contracts could not override the exceptions granted. 
Notion of the lawful user and his rights and obligations: The Directive does not define the term ‘lawful users 
of database.’ This has resulted in diverging interpretations in case law and national transpositions. Moreover, 
the Database Directive has not brought legal certainty for these lawful users. Dr Annemarie Beunen notes [2] 
that the “sui generis right gives rise to many questions of interpretation, to which the Database Dir ective 
does not provide answers” (p. 270). These questions have been left to the Courts to answer. In this context, 
Dr Beunen adds that “the interpretations offered by the European Court of Justice are arguably only a poor 
consolation where an exclusive right is defectively demarcated in the first place.” 
Term of protection: The duration of the sui generis protection, starting at 15 years, could be permanent a s 
long as the database is periodically ‘updated’, even if the update merely equates to checking the accuracy of 
the data. This is out of sync with reality. 
Other = Access to data : The exceptions to the sui generis  protection fail to meet the needs of vario us 
stakeholders, as Dr Annemarie Beunen remarks  [2] that the sui   generis right is arguably over -strong and 
discriminates against both competitors and non -profit users such as researchers, educational institutions, 
libraries, museums and archives” and adds tha t it “fails to balance the commercial interests of database 
producers against the public interests of society at large, such as the free flow of information” (pp. 270-1).

Question 17: Please provide any other information that you find useful regarding the application of 
the Database Directive in relation to the data economy. 
If the EC wants to foster a European data economy, wherein data can be easily made accessible and usable 
to facilitate development of new products and services, the sui generis protection will remain a hindrance, 
as exemplified in our responses. In 2015, the European Parliament already argued [1] that this Directive is 
“an impediment to the development of a European data-driven economy”, and called upon the EC to abolish 
it (§108).

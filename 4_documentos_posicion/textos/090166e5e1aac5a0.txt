Digitization, technological mutations and the appearance of new business models have led to a deep social, 
societal and even political transformation at a global level. The 4.0 economy has become the symbol of this 
change, every day a little more transformed by new tech-driven uses. Stakeholders (States, public authorities, 
companies, civil society) have gradually become aware of the economic and societal issues surrounding data 
and the need to create a digital world of security and trust.  
It is widely accepted that data today constitute an important source of values, opportunities and innovation 
offering broad prospects for growth and competitiveness. The sources of digital power are now considered 
to be in the use of data. This observation leads some authorities to consider  that "a wide opennes s and 
circulation of data would be relev ant a priori and could be encouraged by the public authorities, withou t 
degrading the economic potential of the companies concerned"1.  
Yet, while MEDEF supports the will of the European Commission to adopt a horizontal legislative initiative 
on data in order to create a fa ir and harmonized European data economy  by guaranteeing access to and 
use of data, it should be remembered that data play a key role in the economy and society. Indeed, not only 
do they represent a financial, technical and human investment, but they also add value to the activities of 
companies. Therefore, the y constitu te a crucial strategic and economic assess for theses players in a 
context of sovereignty and international competitiveness.  
Business-to-government data sharing for the public interest (B2G) 
Data sharing between businesses and public administrations (B2G) may be necessary for reasons of public 
interest (medical research, public health, environment, etc.). However, this sharing cannot mean free, reuse 
and open access to third parties, especially competitors, without the consent of the company holding the 
data. If, at this stage, it does not seem necessary for the EU to take additional measures so that public sector 
bodies can access private sector data, B2G sharing must be strictly supervised and harmonized at European 
level to avoid any risk of legal uncertainty and distortion of competition. 
Indeed, while the concept of "openness of data for purposes of public interest" is an important principle, the 
sharing of private sector data with the public sector must be done on a voluntary basis, or at least under 
strictly framed conditions. 
On the one hand, the concept of "public interest" must be defined in a precise and restrictive manner, 
depending on the context and understood in relation to the costs and risks involved for businesses . The 
compensation schemes as proposed in the report of the High Level Expert group on B2G data sharing 2 
constitute an interesting basis (in that they link the compensation to the efforts and investment costs 
provided by the company to make this data available) and it is important that the European Commission 
continues the dialogue with stakeholders on the establishment of adequate compensation systems for B2G 
data sharing. 
On the other hand, it i s important to preserve intellectual property rights, trade secrets and the 
confidentiality of some commercial or strategic information of companies , the disclosure of which would 
jeopardize trade secrets. Indeed, it is crucial that company data shared with  public authorities is not 
considered as public data that can be freely reused by third parties . Access to this data could create a 
distortion of competition to the detriment of these companies. Competitors could reuse this data without 
having borne the costs and investments for the collection and processing of this data. 
In addition, some data is held by the public sector for reasons of national security . Thus, in France for 
example, the device for the protection of the nation's scientific and technical potential (PPST) aims to protect 
some knowledge, know -how and technologies, the embezzlement of which could harm the economic 
interests of the nation. The access of such data could therefore constitute a risk of loss of sovereignty. 
More generally, it is necessary to protect innovation and foster the competitiveness of European 
companies. Beyond the economic challenges, the use of data has become a key element of power and digital

Business-to-business data sharing (B2B) 
With a view data valuation and innovative services creation, data sharing can be beneficial for companies, 
on condition that it is decided between them within the framework of partnership contracts (bilateral or 
multilateral). This implies that companies can choose: 
− their partners, 
− the data they make available, 
− the conditions of access or sharing (and in particular the compensations). 
Contractual (on the model of software licenses for example) and organizational solutions (pooling of 
algorithms, of platforms, of premises, etc.) must therefore be favored and left to the sole hands of companies 
which today are aware of the value of the data they hold and of the need to work with other players or to 
pool their investments. In France, the freedom for each player to conduct his trade policy as he intends and 
to enter into contracts with the people of his choice, in application of the principle of freedom of trade and 
industry, has constitutional value3. 
More and more industries are opting for data pooling infrastructures without allowing free use of this data. 
Pooling data between players makes it possible to train algorithms and make them smarter, which benefits 
all players. Nevertheless, this pooling would be co-financed by the players who would all provide data to feed 
an algorithm. The data would not be accessible or exploitable outside this algorithm. 
Thus, while it is necessary to encourage companies to organize the sharing and use of data among themselves 
by offering them, for example, accessible and secure infrastructures for data sharing (development of 
common data spaces), the voluntary framework and the contractual basis are a sine qua non condition in 
order to give companies the possibility of choosing the data likely to be shared in a common interest while 
protecting business secrets, personal data or even intellectual property rights. 
While access to data considered as essential with fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory conditions should 
be encouraged, these should not be imposed (as is the case with non-essential patents). 
Freedom of contract must there fore be the cornerstone of the European data strategy . Horizontal 
regulation on data sharing between businesses should therefore be avoided, at the risk of discouraging the 
participation of stakeholders in European data spaces. 
Nevertheless, a major obstacle is the lack of fairness in B2B data sharing contracts due to an economic 
imbalance, which concerns both SMEs, start -ups but also large companies, faced with major digital players 
with strong negotiating power and imposing their conditions. It  would therefore be appropriate for the 
European Commission to work on standard model agreements in order to facilitate the conclusion of 
partnerships between businesses for the sharing of their data with terms and conditions that are simple, 
accessible and intelligible to any user (storage, transmission , data ownership, etc.). This reflection would 
make it possible to avoid the introduction of unfair terms and should be carried out in conjunction with work 
on the Digital Markets Act (DMA - Articles 5 and 6).

Portability et interoperability of data 
For a better interoperability of data 
In order to foster a data -driven economy, it is essential to strengthen the interoperability of systems , 
especially within supply chains. 
Businesses are required to use a variety  of solutions and t hose must be able to work together and 
communicate with each other. Proprietary solutions do not allow this and make it difficult, if not impossible, 
to build service offers based on different software or hardware solutions. Open Source technologies greatly 
contribute to improve the portability of data in Europe, especially in the European Cloud market, thanks to 
Open Source components (interfaces, open APIs, etc.). 
Interoperability is therefore essential. However, care should be taken in standardization. Indeed, this could 
harm national or European companies, especially smaller ones, which could have interoperability standards 
imposed by large players. This standardization would have financial and technical implications that are rarely 
accessible to everyone. This would have the consequences of preventing the smallest players from finding a 
place in the data market and from innovating. 
The Commissi on should coordinate standardization policy and encourage the different stakeholders to 
participate, for example with concrete compensations in access to data, in order to ensure that players who 
are not already dominant in certain markets get involved  in the standardization of data, which would also 
make it possible to ensure that the standards adopted do not generate excessive implementation costs for 
the economic players concerned. 
For a better portability of cloud services 
It would be necessary to develop data portability of cloud services so that the user businesses have more 
leeway as to the methods of retrieving and processing their data and, consequently, in the choice of their 
provider. 
However, it should be remembered that many standardization initiatives on data portability have been 
launched by private economic players  (SWIPO code of conduct, ISO 19441 standard) and the European 
Commission should take these initiatives into account by involving different stakeholders. It is indeed 
important to underline that these codes of conduct are not always known or validated by users and that 
they cannot therefore be made compulsory without prior discussions. 
An already developed personal data portability 
The GDPR already provides for a very broad right to portability that is rarely used in practice. It therefore 
does not seem necessary to revise the GDPR on this point, but to make portability easier to exercise from 
a practical point of view for individuals. 
It is also necessary to underline the risk for businesses of seeing the portability procedure instrumentalized 
by competing companies in order to recover the personal data of customers and to exploit them 
commercially. 
Protection of databases 
With the numerous technological advances, it seems necessary to adapt the 1996 directive. 
However, if there is a need to strengthen data protection through intellectual property, any review of the 
Database Directive should be carried out with caution to ensure that trade secrets, confidential business 
information or intellectual property rights are not compromised or violated, which would defeat the purpose 
of the Data Act. 
Regarding databases held by public authorities, it would be necessary to provide for an exception to the "sui 
generis" right insofar as, in the spirit of Open Data, these databases must be easily accessible in order to 
promote information sharing and innovation. This exception would also be explained by the lack of economic

Guarantees for non-personal data at international level 
MEDEF supports the efforts of the European Commission to facilitate the free flow of personal and non -
personal data, while guaranteeing their protection and security. Data flows must indeed be accompanied by 
a high level of data protection and security re quirements appropriate to each market, in particular when 
these data are confidential or fall under sovereignty (national security for example) and require a high level 
of security. 
However, data location measures should only be foreseen when justified and proportionate. Indeed, better 
control of data does not necessarily mean forced location of this data  on a terri tory, but greater 
transparency and the possibility of choosing a partner or a trusted service provider. 
In this sense, MEDEF supports the transparency obligations imposed on cloud service providers established 
in the European market, regardless of the location of their head office. 
In order to strengthen confidence in data sharing services, European companies also need solutions that are 
secure and impervious to foreign laws, or at least transparent with regard to requests for access to data by 
foreign authorities or with regard to the risks associated with the legal disclosure of non -personal data to 
authorities in third countries. 
Thus, MEDEF supports options 1 and 2 proposed by the Commission , i.e. both an obligation for data 
processing service providers to inform the professional user each time they receive a request for access to 
their data emanating from the competent authorities of third countries, insofar as this proves possible under 
the foreign law in question (option 1) and an obligation for data processing service providers to notify the 
Commission, with a view to publication on an EU transparency portal provided for this p urpose, all 
extraterritorial laws provided for by foreign law to which they are subject to and which allow access to the 
data that they store or process on behalf of their professional users. 
However, these obligations must be defined in line with other EU le gislative initiatives  as part of its 
European data strategy, in particular the Data Governance Act (DGA) which aims to introduce adequacy 
decisions on intellectual property and trade secrets of third countries. 
In addition, it is essential  to encourage the conclusion of political agreements, in particular between the 
European Union and the United States (CLOUD Act, Privacy Shield), on data in order to establish clear 
procedures regarding the access to data and establish as a principle transparency about requests for access 
to data held by companies.

We greatly welcome the efforts of the European Commission to take the next steps in the European 
Data strategy by looking into enhanced business-to-government data sharing for the public interest, 
support business-to-business data sharing, and evaluate the IPR framework with a view to further 
enhance data access and use. 
 
B2G data sharing 
In the context of B2G data sharing, we note that the public will need to be convinced of the necessity.  
We therefore caution against casting the net too wide by, for example, making B2G data sharing 
compulsory for high -level categories. Next to privacy concerns related to personal data , (non-) 
personal data can be commercially sensitive. A balancing of priv ate and public interest is called for, 
together with predefined criteria for proportionality and reasonableness. There should be a legitimate 
public interest and a clearly described specific task related to that public interest that justifies 
obligatory B2G data sharing, and the hurdle defining what is ‘legitimate’ in relation to both the public 
interest and the task related to it in this case, would need to be set high. Mor eover, the mandate 
should be as specific as possible, to avoid interpretation differences between government and private 
sector, between countries or over time.  
  
And finally, individual companies entering into B2G arrangements face increased public scrutiny and 
reputational risks. If indeed obligatory B2G data sharing is decided, sector wide sharing arrangements 
are preferable, as they diminish such reputational risks and demonstrate the need of data sharing to 
support the public interest (example: data shared by banks with tax authorities).  
 
B2B and data portability 
We believe that data driven innovation is a key enabler for European economic competitiveness, but 
its role is constrained by data siloed within individual business platforms and environments.  Putting 
users in full control of their own data, allowing them to port their data with them from one service 
provider to another, would help increase productivity and efficiency, would weaken network effects 
that currently create winner -take-most/all-dynamics, and would hence bolster competition and 
diversity of digit al markets. The Digital Markets Act take steps to accomplish this for gatekeeper 
platforms, but enhanced, standardised and near -real-time user-initiated data portability should be 
broadened across all (digital) sectors. Such h orizontal data portability across all sectors, for both 
individuals and firms, w ould unlock wide economic benefits and would increase the effectiveness of 
the right to personal data portability provided for by GDPR.  
Trust and privacy are best served by creating an open data framework that is customer-centric. The 
user (individual or firm) shall be in control and decides who gets access to his/her data and in what 
data driven services he/she wants to partake.  
A standardised, EU-wide means for digital identification is a necessary condition for a single seamless 
digital market to emerge, where users can port their data quickly and seamlessly while ensuring 
privacy and security. The EC proposal for a European Digital Identity framework is an important step 
in this regard. Its mandatory adoption by businesses for digital client interaction would help to ensure 
that the digital ID wallet becomes widely adopted and used, not only in the public sector, but also in 
the private space . The issuing of Digital Identity Wallets may help the portability of data, hence we 
would recommend ensuring alignment between these legislative initiatives.

Further remarks regarding specific questions  
 
• Q20: A fairness test of voluntary contractual data sharing might help, though any limits imposed 
on contractual freedom should be well-motivated and kept as limited as possible. Hence the scope 
of such a test, the criteria applied and other parameters determine the desirability of such a test. 
Moreover, w e note that the concept of fairness is also applied in the Digital Markets Act to 
interactions with gatekeeper platforms , and contract negotiations with one party having a 
“stronger bargaining power” are most likely to occur in that context. Care should be taken to align 
the Data Act and Digital Markets Act here, avoiding overlap and unclear and uncertainty.  
 
• Q21: While we agree that model contract terms for voluntary use in B2B data sharing contracts 
might contribute to increasing data sharing between businesses , in our view this may only work 
if the businesses involved negotiate on equal terms. In digital markets, the contract party which 
manages the platform where clients reside, is likely to have market power. This power may include 
the ability to dictate the terms. A fairness test may counterbalance such ability to dictate terms, 
but it is only able to do so in the particular area where the test applies (also to minimize the breach 
of contractual freedom), and not beyond. Instead, market power should be addressed by the DMA. 
Moreover, specifically for with data, we advocate an open data approach obliging businesses in all 
sectors to enable their clients to port their data in a standardised, instant way  (akin to payments 
data portability under PSD2). This would fully empower users, by putting control of their data fully 
in their hands. It would promote competition, as it would force businesses to create value added 
for customers, in order to convince them to allow access to their data. 
 
• Q50, IoT: The initiative to share IoT (including wearables) data should be with the user, not the 
manufacturer or IoT service provider. Moreover, smart devices should not be obliged to use cloud 
services (which would likely be a prerequisite if companies were to be able to access the data at 
all times). Refraining from data sharing, or k eeping and sharing data locally only should remain 
an option (or even the default) for the IoT user.

DATEV position paper on the EU Data Act

DATEV eG welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on the European Commission’s  
upcoming EU Data Act. 
 
The European Commission emphasizes in its impact assessment the overarching objective to 
increase access to and further use of data, so that more public and private actors can  benefit 
from the vast advantages of data. The balance of data use rights is therefore at the heart of 
the Commission's initiative , with the objective of ensuring fairness in the allocation of 
economic value among actors of the data economy. 
 
As a key European IT player, DATEV shares the European Commission’s overall objective to 
foster the availability and use of data and to create a fair data economy. However, some policy 
options in the impact assessment would not deliver the desired outcome and undermine the 
objective of a fairer data economy and of a more effective data usage. In our submission, we 
focus on two key aspects, where further important arguments should be considered: 
The first topic is the potential introduction of a horizontal and mandatory data access right for 
B2G data sharing. The second relates to the potential introduction of a right to portability 
for business users of cloud computing services.

1. B2G Data Sharing 
In its impact assessment , the EU Commission reflects on the possibility of introducing a 
horizontal right for the public sector to access privately held data for a range of public interest 
purposes. We understand and share the Commission’s ambition to leverage B2G data sharing 
in order to serve the public interest. The analysis of data from the private sector can help 
policymakers to make evidence - and data -based decisions w hen it comes to fighting 
epidemics, mitigating climate change, or improving public services for citizens and businesses. 
 
However, horizontal and mandatory B2G data access rights are not the right way forward. 
Horizontal and mandatory B2G data access rights restrict the freedom of companies vis-à-vis 
the state and are only legitimate in certain emergency situations and under strict conditions. 
Instead, the EU should focus on fostering and incentivising voluntary B2G data sharing. The 
Covid-19 pandemic has shown the great potential of voluntary B2G data sharing. As a first 
step, regulators should tackle the many barriers that hinder the benefits of voluntary data 
sharing from being fully reaped. 
 
Mandatory B2G data access rights are onl y legitimate in certain emergency situations 
and under strict conditions. 
In certain emergency situations, such as pandemics, a mandatory access or transfer of 
private sector data may be justified. A horizontal EU legislation could impose general 
conditions and safeguards under which any B2G data access must take place and warrants 
fundamental rights of the EU Charter. Key aspects ought to be considered: 
 
• The achieved objective through the data analysis must be well defined and justified in 
advance in a clear and transparent manner and on a case-by-case basis. It must be clearly 
demonstrated that the intended data access serves as a proportionate means to a specified 
end. This is the only way to democratically legitimise a mandatory data access or transfer. 
• It should be specified in advance, which critical data categories would be covered by a 
mandatory data sharing regime. This approach would allow companies to assess whether 
they might be affected in a  certain emergency situation  and provide adequate  
transparency, as to what data governments classify as critical . This would also enable 
companies to prepare for such crisis situations in advance and to be able to react 
adequately and timely. The definition of such data categories should be agreed at EU level 
in order to avoid (further) fragmentation in the internal market. A diverse group of experts, 
with representatives from the public and private sectors as well as civil society and 
academia could provide recommendations of which data sets to cover. 
• Transparency obligations  should oblige governments and pub lic administrations not 
only to account for their data access requests but use the requested data to limit excessive 
requests. The specificities of the transparency obligations could be tasked to  above-
mentioned expert group.

• Only data not already held by public authorities should be required to be made available 
via B2G data sharing. This should apply even if the data could be accessed more qui ckly 
and cost-effectively via B2G data sharing.  
• It should be guaranteed that the provided data shall not be re -used by third parties, as 
this can result in a competitive disadvantage, create conflicts with member states’ 
competition law or violate the freedom of contract between companies and third parties. 
• Data protection requirements must be taken into account and legal certainty must be 
guaranteed. This applies in particular to complex constellations such as commissioned data 
processing. 
• Data covered by trade or prof essional secrecy must be exempt from any data sharing 
obligations. 
• Companies should not be obliged to collect any additional data. 
• The legislative framework concerning B2G data sharing should have no effect on the back-
end processes of companies. The obligation should be limited to providing the datasets 
created specifically for the B2G data sharing. The technical specifications and standards of 
the B2G data sharing could be further developed by the above-mentioned expert group.  
• Any costs for companies resulting from B2G data sharing must be borne by the public 
sector. It should be kept in mind that the data preparation preceding the data access or 
transfer is a very time- and resource-intensive process. 
• Companies should not be held liable for the data they share. 
 
Legislative steps towards compulsory B2G -data access should only be taken with great 
prudence.  
Binding laws leading to a mandatory access of government and public authorities to private 
sector data restrict the freedom of companies vis-à-vis the state and can constitute a major 
infringement of the fundamental rights of the EU Charter. A “one size fits all” approach, 
which we would consider a general mandate for states to access private sector data, does 
not adequately take into account the interests of data producers and data holders and 
amounts to expropriation.  
 
When discussing a horizontal right of t he public sector to access privately held data several 
aspects should be taken into account: 
 
• The public sector already has a great amount of data, which is often not used or not 
used in the most effective and efficient ways.  
 
As a first step, Member States must ensure  that they make the best use of existing 
data. In order to achieve this, gove rnments and administrations need to have the 
resources and skills in place. Only with the right skills and sufficient resources public 
bodies will be in the position to have an overview of the available datasets, will be able

to handle them and will unders tand their advantages and disadvantages. This would 
also support the EU’s objective to digitise the public administrations. 
 
As a second step , several questions need to be addressed before introducing 
mandatory access rights: 
o How would governments identify the datasets which private undertakings 
hold? 
o How will governments assess if access to the desired datasets would provide 
the desired insights or objective?  
o At which granularity data should be shared: raw data, processed data or data-
driven insights? 
 
• The focus should be on quality instead of quantity . It must be kept in mind that 
having more data helps, but having the right data is the more important requirement 
for a meaningful data analysis. Before introducing extensive mandatory access rules, it 
must be thoroughly examined which data are actually needed.  
 
• Mandatory B2G data transfers already exist. Governments require companies to 
submit, for example, certain accounting data for tax purposes, to report on 
environmental compliance, or to fill in questionnaires for statistical analysis. 
 
Specific access obligations can be better addressed at a sectoral level, as it is already 
happening today. At the sectoral level, it is easier to define specific objectives as well 
as the data needed to achieve those. Required formats, the level and type of 
compensation, and technical interoperability frameworks also differ heavily among 
sectors.  
 
Europe should focus on incentivising and fostering voluntary B2G data sharing.  
When it comes to B2G data sharing, mandatory access rights lie at one end of a spectrum and 
voluntary data sh aring cooperation at the other. In the EU , there are many examples of 
successful B2G data sharing initiatives in place, as the B2G Data Sharing report1 rightly outlines. 
In the context of the Covid-19 crisis many more examples can be added to this list. The reaction 
towards the pandemic has shown that, once the purpose of sharing data is clearly outlined, 
the willingness to share data increases significa ntly. These positive examples highlight the 
potential of voluntary cooperative data exchanges b etween the private and public sector, as 
they are faster and less bureaucratic.  
 
Therefore, we call on the EU Commission to focus on voluntary data sharing and consider 
incentivising governments to acquire data commercially  and increase governments’ 
digital transition.

Today, there are many risks and barriers that hinder the benefits of voluntary data sharing  
from being reaped.  
A crucial barrier for data sharing are the often very high ex-ante costs associated with it. The 
data preparation preceding the d ata access or tran sfer is often a very time - and resource-
intensive process. In fact, in a data science project 79% 2  of the time is spent on data 
preparation.  
Putting in place attractive compensation mechanisms or simply commercial data acquisition 
/ licensing agreements for companies could be a more efficient way to achieve the desired 
objective. These incentives can be direct (e.g. monetary) or indirect (e.g. reputational). 
 
From a business perspective, it is important to eliminate the associated risks in relation to B2G 
data sharing. 
 
• Competition risks: Prohibition for public sector bodies to use the data in competition 
to the companies providing the data.  
• Competition risks: Public authorities should be not allowed to pool data from various 
private and public sources and share the insights with potential competitors. 
• Effective safeguards to eliminate the risks of infringing rights, especially in the areas 
of personal data, trade secrets, IP, competition law and professional secrecy, ought to 
be put in place. 
• Adequate organisational and technical safeguards and audit rights for the affected 
companies to ensure the acquiring authorities have adequate security and safeguards 
in place to minimise the risks of unauthorized or accidental data disclosure to third 
parties. Sharing the data results in a loss of control ove r the data  and increases the 
risks of unauthorised or accidental data disclosures to third parties. 
 
To reduce these risks, legal clarity and certainty is of utmost importance. A horizontal EU 
framework could mitigate these barriers. 
 
 
2. A right to portability for business users of cloud computing services 
The second policy issue we would like to comment on is the right to portability for business 
users of cloud computing services in EU legislation, which the EU Commission discusses in 
the impact assessment on the forthcoming Data Act. We do not see a need for such a 
right.  
 
We would like to emphasise that it is essential that the technical specifications for data 
portability should be driven by cloud users and cloud providers, as they know best their 
needs. Furthermore, several initiatives, as Gaia-X or the SWIPO Code of Conducts, are 
pursuing the goal of data portability. However, as these initiatives are in the beginning, it is 
too early to fully assess their impact and effectiveness, and to decide on potential alternative 
legislative actions.

Instead, governments should make commitments to use e.g. Gaia-X for their cloud services 
and data processing demands to ensure market demand that would accelerate the 
developments of such offers. 
 
Data portability is important for a functioning data economy. The technical specifications 
should be driven by the industry. 
As European IT-cooperative supporting European values and the European way of combining 
social and economic objectives, DATEV supports all measures that reduce the few but 
substantial negative consequences of the platform economy such as vendor-lock in or 
winner-takes-all markets. However, a right to portability for business users of cloud 
computing services in EU legislation is not a necessary requirement for an effective cloud 
switching.  
 
Most cloud providers already offer processes to facilitate data portability in order to be 
competitive and to be compatible with relevant ecosystems. A right to data portability is 
unnecessary and would lead to overregulation that undermines the dynamics of the market. 
Should the Commission nevertheless decide to introduce the right to portability, it should 
only be introduced as a high-level principle.  
 
We strongly advocate for an approach that puts achieving data portability on a technical 
level into industry’s hands. Effective data portability requires a great effort when it comes 
to standardisation. Standardisation of the tagging and description of data in order to clearly 
define them in terms of content and semantics for further automatic processing and linking 
is the key to data portability. Additionally, standardised interfaces and data formats are of 
great importance. These APIs, open standards and interoperable data formats are, even 
today, already developed and implemented by the industry itself. The most recent example 
for companies coming together to explore new standards and agree on those is GAIA-X –
supported by several governments. This project should be given time to deliver. In a nutshell, 
the precise technical specifications for data portability should be driven by cloud users and 
cloud providers, as they know best their needs and the technical feasibility.

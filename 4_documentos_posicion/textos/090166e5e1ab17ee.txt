 
 
 
LSEG’s response to the European Commission’s Public consultation on the Data Act  
 
About LSEG 
LSEG (London Stock Exchange Group) is a diversified global financial markets infrastructure and data 
business, headquartered in London, with significant operations in Europe, North America, and Asia. 
With extensive experience, deep knowledge and worldwide presence across financial markets, we 
enable businesses and economies around the world to fund innovation, manage risk and create jobs.  
At LSEG, we help to drive financial stability, empower economies and enable customers to create 
sustainable growth through three business divisions: data and analytics, capital markets and post-trade.  
LSEG has major operations across the EU, providing services across all 27 Member States, including 
trading venues, post -trade, risk management, information and technology services. In the EU, our 
regulated entities contribute significantly to Capital Markets Union. 
 
Our response  
We support t he European Commission’s goal of promoting more data sharing across sectors and 
Member States and we welcome the opportunity to comment on the European Commission’s Public 
Consultation on the Data Act. 
As a global leader in data and analytics, our aggregating, normalising, storing and disseminating of data 
for customers improves market efficiency and  lowers participation costs for all market participants, 
including European retail customers. We are committed to use the power of data to advance public 
efforts and tackle societal problems such as the green transition and the fight against financial crime. 
We see the creation of a single market for data as a key step to encourage innovation and incentivise 
a more globally competitive EU economy. Our overarching reflections on the Data Act are structured 
around the three priorities : creating a healthy legal framework  based on voluntary data sharing 
arrangements, encouraging data sharing for the public interest and promoting the international flow of 
data.  
Data sharing for the public interest  
Data sharing can help support broader societal objectives such as climate change and financial crime 
We support in principle the Commission’s goal of promoting more data sharing between the public and 
private sectors. We believe that for the financial services sector  the focus should be on increasing the 
availability of data that is non -financial but intersects with the financial system in ways that can further 
the alignment of the financial sector with broader societal objectives – particularly in relation to tackling 
widely shared problems such as climate change and financial crime. 
As a founding member of the Global Coalition to Fight Financial Crime 1, we are already promoting 
public-private partnership and data sharing  to fight the broad and growing range of financial crime, 
modern-day slavery, green crime and terrorism . In this context, data sharing between the public and 
private sectors is helping us to help detect, assess and minimize the impact of financial crime and how 
it affects governments, the industry and the most vulnerable in society.  
Tackling climate change and broader sustainability challenges is another area in which data sharing 
can be of great benefit. To support this effort, with  partners including the World Economic Forum and

Data sharing in a B2G context should not be mandated 
 
In our view, data sharing in a B2G context should not be mandated. Instead, the industry would favour 
having a flexible, voluntary framework to promote data sharing between the private and  the public 
sector. This framework would need to be underpinned by legal safeguards and transparency from public 
authorities over the intended use of the data, relevant measures to protect commercially sensitive 
information, and limitations on its use (providing safeguards for specific usage rights). The Commission 
will need to ensure the implementation of appropriate levels of security and privacy by the public sector 
for the storage, access to and processing of data in order to increase trust between businesses or civil 
society organisations and the public sector.  For instance, any personal data collected for commercial 
purposes that is to be included in B2G sharing will have to be limited to specific categories of data and 
have their public interest grounds defined in line with GDPR.  Additionally, any efforts to enhance the 
availability of data for use and re -use should be with full respect to the rights of third parties, namely 
intellectual property rights.  
Data sharing in a B2G context should consider the cost implications of data processing 
Any measures directed at enhanc ing data sharing with public bodies  will need to consider the cost 
implications of processing the data. Even when data is shared free of commercial costs, there is still a 
core cost to provide data that needs to be passed along (‘keep the lights on’ cost). Data sharing 
agreements that give business effective incen tives such as fair monetary compensation (covering the 
inherent costs to processing the data)  will help incentivise B2G data sharing collaboration. Moreover, 
we suggest that the Commission al so looks to explore how non-monetary compensation such as tax 
incentives, increased know-how and public recognition programmes, could help incentivise data sharing 
in this context. 
Ongoing efforts by governments to enhance data sharing and maximise the use of data will be critical 
to powering the transition to a healthy, sustainable and digitalised economy and , in exceptional cases, 
access to certain products of services on a complim entary basis or at a preferential rate could help 
support wider economic or societal global crisis . For instance, LSEG supported the efforts of EU 
governments with complimentary access to products and services in response to the Covid -19 crisis 
(such as Refinitiv Workspace and Eikon Web).  
Public-private partnerships can perform a key role to incentivise information sharing 
In summary, voluntary, cooperative solutions are most appropriate for transfers of data to the public 
sector. Public-private partnerships have proved an efficient solution to incentivise collaboration for 
information sharing – a good example is the work by the Global Legal Entity Identifier Foundation 
(GLEIF)3. GLEIF’s work was an industry -led effort to share data with the goal of understanding legal 
entity hierarchical ownership to avoid credit risk exposures . GLEIF, through its oversight committee – 
formed by regulators and by market suppliers – has developed a set of standards on data sharing that 
work for the public and the private sector. 
Business to business data sharing 
Voluntary schemes for B2B data sharing are preferred in the financial services sector  
The starting point for B2B data sharing should be the creation of voluntary schemes that can safeguard 
contractual freedom, respect intellectual property rights, and align with existing sectoral frameworks.

Companies are already sharing their data in the financial services sector, where the expanding value,  
importance and use of data, alongside the increasing use of technology to supply financial services; 
offers a range of benefits – such as greater consumer choice, enhanced risk management capabilities, 
and increased efficiency.  
The financial services sector is highly developed in its use of data, having a well-structured ecosystem 
where the value of services is determined by the market and subject to intense competition. Contractual 
solutions between actors in the financial space are also operating well i n practice today. As such, we 
believe that sector-specific rules on data sharing should only be mandated where market failures have 
been identified and cannot be addressed through existing industry-led initiatives.  
Voluntary, industry-led solutions are the way forward in B2B data sharing  
Companies are required pursuant to existing competition laws to apply fair terms in contracts with their 
clients. As B2B relationships have long been underpinned by freedom of contract, having mandated 
model contract terms could have a significant financial impact on firms and would be costly to regulate.  
There are already some good examples of voluntary, industry led solutions, such  as the efforts by the 
Cloud Data Management Capability 4 (CDMC) work group to develop standards of key controls and 
automation. This voluntary, industry -led effort created a framework that helps manage data risk for 
sensitive data in Cloud and Hybrid Cloud environments and sets standards and guiding principles for 
how data gets managed in the cloud.  
We note that the objective of enhancing data availability could be helpful for certain strategic economic 
sectors. However, important consideration should be given by the Commission to the cost implications 
for businesses from gathering and formatting such data, as well as to their appropriate reimbursement 
depending on the purpose of the data access request.  The EU Data Act should find the right balance  
between respecting existing sectoral frameworks, enabling interaction between sectors to harness the 
potential of the private  sector as a force for societal good,  and rewarding the provision of B2B data 
sharing services fairly.  
Enabling the free flow of data globally will create opportunities for the EU economy 
As the Commission endeavors to realise the potential of the EU’s data economy, it is essential that any 
legislation which limits cross-border data access or attempts to ‘localise’ data storage is avoided.  The 
safeguarding of international data flows promotes European companies’ competitiveness , as they 
operate in a connected environment that goes beyond EU borders.  It also allows them use larger 
datasets and take advantage of new tools such as Artificial In telligence and Machine Learning, as well 
as opportunities created by cross -border cloud computing, such as combining data sets for richer 
analysis and reducing costs by accessing flexible computing power in line with business needs . 
Moreover, free international data flows are crucial for risk management processes, as they enable firms 
to track their operations across multiple jurisdictions and manage risks consistently and 
comprehensively, using data from multiple sources. 
Given the benefits of harnessing cross -border data, localisation measures create incentives for 
innovative companies to relocate. Losing innovative companies to other jurisdictions would of course 
detriment the EU economy’s growth and competitiveness and  would ultimately undermine the goals 
outlined in the EU’s Strategy for Data. Indeed, the data ecosystem has its own geography, driven by 
the technology and economics that underlie it, and the ability to aggregate, store, process, and transmit 
data across borders is critical to the future development of the EU’s financial sector and wider economy.  
The Data Act and the wider EU strategy on Data should support cross-border data uses beyond the EU 
and focus on enabling the free flow of data globally. The more the European Union is able to coordinate 
its efforts with other jurisdictions, the more it will be able to lead globally in the use of data for the 
common good.

Conclusion 
In conclusion, LSEG supports the European Commission’s goal to create a framework to facilitate data 
sharing in business-to-business and business-to-government contexts. It is our view that this framework 
will be most effective if it is based on B2G voluntary data sharing agreements, relies on industry-based 
solutions for B2B data sharing and avoids any attempts to localise data storage.  
We hope that you will find LSEG’s input useful and remain at your disposal for any additional 
clarifications.

OVHCLOUD’S POSITION ON THE DATA ACT 
 
 
OVHcloud, leading European cloud provider, welcomes the opportunity to contribute to the public 
consultation on the Data Act and supports the European Commission’s ambition to promote more 
fairness and legal certainty for data sharing and processing in a B2G and B2B environment. 
 
Ensuring consistency with its long-term engagements and current activities, OVHcloud encourages 
the establishment of a fair European data economy, based on three pillars: fair access and sharing, 
fair competition and fair protection. Such a prerequisite is needed to ensure a trustworthy 
environment for public and private structures when it comes to sharing, switching, storing and 
processing their data to or w ithin the cloud, in line with European rules such as data protection, 
respect of intellectual property and trade secrets. 
 
Only an upcoming, comprehensive, and innovative framework  built at European level  – including 
the Data Act – can lead to more fairnes s and be the architect of a trusted digital economy, 
particularly for the cloud sector . Acting quickly and having a comprehensive set of frameworks for 
the cloud sector is expected by 2/3 of business decision -makers, who are confident about a 
strengthening of the European regulation in the near future1. 
 
 
Fair access and sharing: guarantee clear fairness and transparency principles to stimulate B2G 
and B2B data sharing 
 
OVHcloud supports the objective of increasing B2B and B2G data sharing to the benefit of European 
businesses, public services and ultimately citizens. But clear principles and regulatory certainty for 
all parties involved are needed to this end.  
 
Encouraging B2G and B2B data sharing must be seen positively, under the consideration that any 
data sharing mandate should take into account the data controller and data processor distinction  
and responsibilities, whereby a data processor does not own and control the customer’s data. This 
distinction, especially in case of a mandatory shar ing of data could tackle potential liability issues. 
One way to address this would be to mandate that the data owner is informed by the cloud provider 
of the conditions under which data will be processed. 
 
OVHcloud supports the use of model contract terms.  A contractual and, if appropriate, a voluntary 
approach should be the basis for B2G and B2B data sharing. Not having an approach that is 
compulsory by nature allows better consideration of a private actor’s situation, abilities to respond 
to the demand and business impact.  
 
For B2G sharing situations, the concept of “public interest” should not only be defined and linked to 
a context -specific approach, but also take in consideration the costs and potential risks for the 
private contractor: often only few of them have the size to create and provide the requested data 
without affecting their business. 
 
The promotion of open-source standards reduces barriers to data sharing, ensuring both providers 
and users have more opportunity to use, modify or analyze the shared data. OVHcloud has for years 
supported the conversion to open -source solutions and software, for instance within the Open

To create more transparency, OVHcloud would support the following measures: 
• Fairness and transparency principles, including the obligation to include in the applicable 
terms and conditions in a clear and exhaustive manner: 
o The purpose of the data processing especially if the cloud provider intends to re-use 
the data for its own purpose (an exhaustive list should be provided); 
o The location of the data (including the location from where the data may be 
remotely accessed); 
o The list of data recipients including any sub-processors and their location; 
o The technical and organizational measures implemented to protect the data. 
• Data re -use should be forbidden, except under express authorization by the owner in an 
exhaustive manner (i.e., no general right of re-use). 
• Right of audit available to the owner. 
 
 
Fair competition: Ensures portability and supports a “multicloud” approach 
 
OVHcloud believes in a digital economy based on sovereignty, interoperability, reversibility, and 
portability, that cloud computing can tackle if a comprehensive and adapted regulatory framework 
exists. It is the backbone of OVHcloud’s action since 1999, and we are convinced that a concrete 
application of such a framework would allow the EU economy to embrace cloud computing services 
and therefore bring to private and public actors the tools to advance modernization and efficiency. 
 
A successful digital transformation is when users have access to reliable, secure and trustworthy 
choices regarding their needs and suppliers' choices for both digital services and infrastructures.  
 
Data sharing now covers a broad field of aspects and forms and are part of our daily digital life. In a 
B2B2C environment, sharing data is critical to improve a product, launch a new service, reduce costs, 
under the condition to keep the control on the dat a and not to face restricting choices. Lock -in 
situations, where customers and users are prevented from the advantage of switching to solutions 
or services that better fit their needs and aspirations , are indeed one of the cloud market’s biggest 
issues.  
 
Today, the lack of a regulatory framework that would ensure data interoperability, reversibility, 
portability and fairness in the EU digital market still inhibits cloud adoption in Europe. To ensure a 
fair level playing field, the EU should adopt a set of regulatory frameworks and recognized standards 
that not only put unfair practices in the cloud market to an end, but also allow businesses a 
trustworthy data porting and switching through, by having the possibility to freely choose solutions 
and providers that suit best to accelerate their digital transformation and economic growth. 
 
Self-regulatory ongoing efforts that improve data interoperability , greater transparency and 
encourage the emergence of a European ecosystem already exist and must be preserve d, like the 
IaaS SWIPO Code of Conduct . But it should not be a one -size-fits-all approach: it must be

complementary to a global and balanced approach, including self-regulatory initiatives, codes of

Facilitating data portability, interoperability and reversibility allows organizations to have tailor -fit 
cloud solutions that reflect their diverse requirements. On this basis, the Data Act is clearly a central 
piece of promoting a “multicloud” approach, combining levers that would prevent lock-in situations 
and a lack of data control. 
 
While self -regulation has been the instrument favored so far in the field of B2B sharing, the 
persistence of lock -in situations shows the need to take extra steps towards f airer competition in 
the cloud market: a balanced mix between self-regulatory initiatives, codes of conducts and legally 
binding rights would clarify the respective responsibilities and duties in a provider/user relation.  
 
More specifically and in line with the public consultation on the Data Act: 
• Building on the SWIPO Code of conduct and the principles it formulates could be made 
binding for all cloud services offered in Europe. The code of conduct could be supplemented 
by Standard Contractual Clauses tra nslating its requirement into contractual elements, 
which would improve the negotiating position of cloud users; 
• Legislation could include high -level principles that would recognize the right for cloud 
service portability, as well as more specific set of conditions of contractual, technical, 
commercial and economic nature, including specification of the necessary elements to 
enable data portability. EU legislation, by letting the industry develop standards and 
formats - like with GAIA-X - and by recognizing them, could contribute to increase the use of 
interoperable and open formats by the users. 
 
OVHcloud invites the European Commission to support this “multicloud” approach in its future 
policies, for instance in the future EU Cloud Rulebook that should ga ther all initiatives, from codes 
of conduct and certification scheme to ongoing and upcoming legislation. 
 
Lastly, d espite the need to have tailored rules, we believe that some basic principles can be 
applicable in almost all situations, such as the following measures: 
• The data owner should be in a position to retrieve their data at any time. The regulation 
should prohibit any possibility for the cloud provider to lock-in and retain the data. 
• At the end of the contract, the cloud provider should delete the data  without retention 
(except where required under applicable law). 
 
 
Fair protection: provide efficient safeguards for non-personal data in an international context 
 
OVHcloud very much welcomes the fact that the public consultation on the Data Act addresses the 
issue of safeguards for non -personal data in international contexts . According to a recent study 3, 
only a few non-EU hyperscalers control more than two thirds of the EU IaaS market, exposing user 
data to potential legal and industrial risks. 
 
As global competition intensifies and the volume of sensitive data shared and processed, 
particularly in the cloud, increases exponentially, more and more European public and private 
organizations are becoming aware of the importance of governing their IT systems, the data they 
handle and, finally, choosing a cloud provider that guarantees data security and sovereignty.

According to a recent survey4, the most important criteria for organizations to select a cloud provider 
are data security and data sovereignty, with almost 90% considering data sovereignty as a n 
important driver during the decision-making process. It is also noteworthy that the data sovereignty 
comprehension is mainly driven by two dimensions: GDPR compliance and the data center location.  
 
Therefore, shar ing of data between the EU and third countries requires  efficient safeguards to 
protect Europeans’ non-personal data while ensuring economic growth. 
 
A specific obligation at EU level might be necessary for cloud service providers, to prevent all types 
of European organizations from having to comply with data access requests by third countries’ 
authorities. This obligation could take the shape of additional technical and/or legal measures when 
a request for access would be in conflict with EU or national laws. Such safeguards are particularly 
relevant in cases of sensitive non -personal data (e.g., those related to national security and to the 
public interest). 
 
For instance, a concrete safeguard is the use of certifications that improve the security, performance 
and sustainability of data, like France’s SecNumCloud certification. At the EU level, the ongoing work 
of the European Network and Information Security Agency (ENISA) on cybersecurity certification for 
cloud services is a concrete example of a global measure that would protect sensitive data from any 
request for access coming from a third country. 
 
Another safeguard is to avoid any legal uncertainty provoked by the recent “Schrems II” decision5 
that highlighted the incompatibility of US regulations with GDPR principles, which subject 
companies and public bodies transferring Europeans’ personal data to non -EU companies’ servers 
(even if located in Europe) to legal and industrial risks. To this end, to allow a fair protection for cloud 
service users, OVHcloud supports the following measures: 
• Refrain from data transfers that are not compliant with EU or Member State laws. 
• Refrain from data transfers outside the EU or to a third party without prior information and 
consent of the data owner. 
• Non-European entities (not incorporated nor established) shall be subjected to the Data Act 
if they target the European market and/or process data of European companies,  
• Exclusive EU jurisdiction should be mandatory for sensitive data  (for instance relating to 
national security and defense, key infrastructures such as energy, digital networks, 
communications, hospitals, etc.) 
 
OVHcloud aspires to be the architect of a trusted cloud and has developed an integrated business 
model allowing full control of the value chain and respect for customer data, while fostering 
innovation. Europe's ability to innovate would be severely disrup ted without data sovereignty and 
freedom of choice for EU organizations.

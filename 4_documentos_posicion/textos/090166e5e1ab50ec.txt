ANNEX TO CONSULTATION RESPONSE 
1 
CONSULTATION – Data Act 
 
ANNEX 
Expanded answers to the questions of the public consultation. 
 
>> Section 1: 
Questions “Have you or has your organisation experienced difficulties/encountered issues 
when requesting or responding to requests for access to data, in the context of B2G data 
sharing for the public interest? 
Should the EU take additional action so that public sector bodies can access and re-use 
private sector data, when this data is needed for them to carry out their tasks in the public 
interest purpose?” 
DIGITALEUROPE members have not experienced major issues responding to B2G data 
access requests. DIGITALEUROPE generally believes that there is no need for the EU to 
take additional action regarding business-to-government data sharing. If such action was 
proposed and an EU framework created, it should not lead to any new obligations for 
companies, but only harmonise the procedures for making and processing data requests 
across Member States, particularly when there is a public interest purpose. Any potential EU 
framework should also take into account the sensitive and confidential nature of most 
business data and provide safeguards protecting the business interests of companies 
(against undue requests, risks of data theft/loss, etc.). 
 
Q: “What safeguards for B2G data sharing would be appropriate?” 
As noted, data covered by mandatory B2G sharing must be specified in advance in a 
catalogue, and its purpose specified. Purpose limitation and data minimisation are key to 
ensure that only the data that is necessary for the required purpose need be provided. 
Only data not already held by public authorities should be required to be made available. 
Finally, business-sensitive data, or giving insights into proprietary technology should not be 
part of any mandatory data sharing provisions. 
 
Q: “Which of the following types of financial compensation would incentivise you to engage 
in a B2G data-sharing collaboration for the public interest” 
Market prices would be preferred overall, but depending on the specific collaboration 
partnerships between companies and the public sector, there may be different financial 
compensation arrangements possible, including marginal costs for dissemination and/or fair 
return on investment (ROI).

>> Section 2: 
Q: “Do you agree that the application of a ‘fairness test’, to prevent unilateral imposition by 
one party of unfair contractual terms on another, could contribute to increasing data sharing 
between businesses?” 
The concept of “fairness test” as mentioned in this consultation is excessively vague. In 
general, we believe that contractual negotiations should not be forced. Compulsory access 
or terms and conditions requirements can severely impact incentives to invest and innovate. 
It is also difficult to understand how such fairness test would interplay with upcoming 
Common European data spaces, and with the data sharing intermediary concept and 
associated provisions already set out in the Data Governance Act to support data sharing. 
 
Q: “Do you agree that model contract terms for voluntary use in B2B data sharing contracts 
could contribute to increasing data sharing between businesses?” 
Voluntary model contract templates designed by the industry can be an interesting tool to 
facilitate data sharing. The possibility to use model contracts should not lead to any data 
sharing obligations or restrictions to contractual data sharing arrangements. 
 
Q: “Do you agree that horizontal access modalities based on variations of fair, reasonable 
and non-discriminatory conditions applicable to data access rights, established in specific 
sectors, could contribute to increasing data sharing between businesses?” 
Horizontal data access modalities should be carefully assessed, as promoting stringent or 
intrusive rules could have the effect of freezing development of data sharing as it would 
overall remove incentive to share and innovate. Before considering any such modalities, it is 
crucial to precisely assess potential existing issues and market failures at sectoral level and 
factor in other instruments such as data sharing intermediaries, Common European data 
spaces, etc 
 
Q: “Regarding data access at fair, reasonable, proportionate, transparent and non-
discriminatory conditions, which of the following elements do you consider most relevant to 
increase data sharing?” 
On top of the elements selected, in some sectors, such as healthcare, legal uncertainty 
around the delineation of non-personal and personal data (even on pseudo-/anonymisation) 
is a real barrier and would, if removed, increase data sharing and fairness. 
Additionally, on top of incentivising data sharing, it is important to support the industry in 
identifying (or when relevant, developing) relevant standards regarding data processing, 
documentation, interoperability, portability, etc. EU programmes such as Horizon Europe or 
Digital Europe could help support such activities. 
 
>> Section 3: 
Q: “Do you consider that smart contracts could be an effective tool to technically implement 
the data access and use in the context of co-generated IoT data, in particular where the 
transfer is not only one-off but would involve some form of continuous data sharing?”

There are two challenges with the use of smart contracts to facilitate IoT data exchange: 
scale and governance. 
- Most contemporary blockchain technologies are struggling with high levels of data 
processing. 
- Depending on whether blockchains are centralised or decentralised, different governance 
issues may arise. 
The Commission could support industry standardisation and research initiatives to ensure 
that smart contracts could be widely used in the future for data sharing. 
 
Q: “Do you consider that when individuals request data portability from businesses, smart 
contracts could be an effective tool to technically implement data transfers, in particular 
where the transmission is not only one-off but would involve some form of continuous data 
sharing?” 
While smart contracts could facilitate portability as they benefit from the guaranteed integrity 
of the blockchain, the potential volume of data exchanged would create a massive burden, 
since the entire data payload would have to become part of that blockchain. This would 
impose significant burden on blockchain processing and require extensive compute 
processing capability. 
If smart contracts were to be used for such purposes, standard terminology and templates 
should be developed by the industry to ease business and customer use. 
 
>> Section 5: 
Q: “Do you consider there is a need to establish a right to portability for business users of 
cloud computing services in EU legislation?” 
Any requirements on data portability must be proportionate, take into account sectoral 
differences and different uses of cloud services. We would advise against mandating 
technical specifications for data portability as they would complicate the ongoing 
implementation of the SWIPO CoCs and could stifle innovation and regulatory divergences 
in global markets. 
 
Q: “What legislative approach would be the most suitable in your opinion, if the data 
portability right for cloud users would be laid down in an EU legislation?” 
We support high-level EU principles recognising the right for cloud service portability (which 
has become industry practice), but we consider that they should not mandate any technical 
specifications or implementation details, which should be left to market participants to 
determine (providers and users). 
Data portability in a cloud context cannot be compared to data portability under the GDPR 
where data subjects have the right to receive their personal data in a structured, commonly 
used and machine-readable format. Cloud portability is a much more complex operation that 
can include a significant volume of information not usually structured thus requiring technical 
support and project planning. Rather than creating an artificial new portability right, industry 
codes of conduct such as SWIPO and open standards (developed amongst industry-led

level of complexity and ensure fairness and transparency. 
A high-level principles approach could be part of the upcoming Cloud Rulebook and should

Q: “Do you consider that formally requesting European standardisation development 
organisations to design such standards or the necessary APIs would be an appropriate 
solution?” 
The Commission should rely on global, industry-driven standards to provide the appropriate 
technical solutions, such as ISO/IEC 19941. 
 
>> Section 8: 
Q: “How likely do you think it is that a cloud computing service or other data processing 
service provider that is processing data on your company’s/organisation’s behalf may be 
subject to an order or request based on foreign legislation for the mandatory transfers of 
your company/organisation data?” 
Based on the experience of DIGITALEUROPE members, it is unlikely that cloud providers 
may be subject to an order or request based on foreign legislation for the mandatory 
transfers of a company’s data. However, if it were to happen, this would be considered a risk 
for companies. 
 
Q: “Do you consider that such an order or request may lead to the disclosure and/ or 
misappropriation of a trade secret or other confidential business information?” 
As explained above (question 8.1), there is a low likelihood for such requests. If a request 
was to happen, this could however lead to disclosure of confidential business information or 
trade secrets, and could therefore seriously impact companies. This would also depend 
upon the third country concerned and the fairness and capacity of its judiciary system to 
protect companies’ data. 
 
Q: “In light of risk assessment of your data processing operations as well as in the context of 
applicable EU and national legal frameworks, do you consider that your 
company/organisation data should be stored and otherwise processed” 
Companies should have the possibility to store/process their data anywhere in the world as 
they prefer, in compliance with applicable legislation. Data localisation requirements should 
be avoided and the Data Act should facilitate, rather than restrict, global cross-border data 
sharing.

risk for European companies stemming from the request for access by foreign jurisdiction 
authorities to their data?”

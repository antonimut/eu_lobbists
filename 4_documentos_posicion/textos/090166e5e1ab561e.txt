Considering the – often – complex discussion and necessary understanding of data related 
technological innovation, the character limits in the response template may not give 
sufficient opportunity to provide feedback within the proper context. To provide such 
essential context, ultimately allowing the reader a better overview and understanding  of 
the issues in question, the EBF invites the Commission to consider the following Annex. It 
shall be read complementary and as expanding on, the answers provided in the survey . 
The Annex of the paper include s potential use cases for cross -sectoral data sharing from 
the banking perspective.  
 
I. Business-to-government data sharing for the public interest 
We would like to underline that banks already respon d to many requests from 
different regulatory institutions (e.g. data sharing with tax departments in the event 
of tax fraud, frequent reporting of credit and credit risk data or statistical payments data 
to central banks or supervisory authorities).  
Any extension needs to factor in already generated data or inf ormation, 
standardisation of the requests for data, ensure the right prioritisation of 
requests and be limited to publicly available data . We also note that under the 
European Commission’s Digital Finance Strategy 1, the Commission plans to present a 
Strategy on Supervisory Data. The Roadmap for this Strategy included an estimate of 4 
to 12 billion euros per years for reporting entities to comply with the main reporting 
requirements2, underlining that the financial sector is one of the most data 
intensive sectors when it comes to data sharing with authorities.  
The issue of banking secrecy is also crucial to keep in mind. The recent example of the 
Lactalis case in France highlighted the question of lifting banking secrecy without 
safeguards: the fact that t he public authorities had an injunction to communicate 
customers' bank card invoices in order to identify customers who had stashed 
contaminated Lactalis milk posed a problem with regard to the confidentiality of the data 
communicated.

Finally, the definit ion of public interest is key in the discussion on B2G data 
sharing. A definition is proposed in the consultation, but as the discussion moves forward, 
this will need careful consideration, also in light of the specific situation in different sectors.  
Please see below for the further information and explanations to the questions posed on 
business-to-government data sharing for the public interest in the Survey. 
 
➢ In which of the following areas do you think that, for specific use-cases 
with a clear public interest, B2G data sharing should be compulsory, with 
appropriate safeguards? 
While for the first option under this question – ‘Data (e.g. mobility data from Telecom 
operators, loss data from insurance companies) for emergencies and crisis managemen t, 
prevention, and resilience’ – we select that sharing should be compulsory, we stress that 
the specific use cases should be clearly defined, e.g. such as during a pandemic. 
This is particularly important for non -personal data, as for personal data the ca ses are 
clearly regulated under the GDPR.  
These cases should be clearly restricted to ‘state of need’ situations where 
sharing data with the public sector may help crises management and among other 
points, help to return to a stable business environment.  
Furthermore, an in depth discussion needs to take place, with all relevant stakeholders 
involved, on what “public interest” means in the context of the specific use cases.  
 
➢ What safeguards for B2G data sharing would be appropriate?   
Expanding on our answer in the consultation, we think the following safeguards would also 
be appropriate for B2G Data Sharing: 
• Confidentiality: Limitations on how the data can be used, and requirements for 
the data (or specific fields or attributes) to be anonymised where necessary;  
• Transfer mechanisms: Harmonised transfer mechanisms, with corresponding API 
standards, for the secure and efficient transit of data; 
• Consistent descriptions: Standards for the consolidation of different data types 
across (and within) industry sectors; 
• Trust: Appropriate checks on the quality of data submitted to provide assurances 
on the suitability of its use;  
• Purpose and storage limitation: data should be used for the clearly specified 
reason/use case (see above) and there should be limitations regarding how long 
public bodies may use or store specific datasets before having to destroy  them; 
and 
• Transparent reporting on how the public authority has used the data , how they 
will protect the data and other relevant elements. 
 
➢ Which of the following types of non -monetary compensation would 
incentivise you to engage in a B2G data -sharing collaboration for the 
public interest (select all that apply): 
In addition to our responses in the survey,  the following types of non -monetary 
compensation would incentivise members to engage in B2G data-sharing collaboration for 
the public interest:  
• Increased public data availability for business purposes; 
• Own benefits for provider from a better data basis which is (publicly) accessible;

• Reduced effort for providing additional information by using standardised formats 
or transfer mechanisms. 
However, clarity on intellectual property must be a central part of any B2G  
collaboration. For example, the banking sector, with the notion of banking secrecy, 
should not be treated like other sectors.  
 
II. Business-to-business data sharing  
➢ Please describe the type of data shared, and the type of businesses with 
whom it is shared. 
The financial services sector is one of the most data intensive sectors in the economy. In 
addition to mandatory obligations and data sharing for fraud prevention purposes within 
regulatory boundaries, members share , for example, non-personal aggregated data with 
companies under commercial agreements.  
 
➢ Why does your company share data with other companies? 
Members share data with other companies for several reasons. These include mandatory 
obligations (e.g. under the PSD2 framework ), research and innovatio n, data sharing 
partnerships and even data transfers for social good. When sharing data, it is always done 
in accordance with GDPR and relevant regulations such as banking secrecy.  
 
➢ What benefits from data sharing do you expect to be reaped in your sector? 
Data relevant for financial services is not exclusively in the hands of the financial sector . 
It goes well beyond to other sectors, private or public . In the view of EBF members, the 
greatest, potential benefits would arise from cross-sectional data sharing.  
This can i ncrease competitiveness in digital markets  by facilitating the  creation of new 
data-driven innovative services and products  for customer s (including through more 
personalized services adapted to their needs) , reducing lock -in effects, and putting the 
user at the centre in full control of their data.  
Cross-sectoral data sharing would also provide more additional valuable sources to train 
AI models and also help to bring a better understanding of underlying risks or counterparty 
behaviour in order to improve our risk management. Other areas where data sharing can 
unlock opportunities include process automation, prevention, and assistance (insurance), 
and the fight against fraud. 
 
➢ Has your company experienced difficulties/encountered issues when 
requesting access to other companies’ data? What was the nature of such 
difficulties? 
Members have experienced difficulties/encount ered issues when requesting access to 
other companies’ data. The types of difficulties (in addition to the ones selected in the 
survey and indicated in “other section”) include: 
• Difficulties in accessing data from other non-financial organisations, either because 
data is unavailable, or access is denied; 
• Lack of standardization and appropriate security measures;  
• Difficulties faced by financial institutions accessing certain data types where other 
organisations have greater bargaining power. 
We would like to emphasize once more that d ata sharing must be driven across multiple 
sectors and not just within sectors (e.g. financial services). Increased standardisation of

data types, and formats, across sectors, will be essential to facilitate this effective data 
sharing. 
 
➢ What, in you view, could be the benefits or risks of the options mentioned 
in the three previous questions, for example in relation to incentives for 
data collection, competitiveness and administrative burden.  
At this stage there is too much uncertainty on the content of the fa irness test 
and who will perform it to give a definitive response. If sharing data on a contractual 
basis, then courts competent for contract disputes will be able to judge on this.  The 
definition of “business” is also important to consider . Shall they be obliged to share their 
data with all types of businesses including self -employees having suddenly decided they 
would make a good use of bank data?  
Overall, most EBF members believe that voluntary data sharing alone is not sufficient to 
lift the potential of a data economy. 
 
➢ Regarding data access at fair, reasonable, proportionate, transparent and 
non-discriminatory conditions, which of the following elements do you 
consider most relevant to increase data sharing?  
In addition to the choices indicated in the survey response, availability of stand ards for 
interoperability that would allow data sharing and exploi tation at a low marginal cost (in 
terms of time and money) is  an important element to increase data sharing, as  are 
structures enabling the use of data for computation without actually disclosing the data is 
another important element to increase data sharing.  O the latter, this is an option which 
is especi ally investigated for federated learning based on distributed data of different 
owners. 
Availability of an impartial dispute settlement mechanism, harmonized at European level, 
would also be beneficial as the stakeholders of these ecosystems may come under different 
national jurisdictions, European and non-European, thus complicating the rules of contract 
law (in matters of jurisdiction / law governing the contract). This is indeed a matter of 
legal security for  exchanges and compliance with the application of European data 
protection rules (not just GDPR) and the conditions for exchanging this data. 
We also stress the importance of establishing the distinction between observed and 
inferred data. Data access and data sharing should be available to both individuals and 
firms and cover all observed and input data. That is, data that a user actively provides 
or produces through their use of a good or service.  This would ensure that all users 
have control over their raw data, while protecting the value creation performed by 
the firm holding the data, who may have inferred or derived further insights that 
they should not be obligated to share.

would like to underline that  the assessment of this tool /the use of smart

concept or pilots for Distributed Ledger Technologies (DLT) that make use 
of smart contracts?  
Members indicated that they are using smart contracts or that they have been involved in 
proofs of concept or pilots for DLT that make use of smart contracts. For examples, pilots

on DLT based trade finance applications meant to digitalise the workflow betwee n trade

➢ Do you consider that smart contracts could be an effective tool to 
technically implement the data access and use in the context of co -
generated IoT data, in particular where the transfer is not only one-off but 
would involve some form of continuous data sharing? 
Based on the experience of some members, s mart contracts could be an effective tool to 
technically implement data access and use in the context of co-generated IoT data.  
Yet it is important to keep in mind that IoT usually w orks with a large number of device s 
for which the resulting volume of data would be unmanageable by most of blockchain 
protocols. However, IoT applications which do not rely on the blockchain for data storage 
but instead use it for identity management or coordination are possible. 
We also note that the questionnaire, in its wording, seems to present the use of smart 
contracts as the best and unique means to organise data sharing at IoT level. This would 
not be technologically neutral. There a re plenty of mechanisms and protocols to ensure 
the sharing of data generated by the IoT, even on the fly. It is correct to say there is no 
standardization on this area which may imped e the objective of better data sharing but 
data sharing may be organized  once data are collected by the IoT and sent to the data 
hub. 
 
➢ Do you consider that when individuals request data portability from 
businesses, smart contracts could be an effective tool to technically 
implement data transfers, in particular where the transmission is not only 
one-off but would involve some form of continuous data sharing? 
Smart contracts have a potential to support data transfer as they offer functionalities to 
manage and exchange data. In particular, they could:  
• Be of use to register the data exchange and guarantee that the parties are 
exchanging the allowed information, when and who authorised it; 
• Operate without the need of a central (trusted) intermediary; 
• Provide transparency on who accessed and processed the data;  
• Foster standardisation; 
• Offer automatic enforcement of specific pre-defined rules; 
• Introduce incentive mechanisms to support the data economy.  
However, such theoretic advantages have to be carefully analysed, for example, with 
regard to the legal framework, jurisdictions and GDPR compliance.  
 
➢ If interoperability is an issue for scaling smart contracts, which 
requirements should inform standardisation to scale smart contracts 
across blockchains and/or across ecosystems? Should such standards 
determine in particu lar minimum safeguards for cyber security? If so, 
which best practices would you consider relevant?  
The following best practices would be relevant:  
1. Common standards used when building different solutions would help with 
interoperability. Different blockchain networks use different programming 
languages. A common meta -language would be needed to be translated into 
different implementations with the same execution results (example BPMN notation 
for BPM/Workflow world). Different networks create a problem wi th the smart 
6 
 
contract interactions. If it is the same network, several calls between smart 
contracts guarantee the operation atomicity, but if smart contracts are in different 
networks the atomicity cannot be guaranteed in the smart contract calls. In this  
case, an orchestrator piece would be needed between networks that implement a 
SAGA pattern, in order to guarantee the atomicity. 
2. Network capabilities . There must be a minimum common ground in order to 
obtain interoperability. 
3. Smart contract governance. Smart contract evolution needs to c arefully 
investigate the different technological setup and design of smart contracts. 
Different networks deploy smart contracts in a different way. Ethereum flavours 
deploy smart contracts as a transaction, re plicated thru the network within the 
block and therefore updated everywhere prior to a use. Hyperledger Fabric or Corda 
manages smart contracts out of the chain. 
4. Inter-network identities and security.  Different networks work differently 
regarding network access control, security, etc. Interoperability will require some 
kind of shared practices and/or cross checking.

VI. Complementing the portability right under Art. 20 GDPR  
 
➢ To what extent do you agree with the following statement: “The device 
manufacturer of a smart connected object (e.g. wearable or household 
appliance) should be able to permit whomever they choose to easily use 
the data generated  by the use of that object, without the agreement of 
the user.” 
It must be stressed that access to and/or transfer of data must always be done on behalf 
of and be authorised by the customer, in line with the GDPR.  We note that if the 
information is anonymized, then consent is not required. With this background, we hav e 
responded “somewhat disagree” to the question in the survey.  
➢ Among the elements listed below, which are the three most important 
elements that prevent the right under Article 20 GDPR to be fully effective? 
(can select multiple) 
All of the elements proposed in the question are relevant when it comes to realising an 
enhanced data portability right. We would in particular stress the importance of i) access 
and transfer mechanisms, ii) security, iii) standardisation, and  iv) 
liability/redress.  
 
1. Access and transfer mechanism  
Access and/or transfer data must always be done on behalf of and be authorised by the 
customer and in line with the GDPR.  
Standard dedicated interfaces (i.e., APIs) with real-time direct data access 
between the data controller and the receiving company/user that can take place on a one-
off or recurrent basis are needed. It should be possible for users to initiate the access 
7 
 
and transfer request through the interface of the receiving company, which 
would connect with the data controller for authenticating the user and executing 
the transfer.  
We would also encourage to take into account requirements specific to different sectors, 
such as the banking sector in order to reflect the reality that banks have  already 
successfully developed interfaces allowing data access according to PSD2 that are meeting 
customer needs in terms of security and convenience and continue engaging in 
collaborations across the ecosystem economy. This should be leveraged to the ben efit of 
an efficient development of the data sharing ecosystem. Coherence with existing initiatives 
must be ensured and interoperability across standards is crucial.  
2.  Security  
Appropriate requirements are needed to protect users from unauthorized data sharing. As 
in PSD2, authentication requirements and secure communication must therefore 
be part of the policy option. Security levels should be proportionate with the sensitivity 
of the information exchanged and equivalent to that required in the interface of the data 
controller where the user can access this information to. Authentication of the user should 
be similar in the direct and indirect request channels5.  
With regards to the customer interaction, it is important to strike the right balance between 
security and convenience. Transparency is therefore also an important element and is 
needed in terms of operationalizing data sharing.  
3. Standardisation  
Data formats, exchange protocols, security (authentication requirements and 
secure communication), and API models should be standardised , taking into 
account existing mechanisms and processes for standardisation across sectors, with the 
involvement of industry to develop the technical standards. This would also consider the 
broader initiatives that have been developed over the recent years at industry level, both 
at European and global level, (ISO standards, API technology, etc.) across different sectors 
(e.g., the financial sector).  
Also, while we recognize that it is largely in the competitive space, stan dardized and 
recognizable interfaces may be helpful for users and support building trust in data sharing.  
As interoperability is key, the Data Act should mandate the development of technical 
standards that ensure interoperability between different technol ogical tools ( e.g., APIs), 
with industry involvement, based on international standards. This includes finding a 
common approach in defining technical requirements for portability from a cross- 
sectoral perspective. This is also why the scope should be broa der than IoT devices; 
otherwise, stakeholders from other sectors would be excluded from the work on technical 
specifications and standards, likely to their detriment. 
Predictable development paths of standards and effective, efficient, and transparent 
governance is crucial, as standards will need to be developed over time and will be 
embedded in systems across industries. To discuss how to achieve this common approach 
and avoid a delay in time-to-market and inefficiencies, the European Commission can play 
a key role as a convenor and coordinator, bringing together different stakeholders and 
industry representatives across sectors. This would ensure that the process considers 
current successful developments and that different stakeholders are involved, with 
discussions facilitated in a constructive context. 
The creation of a European Data Innovation Board (The Board) in the European 
Commission’s proposal for a Data Governance Act provides the forum to do so and the 
Commission could consider a role for it in the Data Act. 
 
 
 
8 
 
4. Liability/redress 
A proportionate approach could require firms wishing to access data on behalf of users to 
use appropriate security certificates and include a framework for resolving questions of 
liability and redress. 
Once data is transferred, the responsibility should be on the receiving company. The 
company sending the data should only be liable for the sharing process (e.g., 
authentication, transmission). The user would have to provide consent, and then the 
responsibility would f all on the data receiver. Any further use of the data while being 
accessed (and for the data that based on legal obligations may have to be retained after 
such access has been revoked) shall be used within the possibilities provided in the GDPR. 
In addition to the technical elements mentioned above, we would like to emphasize the 
importance of aligning the proposed actions with other initiatives in the EU policy 
landscape on data to ensure consistency and give legal certainty for individuals and firms. 
 
VII. Intellectual Property Rights – Protection of Databases  
➢ In your view, how does the Database Directive apply to machine generated 
data (in particular data generated by sensor -equipped objects connected 
to the Internet-of-things objects)?  
While the relationship between clients and vendors may be established trough contractual 
terms and conditions, the sui generis right under the Database Directive is more suitable 
to offer protection against third -party infringements as it offers protection against copi es 
of data and thus applies further to the contractual terms established by the database 
producer. 
However, the Database Directive is outdated, and updating it to specifically and explicitly 
contemplate machine generated data or processed/structured data within the sui generis 
protection of databases would provide more legal certainty.

ANNEX 1 – Potential Use Cases for Cross-Sectoral Data Sharing  
When looking at different scenarios and combinations of data, it is important to note that 
that use cases presented below are potential opportunities which could be leveraged 
in practice, in this case, by the banking sector. These must be handled carefully, in line 
with relevant regulation, such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). In this 
regard, any initiative will need to be carefully assessed in such a way that risks 
and opportunities are carefully balanced while respecting the privacy rights of 
individuals. Some of the use cases are more prone to pose privacy risks than others, it 
is essential that each of these, prior to be put in practice once possible legal or other types 
of obstacles are removed, are subject to the scrutiny of proper data protection risk 
assessments. 
•  Green financing and sustainable investment opportunities   
The financial sector has a key role to play in the transition to a more sustainable economy 
including by ensuring that financing is able to flow towards sustainable investments and 
by supporting customers in their own decision making as they make their businesses and 
house-holds greener. Access, for instance, to data related to the purchase of organic 
products, services, consumption, etc. related to energy efficiency, food safety, waste 
management and climate change, would allow financial institutions to provide be tter 
advice on sustainable investments, offering tailor-made proposals on financial instruments 
related to the Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) sector. The tailor -made 
proposal can be sent to the targeted customers (e.g. retail customers, existing investors) 
who have provided access to such data to their banks, who are bound to use such data in 
line with GDPR. 
Two examples are carbon footprint calculations and sustainability 
recommendations and loans for renewable energy installation. 
a) Carbon footp rint calculations and sustainability recommendations: Prices and 
quantities of utilities and goods consumed such as petrol, transport services, 
electricity, or water would allow a more accurate calculation of an individual’s 
environmental footprint, allowi ng comparisons regarding their efficiency and 
recommendations for more sustainable and/or cheaper suppliers. Any access to 
such information requires an explanation of which data are being accessed, for 
which purposes and how it will be further used. The co nsumer could receive an 
enhanced user experience through personalization and relevant information 
provision. Providing broader choices, recommendations and more information can 
help consumers take action to reduce their environmental impact.  There are als o 
benefits for the wider market and economy through the increased competition due 
to more comparison and active recommendations of alternative utilities providers. 
It also helps to increase sustainability by promoting individual action to reduce the 
environmental footprint. 
b) Loans for energy installation: By allowing consumers or firms to share information 
about their energy use and details regarding their property, more providers could 
build specifications to facilitate green loans for renewable energy installation.  A 
customer would share with the bank information held by their energy provider on 
their energy consumption and data on their property from the national cadastre. 
Such access and further use of data would take place in line with GDPR.  Potential 
benefits for the consumer would include the ability to receive advice directly from 
their bank on whether it is in their interest to make an investment in renewable 
energy installation and how this could be financed. By easing the information and 
administrative con-strains on individuals, much wider installation of independent 
renewable energy could be achieved, supporting the economy -wide energy 
transition.

• Additional tools for decision making on personal finances 
Improved insight into spending habits – on behalf of and authorised by the customer- can 
allow the development of services that support users in improving their grip on their 
finances via personalised advice. Timely and relevant financial advice supports financial 
inclusion by helping to  overcome traditional information asymmetries and helps address 
challenges around financial education. To be able to provide these advisory services, data 
is needed from non-financial sector players. 
For example, access to customer purchase logs and wish l ists in a defined e -commerce 
marketplace prior to the purchase being made could help to improve personalized financial 
advice. The customer could provide access to this data to their bank or other financial 
advice providers when shopping online, after bein g made aware of such information will 
be used and what to expect from such use.  
This could help the customer in assessing whether they can afford a certain item given 
their current financial situation and future financial needs. The customer could even be 
provided with the option to find alternative, cheaper, or greener suppliers for a given 
product. The result is a tool which helps to manage customer finances in an anticipated 
way, not only at an ex-port manner where a purchase has been made. Given the sensitive 
nature of the data, this use case should only be carried out once a proper data protection 
impact assessment has been conducted and the risks have been adequately mitigated.  
 
• Access to public sector data to help improve digital financial services  
Digitalization of public authorities and access to publicly held data is an important enabler 
for (cross-border) digital financial services to develop. Many public registries hold data 
that are highly relevant for developing digital financial servic es. As an example, digital 
land registries will allow for a digital customer journey when buying real estate or when 
re-mortgaging. The Danish digital infrastructure for this serves as a good example. 
Similarly, access to tax returns and company accounts, allow banks to access – on behalf 
of and authorised by the customer - basic financial data for credit assessments. Having 
access to passport registries would allow banks and payment service providers to improve 
the quality of customer onboarding and ongoing AML due diligence. For this, possible legal 
obstacles should be removed.  
Taking the example of mortgage lending case processing, access to housing and land 
registries would allow banks/mortgage banks to connect directly with land registries to 
retrieve data (ownership and existing liabilities) and to register rights directly without 
costly paper -based processes. The digital registration ensures that all registration 
processes are carried out in a uniform and efficient manner, both across the financial 
sector and between the sector and the land registration office. This also allows financial 
institutions to focus efforts on customer advisory services rather than cumbersome 
administrative processes. The result could be a faster and less costly case processing when 
buying real estate or re-financing existing mortgages.

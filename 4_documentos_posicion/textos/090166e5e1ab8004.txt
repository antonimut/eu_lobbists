Salesforce & Data 
 
Salesforce appreciates the work of the European Commission to create a robust foundation for the 
free flow of data. Today, data is at the heart of every digital transformation. Organisations need to 
be empowered to protect, analyse, understand, and communicate data to leverage it to its full 
potential, especially in times of change or crisis. The global COVID-19 pandemic has accelerated 
tremendously the digital transformation imperative: flexible or remote work has become the new 
normal; apps are increasing in importance; there is a significant rise in digital communications; 
digital communities are growing; and, most importantly, there is a growing need for data-based 
decisions by governments and businesses about their response to the crisis.  
 
At Salesforce, trust is our number one value. We recognise privacy as a fundamental human right 
and integral to trusted relationships between organisations and their stakeholders. The protection 
of our customers’ data is paramount, and we safeguard that data with a robust and comprehensive 
privacy and security program. To that end, we have developed a privacy programme that accounts 
for the ever-evolving landscape of global data protection laws. In November 2015 we received 
approval from European data protection authorities for our Processor Binding Corporate Rules 
(BCRs). We became an early member of GAIA-X and we have declared our adherence to the EU 
Cloud Code of Conduct. We have a dedicated privacy website (salesforce.com/privacy) which 
provides information on relevant privacy laws, our products’ third-party audits and certifications, 
and guidance for customers. We also provide real-time information on system performance and 
security on trust.salesforce.com.

Improving portability for business users of cloud services 
 
We support the Commission’s goal to prevent the lock-in of cloud customers and we believe that the 
self-regulatory approach as implemented by the SWIPO Codes of Conduct can adequately address 
these concerns. The SWIPO Codes of Conduct are the result of a long process to which many cloud 
providers and cloud users contributed their time and expertise. The Codes have been operational 
since July 2020 and have not yet had the chance to prove their added value for cloud users. Their 
recognition in the market, especially among smaller users who would be able to benefit the most 
from the Codes, is still limited. For instance, for Salesforce, so far, it has only been in one instance 
that a customer requested information about the SWIPO Codes. Therefore, we would recommend to 
the European Commission to give more time to cloud users and cloud providers to become familiar 
with the Codes and for cloud providers to consider signing up. In our experience, lack of clarity about 
what portability entails is very often the reason why porting and switching can prove challenging. 
The SWIPO Codes are helpfully addressing this issue by focusing on transparency.  
 
Additionally, we believe that there needs to be a more in-depth analysis by the Commission of the 
concerns raised by cloud users with regard to vendor lock-in and whether the issues encountered by 
users are of a contractual nature. Challenges with portability of data and services between cloud 
providers are often a result of the different technical environments between cloud providers, not of 
malicious intent on the side of the cloud provider to prevent their customer from switching 
providers. Cloud providers should compete on innovation and functionality and should want their 
customers to want to choose them because they offer the best products. “Perfect” portability will be 
almost impossible to achieve. Migration projects can be highly complex because of the richness and 
sophistication of cloud services. This complexity should not be mistaken for efforts on the part of the 
cloud provider to “lock in” the cloud user. Cloud providers who want to attract users will develop the 
tools to facilitate the migration from other providers.  
 
Lastly, requiring interoperability and certain standards across all cloud players could stifle smaller 
cloud providers and create barriers to entry.  
 
Safeguards for non-personal data in international contexts 
 
As an enterprise software company, trust is key to our work which means being transparent with our 
customers is critical. The data our customers submit to our services belongs to them and our 
customers control how it is used. Salesforce handles data on behalf of our customers, and our 
systems are designed to allow our customers to maintain access and control. We provide customers 
with multiple technical controls for protection of their data, including pseudonymisation, access 
controls, and encryption, together with the right to audit our services. We are not the owner of 
customer data, and we strongly believe that any governmental agency seeking access to customer 
data should address its request directly with that customer where possible. As a company, 
Salesforce looks at the protection of our customer data as paramount and treats all of the data we 
are entrusted with the same high level of data protection. 
 
As primarily a Business-to-Business enterprise software provider, it is rare for Salesforce to receive a 
request from a government agency to access data belonging to a customer. Due to the nature of our 
business, Salesforce generally does not process data that is of particular interest to U.S. or other 
non-EU law enforcement or intelligence services and we do not have a history of sharing EU 
customer data with these parties. Trust starts with transparency. Unless prohibited by law, 
 
Salesforce always notifies a customer when it receives a request for that customer’s data, including a 
government request. We review each government request for customer data on a case-by-case 
basis and only comply if and to the extent we determine the request is lawful. When reviewing the 
lawfulness of a government request, we take into account all applicable laws, including the laws of 
other jurisdictions. We require governmental agencies to follow the required legal process under 
applicable laws, such as issuing their request via a subpoena, court order, or search warrant. Where 
we believe a government request for customer data is invalid or unlawful, we would challenge it. 
Our principles for government requests for customer data are publicly available for all our customers 
and stakeholders to review.  
 
Third-country access to data has become a topic that many governments around the world are 
looking to address. Governments must work together to reconcile the concerns of national security 
interests with that of the protection of fundamental rights. In this context, any new requirements 
governing third-country access to data could further cast companies in the role of global arbiter in 
these sensitive topics. The OECD is working to establish broad agreement on principles to ensure 
trusted government access and the areas of consideration include transparency and constraints on 
government access. In the view of the OECD according to a statement from their Digital Economy 
Policy Committee, “concerns that the absence of common principles for trusted government access 
to personal data may lead to undue restrictions on data flows resulting in detrimental economic 
impacts”. Salesforce views the work of the OECD to be closely aligned with the goals of the EU to 
ensure both the proper guardrails for third-country requests and economic growth. A multilateral 
approach would set a broader precedent that would alleviate the concerns articulated in the EU 
Data Act more fully. 
 
Our recommendations: 
 Proportionality of risk: Any policy/regulatory action taken by the European Commission 
should acknowledge the different degree of risk presented by law enforcement access 
requests in the business-to-business vs. the business-to-consumer context. The likelihood of 
such access requests targeting business-to-business cloud providers is considerably smaller. 
We believe that any new requirements on cloud providers should be proportionate to the 
level of the risk for the customer.  
 Clarity on requests: The Commission could provide more clarity around its concerns for 
international access of non-personal data and the danger it presents for IP and trade secret 
theft. Our customers entrust us to hold and process their data as they instruct; as such, it is 
likely that any access request would be better directed to the data owner. We also believe 
that any legal requirements restricting the flow of non-personal data should be 
accompanied by clear provisions on the interplay with the personal data held by a cloud 
provider, especially in the cases where the data sets are inextricably linked (as is clarified in 
the Regulation on the Free Flow of Non-Personal Data).  
 Multilateral solutions: The Commission should continue to push for a global framework on 
trusted government access to data. This is a global problem that demands a global solution. 
Organisations like the OECD are working to come to an agreement on these issues, which 
would set a robust precedent on trusted access and provide a more even playing field for 
European companies.

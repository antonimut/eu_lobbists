The European Publishers Council is a high level group of 28 Chairmen and CEOs of  leading European 
media corporations ac tively involved in multimedia markets  spanning newspaper s, magazine s, 
books, journal s, marketplaces, internet and online d atabase publishers, and radio and TV 
broadcasting.  
 
The European Publishers Council welcomes the opportunity to respond to the Commis sion’s public 
consultation on the Data Act and has submitted its response to the questionnaire. 
 
The objective of the Data Act is to “propose measures to create a fair data economy by ensuring 
access to and use of data, including in business-to-business and business-to-government situations”. 
The Commission has set out to achieve this objective by adopting a horizontal legislative initiative 
(‘the Data Act’), which would complement the Regulation on Data Governance. 
 
Overall, EPC supports the Commission’s o bjectives to improve access to and use of data in the EU.  
However, data is an essential component of a publisher’s activit ies, allowing for the creation of 
strong value . Therefore, d ata sharing should not come at the expense of weakened intellectual 
property protection. Voluntary data sharing must remain the basis of any future consideration of 
the European Commission.   
 
I. Business to Government data sharing for the public interest 
 
EPC strongly believe s that any business to government data sharing mechanism  should be based on a 
voluntary basis.  Considering the high commercial value which businesses can derive from data in a highly 
competitive environment, B2G data sharing should not  be made compulsory, or risk undermining existing 
markets based on access an d use of data.  We also have concerns that the principle is stated too 
broadly (e.g. “develop evidence -based policies”), and could lead to government overrea ch in our 
journalistic activities. Strong and broad journalistic protections should be put in place,  but news 
media’s corporate business information in the public interest should be accessible provided that

• A clear and harmonised definition of what co nstitutes the public interes t: Any data sharing based

such data (e.g., APIs). 
 
II. Business to business data sharing 
 
Sharing data with their partner is essential for publishers to carry on their activities , and relies on 
contractual freedom.  Data sharing based on contractual freedom already allo ws for many B2B and B2G 
collaboration models. EPC believes that no regulatory intervention is necessary nor appropriate at this  
stage.  
 
B2B voluntary data sharing should be extremely respectful of privacy and data protection rules in case 
individuals are the origin of the data. In particular, publishers sharing data should be kept informed of all 
the data processing performed by the partner receiving the data, in order to ensure compliance with the 
GDPR.  
 
In specific cases, very large economic players have been able to impose on publishers a co-controllership 
framework for the collection of data , due to the imbalance of negotiating power . In EPC’s view, 
publishers should remain free to decide the framework of collaboration with their vendors (co -
controllership or  vendors acting as processors). The ongoing Digital Markets Act proposal, as well as 
competition authorities should address issues related to data access by large gatekeepers.   
 
 
III. Clarifying rights on non-personal Internet-of-Things data stemming from professional use 
 
Voice assisted technology is  the IoT related technology most relevant for publishers  at this time . Users 
are now using voice assistants to  rapidly access information, such as news, radio content or  general 
information such as  weather, making such technology a new and growing media channel for publishers.

EPC members with audio services linked to their news and magazine media brands and radio  
broadcasting operations, are increasingly reliant on platforms incorporating voice assistant  technologies 
to make available their radio stations and audio products, including podcasts.  
 
Data and transparency are key element s to be taken into consideration for IoT and for voice assistants  
especially: 
 
• Users of the voice assistant: Users should have access t o transparent information  regarding 
default news sources and other types of content  used by platforms, as well as  information about 
their ability to change and select preferred sources of information  are essential. In addition, 
users should receive informa tion regard ing the collection  and use of their data through smart 
speakers and other voice assistants. 
• Business users of the platforms: There is a clear lack of transparency in how the  platform 
operators make decisions which news outlets are chosen to prov ide news and which are not.  In 
addition, voice assistants and smart speakers are now collecting a huge amount of  data which is 
being used and retained by platforms. While publishers and other  partners do not have access to 
such data, platforms remain in a position to derive  value from them, and strengthen their 
position as gatekeepers. The ongoing Digital Markets Act proposal, as well as com petition 
authorities should address  issues related to data access by large gatekeepers.   EPC supports 
adding ‘platforms that incorporate voice assistant technologies’ to the list of online  
intermediation services in the Digital Markets Act.  
 
IoT objects will hugely increase the amount of data available for use thus increasing opportunities 
for innovations. It is i mportant that the European Union maintain s a simple and harmonized legal 
framework for data.  
 
IV. Intellectual Property rights – protection of databases 
 
The EPC considers that the Database Directive (Directive 96/9/EC) has achieved its core objectives 
and we  would t herefore oppose strongly any reduction in the scope of protection of the sui 
generis/database right.    
 
Databases play a vital role in the cultural and ec onomic development of the digital market and, as 
such, members of the EPC derive continuing benefit f rom their leg al protection  through the sui 
generis right  for the protection of the selection and arrangement of content and data , including 
classified advertisements and listings  which are now typically organised through publisher owned 
online marketplaces.  
 
According to recital 39 , the Directive “seeks to safegua rd the position of makers of databases 
against misapp ropriation of the results of the financial and professional investment made in 
obtaining and collection the contents by protecting the whole or  substantial parts of a database 
against certain acts by a user or competitor”.  There is a clear and continuing need for publishers to

protect their investments in the creation of databases against such misappropriation including the 
parasitic behaviour o f “free -riders” and dishonest competitors who seek to misappropriate the 
results of the collection of data and information undertaken by the database maker.   
 
Both the copyright and sui generis rights help protect investment which are important in the 
encouragement of creation and innovation.  Taking into account technological innovations since the 
Directive was implemented,  we are of the view that machine generated data  including data 
generated by sensor -equipped objects connected to the Internet -of- things objects is protected by 
the Directive,  but if there is a legal doubt , that should be clarified , including to protect against 
unauthorized use.  
 
The unauthorised exploitation of publishers’ news and classified advertising databases is technically 
easy f or “free -riders”. Sui generis/database right is an important tool for publishers when they 
provide good quality data to the public/users especially in digital form. Without an appropriate sui 
generis/database right, reasonable protection for investments by the publishers would be absent.  
 
As regards the sui generis right itself, Articles 8 an d 9 contain s ignificant rights for lawful users and 
exceptions to that right which, in EPC’s view, strike an entirely appropriate balance of interests.  
 
Unfortunately, however, the CJEU has weakened the protection afforded to database makers to 
protect their investment . EPC considers that the distinction drawn by the CJEU between the 
(unprotected) investment in the creation of data and the (protected) investm ent in the  obtaining 
data is inconsistent with the Directive’s core objective, and has significantly limited the possibility to 
protect a wide variety of databases  that we feel should benefit from this legislation. This has 
created serious and  perverse outcomes as p ublishers have not been able adequately to challenge 
and stop the systematic extraction of both substantial, and insubstantial parts  with harmful 
cumulative effect of their properties. 
 
V. Safeguards for non-personal data in international contexts 
 
EPC members do not support the development of data localization requirements, and rely on their ability 
to store data anywhere in the world that meet their business interest, in full compliance with existing 
legislation such as the GDPR . This issue in pa rticular should be solved at an international level between 
countries.

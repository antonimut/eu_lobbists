How to Effectively Protect the Travel Ecosystem 
The travel industry represents a clear example of how the pandemic has radically altered the landscape and expectations of 
all stakeholders of the ecosystem, such as travel agencies, airlines and, of course, consumers. An important issue faced during 
the ongoing pandemic, is securing, and protecting the payment from travel intermediaries  (e.g., travel agents) to travel 
service providers (e.g. airline, hotel) primarily due to the lack of clarity in the existing legal framework. Ensuring the existing 
legal framework remains fit -for-purpose and aligned with emerging realities in the sector and would ultimately safeguard 
that consumers are adequately protected when the book their travel package. 
Top-Line Approach 
➢ Mastercard is interested in ensuring that all participants are properly protected, placing special focus on the most 
vulnerable part of the value chain: European consumers. 
➢ European legislation in combination with the rules of payment networks, are central in maintaining the liquidity of 
market players in the travel industry, while ensuring a high-level consumer protection. 
➢ Mastercard supports a proposed review of the Packaged Travel Directive which should aim to  address rules and 
provisions for refunds between travel service providers and travel intermediaries. 
➢ This would lead to higher protection on refunds, increased transparency to consumers seeking refunds, and mitigating 
liquidity risks for travel intermediaries. 
➢ A review should in corporate provisions that reflect the changing landscape and ensure  that when the travel 
intermediary (e.g., travel agency) acts as the ‘Merchant of Record’, it should be the entity liable for refunds to the 
consumer, while also being protected from emerging situations. 
Situational Analysis & Risk 
While there are many ways in which a consumer can purchase a hotel/flight/cruise – the primary scenario which can have a 
significant impact on consumer protection, liquidity risk and payment, is when the consumer reserves the hotel/flight/cruise 
indirectly via a travel intermediary (e.g., a travel agency). This implies one of the following scenarios in the value chain: 
a) The travel intermediary acts as a ‘booking intermediary’, the booking is made with the travel intermediary, but the 
payment goes directly from the consumer to the travel service provider. 
b) The travel intermediary acts as the ‘merchant of record’, the booking is made with the travel intermediary (e.g., travel 
agency) and the payment goes to the travel intermediary who in a second payment pays th e travel service provider 
(hotel, airline, cruise company). 
The second scenario in the value chain (b), effectively also leads to the risk towards the travel intermediary ( e.g., travel 
agency) as it is liable for the refund to the consumer (within a period defined by the law). However, the travel intermediary 
might not have the consumer’s funds anymore as it might have proceeded to pay the travel  service provider (hotel, airline, 
cruise company). Under the current legal framework, the liquidity of the travel intermediary is dependent on the willingness 
and pace at which the travel service provider sends the consumer’s funds back to the travel intermediary. 
During normal circumstances, this could be considered as just a part of doing business, but in the middle of an external shock, 
such as Covid -19, where this case is multiplied by the hundreds or even thousands, it amplif ies liquidity risks for travel 
intermediaries and consequently on the ability for consumers to be made whole again. 
Suggestions 
To mitigate these risks, Mastercard is of the view that the proposed review of the Packaged Travel Directive represents a 
great opportunity to introduce provisions or rules that would ensure a fairer distribution of the burden for refunds between 
travel service providers and travel intermediaries, which will as a consequence increase consumer protection. 
More specifically, the Directive should clarify or introduce rules that when the travel intermediary (e.g., travel agency acts 
as the ‘Merchant of Record’, it should be liable for refunds to the consumer. In cases where the Consumer has purchased 
the travel service , as part of travel package, from the travel service provider (e.g., booking a flight on the website of the 
airline, and also booking a hotel from the website) directly, or in the case were the travel intermediary ( e.g., travel agency) 
is acting as the ‘booking’ intermediary, then the liability should lie with the travel service providers. 
A complementary avenue that could be explored is to protect package organizers by introducing rules requiring the payment 
from package organizers to providers of travel services to be protected in a trust, where the funds w ould only be released 
once the consumer has utilized the purchased services. This would protect the funds of travel intermediaries acting as the 
‘Merchant of Record’ and using non- secure forms of payment when paying travel service providers. 
This would effectively ensure the legal framework remains current, offering higher protection and transparency for consumer 
reimbursements, and mitigate liquidity risks for travel intermediaries.

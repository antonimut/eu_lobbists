SES Comments on European Commission  ESSCS Inception Impact Assessment 23 September 2021     Executive Summary  SES shares the European Commission’s objective to make the EU a driving force in space policy in the next decade and beyond. Faced with increased competition from third-country private companies, as well as state-owned entities, EU players remain at the forefront of global technological innovation. Con-nectivity needs will only increase and as the global pandemic has shown, digitization will be a critical part of economic recovery.  The success of the EU Secure Space-based Connectivity System (ESSCS) will be guaranteed through a public-private partnership, whereby the private sector would bring its expertise to bear (efficiency, technological innovation), while public policy objectives (security, sovereignty, connectivity) would be set by the EU and its member states. Funding mechanisms and business models are yet to be defined in detail, but our preference is for a PPP with a concession model and/or an availability model. We do not see Options 3a and 3b as mutually exclusive, rather coexisting depending on the use cases and business segments and market needs that need to be met.  In our submission, we discuss the relative merits of each scenario proposed by the European Commis-sion and explain the reasons behind our choices. We trust this paper will equip DG DEFIS with solid arguments to make an informed decision about the future constellation.   1. Introductory remarks  SES thanks the European Commission for the opportunity to comment on the inception impact assess-ment (IIA). We applaud the general objectives and agree with the overall framework of the ESSCS project. Our comments seek to inform the assessment in the light of the overall policy objectives, with a particular focus on the legal and economic perspectives.  We believe SES is already doing many of the things that ESSCS hopes to achieve. Still, we alongside all European stakeholders need ESSCS to allow us to take the next steps towards achieving the policy goals set by the European Commission, mainly in funding technologies and programs that would not happen without institutional support.   We need innovation, which is why public support is necessary to develop the technologies both in the air and on the ground, above and beyond what we are doing today.  The key here is not necessarily global, which implies a full LEO constellation, but rather to focus on the principal geographies and markets indicated as of strategic interest by the European Commission (EC), namely Africa, the Arctic and the Atlantic regions. This is in sync with the markets identified in WP1 of the ESSCS study. These can better be served by a hybrid of various orbits, taking advantage of existing and committed assets and creating a more sustainable and less costly architecture.  We trust our suggestions below will contribute to the development of a stronger European space indus-try, better positioned to compete globally.

2. Overall comments  As a leading European industry player and European success story originated in Luxembourg, SES is one of the world’s largest satellite operators by fleet size, services offered and revenue. SES is a key contributor to the EU’s economy. On a recent visit to SES headquarters in Betzdorf, President Ursula von der Leyen discussed EU recovery funds and space policy and plans, and the important role SES is called on to play as a European champion.   SES is fully committed to ensuring the success of the ESSCS.  We share the European Commission’s vision to build an autonomous, resilient, future-proof infrastructure able to deliver secure and affordable connectivity to EU citizens. It is essential that the new constellation remains economically viable and sustainable, while it serves the growing needs of the population, including its governments, businesses and citizens.   3. Our views on ESSCS  SES supports the European Commission’s aspiration for a truly European ESSCS program that relies on satellite operators that are well-established in Europe. SES has the expertise to support the EC in its design, development and operation.  ESSCS should be built as an integrated multi-orbit constellation and gradually deploy and scale up – leveraging existing/committed MEO/GEO assets to be complemented by LEO assets, and take full ad-vantage of the relative merits of each obit, based on relevant use cases.   We believe the ideal business model for ESSCS should be a PPP model with a greater responsibility and risk-sharing borne by the private actors.   A PPP model (option 3a or option 3b) fits best with all complementary architecture components and will help the EC achieve its strategic objectives. Such model would ensure:   ▪ Timely delivery of the overarching EU system, ▪ Implementation of back-to-back procurement, ▪ Flexible partnership with the European Commission,  ▪ Re-use of existing, committed, and planned satellite assets thereby contributing to space sus-tainability and providing a more ‘green’ solution, ▪ Antitrust compliance in relation to the collaboration between the stakeholders across all phases of design and implementation, ▪ Mapping of the right business model with every architecture component, ▪ Positioning of satellite network operators as the natural interface with the European Com-mission.   4. Detailed comments on IIA  4.1 Sovereignty/strategic autonomy  ESSCS is an essential tool for the EU to:  ▪ Contribute to EU strategic autonomy and competitiveness – China, Russia and India are increasingly seen as rivals, while the US and UK are engaged to ‘privatize’ space. European space industries are competing with the ambitious constellation projects currently deployed or planned outside of the EU, often benefiting from significant public funding. ▪ Build sovereign secure satellite-based solutions for defense, cybersecurity and civil gov-ernmental applications.

Discussion of the proposed policy options  Having carefully reviewed the 6 policy options outlined by the European Commission, SES believes that options 3a and 3b present by far the most effective and efficient models for achieving the EC’s requirements.   Given the EC wishes to promote the ongoing digital transformation of the EU economy by increasing the global space-based connectivity available in the EU, it is critical that this connectivity be (1) ubiqui-tous, secure and reliable, (2) affordable and (3) safeguard EU sovereignty. In this context the Baseline Option 0 is not viable and therefore not considered further.   Options 3a and 3b achieve the best balance of outcomes for the EU and member states when consid-ering these policy objectives and applying the EC’s stated criteria (above) relating to: (1) scope/quality, (2) cost and (3) sovereignty/control for the following reasons:

 Option 1: EU owned space infrastructure: A dedicated space and ground infrastructure is developed, operated and fully owned by the Union and potentially Member States. Connectivity services are pro-vided by an EU body or delegated entities, with possible retail commercialization agreements to address private users. The approach resembles that used for setting up Copernicus and Galileo under the EU Space Program.   This option may have potential to meet the third criterion (safeguarding sovereignty/control). However, increasingly, member states recognize that it is possible to safeguard sovereignty and to achieve the control they require through other means (contractual, security and regula-tory) without legal ownership, and without having to assume the burden of developing, owning and operating critical infrastructure themselves.    Ownership brings significant burdens and involves significant additional risk, e.g. the owner will be responsible for the costs of disposal/decommissioning; residual value rarely (if ever) exists in infrastructure assets of this nature and the owner bears the residual liability.    The EU and member states are seeking greater connectivity. This necessarily means that the system will not be a standalone (‘closed circuit’) system but will have to interface with other systems. This in turn means that the EU will have to put in place other means of control (con-tractual, security and regulatory) regardless of ownership.    Option 1 also involves the EU taking on significant development and operational risks and as-sumes that the EU (or Member States) will have the necessary resources, capacity and exper-tise to develop and operate the space and ground-based infrastructure itself. Whilst it is likely that it may have some of the necessary expertise in this area (and possibly world leading ex-pertise in some areas), it is unlikely to have the breadth and depth of expertise that private sector partners will bring. The private sector is also likely to be quicker to adapt to changes of circumstance and more able to develop new expertise rapidly to respond to changes in tech-nology, etc.  Extracting greater efficiency (higher performance/connectivity at lower cost) from such systems increasingly requires greater system integration expertise, innovation and in-creased commercialization, and private sector partners (whose core business is in this space) will bring this. They will also bring significant expertise in managing risks of all kinds.     If the infrastructure is entirely owned by the EU or Member States, it may not be possible to achieve system enhancements or efficiencies that might otherwise be possible from ‘piggy-backing’ on and/or integrating into other systems operated by a commercial operator under a PPP arrangement. There is likely to be a higher level of duplication/latency – and accompanying costs.    To overcome this, it may be possible for part of the system to be owned by the private sector partner (for it to be integrated into its existing systems); however, this would leave the EU or Member States owning part of a system which would be inadequate or useless on its own.  It would also prevent the system from providing the digital independence and sovereignty that is required to meet the EU’s objectives.    In summary, it is unlikely that this model will maximize the benefits in respect of the first and second criteria and any benefits in respect of the third criterion may be illusory.     Option 2: Fully private infrastructure: The system conception and deployment, its operations, maintenance, and the provision of services would be entirely left to commercial operators. For the core public secure connectivity services, the EU would act as public anchor tenant, federating the needs of EU and member states public stakeholders for secure satellite services and thereby creating a critical demand mass.

  This model necessarily means that commercial operators will have to design and develop the necessary technology, finance the capex, carry some ‘market risk’/’demand risk’ for the com-mercial revenues and ensure the commercial success of the system in competition with provid-ers of other (existing or future) systems that may provide similar connectivity services. It is notable that the original Galileo structure failed, in part, because the commercial operators were unwilling or unable to take a similar risk and/or finance such a structure.    Option 2 is the model least aligned with the EC’s criteria, and has the most in-built tension. Along with Option 4, it is the least likely option to meet the EU’s stated objectives, especially in terms of safeguarding EU sovereignty and security. This is because as the commercial operator will own and finance the infrastructure it (and its shareholders, investors and/or lenders) will expect to have a significant degree of control of the assets, how they are developed and how they are used. However, in conflict with this, the EU (and member states) will need and expect to exert control by various means (contractual, security and regulatory) to meet its objectives and the need to ensure digital sovereignty/control.    This option does not deliver the benefits for the EU and Member States that are commensurate with its contribution to enabling the system’s development. As the ‘anchor tenant’, the EU and member states will effectively be using their own technical/financial resources and political cap-ital to underpin and promote what is otherwise a commercial opportunity for the private sector. It is likely that, for this structure to be ‘bankable’, the risk allocation under the anchor tenancy may be onerous to the EU and may go further than the EU and/or member states are willing to accept in circumstances where the model is being fully designed and developed by the private sector.     Even if the commercial operator is willing and able to finance the capex on the balance sheet (which will be more costly), the EU’s ‘anchor tenancy’ itself will (in all likelihood) have catalyzed innovations and commercial opportunities for the private sector. Therefore, the EU and member states may (justifiably) wish to share in the exploitation of such benefits which otherwise accrue only to the commercial operator. However, it may be difficult for the EU to negotiate a share of such benefits under a model which is intended to be fully developed, operated and exploited by the private sector and where the private sector is required to take on so much of the risk involved. This would represent a risk of high imbalance between the interests of the private sector and the public sector, to the detriment of the latter.    The proven/’best practice’ way in which States achieve control and receive a share of the ben-efits (in an orderly/aligned manner) in relation to such critical infrastructure and services is through a PPP model adopting a variation on Option 3a or 3b.   In summary, this model would give excessive weight and responsibility to the private sector, at the risk of depriving public authorities from delivering on their sovereignty objectives. Eco-nomic efficiency and public policy objectives need to be balanced for ESSCS to be a success.    Option 3a: Public-Private Partnership – Concession: The investment required for system concep-tion and deployment is ensured by a Public-Private Partnership, with a combination of EU funds, mem-ber states funds and private investments. The operations are ensured by a concessionaire who takes the commercial and operational risk, in particular for commercial services. The EU would commit to appropriate long-term service payments to cover the demand of EU institutions and member states regarding public secure satellite services.  SES prefers Options 3a and/or 3b. However, the suitability of each depends on the results of the study and the proposal that will be adopted by the European Commission. In the same spirit, 3a

As the necessary funding is likely to be provided from a combination of EU funds and private investment, both sides will have visi-bility of the project finances, appropriate levels of control and a share in the benefits. Adopting a PPP concession model will also allow key project risks to be transferred to the private sector, where appropriate and where the private sector is in a better position than the EU or Member States to manage such risks, while at the same time allowing the EU and member states to control critical elements of the project, the system and the technology involved.   As the EU and its PPP contractor have the relationship of joint financial investors, as well as that of customer and supplier, they are more invested in the success of the project and the model encourages collaboration. The PPP arrangements can be structured to provide powerful incentives (for both sides) to ensure that all of the relevant policy objectives are achieved and that the project is a success. Project governance is through the Shareholder Agreement and the board of the concessionaire, which is a flexible and proven model for dealing with delivery and change.    This model also provides various mechanisms by which the sharing of benefits and risks can take place. This allows the EU to design a structure in which the private sector is properly incentivized. The model is potentially less costly than an availability-based PPP model, as a greater proportion of the overall costs involved are likely to be met directly through commercial-ization of the system under a concession arrangement. However, given the variety of different incentives and sharing mechanisms that can be used, either model can be designed to deliver excellent cost certainty and value for money, both of which are likely to be critical issues for the EU and Member States.  Option 3a, whilst it may be adapted to certain use cases, is not the most pertinent for others. Under this model, private sector stakeholders can expect a sustainable return on their invest-ment. However, there need to be clear delineations between the expected return on investment from the private sector and the socially acceptable level of public expenditure that will be re-quired.    Option 3b: Public-Private Partnership – Availability model: The investment required for system conception and deployment is fully ensured by a private sector contractor. The EU would commit to long term availability payments regarding the use of the system, complemented by start-up payments during the construction and deployment phase. Tailored incentive schemes would reward compliance with the availability, quality, performance and security requirements of the EU and member states. Out-side of the reserved capacity for use by the EU and member states, the contractor could be tasked to market services to public, commercial and private customers with the revenues subject to a profit-shar-ing approach.   This model best aligns risks with the party best able to deal with them. The private sector pro-vider develops and deploys the system. Once deployed, the private sector then maintains and operates the system and delivers the relevant services, subject to a bespoke performance re-gime and an incentive scheme, which measure and reward quality and performance (etc.). It will be critical to define fair criteria for the assessment of such performance. These criteria will need to be agreed jointly by the private sector and the public authorities. The EU, which is seeking to build the necessary infrastructure and develop the market for its own purposes, commits to an availability payment which supports and underpins the private sector’s revenues to ensure that its objectives are achieved, to be complemented by start-up payments. This integration not only delivers costs savings, which will be important to affordability, but also facilitates third party revenues because the provider will be able seamlessly to bring customers from other assets/its network (etc.) onto this system.    The private sector will usually bear the residual liability risk under an availability-based PPP model, and there are established market precedents to protect the EU and Member States in the event of termination.    This is the most proven model. For this reason, the procurement timetable for Option 3b will be swifter than 3a and the risk of market failure reduced. Provided that the overall risk allocation position is sensible and involves the transfer of only those risks which can be managed effec-tively by the private sector, Option 3b is also likely to deliver the most competition amongst potential private sector partners and investors and reduce the likelihood of potential legal chal-lenges. That, in turn, is likely to drive more innovation, more efficiency and better value for money for the EU and Member States.  In summary, we are currently of the opinion that Options 3a and 3b look to offer the best formula, combining private sector expertise and efficiency with public sector sovereignty and resources. Whether the optimal option is 3a, 3b or a combination of both based on the various use cases remains to be assessed in light of the study’s outcomes. SES is prepared to become a financial investor in scenario 3a and preferably in scenario 3b. How SES can best help SES and other European satellite network operators can bring real value to ESSCS and support the EC in its design and development.   Together with other major EU satellite network operators, OEMs, and telecommunications operators, we are currently involved in the initial phase of the ESSCS study.  What we bring to the table:   ▪ Financial commitment from SES,  ▪ Proven expertise in designing, launching, and operating profitable GSO & NGSO networks, ▪ Innovation (mPOWER, cloud, etc.), ▪ Readiness to leverage EU’s industry expertise and to invest cash into an innovative EU sovereign constellation, ▪ Early start of service and a sustainable solution based on re-use of existing and committed satellite assets in GSO & NGSO networks, ▪ Expertise in and potentially an early start of service on QKD.   Conclusion  SES will be looking forward to the next announcements by the European Commission. We will continue collaborating closely with the European Commission and providing feedback as the project evolves. SES is available to provide DG DEFIS additional information as and when requested.  It is critical that European space industries continue being consulted throughout the next phases of the ESSCS project.   We are eager to contribute to the next phase of the project, once the Commission has decided on the shape of the constellation, its governance, and the institutional arrangements to ensure ESSCS is a global success with deep European roots.

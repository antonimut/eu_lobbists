FIVA submission 
Introduction 
FIVA’s purpose is to protect, preserve, and promote motoring heritage i.e. the millions of historic 
cars, motorcycles and trucks and busses which individuals own and maintain because of their passion 
for their vehicles. FIVA’s response to this consultation is directed by its mission to preserve this 
heritage – and this includes current historic vehicles, potential historic vehicles and the historic 
vehicles of the future. 
FIVA defines a historic vehicle as: “a mechanically propelled road vehicle: which is at least 30 years 
old; which is preserved and maintained in a historically correct condition; which is not used as a 
means of daily transport; and which is therefore a part of our technical and cultural heritage.” An EU 
legal definition is provided by Article 3(7) of the EU Roadworthiness Testing Directive 2014/45/EU1.  
One of the tenets of Directive 2005/64/EC is to ensure the reusability of vehicle parts - when an 
individual preserves a historic vehicle, they are negating the need to end the life of the vehicle 
because they are reusing the whole vehicle. This is reflected in the ELV Directive which makes clear 
that it does not cover historic vehicles as Recital 10 states that: Vintage vehicles, meaning historic 
vehicles or vehicles of value to collectors or intended for museums, kept in a proper and 
environmentally sound manner, either ready for use or stripped into parts, are not covered by the 
definition of waste laid down by Directive 75/442/EEC and do not fall within the scope of this 
Directive. This Recital reflects well the historic vehicle park and the actions and intentions of historic 
vehicle owners.   
The ELV Directive uses the definition of “waste” as provided by Council Directive 75/442/EEC on 
waste; this has been superseded by Directives 2006/12/EC and 2008/98/EC all of which define 
‘waste’ as any substance or object which the holder discards or intends or is required to discard.  
Issues of concern 
Mandated determination of an ELV 
FIVA is aware of instances where vehicle owners importing or exporting vehicles have been denied 
the opportunity to renovate and then preserve a vehicle as local authorities have determined that a 
vehicle is waste.  There have been a number of such cases in Sweden involving both intra-EU and EU 
export and import – in each case the authorities have ignored the fact that the owner had stated

Such situations are perverse given that the intended actions of the owners would: 
1. Avoid ending the life of a vehicle requiring environmentally costly recycling /treatment of the 
waste 
2. Ensure the reusability of a vehicle 
3. Generate work (usually for SMEs) - the restoration of a historic vehicle is a significant 
economic activity  
4. Use the restored vehicle sparingly and safely, and maintain it to a high standard - the time 
and money is invested because of a passion for the vehicle hence there is an inherent desire 
to look after it.   
Dismantling/restoration of a vehicle  
Often a vehicle is dismantled, even almost completely, for the purpose of renovation. This process 
needs to be recognised as legitimate – i.e. the vehicle is not an ELV just because it is being 
dismantled for the purpose of renovation. 
Also, the economic cost of renovation is commonly much greater than the value of the vehicle - the 
value of the vehicle itself is realised once the repair/renovation is complete. This needs to be 
recognised particularly if a vehicle is being sold to an owner in a third country where the restoration 
is due to take place.  
A vehicle which is in a state to become waste may contain a functionally complete component (an 
engine, a gearbox etc) which has significant value as a replacement in a historic, or potentially 
historic, vehicle. These components must be allowed to be saved for use in repair/restoration of 
historic vehicles. This possibility is necessary and is consistent with the words of Recital 10: “… either 
ready for use or stripped into parts…” 
Vehicle registration 
In some countries it is possible for a vehicle to be de-registered if it is not being used for a period of 
time. In those countries, historic vehicles are often de-registered during a period of restoration or 
just because the vehicle is not planned to be used for a while; equally many vehicles in museums are 
not registered and some vehicles are active but used off-road and so may not be registered. Linking 
ELV status to registration would require historic vehicles in this situation to be classified as ELV and 
so have to be destroyed. This would clearly be wrong. 
Proposals to address the above concerns 
Safeguards are needed to realise the intent of Recital 10 of the ELV Directive. I.e, the system must: 
- Provide for the presumption that only an owner can determine whether a vehicle is “end-of-
life” to avoid owners from being prevented from restoring their vehicles 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
- In the event that authorities lawfully seize a vehicle because it seems to be abandoned or is 
causing a hazard, the vehicle will not be waste until the owner or keeper has confirmed they 
will not reclaim it 
- Provide for the presumption that if an owner sells a vehicle (or exports it for the purpose of 
sale) it does not mean that the owner is discarding the vehicle in the context of the definition 
of waste as provided by the Waste Directives  
- Allow for the dismantling of a vehicle where the purpose is restoration – i.e. the dismantling 
is not “destruction” nor is the vehicle an ELV just because it is being dismantled for 
restoration 
- Allow for functionally complete components (engines, gearboxes etc) to be retrieved from an 
ELV in order to be reused in a historic vehicle 
- Allow for the fact that repair/restoration values for historic vehicles may be disproportionate 
to the vehicle value 
- Do not connect the registration system to the ELV system as in some countries it is possible 
for historic vehicles to be de-registered if they are not being used for a period of time, are in 
museums or are being used only off-road. 
The export of old vehicles to third countries 
The consultation paper notes the possibility to regulate/ban the export of certain old vehicles to 
third countries. Such a measure has the potential to impact upon the legitimate extra-EU sale of 
historic vehicles. Clearly this must be avoided. The definition of a historic vehicle as provided by the 
Roadworthiness Testing Directive could be helpful in this context as it makes clear the distinction 
between a vehicle which is just “old” and a historic vehicle. However, as noted above there are some 
circumstances where a vehicle is being sold and exported because the purchaser intends to restore 
the vehicle. In these circumstances, the type of vehicle, the nature of the sale and the intent of the 
purchaser will provide ample information for it to be clear that the export is taking place to preserve 
a part of motoring heritage rather than being the sale of an old vehicle which is inappropriate for 
further use in a third country.  
If measures are implemented in the Directive to ban the sale of “old” vehicles to third countries, the 
Directive should also make provision for the legitimate sale and export of historic vehicles or of 
vehicles due for restoration by the new owner.  
Scrappage schemes 
A further concern is the possibility that motoring heritage can be lost as a consequence of an owner 
taking advantage of a scrappage scheme in return for a discount on a new car. For example, in 2017 a 
1959 Standard Ten was offered to a Ford dealer in the UK under a voluntary dealer-led scrappage 
scheme. Media interest helped Ford to recognise the heritage value of the vehicle and rather than 
scrap the car, the company ensured that the car is now displayed in a museum.   
Therefore, if a requirement is to be placed upon owners to declare a vehicle an ELV upon it reaching 
the end of its life, there must be the possibility to avoid ELV status so the potential for it to become a 
historic vehicle remains – i.e. in case a third party becomes aware of the vehicle and wishes to 
purchase it specifically to preserve the heritage.

REVISION OF ELV DIRECTIVE 
 
 
 
The End-of-Life Vehicles Directive 2000/53/EC ("ELV Directive") was adopted in 2000 to minimize the 
environmental impact of end -of-life vehicles and to improve the efficiency of all economic operators 
involved in the vehicle life cycle. In 2021, the Europe an Commission has completed the evaluation of 
this Directive, has identified many shortcomings (loss of traceability of 6 million vehicles per year, end-
of-life costs borne mainly by ELV centers and authorized shredders, non -involvement of insurers, etc.) 
and is now working on an impact assessment to support a possible revision of the Directive. A public 
consultation is available on the Commission's website and will end on 26 October 2021.  
 
 
The Federation of Recycling Companies (FEDEREC) represents the authorized economic actors who are 
involved in the recycling of end -of-life vehicles (ELV), from the collection and administrative 
management of the vehicle to the recycling and energy recovery of the materials. In this context, 
FEDEREC has a strong interest in the current revision of Directive 2000/53/EC. 
 
In addition to the answers to the questionnaire of the current public consultation, here are some 
additional details that the federation wishes to provide.   
 
Indeed, the recycling professionals consider that this revision is the opportunity to solve certain 
problems whose stakes are consequent for the sector and, thanks to 20 years of hi ndsight, to bring 
some clarifications to the current text.  
 
1. Define ELV and distinguish it from used vehicles 
 
The public consultation states that "it is difficult to distinguish on a  case-by-case basis whether a 
vehicle is a used vehicle or an end-of-life vehicle. Indeed, the definition of ELV is currently too imprecise 
and there are no tools or binding provisions to differentiate these two types of vehicles. While the 
export of ELVs to non-OECD countries is prohibited, this is not the case for used vehicles.  
 
In order to fight against the illegal export of ELVs, FEDEREC proposes to adopt new conditions for the 
export of used vehicles and in particular to require the presentation of a  valid technical control 
certificate to authorize their export.  
 
2. Set reuse targets to support the transition to the circular economy in the European Union 
 
Recycling professionals consider that spec ific reuse targets could be implemented, independently of 
recycling, to support the transition to the circular economy within Europe. However, older ELVs will 
not be suitable for the reuse market. It will therefore be necessary to set rates according to th e age 
of the vehicles.

3. Combine recycling targets and incorporation obligations 
 
Currently the ELV Directive does not set any material -specific recycling targets. However, setting 
material-specific recycling targets would increase the separate recycling of the materials covered by 
these targets. Today, some materials are recoverable but have no outlet. According to FEDEREC, such 
a measure cannot be dissociated from an obligation to incorporate raw materials from recycling in 
new vehicles put on the market, especially as it will increase recycling costs for the recycling 
industries. 
 
Furthermore, we believe that it is reductive to consider that pre-shredding recycling operations are 
the only qualitative solution . Sorting technologies downstream of the shredding plant (post -
shredding) are increasingly efficient and allow the separation of the materials making up the vehicles. 
ELVs are waste products with specific characteristics that must be taken into account: their age, their 
condition (crashed vehicles, burned vehicles) will have an impact on the fire risk and on the safety of 
the employees in char ge of dismantling, as well as on the capacity of certain parts to be reused or 
certain materials to be recovered.   
 
4. Involve all stakeholders to increase recycling performance 
 
The ELV Directive evaluation report published by Trinomics in September 2020 highlighted several 
interesting points. 
 
Firstly, according to this report, more than 80% of the cost of end -of-life vehicles is covered by the 
approved ELV centers and shredders. However, the existence of an extended producer responsibility 
mechanism assumes that the economic actors who place the products on the market (manufacturers, 
distributors, importers) are responsible for the entire life cycle of their products (from eco -design to 
end-of-life). However, the figures show that manufacturers make too little financial contribution to 
the recycling of their vehicles. Although the ELV recycling industry has proven that it is capable of 
achieving the objectives imposed by the ELV D irective through a self -financing mechanism, the 
industry will have to face new challenges: the arrival of hybrid and electric vehicles, the treatment of 
persistent organic pollutants (POPs), the lack of outlets for the elimination of final waste, etc. The se 
future requirements will have a cost and the Federation considers that obtaining financial support 
from manufacturers is necessary to maintain compliance with European rates and contribute to 
quality recycling. 
 
Secondly, the report also  notes the absence of any reference to insurance companies in the ELV 
Directive. However, the latest of ADEME (french environnemental agency) report indicates that 
insurance companies and mutual insurance companies represent 14% of the supply sources of 
approved ELV centers. Rherefore, it  seems ne cessary to involve these players in the end -of-life 
process.

5. Establish binding mechanisms to effectively combat the illegal sector 
 
The public consultation indicates that 30 to 40% of vehicles disappear in the European Union without 
being notified to the national registers of the Member States. Two explanations have been put forward 
: 
• Not all exported used vehicles are declared in the registers;  
• Not all end-of-life vehicles are treated in authorized treatment facilities. 
 
The Federation considers that mechanisms could be put in place at the European Union level, some of 
which already exist in certain member countries, to effectively fight against the illegal sector.  
 
Firstly, FEDEREC considers that the possibility for private individuals to stop paying the insurance fee 
for their vehicle should be made conditional on the provision of a transfer for destruction by an 
approved ELV center. This practice has been implement ed by the Czech Republic and has proven its 
effectiveness. 
 
Secondly, the federation considers that the sale of used parts online should be restricted to approved 
ELV centers , as there is no indication that dismantling and depollution have been carried out  in 
accordance with the environmental rules in force.   
 
6. Require the introduction of eco-designed vehicles 
 
At the moment, the ELV Directive encourages eco -design but does not impose anything on 
manufacturers. However, with the massive arrival of the electric vehicle, we consider that the end of 
life of the vehicle must be anticipated from its conception.  
 
For vehicles already on the market, the federation is encouraging real cooperation between 
manufacturers and recycling companies. At present, although there are still few hybrid and electric 
vehicles on our members' premises, all of them seem to cause problems in terms of the environment, 
staff safety and fire risks. 
 
Furthermore, with regard to the hazardous substances to be banned from use in vehicles, the industry 
considers that these should be specified not in the ELV Directive but, for example, under c hemicals 
legislation such as the REACH Regulation. In addition, criteria relating to the impact of life cycle analysis 
(e.g. recyclability) should be integrated into the mechanism for assessing exemptions to the substance 
ban list. Recycling professionals point out that it is also necessary to avoid industrial secrecy in order 
to achieve quality recycling.  
 
This last point also applies to parts: car manufacturers need to design parts in such a way that they can 
be more easily disassembled for reuse, remanufacturing, recycling and recovery. To do this, it will be 
necessary to take into account the date of design of the vehicle and to inform the recyclers of the 
location of the components. At present, recyclers do not have access to information on all the batteries 
present in hybrid and electric vehicles (engine batteries, memory batteries, etc.).

At the same time, the reuse of used parts should be facilitated  (e.g., require vehicle manufacturers 
to allow parts to be unlocked so they can be reused and disasse mbled). This market is crucial and 
demand varies greatly depending on the age of the vehicles. It is therefore necessary to take into 
account the logic of the market, especially for storage issues. 
 
Still on the components and materials used in vehicles, some of them sometimes contain substances 
of concern and critical raw materials. Eco -design could facilitate the identification and separate 
disassembly of these components and materials by, for example, printing on visible and accessible 
parts, by developing procedures and tools available to approved ELV centers and by anticipating that 
the removal of certain parts will not cause damage to the part or its environment and will not endanger 
the operator in charge of disassembling/recycling this part. For example, in France, the neutralization 
of airbags is a real challenge for approved ELV centers and current legislation does not take into 
account the practical consequences of this neutralization. 
 
FEDEREC also considers that the IDIS database is not adapted to the industry because of the diversity 
of models with regard to the industrial dimension of recycling processes.

EU Roadmap on food waste reduction targets - consultation response
Oddbox welcomes the roadmap as a purpose-led business with a vision to ensure thatall food grown is eaten. We strongly recommend that the Commission models a 50%reduction of FLW (food loss and waste) for all EU member states under Step 2 Option 3(rather than 40-50% reduction) and puts forward a legislative proposal for all memberstates to meet a 50% reduction of FLW from farm to fork by 2030, ideally by Q4 of2022. The EU and member states are already committed to meeting SDG 12.3 toreduce food waste by 50% by 2030; to be consistent with fulﬁlling this commitment,the Commission should create a legislative framework to ensure members states meetthis target. Lower targets proposed in options 1 and 2 are inconsistent with the EUmeeting its international obligations to SDG 12.3, and therefore should not beconsidered for a legislative proposal. If options 1 or 2 are modelled in the impactassessment, we recommend that this be purely for comparative purposes, not forconsideration as EU targets. For member states who have pre-2020 baseline dataavailable, we recommend it be admissible for countries to achieve 50% reduction froma 2015 or later baseline, to reﬂect existing progress. Achieving a 50% reduction in FLWby 2030 will also help the EU meet its commitments under the European Climate Law,the Global Methane Pledge, the Circular Economy Package, and European Green Deal.
We anticipate that modelling may reveal that voluntary commitments for businessesand consumer education will not deliver reductions at sufﬁcient speed to deliver SDG12.3, based on evidence from the UK where a decade of voluntary commitments bybusinesses have yielded limited progress(1) . We therefore strongly recommend that itis vital to include in modelling the potential effects of regulation to deliver moreambitious faster-paced FLW reduction – including types of regulation which have notyet been introduced in EU member states – such as mandatory FLW public reportingand reduction targets for food businesses over a certain size, strengthened UnfairTrading Practices regulation with an explicit focus on preventing food waste risks andcosts being pushed onto suppliers, extended producer responsibility schemes for foodwaste and levies charged on supermarkets based on food wasted in their supply chains,and increased taxes and bans on sending FLW to incineration and landﬁll2 . If modellingof the feasibility of member states reaching binding food waste targets is conducted bymember states themselves, we recommend that the Commission require memberstates to consider the potential effects of regulation beyond voluntary agreements bybusinesses. For instance, the UK government is planning to introduce mandatory foodwaste reporting for all businesses over a certain size, after a House of Commons EFRAcommittee found that the lack of businesses reporting their food waste data “showsthat a voluntary approach is inadequate”(3) . Only 60 businesses in the UK havepublicly reported their food waste data after a decade of voluntary agreements.

We strongly recommend the Commission should adopt Option S1 to provide equaltreatment for all stages of the supply chain and comprehensive coverage rather thanlimiting scope to cover retail and consumer sectors only. This is in accordance withguidance from Champions 12.3 that SDG 12.3 should be interpreted as a target toreduce FLW by 50% across the entire supply chain from the point food is ready toharvest or slaughter, saying that states “should apply the “halve per capita” in practiceto food losses [i.e. pre-retail food waste], as well, not just to food waste” – and that thisshould cover “from the point that crops and livestock are ready for harvest orslaughter through to the point that they are ready to be ingested by people”7 . EUFUSIONS data shows that over three times more food is wasted in the EU processingsector, and two times more in food service, than in the retail sector8 and thereforeinclusion of these sectors is vital for both fairness and to ensure comprehensivecoverage. Since relatively little food is wasted at retail level, a restriction of scope tocover retail and consumer food waste only will unfairly limit food waste reduction toprimarily consumers, whilst leaving the majority of businesses unaccountable for foodwaste reduction. Food waste is often highly concentrated in a smaller number of largefood businesses, in contrast to the dispersed nature of consumer food waste acrossmillions of households – and therefore reducing this waste requires action from asmaller number of actors, with higher potential for faster progress. A whole supplychain approach is required, to ensure joined-up rather than siloed thinking, and avoidperverse side-effects like the risk of food waste being moved further up the supplychain rather than reduced. Current evidence suggests that businesses collectivelygenerate comparable volumes of food waste to households, and therefore it isproportional for them to be held equally accountable. If all food waste at primaryproduction is included in ﬁgures (see below), food businesses may waste considerablymore food than households, collectively. We also recommend mandatory MS FLWreporting be extended to include food left unharvested and ploughed back in, andlivestock mortalities on-farm, and that MS report this data by 2024 as a baseline forthe inclusion of all primary production food waste in mandatory reduction targets. Thisis to reﬂect vital new data: WWF’s recent ‘Driven to Waste’ report is the mostcomprehensive review of data on primary production FLW to date - it ﬁnds that about150 million tonnes of food is wasted on European farms (14.6% of total production),based on 175 of the most up to date European studies9 . Signiﬁcantly, the WWF studyﬁnds that higher-income countries experience higher levels of per capita food waste atprimary production than in developing countries. FUSIONS estimates that EUcountries waste 9 million tonnes at primary production were based on only 6 studiespre-2016, are outdated and need urgent updating10 . Given that the population of theEU is roughly 60% of the population of Europe as a whole, and assuming uniform percapita food waste rates across Europe, total primary production food waste in the EUis therefore likely to be about 90 million tonnes, based on the up-todate WWF data.

This is a greater volume than previously estimated for the EU’s total food waste fromall supply chain stages taken together (88 million tonnes)11, based on a far strongerevidence base than FUSIONS data for most supply chain stages in 2016. Continuedexclusion of unharvested food, food left ploughed in and livestock moralities thereforerisks up to half of the EU’s food waste being excluded from measurement andreporting. There is also a considerable risk that if the majority of food waste at primaryproduction is not measured, food waste “reduction” at manufacturing and retail levelwill simply be achieved by pushing food waste onto their suppliers in primaryproduction, to erase it from ofﬁcial statistics. There is extensive evidence that retailerpolicies already push the costs and risks of food waste onto their suppliers, throughmeans such as last-minute order cancellations, ﬂuctuating cosmetic standards,inﬂexible marketing of products, and inﬂexible demands for consistent supply leadingto institutionalised over-production12 . These recommendations have widespreadsupport. In 2017, 67 European organisations called for an EU FLW “reduction target of50% by 2030 to be speciﬁed as farm to fork […] binding at EU member state level”13 .In 2020, many organisations reiterated this policy ask, calling for “binding targetscommitting to a 50% reduction by 2030 of all food waste from farm to fork, and bringforward the proposal of binding targets on food waste to the whole supply chain asearly as possible, ideally to 2022”14 . We recommend Options E2 and T1/3 hybrid asour ﬁrst preference: for all MS to have a legally binding target to reduce their FLW to auniform kg per capita food waste which is the same for all member states (T1), set at alevel calculated to ensure overall EU-wide 50% FLW reduction by 2030 (T3). This is toensure that countries starting from higher per capita waste levels take more actionthan countries starting from a lower per capita food waste baseline. However, wewould also ﬁnd Option E1 acceptable – for each member state to have a legally bindingtarget to reduce their food waste by 50% by 2030. We recommend that the mostimportant criteria for the targets to meet are that 1) the targets are legally binding atmember states level, 2) the targets collectively mean that EU food waste is reduced by50% by 2030, and 3) that legislation is introduced as quickly as possible to givemember states time to achieve these targets by 2030. Therefore, if a T1/3 hybridwould be too time-consuming to agree and delay the implementation or endanger theachievement of the MS targets, we would be supportive of E1 if it would speedimplementation.

Facts and Position Paper
Microplastics
1. Introduction
In search for the causes of the increasing amount of 
plastic waste in the oceans and of microplastic partic-
les in the environment, the textile industry is also in 
focus due to the possible abrasion of plastic fibres. 
Numerous unsubstantiated allegations are put in the 
spotlight and textile is disregarded as part of the soluti-
on with the help of environmental and filter technology.
The German textile industry is facing the public discus-
sion about its possible contribution to the occurrence 
of microplastics in the environment and is willing to co-
operate constructively on appropriate solutions to the 
problem.
At the same time, however, the textile industry first of 
all expects that a scientific factual basis for the micro-
plastics problem will be established and that possible 
solutions will be developed and discussed on this basis.
2. Background
Microplastics are defined as „microparticles of non-bio-
degradable plastics with a size of less than 5mm“. In 
addition, a distinction must be made between primary 
and secondary microplastics.
The following main case groups can be distinguished:
-  intentionally added microplastics, e.g. in cosmetics 
and paints (primary microplastics)
-  microplastics caused by the decay (including photo-
oxidative processes) of larger plastic parts such as 
bags and bottles („plastic litter“) in nature (secondary 
microplasty)
-  microplastics from abrasive effects, e.g. fiber abrasi-
on from clothing, abrasion from car tires (secondary 
microplastic)
Previous results of investigations and research pro-
jects
1 show that microplastics are very widespread and 
occur in very different concentrations and size distri-
butions in different environmental media. The range 
of the results in relation to the quantities found in the 
samples and the extrapolated total quantities in the 
environment is still very large. Also there are still very 
different statements about the main sources of input.
So far, there does not seem to be any reliable evidence 
as to whether microplastic particles pose a toxicologi-
cal hazard. Animal experiments in which mice were fed 
with extremely high doses of microplastic showed no 
harmful effects on the organs of the animals
2. Howe-
ver, it is also said that the particles are not retrievable 
as soon as they enter the environment and the mere 
presence of the particles is therefore a cause for con-
cern. Moreover, they are decomposed very slowly in 
the environment (sometimes over several hundred 
years) and accumulate as they continue to enter the 
environment.

3. Role of the textile industry
According to the available knowledge, microplastic 
particles are released into the environment from texti-
les in the form of fibres of different sizes. This propably 
occurs mainly when washing textiles, but to a lesser 
extent also during production and daily use. It should 
be noted that a large proportion of clothing consists of 
natural fibres such as cotton, which decompose rela-
tively quickly in the environment and are therefore no 
longer a problem.
Fibre abrasion from textile washing does not seem to 
be by far the largest source of microplastic compared 
to tyre abrasion, for example
3. In Germany, according 
to available studies, fibre abrasion from textile washing 
is around 70g per person and year compared to ap-
prox. 1.2kg per person and year for tyre abrasion.
While the abrasion of car tires is discharged directly 
into the environment, the waste water from textile pro-
duction and domestic washing is fed to a sewage treat-
ment plant. According to previous knowledge, signifi-
cantly more than 95% of fibre abrasion in Germany is 
retained in sewage treatment plants. Due to the subse-
quent sludge incineration, it can be assumed that the 
fibre abrasion from this input path will be eliminated to 
a very large extent.
Sewage treatment plants and also catch baskets in 
street drains can also be further improved with textile 
filters (e.g. stainless steel mesh, fleece cloth) and their 
elimination rate of fibre abrasion can be increased to 
over 99%. This makes it clear that textile materials can 
be a very important part of the solution to the micro-
plastic problem.
Various research projects are currently being carried 
out on the sources of microplastics and what can be 
done against microplastics in the environment. At the 
same time, further research is being conducted into 
whether and, if so, what ecotoxicological risks the pre-
sence of microplastics entails.
A very large research project with great relevance for 
the textile industry is the „TextileMission“
 project4, which 
is currently being carried out by the Federal Ministry of 
Education and Research (BMBF) as part of the funding 
priority „Plastics in the Environment, Sources, Sinks, 
Solutions“. The project is dedicated to the following to-
pics, among others:
-  Status Quo Analysis: the microplastic emission of 
marketable textiles
-  sewage treatment plant technology: the retention 
capacity of different clarification stages
-  holistic perspective: sustainability aspects of alterna-
tive materials
-  textile research: biodegradable materials/new cutting 
and processing possibilities
-  product development: production and testing of 
prototypes
The project is supported and carried out by the Bun-
desverband Sportartikelindustrie (BSI), the Hochschu-
le Niederrhein, the TU Dresden and the WWF. The 
project is expected to be completed and the results 
presented spring-2021.
The European Textile Federation Euratex is involved 
in a cross industry agreement
5 between five European 
industrial sectors on the subject of microplastics. The 
aim of this voluntary cooperation is to intensify re-
search and standardisation efforts in order to find new 
solutions which help to reduce the impact of microplas-
tics on the environment.

4. Solutions
Various proposals are being discussed to reduce fibre 
input through textiles into the environment. These in-
clude, but are not limited to:
-  equipment of sewage treatment plants and road 
drains with textile filters to collect microplastics: 
Previous investigations and tests have shown good 
results. Textiles can therefore be an important part of 
the solution to the microplastic problem.
-  further development and improvement of biode-
gradable fibres: These fibres have been developed 
for years and are already in use. However, further im-
provements and optimizations can take place here. 
This requires further financial support for research.
-  more recycling of plastics: Even today, unmixed 
plastics such as PET from bottles can be recycled 
very well and can be used, for example, in the manu-
facture of clothing and shoes. This should be further 
expanded by increasing the collection of single-vari-
ety plastics, which would reduce the input of plastics 
into the environment and thus ultimately the formati-
on of microplastics.
5. Outstanding issues and research needs
It remains an open question how large the quantities 
of microplastics from textiles that end up in the envi-
ronment actually are. The ecological concern of micro-
plastics have not yet been proven, but the precautiona-
ry principle requires that microplastics should not end 
up in the environment.
From our point of view, there is a further need to fi-
nancially support certain research into production pro-
cesses, wastewater treatment or the development of 
biodegradable fibres. Further funds should be made 
available for this purpose.
This also applies to processes and applications that 
already exist, but whose practical application is not 
economically feasible for companies in international 
competition.
6. Summary and requirements
-  The textile industry is open and constructive to the 
problem of „microplastics from textiles“.
-  We hope and expect that politics and the public will 
deal with the topic rationally on the basis of facts.
-  Further research and corresponding financial res-
sources are necessary in order to better understand 
the problem of „microplastics from textiles“ and to 
develop possible solutions.
-  The textile industry is happy to contribute construc-
tively to these solutions.
-  Textiles can be an important part of the solution to the 
microplastic problem, for example as filter materials.
-  All solutions must be implemented on a global, at 
least Europe-wide and voluntary basis.
-  Further financial support is needed to implement 
solutions, because companies cannot finance them 
on their own in international competition.

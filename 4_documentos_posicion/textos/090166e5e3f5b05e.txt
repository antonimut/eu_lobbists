ELV Directive 
This document supports Jaguar Land Rover’s (JLR) responses to the questionnaire on the 
revision of the ELV Directive. While most elements are broadly in line with the position of  the 
automotive industry, JLR would like to reiterate its commitment to  deliver on its Reimagine 
strategy, and to achieve net zero carbon emissions across its supply chain, products and 
operations by 2039, thereby supporting the EU Green Deal’s objectives. 
JLR is of the opin ion that a vehicle is a product that should be considered across all stages of 
its life cycle, from upstream value chains through production and the use phase, and down to 
recycling. This document therefore emphasises the need to keep a whole life cycle perspective 
and highlights some of the challenges raised by the upcoming revision of the directive, in order 
to make the new legislation a success story for the circular economy. 
Support the market for spare parts from ELVs 
The reuse for spare parts is a well -functioning market economy, based on the experience of 
ATFs. Setting dismantling targets for reuse is not the most efficient solution to promote re-use 
of spare parts from ELVs, as demand for the parts of a specific vehicle greatly varies depending 
on its model year and characteristics. Dismantling parts from old models (e.g. EU3 or EU4) for 
which there is no more demand, may even have negative environmental effects. 
Some countries seek to encourage demand by implemented effective measures that support 
the reused parts’ market: 
• Netherlands or Sweden, as well as the USA, achieve high rates for reuse and reused 
genuine parts in the repair parts market, with supporting insurance policies such as a 
reduced insurance premium for clients accepting repairs with reused parts whenever 
they are available. 
• Specific measures to offer and promote reused parts in car repair workshops have been 
applied in France since 2017, although it may be too earl y to evaluate the results of 
these provisions. 
An important measure would be to require a valid ATF registration number for selling reused 
parts on online marketplaces and ban the sale of fraudulent spare parts from illegal ATFs. 
Clarifying in the EU legislation that parts dismantled from ELVs for reuse (with all the required 
traceability) are not waste and can be handled and can be transported without restriction 
between EU member states could also support the dismantling and trade of ELV parts for reuse 
across the EU. The final choice should be up to the customer rather than depend on a target 
regulated by law. 
 
Improving the general conditions for secondary material 
JLR is aiming to reduce the use of virgin material from fossil sources by further increasing the 
use of (all) secondary materials wherever it makes sense from a technical, economic and 
environmental perspective. JLR is therefore actively engaged with its supplier base to increase 
the recycled content in new products.

JLR believes that a recycled plastics target can be introduced, on the condition it remains 
reasonable and contributes significantly to the reduction of the environmental impact of the 
final product. 
The prerequisites to establish such a target can be summarised by four essential elements: 
“sufficient quantity”, “acceptable quality” and “consistency over a defined contractual 
duration”, with a “competitive price compared to virgin material”. 
In this context, if recycled plastics content targets were to be considered, JLR would request 
to take the following principles into consideration: 
• Due to the lack of availability of the required volumes of materials, any targets could 
lead to a de-facto restriction of production or type approval. If all industrial sectors have 
to comply with specific post -consumer recycled material targets, it will likely lead to 
cannibalisation of the most desirable waste streams. 
• Closed loop recycling of long living products is only possible on a case -by-case basis 
because of the degradation of the material and other technical and legal issues e.g. 
legacy substances: 
• The larger the Challenge CL, the more virgin material needs to be added to the recycled 
plastics in order to fulfil the  state-of-the-art requirements and to compete with new 
plastics. 
• A minimum transition period of 5 years between the publication of such a requirement 
and its enforcement to new vehicle types should be granted, given the long and 
complex product development  cycles and contractual supplier agreements of the 
automotive industry. 
• Mechanically recycled post -consumer recyclates are technically eligible to a limited 
number of automotive applications, due to the high quality and performance 
requirements of new prod ucts. Time and significant R&D efforts are required to 
increase this potential substantially. 
• Post-industrial recyclates are essential to expand the use of recycled polymers to 
visible and technically challenging applications. It should be considered as a first 
essential step towards a wider adoption of post-consumer recyclates. 
• Any target should be based on technologies and materials that are currently available 
on the global market. A large share of vehicles registered in the EU are not produced in 
the EU. Supply must also be guaranteed on the non-EU markets. 
• Such a target requires coordination across the entire global supply chain, which entails 
a large management workload. To achieve this goal at the lowest possible cost for 
consumers, component and mate rial suppliers should be required to incorporate a 
specific share of recycled plastics content within their production.

• Development of a new UN -ECE GTR (or ISO 22628) including clear global definitions

• A certified mass balance approach should be elaborated and recognised to calculate

Reducing the number of unknown whereabouts 
A recent implementation assessment of the ELV Directive suggest ed the implementation of 
temporary deregistration fees to prevent the issue of missing vehicles, i.e. vehicles that 
disappear without notification to the national registry. Instead, JLR would re commend the 
establishment of a regular notification system, which should be carefully developed and allow 
exemptions for special cases (e.g. seasonal vehicles like convertibles) as well as reduced 
notification burden  for dealers, etc., in order not to penalise owners who truly want to 
temporarily deregister their vehicles. 
In this context, the certificate of destruction needs to be strengthened. An automatic deletion 
from the registration system after seven years without information about the whereabouts of 
the vehicles, like e.g. in Germany, must be stopped in the future. This would create the basis 
for reliable information on the whereabouts of end-of-life vehicles. The final deregistration of a 
vehicle for the purpose of recycling should only be allowed by the mandatory presentation of 
a certificate of destruction (without additional costs). 
 
Illegal exports 
Given that the ELV Directive falls under the waste legislation regime, the export of goods 
should not be regulated in its revision.  Nevertheless, JLR believes that an enhanced 
enforcement of existing measures would help to improve the situation. 
 
Promote a circular econo my through design -for-sustainability 
and the use of “best available technology” 
The dismantling of vehicle components prior to the shredding can offer a useful option to 
improve the recycling of end -of-life vehicles. This however cannot be seen as a genera l rule 
and strongly depends on several factors. Vehicle components that are likely to be re -used or 
re-manufactured are already dismantled from old vehicles today. The dismantling of additional 
specific vehicle components can make sense in some particular cases, e.g. for larger plastic 
parts that are composed of a single material, or electric traction motors. For all other parts, the 
same goal can be achieved by using advanced sorting technologies after the shredder (e.g. 
Sicon post-shredder technology – PST).  The use of best available techniques applied to PST 
should be recommended. 
The dismantling for the purpose of “detoxification” of the waste stream has been proven to be 
economically inefficient in most cases, and even harmful to the environment – due to a much 
higher carbon footprint caused by logistics.

A dismantling obligation, by definition, is counterproductive to the intended objectives. In order 
for recycling to be successful, JLR recommends to only insist on dismantling if scientific 
evidence demonstrates intended objectives cannot be achieved otherwise. 
 
Substances of concern (SoC) and critical raw materials (CRM) 
For JLR, m aking SoCs/CRM identifiable in products requires an approach based on 
technological neutrality. Even so, s uch measures will not lead to a full transparency of 
SoCs/CRM in durable products, as the only information that can be made identifiable is what 
is known to be a SoC/CRM at production time, whereas the information will be needed 15-20 
years later. 
JLR also requests the Commission to better define SoCs in order to provide further elements 
on this topic. 
 
RRR and recycling capability 
Questions 22 and 23 suggest conditioning the issuance of a certificate of compliance with the 
manufacturer providing evidence of actual recycling capacities for a given material or 
technology. It remains unclear how an ‘innovative material’ is supposed to be defined. 
It must also be noted that there is a period of 15 to 20 years between the 3R type-approval of 
a vehicle and the need to have the appropriate recycling technology available for its recycling. 
Already today, manufacturers are developing new recycling technologies together with the 
recycling industry. 
 
Sustainable sourcing 
JLR believes that the introduction of further requirements on ethical sourcing of raw materials 
in the ELV directive is not necessary. EU-wide supply chain due diligence legislation is expected 
to be published soon, in addition to the existing OECD guidelines and CSR Directive . JLR have 
set up processes for sustainable sourcing on a voluntary basis (e.g. leather traceability). JLR is 
also a member of the Drive Sustainability initiative, whose mission is to work to improve the 
social, ethical and environmental performance of automotive supply chains. 
 
Using existing information tools 
IDIS is the free, central information platform  for all dismantling companies, in which vehicle 
manufacturers bundle all the necessary information for a safe and environmentally friendly 
treatment of end-of-life vehicles. In addition to generally applicable safety information on the 
handling of, for in stance, airbags, gas or electric and hybrid vehicles, JLR-specific treatment 
and disassembly instructions, e.g. for the removal of high-voltage batteries, are also available. 
The system provides all the necessary information, is constantly being further de veloped and 
goes far beyond the legal requirements. From the user's point of view, it is a self -explanatory,

simply structured and user -friendly tool. As part of the inspection of the ATFs, it should be 
ensured that IDIS is used consistently. 
Question 38 of the consultation suggests that additional information should be provided free 
of charge by producers to ATFs. JLR strongly believes that ATF operations are already 
profitable, and that only information related to safe removal should be provided free of charge. 
As any other operator, ATFs have to buy their information and tools.  In addition, plastic parts 
are labelled with the material code since many years in order to enable recycling. 
JLR is continually engaged with the waste operators in order to identify the need of additional 
data and to improve data availability and accessibility. Additionally, the SCIP database provides 
complementary information on the presence of SVHCs in new products. 
 
Fostering the self-sustaining innovative circular economy 
JLR complies with its  Extended Producer Responsibility and successfully implemented the 
different obligations through appropriate measures for each sub-area. 
It begins with our high and targeted investments in the development phase in order to design 
our vehic les for later recycling and to ensure high recycling rates. The achieved average 
recycling rate above 85% within the EU is strong evidence of that, and constitutes the highest 
number compared to products from other industries. 
Our take-back systems are guaranteeing the return of any ELV. Both individual and collective 
systems are implemented under consideration of the specific requirements of the respective 
member state. In order to keep these successfully operating networks, freedom of choice in 
the design of such systems is still required. 
The value of an entire ELV is covering all costs related to collection and dismantling, as well as 
recycling and recovery of all material streams. 
JLR therefore calls  calling for a holistic approach in order to assess th e cost and revenue 
situation of a complete vehicle fairly, and not to focus on single material streams only. An 
additional external monetary compensation by OEMs is not necessary, would disrupt an 
established market and thus should not be regulated by law. Considering the increasing prices 
of raw materials and crude oil, raw material extraction from ELVs is expected to become more 
and more attractive in the future.

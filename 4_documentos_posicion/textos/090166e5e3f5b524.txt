FuelsEurope’s narrative accompanying the response to the Open Public 
Consultation “Integrated water management – revised lists of surface and 
groundwater pollutants” 
FuelsEurope has prepared this ‘Extended Submission’ for The Open Public Consultation on the current 
condition and pollution levels in surface and groundwaters. For these questions we have filled them 
on the basis of an Association answering (via our Members) from the perspective that is representative 
of our members. This narrative provides context around answers given in the questionnaire 
submitted, and provides some key points for the European Commission to consider when identifying 
potential further actions. 
Introduction 
Context – From an industrial perspective the importance of harmonised EU-level regulations is well 
recognised, and all parties benefit when the process is less complex to manage. Although knowledge 
is good in the areas of the various ‘Water Directives’ - the use of a framework which avoids duplication 
between directives, or reduces the potential for confusion in trying to interpret which directive 
applies, and when, is highly desirable.   
FuelsEurope recognizes that sound principles of science,  technology, engineering and mathematic s 
can be used to derive an actionable approach which can protect human health, water and the 
environment. Setting standards at a non -science derived standard may introduce unnecessary 
‘compliance’ actions or additiona l control / remediation where a more science-based, sustainability 
driven framework is in place. 
Q2 & Q3. How concerned are you about the presence of these substances in European (Q.2. surface 
water bodies) and (Q3. Groundwater bodies)?  
A2 & 3. Context – For FuelsEurope, the Membership is not considered to be highly active in the area 
of Pharmaceuticals, Microplastics, Pesticides or Fertilizers. As operators of refining industrial facilities, 
the Member Companies of FuelsEurope would say that answers have been given from a ‘general 
perspective’ – rather than a detailed commentary on these areas.   
We would say as ‘newer’ substances of concern - it seems from a ‘general perspective’ viewpoint that 
there is a need for an agreed de finition of "microplastics”, and some understanding of standardized 
methods for sampling / monitoring and potentially analysing microplastics. 
We further note, that although Industrial chemicals & (Heavy) Metals may be consumed in our 
processes or even produced, however, there is technology in place at our Members’ sites to prevent, 
reduce and, where not possible, treat emissions. Further, whilst it is true that accidental releases can 
happen, our Members have plans in place to prevent this, and can to a la rge degree mitigate the 
impacts of such events.

Regarding “Lack of substitution of harmful chemicals by safer alternatives ” - a requirement for 
substitution can ignore that in the Refining Industry the governing BREF structure requires effective 
control of operations under Best Available Technologies (BAT), managing such ‘harmful chemicals’.  
- There is genuinely a challenge in actually finding alternative chemicals with acceptable 
properties, without even considering the process changes that would be required & enhanced 
product quality checks that might be needed.  
- Substitution is rarely a straightforward 'drop in' – where viable, of course replacement by safer 
alternatives is preferable.  
- In the l onger term , there can be challenges when more information about a ‘more safe’ 
alternative becomes available, suggesting a previously unknown concern – the risk of a 
‘regrettable substitution’ cannot be ignored when demanding substitution without full  
understanding of such a replacement in a process. 
 
Where looking at a “Lack of mitigation measures and/or restoration measures to recover heavily 
affected water bodies” – it is important to separate the two parts of this sub-question...  
- Initial mitigation measures are in place with our Members well placed to control surface water 
and groundwater pollution under licencing regimes of Member States , and equipped to 
prevent accidents.  Given current technology and ability to respond, restoration of single 
events is manageable if an accident happens. 
- Many such bodies of water, and in particular surface water, and to some degree bodies of 
groundwater are however impacted by events. Here multiple stakeholders may be required 
to undertake ‘restoration measures’. Finding causative parties might not always be possible. 
In such complex cases, a method for fair apportionment of responsibility needs to be found. 
It shoul d also be noted that sustainable actions – such as through a process of natural 
attenuation, may be the most viable long-term solution for restoration of heavily affected 
water bodies. An action such as this may be a ‘solution to’, rather than ‘contributing to’ surface 
water and groundwater pollution. 
 
On the topic of “Lack of use of ‘precautionary’ and ‘polluter pays’ principles when assessing risks from 
new ‘emerging’ substances” again – even for new emerging substances - separation of the two parts 
of the question are valuable. 
- As with all areas of managing surface water and groundwater pollution, sound science as a 
basis is required, and for emerging substances REACH Regulation addresses issues of new 
material implementation - setting the boundaries and defining the rules for risk assessment - 
meaning any Precautionary Principle can at least have a solid basis. 
- Polluter Pays is a separate topic (see our previous mitigation/restoration related input).

Q6. To what extent do you think the following strategies are effective in addressing surface and  
groundwater pollution?  
With regard to the effectiveness of “Regulations at EU level to ensure pollutant presence and 
exceedance are minimised through stringent standards” – the regulation is effective, though it should 
be noted it is not homogeneous over  all sectors or activities. EEA Report No 7/2018 examined the 
outputs of what is effectively EU water policy, highlighting that although results were mixed, data 
quality was improving, issues were identified and there was indeed a positive improvement in water 
quality overall. The report noted “Member States have made marked efforts to improve water quality 
or reduce pressure on hydromorphology. Some of the measures have had an immediate effect; others 
will result in improvements in the longer term.”  When considering implementing ‘newer’ regulation, 
the position should be to fully understand how effective existing regulation is now that Member States 
have more fully implemented it. 
- Looking particularly at the controls of the Refining industry, as has been m entioned, the so-
called “REF BREF” under revision, shortly has evolved to manage our Members’ ‘point source’ 
discharges, effectively helping to contribute to improving water quality. 
 
With regard to the effectiveness of “Regulation on soil protection” from our sector perspective, the 
link with surface and groundwaters is broadly effectively managed. 
With a 2021 Soil Strategy OPC released, our comments have been given extensively in that response. 
Any EU ‘high level criteria for soils’ to be in good health i s only desirable if there is a science-led 
framework with a locally managed and controlled risk-based assessment process that drives 
‘prevention’ as better than ‘cure’ and when action is required, prioritises essential actions over 
important actions.  What is undesirable is a single set of criteria that does not take into consideration 
specific local conditions.  
Q7. What in your opinion should the European Commission improve to ensure its policy actions / 
strategies address more effectively surface and groundwater pollution? 
Broadly considering the desire for more swift updates of lists/annexes and inclusion of new pollutants, 
the key principles which should be applied to any strategy to more effectively address surface and 
groundwater pollution, are: 
- To ensure there is an overarching risk-based, sustainable, and sound science led framework.  
- For the EU organisations to provide absolute, simple clarity on the process that will be 
followed and the timescales allowed for data gathering and interpretation to be undertaken 
if delivering fast updates (the overall system between all directives is currently complex). 
- All data collection has to be in conjunction with standardized and science -based monitoring 
methods;  
- EU regulators shall ensure that a minimum standard of data quality is maintained by regional 
and local authorities before sending to national/ EU level as a basis for regulatory initiatives.

Data collection on the events leading to pollution in water bodies needs to be proportional to the 
event and s hould demonstrably have a cost-benefit assessment undertaken to ensure that the 
minimum representative data required to fairly understand an event is gathered and recorded.

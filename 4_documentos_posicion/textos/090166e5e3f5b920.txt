Revision of the End-Of-Life Vehicles Directive 
European Copper Institute submission to support our response to the Commission 
consultation (October 2021) 
 
Missing vehicles and Illegal export of ELVs  
A huge amount of critical raw materials are needed to achieve the EU Green Deal goal of 
decarbonising road transport. The Commission has proposed that all new car sales should be 
100% from Zero Emission Vehicles by 2035, a dramatic increase from 5% in 2020 1. Producing 
enough battery cells to meet the expected rise in demand for battery electric vehicles will 
require significant amounts of critical raw materials from mining and recycling.  To meet this 
demand, and in line with the EU’s Circular Economy agenda, it is therefore important to facilitate 
the recovery and recycling of these raw materials from end-of-life vehicles (ELV).   
To this end, it is important to improve the ELVD provisions aiming to reduce the number of 
ELVs at unknown whereabouts.   
Problem: In 2014, 4.66 million ELV s in the EU , representing 39% of the total vehicles being 
decommissioned, were at ‘unknown whereabouts’2. This share has remained at a constant level 
since 20073. The high number of ELVs at ‘unknown whereabouts’ is for a large part due to vehicle 
dismantling at illegal sites and exports of ELVs outside of Europe as used cars4. 
ECI proposal: We believe that action at EU level is justified to reduce the number of ELVs at 
‘unknown whereabouts’, given that national measures alone have not been effective to date.  
To effectively reduce the number of vehicles at ‘unknown whereabouts’, we suggest adding the 
following provisions in the revised ELVD . These proposals are to a large extent based on the 
existing legal frameworks in the Netherlands, Italy and Austria, which we believe provide a good, 
workable basis for improving the ELVD: 
1. Registration of any road transport vehicle when the owner is a resident (or registered 
company) in the given Member State. From that moment the owner will be bound by 
at least two payment obligations (insurance and roadworthiness test) which will work 
as incentives to dispose of the vehicle in an authorised treatment facility (ATF) when it 
reaches its end of life. 
2. Provide t wo exe mptions from the obligation  to pay insurance and roadworthiness 
test: 
i. For vehicles with suspended registrations, for a maximum of three years to be 
renewed every year. While suspended, the vehicle is no longer authorised to be 
in use.

Recycled content targets 
ECI supports the aim to reduce landfill and incentivize more recycling to make sure that losses 
of high value recycled copper are avoided. Taking the example of vehicles’ wiring ha rnesses, 
the major recycling value lies within copper conductors in cables. Ensuring that ease of 
dismantling is put at the forefront of the full life cycle considerations at the design stage of a 
vehicle is of paramount importance. This will allow dismant ling companies to immediately 
detect those high -value parts of the vehicle and recover high -grade copper, instead of 
dismantling other wire parts with less value that are used in many different sub-quantities, thus 
making it more difficult and time -consuming to assess whether they can be recycled for the 
same electrical application as used for before. Besides wires, this rationale can be extended to 
all vehicle parts where most value of materials needs to be maintained at End of Life (EoL).  
With the same goal of ensuring that the high value of recycled copper is fully leveraged, ECI is in 
favour of an End-of-Life Recycling Rate (EoL-RR) instead of a recycled content target for copper 
in ELVs . As the Commission acknowledges in the questionnair e, copper from ELVs is already 
being recycled at a high rate. An End-of-Life Recycling Rate is beneficial as it shows the efficiency 
with which the metal contained in EoL products is collected, pre-treated and recycled, and gives 
flexibility to economic operators to reuse the secondary copper in the applications they deem 
most appropriate . We believe economic operators should have the flexibility to redirect 
recovered copper and copper alloys in other applications too.

This said, the role of primary metal production should by no means be underestimated, 
especially in view of the increasing demand for electric vehicles, relevant infrastructure and 
other electrification-related products, which lead to around three times more copper demand 
than currently used in internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles, and therefore more copper 
value retained at the EoL. 
Overall, to increase recovery of copper and copper alloys,  a more coordinated optimization 
approach between actors along the recycling chain is needed. The Copper Alliance has mapped 
the multitude of different alloys, structured them by common copper alloy families, and 
allocated them to five main field of applications, incl uding transport uses (see Figure 4 here). 
This helps in the knowledge of copper alloys that are found in ELVs. New technologies and 
techniques may help improve the copper recycling process, especially in terms of dealing with 
contaminants in the copper scrap stream.  
A holistic, life cycle thinking already from the design stage of vehicles will make recycling more 
effective and efficient at the product’s EoL. Sensor-based technologies promise the economical 
and practical recovery of materials and products that can directly be sold on the market instead 
of shipping these metal fractions to third countries for manual sorting. Such technologies and 
cross-industrial synergies need to be rolled out at larger scale to leverage the benefits they can 
bring to the environment and the economy.  
EoL recycling routes have yet to be optimized for electric vehicles. As the deployment of battery 
electric vehicles accelerates, we expect that further recycling opportunities as well as challenges 
will arise for copper  in the  ELV space , given the higher copper content of battery electric 
vehicles. In this respect, the consequences to the recycling stream of narrowing the scope of 
the ELV exemption in Annex II of the ELVD  in relation to  the phase-out of lead -containing 
materials and components in vehicles  should be equally considered . There are numerous 
challenges with segregating leaded and non -leaded copper alloy scrap . Transitioning to lead -
free alloys could have the unintended consequence of downcycling and reduced recycling rates 
if incompatible scrap grades cannot be effectively identified and segregated in ELV scrap. 
Ethical sourcing 
The value of voluntary standards: the example of the Copper Mark 
The International Copper Association, ECI’s parent organization, supported the development of 
a framework to independently verify the performance of copper producers against 
internationally recognised responsible production criteria. The Copper Mark (a company 
registered in England and Wal es) was established in December 2019 with a commitment to 
include the perspectives of impacted stakeholders in its governance structure. Today, the 
Copper Mark manages the assurance framework for copper producers and meets the core 
expectations for good governance, adhering to the Credibility Principles of the ISEAL Alliance. 
 
Launched in March 2020, the Copper Mark Assurance Process  already includes 25 copper 
mining, smelting and refining sites. Sites that have received the Copper Mark include two of the 
largest copper mines and between them exceed circa 10% of the annual global copper 
production. The Copper Mark requirements  for responsible production address  all major

Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) issues and require compliance with national and 
international law. The requirements support a green transition that does not harm people and 
the environment. For example, the Copper Mark already requires the quantification of emissions 
and energy use and reporting in line with international reporting protocols. 
In addition to its focus on responsible production, the Copper Mark worked with the 
International Lead Association (ILA), the International Zinc Association (IZA), the Nickel Institute 
and the Responsible Minerals Init iative (RMI) to develop the Joint Due Diligence Standard for 
Copper, Lead, Nickel and Zinc. The Joint Due Diligence Standard  is designed to align with the 
OECD Guidance for responsible sourcin g of minerals, making copper, lead, nickel and zinc 
producers conduct due diligence on their supply chains. The Copper Mark is currently 
undergoing the OECD Alignment Assessment for this standard, with results expected before the 
end of the year. 
Each site is independently verified to meet the Copper Mark’s criteria through a rigorous site -
level assessment process using internationally recognised audit methodologies (ISO - or ISA -
based). The Copper Mark recognises that core expectations evolve over time and become more 
demanding. For this reason, each site is re-assessed every three years against updated criteria. 
Our position 
We believe that voluntary standards play an important role to support regulatory requirements. 
They should be seen as a tool that supp orts companies in meeting regulatory requirements. 
They can reach industry actors, build capacity, and provide a recognition to their participants for 
good practices that is helpful to lay the foundation for regulatory compliance. Voluntary systems 
can als o help build consensus on requirements and adapt more flexibly to changing 
expectations. 
We put forward that the certifications of existing voluntary standards, such as the Copper Mark, 
should be accepted as evidence of compliance towards regulators, custo mers and end users, 
especially when such schemes undergo formal recognition processes such as the OECD Guidance 
Alignment Assessment. 
The integration of s uch voluntary standards remains highly relevant also considering the 
upcoming Sustainable Corporate Governance law, with which product-specific legislation, such 
as the End -of-Life Vehicles Directive, should align to avoid duplicate audits and onerous 
processes for companies. 
We therefore firmly believe that it would be a disservice to the multiplicity and magnitude of 
efforts required to trigger systemic, sustainability -oriented change to limit or dismiss the role 
that voluntary initiatives can play alongside policy and regulation.

 
ReFuel EU Maritime 
Clean Air Task Force (CATF) welcomes the EU climate ambition, leadership on 
decarbonisation, and pursuit of policies to decouple greenhouse gas emissions 
from economic growth. The maritime sector will require fundamental changes to 
become more sustainable. If the sector were a country, it would rank sixth among 
countries with the highest GHG emissions, ahead of Germany and the UK. 
Moreover, according to the International Maritime Organization (IMO), the 
sector’s greenhouse gas emissions “could grow between 50% and 250% by 
2050”1.   
CATF therefore welcomes the Commission’s proposals to address the emissions 
from the maritime transport and promote alternative fuels. We believe however 
that the Fuel EU Maritime can be improved.  
CATF is concerned by the inclusion of liquefied natural gas (LNG) as a potential 
green option and its subsequent incentivisation in the Fit for 55 Package.  
KEY RECOMMENDATIONS 
Fuel EU Maritime 
• Include a sub-target for hydrogen and ammonia, making it compulsory 
that a part of the GHG target should be met by ammonia and renewable 
or low-carbon hydrogen.  
• Include a multiplier of at least 4 for the companies using hydrogen or 
ammonia 
• Allow shipping companies to trade over -compliance credits from 
hydrogen and ammonia ships in the credit exchange mechanism 
• Adapt the equation in Annex I to reflect the methane emissions on the 
full lifecycle of LNG 
 
Alternative fuels for shipping 
GHG emissions from the maritime sector can be reduced by shifting from heavy 
fuel oil and marine diesel to zero-carbon fuels (ZCF)2, mainly hydrogen (H2) and 
ammonia (NH3). They can be converted to energy in compatible fuel cells, 
reciprocating engines, turbines, and other machines. 
Ammonia will be key in decarbonising the shipping sector, especially for long 
trips, as long as the hydrogen and the nitrogen used are produced with little to 
no greenhouse gas. It can either be used in fuel cells or in retrofitted or purpose-
built versions of current internal combustion engines3.

handling, it should be noted that ammonia is a commonly traded commodity with

LNG and its climate impact 
While from a technology readiness perspective, LNG is more mature than 
hydrogen and ammonia for shipping,  the environmental and health benefits of 
LNG compared to conventional fuels, such as heavy fuel oil (HFO) and marine 
gas oil (MGO), are contested among the scientific community. 
On the positive side, using LNG over HFO improve air quality in the maritime 
sector by reducing emissions of NOx, SO2, and particular matter. However, LNG 
is not the only option to improve air quality until e-fuels are ready to be deployed 
at a large s cale. An option could be to use cleaner marine gas oil with 10 ppm 
sulphur content, in combination with selective catalytic reduction (SCR) systems 
and diesel particulate filters (DPF). This option would have a similar positive 
impact on air quality as LNG at a lower cost.  
On greenhouse emissions, LNG leads to either limited improvement or even 
higher emissions. Studies demonstrated that LNG’s GHG footprint is either 
similar to that of MGO (the cleanest oil-based marine fuel) or worse4. While ships 
running on LNG emits about 25% less CO2 than traditional marine fuels, we need 
to take into consideration the greenhouse emissions of the full life cycle of LNG 
to assess its full climate impact, and pay particular attention to its methane 
emissions.  
LNG is mos tly methane, a greenhouse gas 86 times more damaging that CO2 
for global warming over 20 years. The assessment of the GHG emissions LNG 
require the inclusion of upstream emissions from leakage during extraction, 
processing, and transport, as well as downstream emissions. 
Larger well to tank (WTT) emissions for the LNG supply chain as well as methane 
emissions during shipping nullify any GHG improvement compared to diesel 
(MGO) or bunker oil (HFO). Studies demonstrated that, when looking at the full 
life cyc le of LNG, the environmental benefits of using LNG instead of 
conventional fuels are either very limited or inexistant5. For shipping, depending 
on the methane leak level, LNG would at best offer a 10% reduction of the GHG 
emissions than MGO or HFP. With higher methane emissions, LNG could have 
up to 9% higher GHG emissions than conventional fuels6. 
Moreover, the LNG marine engine most commonly used (low-pressure dual fuel 
(LPDF), medium-speed, four-stroke) is actually the one with the higher leaking

The economic cost 
While a switch to LNG wouldn’t bring a positive change for GHG emissions, it 
would in addition come with a hug e cost. The deployment of the LNG 
infrastructure would require major investments, that would be better used on 
future-proof zero -carbon alternatives. This deployment would require 
investments of at least $3bn and up to $22.2bn. LNG projects already receive d 
above $500 million through TEN-T and CEF funding8. 
The option of marine gas oil with 10 ppm sulphur content with selective catalytic 
reduction (SCR) systems and diesel particulate filters (DPF), would have lower 
cost than LNG as it wouldn’t require investments in new ships or infrastructure.  
Moreover, as the Commission considered that LNG should only be used in 
transition, the investments required to develop LNG infrastructure would either 
not be monetised and lead to stranded assets, or would justify their use after 
zero-carbon options become available.  
Given the GHG emissions from LNG, CATF doesn’t believe it should be used as 
a transition fuel until hydrogen and ammonia are ready, as it would be a counter 
intuitive use of public funds for no real climate benefits while e-fuels will already 
be available in a couple of years. 
Ammonia produced from renewable or low -carbon hydrogen will be the lowest 
cost zero-carbon option out to the 2050s9. But the costs of producing and using 
hydrogen and ammonia as marine fuel s will exceed the costs associated with 
conventional bunker fuels for some time. Policy interventions are therefore 
necessary to stimulate demand and technology development zero-carbon fuels. 
EU support would be better used for e-fuels rather than for LNG.

Taking methane into consideration 
CATF welcomes the introduction in the Commission’s proposal of a goal -based 
fuel GHG intensity target, becoming more and more stringent with the time. 
However, while it is a positive step forward, this measure will not be sufficient to 
support the developm ent and deployment of hydrogen and ammonia in the 
shipping sector. As this target is technological neutral, it risks incentivising LNG 
as the as the cheapest alternative fuel eligible until 2040.  
The Fuel EU Maritime targets are expressed in Well -to-Wake (WTW) CO 2-
equivalent emissions , with the equation in Annex I using  a global warming 
potential (GWP) of 100 years. However, methane is a short-lived pollutant in the

Moreover, the equation in Annex I does not seem to account from methane from 
leaks (of LNG boil off gas) or fugitive methane emissions from other parts of the 
LNG supply chain, like liquefaction, storage, tank loading, regasification, etc. To 
better reflect the actual GHG emissions of a fuel, upstream methane leakage 
should be included on the WtT side of the equation. 
As most of the GHG emissions of LNG are due to methane emissions, the 
proposed equation risks underestimating the potentially damaging impact of LNG 
for the climate and lead to an increase in its use despite its GHG emissions. This 
is even more problematic as the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report highlighted the 
key role that reducing methane emissions could and should play in slowing down 
global warming and avoid some of the non -return tipping points of climate 
change10.  
Supporting e-fuels 
To boost the use of ammonia and hydrogen, the proposal would also need to be 
amended to include a sub -target for hydrogen and ammonia, making it 
compulsory that at least half of the GHG target should be met by ammonia and 
renewable or low -carbon hydrogen. Studies demonstrated that this sub -target 
should be realistic with the potential uptake of these zero carbon fuels in shipping 
by 2030. 
Another option to support the deployment of e -fuels would be the inclusion of a 
multiplier of at least 4 for the companies using hydrogen or ammonia, as it would 
be the minimum for the ammonia to become cost-effective.  
Finally, the legislation should allow shipping companies to trade over-compliance 
credits from hydrogen and ammonia ships in the credit exchange mechanism, as 
it would create a powerful incentive for companies to inv est in these alternative 
fuels.

Assessment of EU regulation for the maritime industry alternative fuel supply  
Who are we 
Gas Infrastructure Europe (GIE) represents almost 70 members from 26 European countries and is the European 
association of the gas infrastructure operators: pipelines, underground gas storage and LNG terminals. The European 
Union (EU) set itself the ambitious objective of being carbon neutral by 2050, and is in the process of consequently 
raising its 2030 climate-related goals. Targets increase, as does the urgency for transport to accelerate its 
decarbonisation.  
This is not an easy challenge. Maritime transport is a sector where greenhouse gases (GHG) emissions, CO2, NOx, 
SOx and particulate matter are especially hard-to-abate. At the same time, cutting GHG emissions must come hand-
in-hand with affordability and technology availability, in order to guarantee a successful transition towards carbon 
neutral transportation. 
Against this background, Liquefied Natural Gas - LNG - has great potential to drastically cut GHG, CO2, NOx, SOx and 
particulate matter from shipping, as an enabler to integrate a growing share of bioLNG and synthetic LNG (e-
methane) in the European fuels mix. It is a solution that is available today, ready to decarbonise shipping fleets at a 
reasonable cost. From its production to its use as a fuel, LNG, and increasingly bioLNG and synthetic LNG, are a key 
solution to kick-start transport’s transition towards carbon neutrality without further delay. 
Executive summary 
Maritime sustainable supply chains, whether based on bioLNG, e-methane, methanol, refined fuels, are based on  
complex existing infrastructures, bringing a secure, flexible supply in all ports from production sites scattered within 
EU or even overseas. Volumes considered are 44 Mt, i.e. 550 TWh, the equivalent of a 12,5% of current EU gas 
demand. 
Some of these supply chains also rely on the EU internal gas market, that is based on a single commoditized product. 
To allow an efficient utilization of these supply chains two products should be traded separately, as soon as the 
sustainable fuels are either injected in a grid or liquified: 
- The molecule, supplied and traded as a “grey” molecule; 
- The sustainability production certificate.    
Full certification will only be acquired when the whole GHG emissions of the production to final use chain is 
computed. 
The alternative, i.e. imposing a physical bundling of the molecule and of its certification from production until the 
end-user, would massively complexify the logistic chain and the trade. Such a complexification could delay if not 
block the development of alternative fuels, and create significant additional emissions and pollution linked to useless 
duplicated infrastructures and much more tanker trucks on EU roads. There is no obvious interest in putting in place 
such a bundling.  
Fuel EU maritime draft regulation is imposing to the shipping industry a massive shift to sustainable fuels, imposing a 
deep transformation of the whole supply chain. It would not be understandable for the RED II implementing 
directive to seriously undermine this transformation. 
RED II implementing regulation should propose in clear terms a certificate that could be traded separately from the 
logistic chain as soon as the sustainable fuel is either liquified or injected in a grid.

What are the expected functioning of the maritime fuels supply chains ? 
BioLNG 
From biomethane injected in the EU grid 
Most of the biomethane produced today in Europe is injected in the gas grid. The most efficient way to allow the 
shipping industry to access this biomethane is to rely on Guarantees of origin. 
At the exit of the biomethane production plants, for each MWh of production, two distinct products are sold : 
- 1 MWh of molecule, injected into the grid as a “grey” methane molecule; 
- 1 MWh of Guarantee of origin, that ideally the maritime industry should be able to buy, in competition with 
other end-usages (industry, heating, road transport…). 
In most cases, ships will be bunkered with molecules physically coming for LNG terminal tanks. Though, this process 
is optimal : 
- There are of no double counting of biomethane, and shipping industry is indeed financially supporting the 
development of the biomethane industry via Guarantees of origin; 
- Physically,  
o on a short term basis, adding 1 MWh of biomethane in the European gas grid is pushing out 1 MWh 
of the marginal gas supply of the European gas grid, marginal resource than can be under market 
conditions either LNG or pipe gas from e.g. Russia or Norway. LNG, with more flexibility in its 
destination, if often the marginal supply.  
o On a longer term basis, the operation is even more interesting : giving access to this injected 
biomethane to the shipping industry is incentivizing shipowners to invest in new (bio)LNG propelled 
ships, meaning that this biomethane is effectively substituting heavy fuel oil or VLSFO, which has 
even more positive climate (and local pollution) impact.    
In the long run, as LNG terminals will import more and more bioLNG and /or e-methane1 instead of methane. 
Molecule physically used by the shipping industry will become more and more of sustainable nature. Injected 
biomethane GO will therefore become a flexibility tool to swap sustainable vectors. 
From grid liquefaction 
In most cases, main ports have an LNG terminal within or close to the port. Liquefying gas from the grid in such cases 
is therefore a non optimal operation : it is more efficient to use already liquid gas available in the LNG terminal than 
to simultaneously regasify gas in the terminals and inject it in the network,  while a few kilometers away gaseous gas 
is liquified from the network. 
Grid liquefaction could be one of the possible options to assess only when no liquid gas from the terminals is 
available in the vicinity of the port (could be the case for fluvial shipping for instance). 
From biomethane liquified at the production site 
A significant part of the biomethane production has a difficult access to the gas grid (far from any network, on the 
wrong side of a river or from a highway, due to distribution grid congestions…). For instance, in France, it represents 
a more than 25 TWh potential out of a total French potential of 150 TWh (only for methanisation, not taking into 
account pyrolysis of wood waste for instance). This production is loaded in tanker trucks. Bringing this production 
directly to the LNG terminals is possible, but not optimal : it would mean lots of tanker trucks making long distances,

and adding additional truck discharging bays along with the existing truck loading bays. They can also be used for

What would be here again the most efficient solution would be a Guarantees of Origin system (or an equivalent

- 1 MWh of “grey” LNG, to be distributed in the neighboring fueling stations; 
- 1 MWh of Liquid biomethane certificate, that could be bought by the shipping industry. 
Here again, it would greatly improve the logistics, maximize the climatic benefit, and in the long run, as LNG terminal 
will import more and more bioLNG and /or e-methane, it will become a simple swap between sustainable vectors. 
For imported liquid biomethane in the terminals 
Initially, there will be limited number of bioLNG carriers arriving in EU LNG terminals. It will be much more efficient 
to allow virtual swaps between the different EU terminals, instead of having to do multiple partial dischargement of 
the bioLNG carriers in the different terminals, or dedicating bunker vessels to bring BioLNG from one terminal to 
another. 
As the number of bioLNG carriers will increase, the virtual swaps will become less necessary. 
Conclusion 
The development of certificates than can be traded independently from the molecule is a key factor of success to 
bring biomethane to the shipping industry in a optimized way.  
First, this removes the need to duplicate useless infrastructure (liquefaction units close to the terminals, BioLNG 
tanker trucks charging bays in the terminals, additional tanker trucks and drivers, bunker vessels commuting 
between EU terminals…). Avoiding these investments is of course key to avoid associated financial and climatic 
impacts. Moreover, these investments would instantly lose all their interest if the regulation changes, so nobody 
would want to undertake them, and the market would be blocked. 
Secondly, it allows the shipping industry to have access to the injected biomethane, which is essential especially till 
the local liquified production and the bioLNG imports has sufficiently developed. 
e-methane 
The problematic for e-methane is very similar than for biomethane. Once the e-methane is produced, it goes 
through the same existing methane infrastructure as the biomethane, so all the issues presented for the biomethane 
are also applicable for e-methane.  
An additional point for e-methane is linked to the H2 and the CO2 it is made of.  
For H2 electrolysers connected to the electricity grid, they could produce a mix of “green” (when electrolysers 
production coincides with their PPA renewable electricity plant production) and “grey” or “low carbon” H2 (the rest 
of the time, when produced from plain grid electricity). Here again, allowing to manage in a flexible way this 
potential mix of “green” and “grey” or “low carbon” H2 with certificates independent from the molecule avoid the 
need for duplicating costly infrastructure. 
There is also the additional question of the source of the CO2.  
CO2 can be of biogenic origin. Typically this can be the case when biomethane and e-methane are produced jointly. 
Indeed, the biomethane production process means the plants are capturing enough carbon to produce roughly as 
much CO2 as biomethane. This biogenic CO2 can be used to synthetize e-methane with biomethane. In that case, 
there are no doubt e-methane is qualified as renewable. 
But e-methane could also be made from : 
- CO2 directly captured in the air; 
 
4 
 
- CO2 as a by-product of a chemical process (e.g. in the production process of lime, of steel), that is “fatal” 
CO2; 
- CO2 that is captured and stored on board of ships, and that can be reused once brought back in ports to 
produce more e-methane. 
All these alternative sources are neutral from a GHG emissions standpoint (save eventual energy needed in the 
processing of the CO2) and should be recognized as such.  
Also, there could be a succession of different regulations along the production process of the e-methane : 
- H2 and possibly CO2 production are facing the stricter regulations linked to production sites, requiring strict 
physical tracing of the molecules. 
- Then H2 and CO2 could be transported through a grid or shipped to the methanation plant. They are exposed 
to the transport regulation. 
- Then the methanation plant combining H2 and CO2 is again exposed to the production site regulation.  
Regulation should be compatible with this succession of rules, and avoid that H2 and CO2 are considered as basic raw 
materials that must be strictly physically traced even when transported through a grid or shipped. 
Bio or e-Methanol 
Bio or e-methanol are very similar to e-methane, save that they have one additional step in the process, adding 
complexity. Indeed, methanol is largely made from methane, so it embarks all previous difficulties, plus an additional 
synthesis step. 
This additional synthesis step can create additional complexities if the plant is not only producing methanol, but also 
other products  or by-products. In such cases, how to convert the certificates of different intrants  over the different 
produced molecules needs to apply an allocation rule than must be justified and recognized.

Impact of certificates on trading and on shipping industry supply contracts     
For gaseous fuels 
One additional advantage of having certificates than can be traded independently from the molecule is the 
compatibility with the Internal European Gas market. The basic principle of the European gas market is to trade a 
single gas product2, the natural gas, on the largest possible virtual hubs. 
Having to trade two products, e.g. natural gas and biomethane, is already not compatible with the very basics of the 
EU gas market.  
Even worse, each RED II certificate specifies the GHG content of the associated production. This GHG contents varies 
widely from one production site to another. The RED II certification is only finally acquired when the fuel is 
consumed, as all GHG emissions from the production to the final consumption have to pass thresholds. These 
thresholds depends on the end-user sector (electricity production, transport, heating).

Having to deal  bundled products (molecule + production certification) will not fit with the internal gas market;

additional back-up contracts to cope with each producer maintenance of production incident, for instance by having 
to buy upfront some biomethane in a storage facility. 
This issue is first concerning the injected biomethane, e-methane or H2 injected in the gas networks, as most trading 
are taking place for gas in the networks. Though, even for liquified molecules, being able to trade separately 
molecule and production certificates would vastly simplify supply of ships : 
• For the molecule, it would allow e.g. to buy LNG on different time horizons and optionalities to ensure 
flexibility and security of supply at minimal costs 
• For the certificates, as they are valid 12 months, it should allow suppliers to have a sufficient backlog to 
ensure adapted supply to shippers.   
On the contrary, the bundle product, with no access to the market, will entirely be dependent of the molecule 
logistics. 
For refined oil products 
Up to now, certification of biofuels was essentially required by suppliers themselves to prove their biofuel 
incorporation rate obligations. Connection with trading was therefore not obvious. When shipping companies will 
need to prove the certified nature of the biofuels in their ships, it could be interesting to check how an obligation to 
trade a bundled product molecule + certificate could or not impact the supply of ships.   
Two EU texts currently under discussions directly impacting this supply chain 
Fuel EU maritime 
The Fuel EU maritime draft regulation is creating an obligation on shipping companies to incorporate alternative 
fuels, with a defined calendar. This means a massive quantity of alternative fuel must be produced (more or less of 
75% of the demand in 2050. This demand is currently standing at around a 550 TWh/y). This is a formidable

EU second renewable energy directive (RED II) and its draft implementing regulation 
RED II directive itself 
The EU renewable energy directive, under its current form, is setting a framework with several key elements. Among 
others, can be quoted : 
- for disclosure to end-customers purposes, the emissions of guarantees of origin, 
- a binding sustainably certification framework, including notably :  
o the general principle of the mass balance, 
o some computation elements for the GHG emissions included in this certification. 
- A Union database supposed to track all renewable fuels transactions.  
 This framework is leaving ample interpretation ground, to the point the need for an additional implementing 
regulation has been identified.  
RED II implementing regulation 
This implementing regulation is coming in a late stage of the process : sustainability certification should have been 
implemented by the 1st of July 2021, but had to be postponed given the insufficient clarity of the current texts. 
Alas, the current draft implementing is creating even more uncertainty.

None of the terms used are defined in the text, and could all be interpreted in different ways : 
- “product group” is defined by a reference to article of the RED directive that does not mention product 
group. It is not defined whether a sustainable and a non sustainable product could be in the same product 
group, nor whether renewable H2 could be in the same product group as CH4. 
When products are physically identical (e.g. natural gas, biomethane and e-methane), there is an exception 
allowing the mixing, so in that case the definition of “product group” is of no consequence.  
With this lack of clarity, it is dubious whether H2 can be mixed with methane, or whether methanol can be 
mixed with VLSFO.   
- There is no clear distinction between blending and mixing, whereas the rules applied are very different. It 
could be expected that the “process” refers principally to the refining process, but it’s not defined. In 
particular, the case of methanol and e-methane where there could be successive steps of mixing and 
blending is not considered in the directive. 
- What is meant by “a processing or logistical facility, or a transmission and distribution infrastructure or site” 
is not clear. In the case of (bio or e-)LNG ships, the optimal solution would be that the transmission and 
distribution infrastructure includes all existing liquid or gaseous methane infrastructure once the methane is 
either injected of liquified : the EU transmission and distribution grid, the LNG terminals, the tanker trucks, 
the fueling stations, all the storages (from the largest underground storages to small intermediate LNG or 
CNG tanks), the bunker vessels, the LNG carriers… 
This is far from being clearly the case. Even the article “a” in front of “transmission and distribution 
infrastructure” is creating a huge uncertainty : does it define the EU gas grid, only the grid owned by a single 
operator, a pipe ? 
These uncertainties mean bunkering of sustainable methane, hydrogen or methanol could be seriously hampered 
with costly duplicated infrastructures and logistical costs and constraints, with negative consequences on 
European GHG emissions.   
Situation is not any clearer for refined liquid biofuels : the current practice for road transport is that when these 
fuels had paid their taxes out of the refineries or the main oil storage sites, mass balance downstream of these 
points is simply understood as an accounting exercise by each supplier, with no physical tracing, and no follow-up of 
the rate of biofuel in each fueling station. The certification is done to ensure suppliers are respecting their global 
incorporation obligation.  There is neither a need nor an obligation to follow a physical or commercial incorporation 
rate at the level of each fueling station.  
Before Fuel EU maritime, there was no obligation for maritime bunkers, so no generalized example of what 
application of mass balance for maritime liquid refined fuels could be. 
With all uncertainties mentioned here, application of this implementing regulation to liquid refined biofuels may be 
much more constrained that what is currently implemented for road transport, and potentially even more 
constrained than for gaseous or liquified fuels, because of the C14 test applied to “blended” fuels.      
Transaction tracking requirements 
RED II implementing regulation is adding transaction tracking requirement that imposes a follow-up of each certified 
MWh in a future Union Database, yet to be created. The issue is that there are no provision to track separately 
certificates and molecules. Current interpretation of this requirement of the text is that it is required to trade 
bundles of molecules and certificates.  
As explained in the first part of this document, this is a serious issue for supplying ships with the right level of 
security and flexibility at the best costs.

CO2 related subjects 
RED II implementing regulation is stating “Auditors shall verify that the estimate of emissions saving from capture 
and replacement of CO2 is limited to emissions avoided through the capture of CO2 of which the carbon originates 
from biomass and which is used to replace fossil-derived CO2.” 
As there are several categories of sustainable fuels in RED II (biofuels, renewable fuels of non biological origin, 
recycled carbon fuels), and the article is not specifying the perimeter of this limitation, it is not obvious to say 
whether e-methane or methanol produced with CO2 from direct air capture, from CO2 captured onboard ships, or as 
a co-product of an industrial process could be certified. 
For the latter category, a political discussion is required at EU to understand what industrial CO2 could be accepted : 
for instance, when lime is produced from limestone, CO2 is a fatal chemical by-product. This CO2 should be 
considered as a waste and be recognized as a certifiable source of CO2 for sustainable fuels. Obviously, the CO2 
produced when steam reforming natural gas in H2 could not be considered as a valid source to produce e-methane, 
but should be restricted to capture and storage. 
 
  
Conclusion 
To supply the shipping industry, a dual system should be put in place : as soon as the sustainable fuel is either 
liquified or injected in a grid, two different products should be emitted, a sustainability production certificate and 
the molecule, considered as a “grey” molecule. Shippers should be able to buy separately those two products.  
Full certification will only be acquired when the whole GHG emissions of the production to final use chain is 
computed. 
The alternative, i.e. imposing a physical bundling of the molecule and of its certification till the end-user, would 
massively complexify the logistic chain and the trade. Such a complexification could delay if not block the 
development of alternative fuels, and create significant additional emissions and pollution linked to useless 
duplicated infrastructures and much more tanker trucks on EU roads. There is no obvious interest in this bundling.  
Fuel EU maritime draft regulation is imposing to the shipping industry a massive shift to sustainable fuels, imposing a 
deep transformation of the whole supply chain. It would not be understandable that the RED II implementing 
directive would seriously undermine this transformation. 
RED II implementing regulation should propose in clear terms a certificate that could be traded separately from the 
logistic chain as soon as the sustainable fuel is either liquified or injected in a grid.

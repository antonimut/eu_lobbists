European Fermentation Group’s views on the FuelEU 
Maritime Regulation 
The European Fermentation Group (EFG) shares the European Commission’s ambition towards climate 
neutrality by 2050. As such we welcome the European Commission’s proposal on the FuelEU Regulation 
which aims to reduce emissions from shipping. We also see an opportunity to horizontally align FuelEU on 
sustainable transport fuels with the Renewable Energy Directive (RED II) and the ReFuelEU Regulation pro-
posal, to ensure the principles of coherence of feedstocks used for sustainable biofuels, Cascading Use, 
Waste Hierarchy, and the Union’s Sustainability Criteria are applied to all modes of transport. We are con-
cerned that – if this opportunity is not seized – any increase in demand for crop-based biofuels, created by 
FuelEU, would divert feedstocks such as molasses from food and feed uses  and indirectly lead to more  
emissions.   
 
EFG’s Key Messages 
We are pleased with the European Commission’s intention to reduce emissions from shipping. The FuelEU 
Regulation would benefit further from aligning rules with REDII and the ReFuel EU Regulation proposal 
regarding sustainable biofuels in road transport, shipping, and aviation.  
• Under RED II, a cap of 7% for crop -based biofuels, such as bio-ethanol made from molasses is 
included. Molasses and low-green syrup 2 are not considered as feedstocks for sustainable bio-
fuels and thus, are not listed in Annex IX part A and B because they are used for food and feed. 
• Molasses and low -green syrup 2 a re valuable co-products of the sugar production with a high 
nutritional and financial value. EFG members rely on sugar and sugar co -products to produce 
citric acid, lactic acid, vitamins, amino acids for animal feed and penicillin for antibiotics from 
molasses. Producers require yeast for various fermenting processes . Yeast is also needed for  
bread and bakery goods, wine, and beer.   
• The competitiveness of the European fermentation industry would be adversely affected  by 
increased use of molasses and low-green syrup 2 for biofuels in any mode of transport. Thus, we 
share the concerns raised by NGO Transport & Environment, that the proposal could incentivi se 
first generation crop-based biofuels.1   
• The availability of EU-sourced molasses at food grade quality is key to the success of our indus-
try. Sourcing non-EU molasses is already necessary for the EU Fermentation industry but it is nei-
ther a cost-effective nor a viable solution due to quality concerns.  
• Therefore, we call on the European Commission and the co -legislators to consider similar rules 
regarding crop-based biofuels for shipping as for road transport under RED II. Especially consid-
ering the size of the shipping sector and the potential impact on la nd use change and indirect 
emissions, caused by an increase demand of these feedstocks.

The European Fermentation group maintains that molasses , and other crop -based feedstocks, are not a 
suitable feedstock for sustainable biofuels for any mode of transport, including aviation:  
• Molasses are neither waste nor residues, but high value nutritional co-products of the sugar industry 
when transforming sugar beets into sugar. Molasses are used for food and feed applications, which 
should be prioritised.  
o This is recognised both by the sugar and the molasses industries. Molasses have a high financial 
value. Molasses prices are based on raw sugar content, the resulting prices are nearly equi valent 
to sugar prices in relation to raw sugar content.  
o Classified as feed materials,2 molasses are subject to technical regulations to guarantee food safety. 
Adding molasses and low-green syrup 2 to Annex IX would be contrary to the principle of the Waste 
Hierarchy.3  
• Any increased demand for molasses , e.g., for shipping fuels, would likely create significant indirect 
GHG emissions, while excluding food- and feed-based biofuels reduces GHG emissions.  
o Indirect emissions are caused by the associated need of the fermentation, bakery, brewery and 
animal feed industries to replace, when possible 4, molasses by other feedstocks. 5 This creates in-
direct land use change related emissions through increased the sugar production in third countries 
to satisfy the growing European needs. 
o In the road transport sector for example: Excluding all food- and feed-based biofuels (as molasses 
based bio-ethanol from sugar beets), as well as intermediate crops, from the RED II road transport 
target would result in GHG savings of 66 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent in 2030 and an average 
GHG abatement cost reduction of 64%.6 Similar trends are expected for shipping.

• High value food and feed uses should be prioritised over low-value energy uses especially when dis-
placing historic uses. Prioritisation of uses needs to be in line with the circular economy, resource 
efficiency principles and the intention of the Renewable Energy Directive.  
o During the previous revision of the directive (RED II), the co-legislators jointly agreed that molasses 
should not be added to Annex IX of the Directive. This would divert molasses from their historic 
uses in the food and feed sector rather than creating and incentivising new, unused feedstocks for 
biofuels.7  
o Any measure encouraging the use of molasses for energy would substantially divert their usage  
from food and fee d. This would increase demand for molasses. Given the global shortage of mo-
lasses, this would add to the existing deficit in EU molasses supply.8  
• Molasses are scarce: The availability of EU-sourced molasses at food grade quality is key to the suc-
cess of the European fermentation industry. Any added competition for European high-quality mo-
lasses from the biofuel industry would be detrimental to the European fermentation industry. 
o The European yeast and fermentation industry heavily relies on molasses to manufacture food and 
feed products. There are not enough molasses available in EU. The EU needs to import more than 
1.8 million tons of molasses every year, resulting in a deficit in the supply of molasses.9 
o Molasses in Europe are produced from sugar beets, their production is vulnerable to climatic and 
other environmental factors such as pests, which further increase their scarcity. 
o The competitiveness of the European fermentation industry would be adversely affected by in-
creased use of molasses and low -green syrup 2 for biofuels in any mode of transport. Thus, we 
share the concerns raised by NGO Transport & Environment, that the proposal could incentivize 
first generation crop-based biofuels.10   
Promoting or providing incentives for the use of molasses for biofuels would divert them from well-estab-
lished food and animal feed applications. This would put at risk the existence of traditional sectors of the 
European bioeconomy dependent on molasses. Thus, the European fermentation industry strongly recom-
mends not to encourage first generation crop-based biofuels  made from molasses  in any mode of 
transport.

About the European Fermentation Group (EFG) 
The European Fermentation Group is the voice of the European 
fermentation industry vis-à-vis EU institutions, national govern-
ments, and civil society. The main objective of the EFG is to en-
sure that EU policies promote the competitiveness of the fermen-
tation industry contributing to the development of a sustainable 
European bioeconomy. Our companies mainly produce citric acid, 
lactic acid, vitamins, amino acids for animal feed and penicillin for 
antibiotics. 
 
Our Members have 19 industrial plants in 11 European countries 
(7,065 direct jobs and around 25,000 direct and indirect jobs).

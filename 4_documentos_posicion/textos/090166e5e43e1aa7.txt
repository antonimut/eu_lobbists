 
 
 
 
Edison’s response to the public European Commission’s consultation on the 
regulation on the use of renewable and low-carbon fuels in maritime transport 
and amending Directive 2009/16/EC (‘Fuel EU Maritime’)  
 
Edison welcomes the possibility to share its considerations on the European Commission (EC)’s proposal for 
a new ‘Fuel EU Maritime’ regulation. This process of revision is timely, as are the EC’s efforts to consult with 
stakeholders from the private sector that are engaged in the energy transition and whose business will be 
impacted by the important legislative proposals that are currently under inter-institutional negotiation.  
We fully support the European Green Deal’s objectives of cutting greenhouse gas emissions by 55% by 
2030 and of reaching carbon neutrality by 2050. We agree with the EC that all transport modes, incl uding 
maritime transport, must contribute to such emission reduction efforts. In our view, t he proposed Fuel EU 
Maritime Regulation goes in the right direction, by guiding investment towards low - and zero -carbon 
technologies.  
We assess that the proposed Fuel EU Maritime regulation provides welcome incentives for the adoption of 
specific technologies such as advanced biofuels and other renewable and low-carbon fuels, namely including 
hydrogen-based synthetic fuels. Opportunities are created in the provision of shore-side electricity services. 
All of this is key to reach European decarbonization objectives.  
We also very much welcome the affirmation of the principle of technological neutrality in the definition of 
the objectives of the regulation . Indeed, all energy vectors capable of reducing emissions should be 
favoured relative to the status quo , which is dominated by oil pro ducts. The principle of technological 
neutrality is essential to deliver a cost -effective energy transition and to avoid postponing decarbonization 
by waiting for breakthroughs in the lowest carbon options  that might materialize much later than currently 
anticipated. Supporting the most cost -effective and ready -to-use technologies  will lead  to a speedy and 
substantial reduction in the sector’s CO2 footprint, while laying the ground for a deeper transformation once 
technologies that are not yet economically viable will be ready to be adopted at scale.  
The proposed GHG emission reduction thresholds with intermediate milestones appear reasonable . 
Namely, the intermediate threshold of -26% CO2e/MJ from 1 January 2040 appears to show the legislator’s 
intention of allowing transitional technologies to play an important role in decarbonization . These 
thresholds are reasonable because they take into account the reality on the ground in terms of technology 
readiness, the need to preserve the EU maritime sector’s international competitiveness, and specificities of 
the maritime sector. These include interdependencies between supply, distribution and demand of fuels and 
the long-life span of assets , which are rightfully recognized by the EC as important factors that need to be 
considered when sketching a decarbonization pathway for this sector.  
In addition to greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions,  we emphasize that  the maritime sector is currently 
responsible for substantial emissions of local pollutants (SOx, PM and NOx), mostly due to the widespread 
use of heavy fuel oil  (HFO). We feel that t he scale of th is problem is often overlooked  in the political and 
public discourse. A recently published report shows that cruise ships alone are responsible for 155 thousand 
tons of NOx, 10 thousand tons of PM and 62 thousand tons of SOx emissions in the EU. In particular, health 
damaging SOx emissions from cruise ships are ten times larger than SOx emissions from all of the 260 million 
cars on EU roads. Impressive figures like this one point to the need for a speedy deployment of alternative 
fuels in the maritime sector , for which timely and well-coordinated investments in ships and infrastructure

are urgently needed. International engagement aimed at expanding IMO Emission Control Areas (ECAs) to 
new regions would help favouring the uptake of less polluting fuels. 
Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) is a mature technology that can already be adopted at a very large scale in the 
maritime sector. There, it is estimated to deliver up to a 25% CO2 emission reduction and a 90% reduction 
in NOx emissions. Moreover, switching from heavy fuel oil to LNG means completely eliminating harmful 
SOx emissions . Besides, investing in LNG and LNG infrastructure in the maritime sector does not create 
carbon lock-in effects. To the contrary, it actually paves the way for the later adoption of biogases (including 
bio-LNG itself) in a sector that is currently dominated by oil products. 
As a general principle, we believe that the Commission’s decision to adopt a Well-to-Wake (WtW) approach 
is understandable given the need to track greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions along the value chain. However, 
it then becomes particularly important to ensure that the Fit-for-55 and other legislation consistently adopt 
Well-to-Wake (WtW) approaches in order to guarantee consistency and a level-playing field. 
While agreeing with the general principle, we believe that  the WtT (Well-to-Tank) emission evaluation 
parameters contained in Annex II risk creating significant uncertainty  on the possibility for an uptake of  
LNG. As the text itself recognizes, the ‘lack of information on future regulatory requirements’ can be a major 
stumbling block for decarbonizing the sector.  Uncertainty and contradictory signals should therefore be 
avoided. In particular, more clarity would be needed on how the WtT default parameter of 18.5 gCO2e/MJ 
for LNG contained in Annex II Column 4 has been determined , given the fact that  studies on methane 
slippage are still ongoing and there is still a lack of consensus on the scale of the phenomenon . We believe 
that better avoidance of methane slippage can also be achieved at a TtW (Tank-to-Wake) level than what is 
reflected in the default values mentioned in Annex II Column 9.  
By placing LNG almost on a par with the most polluting fossil fuels, such criteria undermine attractiveness 
in LNG investment. The uncertainty created by Annex II risks discouraging the very investments in mature  
and environmental-friendly technologies, such as LNG, that the legislator appears to be willing to promote 
through this Regulation. We advise the legislators to update those criteria on a yearly basis in light of newly 
published data  and newly achieved technological progress  (and, possibly, of estimates on expected 
technological progress), so as to certify the actual carbon intensity or  potential for methane leakage. This 
would provide clear indications to investors. 
We would also like to highlight that a penalization of transitional investment in LNG would lead to 
inconsistency with other Fit -for-55 provisions , and notably the requirement of the Alternative Fuel 
Infrastructure Regulation (AFIR) for Member States to build LNG charging infrastructure and ensure adequate 
coverage by 2025.  
Finally, we would suggest to integrate the proposal by foreseeing the possibility of developing a common 
methodology to enable the virtual blending of renewable, decarbonised and low -carbon fuels . The 
deployment of these fuels is and will be growing everywhere in Europe, but the necessary logistics for their 
actual use may not be ready in the short term. To this end, virtual blending could stimulate more liquidity on 
the market and incentivise the ramping up of volumes and, subsequently, infrastructure.

Keeping on Pace for Net Zero 
Submission on the Fit for 55 Proposals 
November 2021 
 
Iogen Corporation (“Iogen) supports “Fit for 55”, the transformational changes that are required to 
achieve the EU’s 2030 decarbonisation goals and climate-neutrality by 2050. Indeed, we as a company are 
fully dedicated to using our world -leading advanced biofuels technologies to develop deep  carbon-
negative fuels based on green biohydrogen, a process we have successfully commercialised. Many of the 
specific proposals in the Fit for 55 package could affect the use of this important contribution to reducing 
greenhouse gas emissions, both positively and negatively, as set out below. We hope the measures as 
ultimately adopted by the EU legislator will reflect our constructive suggestions. 
 
BIOHYDROGEN FOR DEEP DECARBONISATION 
Large volumes of renewable hydrogen are needed  
Renewable hydrogen does not emit CO2 and can be used not only as a fuel, but also as a feedstock and a 
means of transporting and storing energy, making it an indispensable and flexible decarbonisation tool 
across the transport, industry, buildings and power sectors.  A key aspect of hydrogen’s appeal is its ability 
to serve difficult-to-electrify applications.  
Hydrogen is expected to grow from 2% of the EU’s current energy consumption to up to 24% in 2050.
1 
This growth repres ents a staggering commitment, especially considering that virtually all of today’s 
hydrogen is produced from fossil fuels, causing 70 -100 million tonnes of CO2 every year. 2 This 
commitment will be best served if policy -makers ensure important pathways for renewable hydrogen 
production are not excluded from Fit for 55.   
On the supply side, the EU Hydrogen Strategy calls for 6GW hydrogen capacity/1 million tonnes hydrogen 
production by 2024, and for 40GW hydrogen capacity/10 million tonnes hydrogen production by 2030.
3 
The Fit for 55 RED II revision proposal creates dedicated demand-side targets for RFNBOs in both industry 
(50% of hydrogen use by 2030) and transport (2.6% of energy in transport by 2030), which together will 
require 11 million tonnes of hydrogen production .4 These very aggressive goals  require all hydrogen 
routes, including electrolysis and biohydrogen. In fact, all of the currently-proposed electrolyser projects 
due online by 2024 represent only 5.2GW capacity and those due by 2030 represent only 22 GW capacity.5

Biohydrogen can deliver today 
While some sources of biohydrogen (pyrolysis and gasification) are emerging, biohydrogen from anerobic 
digestion can deliver substantial low carbon hydrogen today. The technology is relatively simple: 
anaerobic digestion converts waste ( e.g. from animals and field residues) into biogas which can be 
upgraded to biomethane. Iogen has developed and patented a number of specific technologies that 
promote this process.  
Iogen is currently  generating low carbon biohydrogen to decarbonise industrial hydrogen used in 
refineries, which comprise 45% of hydrogen use in the EU, or 3.7 million tonnes. 7 90% of the resulting 
renewable hydrogen would be incorporated as a renewable component into the fuel.  Decarbonizing 
refinery hydrogen represents almost four times the EU’s 2024 production goal, and over one -third of its 
2030 goal.8 If properly nurtured, this demand could significantly stimulate Europe’s renewable hydrogen 
production now, rather than in the ten years it will take to get industrial electrolysis on line. 
Biohydrogen is “BECCS ready” 
Beyond 2030, bio-hydrogen is also “BECCS ready” for the longer term. As hydrogen and CCS infrastructure 
develops, the carbon footprint of biohydrogen will be unparalleled. CO2 can be removed and sequestered 
both when  the biomass is converted to biomethane and when the biomethane is used to make bio -
hydrogen, resulting in deep negative emissions.  A recent UK government study determined that hydrogen 
from bioenergy with CCS can achieve 3x the GHG savings of hydrogen from electrolysis .9 This is Iogen 
Corporation’s vision for carbon-negative fuels, which we are commercialising in California and Europe.  
 
Biohydrogen is scalable 
Technological advancements, including ones pioneered by Iogen, allow much more plentiful field residues 
to also be converted into renewable natural gas, expanding the known sources of supply 100 times  over 
landfill and current manure management, with concomitant carbon removals. This significance of biomass 
conversion is fully recognised by the IEA, which estimates a total of 45 EJ potential capacity of biomethane 
in 2050.10

Fit for 55 measures directly affect biohydrogen  
The Fit for 55 package in many ways promotes the use of both biomass and renewable hydrogen, but does 
not sufficiently link the two, and in some instances may actually work against this linkage. One reason for 
this, as noted above, is the understandable focus on producing hydro gen through industrial -scale 
electrolysis. But further, biomethane is seen as coming largely from landfill and manure management, 
which, while important in methane, is constrained in terms of volume, notwithstanding the IEA’s robust 
estimates. And on the demand side, biomethane is normally seen as feeding directly into district heating 
and compressed natural gas (CNG) vehicles, both relatively limited markets. As set out above, the supply 
and demand for biohydrogen are both much more robust than these assumptions would suggest. 
As it strives for carbon neutrality by 2050, and for its more immediate goal of reducing greenhouse gas 
emissions by 55 percent by 2030, the EU should incentivize this biological pathway to replacing fossil fuels 
and reducing atmospheric carbon dioxide. Many aspects of the Fit for 55 package can help with this. But 
in any event, the EU should ensure that it places renewable hydrogen from biological sources on an equal 
footing with other renewable hydrogen sources and avoids disincentivizing it, including through measures 
that work against mass balancing or require certain carbon -based testing regimes that biohydrogen by 
definition cannot meet. 
The specific Fit for 55 measures that directly affect the biohydrogen pathway are: 
• the Renewable Energy Directive (RED II) revision proposal 
• the Refuel EUAviation proposal 
• the FuelEU Maritime proposal 
• the Energy Taxation Directive revision proposal (ETD) 
• the Emissions Trading System revision proposal (ETS) 
• the Regulation for CO2 Emissions for Cars and Vans revision proposal  
 
MAXIMISE THE IMPACT OF THE RENEWABLE ENERGY DIRECTIVE 
Iogen strongly supports the RED II revision proposal. The success of transport decarbonisation policy will 
be a key determinant of the success of overall climate and energy policy. Transport emissions contributed 
24% of all EU g reenhouse gas (“GHG”) emissions in 2019 ,

Iogen urges the European Commission to consider the following recommendations, focused on ensuring 
that the EU remains on pace to reach net zero in 2050:

Leverage a GHG standard for maximum impact 
The reorientation of RED towards a GHG standard over the prior volumetric standard is a laudable shift. 
This updated methodology will promote renewable and low carbon fuels  in accordance with their 
greenhouse gas performance . This in turn  will provide a catalyst for supply chain and technology 
efficiencies and continuous improvement beyond minimum emission reduction thresholds. 
The use of a GHG standard will also facilitate a simple and comprehensive terminology for all renewable 
and low -carbon fuels and a pan -EU system for certifying those fuels.  All such fuels  should have GHG 
thresholds, consistently expressed in terms of required life-cycle GHG savings relative to a fossil fuel 
comparator. Fuels will then be differentiated based on carbon intensity, which should be reported to 
maximise transparency. This approach is consistent with the Climate Target Plan and the Energy System 
Integration strategy, and with the goals of transparency and technology neutrality (on which more below). 
 
Prioritise advanced biofuels 
As set out above, biohydrogen can be a valuable partner to RFNBOs in the decarbonisation of hydro gen. 
The best way to promote biohydrogen is to ambitiously promote Annex IX -A advanced biofuels for 
transport in the RED II Revision with ambitious subtargets. This has a two -fold benefit: it increases the 
supply of renewables from existing assets immediately and also acts as a stepping stone to net zero – both 
by facilitating the broader roll- out of renewable hydrogen, hydrogen infrastructure and BECCS, and by 
supporting investment in hydrogen-based road fuel production today that can be converted to renewable 
maritime and aviation fuel production as road vehicles electrify.  
We agree with the pairing of a GHG target for renewables in transport with an increase to the existing 
subtarget for Annex IX -A advanced biofuels. This pairing provides a catalyst for supply chain and 
technology efficiencies and continuous improvement beyond minimum emission reduction thresholds, 
through the GHG target, but also supports high -potential, game -changing innovations like the 
development of ultra negative emissi ons bio-hydrogen, through the advanced subtarget.  We welcome 
the transparent differentiation of fuels based on carbon intensity, supplemented with narrow subtargets 
to ensure that innovative, high-potential options requiring special support are fostered. W e believe that 
the end result will be the best possible incubator for net zero solutions. 
Because of the nature of the advanced subtarget, we believe that additions of new feedstock to Annex IX-
A should result in incremental increases to the advanced subtarget itself, to ensure that the support for 
game-changing innovation is not muted by its own success.  
We also believe that it is essential for the Commission to avoid introducing subordinate legislation (in the 
form of Delegated Acts, Implementing Regulations and the like) that restricts or impairs the functioning 
of the RED II Revision vis a vis the advanced subtarget. Examples of such restrictions are the improper 
limitation of co -processing renewable fuels by restricting use of the long -recognised mass balance 
principle and the imposition of requirements for C14 testing (which cannot detect renewable hydrogen 
content).

Level the playing field for qualified fuels 
A centralised database that tracks fuels in an EU-wide mass balance system, with the existing framework 
for voluntary schemes covering portions of the value chain outside the EU, will increase transparency and 
traceability and minimise the risk of fraud. The mass balance approach is a robust chain of custody system, 
as it is specifically designed to trace the flow of materials through complex value chains and  provides the 
stringency of a physical link between all stages of custody, unlike book and claim systems. 
The database will support a certification system that covers all energy end use secto rs and their supply 
chains on a life-cycle emissions basis. This harmonised system with common standards will ensure a level 
playing field for all fuel types.  
 
INTRODUCE WELL-ALIGNED AVIATION AND SHIPPING POLICY 
The dedicated FuelEU Maritime and RefuelEU Aviation proposals represent an important step-change in 
EU climate policy. We welcome th ese proposals, and encourage the Commission to maximise their 
alignment with the balance of the Fit for 55 initiatives and the key principles on which they are based, 
within their context as difficult -to-decarbonise sectors  that require a stable, long- term regulat ory 
foundation. 
The proposed basis for the RefuelEU Aviation proposal, a volumetric obligation, lacks consistency with the 
RED II revision proposal and the imperative to measure actual carbon intensity of the various SAF options. 
The basis for the FuelEU Maritime proposal, well-to-wake GHG emissions, is well-aligned with other EU 
climate policy and looks at the complete climate impact picture.  
As set out above, it is essential that these regimes ensure that all sources of renewable hydrogen – beyond 
only hydrogen from electrolysis – are enabled, particularly in hard-to-electrify applications. In particular, 
renewable bio-hydrogen has comparable GHG savings to hydrogen from 100% renewable electricity (and 
much higher savings when paired with CCS). It is also essential to avoid distortions of the climate impact 
picture, which would occur if electricity were to be given a zero -emission factor. This would materially 
misrepresent the emissions profile of the electrical grid across the EU and unnecessarily curtail the climate 
impacts of FuelEU Maritime. 
 
REINFORCE ADVANCED BIOFUELS AND HYDROGEN IN THE ENERGY TAX 
DIRECTIVE 
The ETD can and should provide  particularised support to advanced biofuels, a priority under the RED II 
revision proposal. Almost 20 years old, the ETD is due for an upgrading to reflect the vastly different 
circumstances in which it now operates. Iogen supports the proposed revisions, which make the ETD a 
more effective tool that is better aligned with the EU’s evolved climate objectives. Increasing and indexing 
minimum tax rates, including new sectors (aviation, maritime) and products (hydrogen) and reducing the 
opportunity for Member State exemptions and rate reductions all bring the ETD closer to fulfilling the 
goals of 55% in 2030 and net zero in 2050.  
The most important policy change in the proposal is  the linkage of the act ual environmental impact of 
energy products to the minimum tax rates applicable to them. The shift away from volume-based taxation, 
which favoured high-energy content fossil fuels  and created perverse incentives , is critical to reshaping 
the ETD into an effective decarbonisation tool.

The ETD revision proposal recognises that promoting advanced biofuels, and advanced and other fuels 
made with renewable hydrogen, is essential and that these fuels have a superior environmental footprint 
to other energy vectors. As discussed above, hydrogen -based fuels have the potential, with CCS, to 
achieve deep negative emissions and the ETD should be structured to incent these fuels commensurate 
with their positive climate impacts. Assigning all of these critical fuels the lowest minimum tax rate , 
significantly below the reference rate, is an appropriate path to achieving the EU’s audacious ambitions 
over the next 30 years.  
 
STRENGTHEN CO2 STANDARDS AND MEASURE WHAT MATTERS 
We support ambitious CO2 emission standards for cars and vans  that recognise the important role road 
transport must play in decarbonisation. Such emission standards will best play their role where they take 
a true measure of the carbon footprint of the available fuel  alternatives. Tank -to-wheel emission  
measures fail to account for the full life-cycle emissions associated with each fuel option. It is the complete 
well-to-wheel carbon profile of a fuel that should drive government policy and consumer behaviour, 
because it is the well-to-wheel emissions that will determine our real progress toward 55% in 2030 and 
net zero by 2050 and should therefore determine the technologies to be supported. This approach would 
harmonise with other elements  of Fit for 55, such as the R ED II revision proposal, FuelEU Maritime and 
the ETD revision proposal. 
Given the need for road transportation to “catch up” to the climate progress made in other sectors, the 
broadest range of qualified fuels and technologies should be encouraged in early  years. Moreover, road 
transport encompasses a key hard-to-electrify sector in the longer term, heavy goods trucking. Measuring 
what matters in this way on the demand side , combined with support for renewable hydrogen from all 
sources on the supply side, creates a virtuous cycle that can quickly ramp up the use of hydrogen -based 
fuels to serve the EU’s bold climate goals. 
 
INTRODUCE A SEPARATE EMISSION TRADING SYSTEM FOR ROAD TRANSPORT 
We support the proposed ETS proposal for road transport and its structure. Given the lack of progress in 
decarbonisation of transport to date, a policy mix is needed and a consumer price signal beyond CO2 
emission standards will be necessary to pair with renewables targets and subtargets.   
A separate ETS for road transport is preferable for multiple reasons. Demand for road fuels is influenced 
by other factors than the sectors currently covered by the ETS and the system must reflect this. The 
abatement costs in road transport are relatively higher and low  risk of carbon leakage means free 
allocation should not be allowed. 
The separate ETS for road transport as envisaged by the European Commission will accelerate biofuel 
usage more expeditiously than regulation alone or the extension of the existing ETS to transport. For these 
reasons it is the best means to augment the RED II revision proposal. We applaud the Commission’s policy 
imperative to ameliorate the possible regressive effects of the ETS for road transport.

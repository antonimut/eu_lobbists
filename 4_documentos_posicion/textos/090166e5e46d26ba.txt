Feedback on Energy Efficiency Directive legislation proposal within the Fit for 55 Package  
 
Executive summary  
 
Aughinish Alumina (AAL) is own by Rusal and AAL is the largest alumina refinery in Europe. Based on 
Ireland’s West Coast, it is also the one of the largest energy consumer in the country. AAL operates in 
the 5% lowest carbon intensity  within the alumina in dustry globally. In 2020 AAL was ranked 4 th 
lowest alumina plant carbon  footprint worldwide at 0.51 tCO 2/t versus a world average of 1.29 
tCO2/t product  by Commodity Research Unit (CRU) . AAL  has the potential to become the world 
benchmark for low-carbon intensity alumina manufacturing.  
 
Rusal and AAL are  fully committed to delivering on the European Climate law 2030 targets and to 
transitioning the refinery to climate neutrality by 2050. The current level of gas prices on the energy 
market call for the urgent adoption of a holistic approach from energy market design to create 
incentives for materials decarbonisation. The competitiveness of the European raw material industry 
and carbon leakage concerns are at stake. 
 
To achieve those goals, AAL would like to make the following points and recommendations: 
 
• The energy efficiency improvement will require significant investment in parallel to investing 
into renewable energy technologies to decarbonise completely alumina refining.  All these 
investments for both efficiency and renewable technologies will require financial support.  
• Retain free allowances in the EU ETS at least until at least 2035 to safeguard against carbon 
leakage and facilitate the investments needed to transition to climate neutrality  which will 
include investing in capital intensive efficiency projects and avoid introduction of the Cross 
Sectoral Correction Factor (CSCF).  
• Adopt the elements in the latest EU ETS proposal that include doubling the size of the 
Innovation Fund and introducing Carbon Contracts for Difference (CCfDs). 
• The ongoing review of the EU State Aid Guidelines for Climate, Environmental protection and 
Energy post -2020 (CEEAG) must accompany and be coherent with the Fit for 55 package, 
addressing both the cost and investment challenges stemming from this package.

1. European alumina production is key to ensure EU’s aluminium strategic autonomy 
 
Alumina production is an energy intensive process, necessary to produce aluminium – both alumina 
and aluminium are key strategic raw materials in Europe. AAL is a critical link in the aluminium supply 
chain in Europe, supplying 30% of Europe’s annual requirement of alumina. AAL employs 650 staff and 
contractors in County Limerick, Ireland, and it is contributing a €130 million annually to the local 
economy. 
 
The aluminium value chain covers many sectors from bauxite mining, alumina refining, primary 
aluminium (smelting), semi-fabrication of products and recycling or secondary production (Figure 1.1).

Aluminium has become essential for low carbon technologies and can be found in many sectors of the 
economy such as transportation, construction, the electrical sector and in packaging. Aluminium is 
increasingly used in low-carbon transition technologies such as electric battery cases, battery cells and 
in hydrogen fuel cells. Due to its light weight, it is a preferred material of the cradles and blades of the 
wind turbines, also being used in permanent magnets and it is widely applied to photovoltaic panels, 
electrical connections, cars, and building finishes, such as windows, ventilation, and drywalls.

green transition (European Aluminium vision 2050 report, 2019).

2. Carbon Leakage  continues being a major  threat during the industrial t ransition to climate 
neutrality 
 
The CO 2 emissions of the aluminium value chain are principally embedded in the alumina refining 
phase and in the smelting process (Figure 2.1). Unfortunately, alumina refining, and aluminium 
smelting are hard -to-abate processes due to the  quantity of energy required . There is currently no 
reliable nor continuous renewable energy source available in large quantity required for alumina 
refining in Europe. Additionally, there is no technology that is today commercially available to 
decarbonise heat and steam for alumina production  or alumina calcination in Europe. Alternative 
technologies are being developed at pilot  scale and require large capital investment and important 
operational costs.  
 
AAL is quite advanced in its decarbonisation road map to implement efficiency projects and 
introducing process electrification using renewable electricity. AAL is also investing in R esearch & 
Development (R&D) and working with many National stakeholders to be ready for the move to 
renewable hydrogen, which could become a real solution once in place. However, in 2021, there is no 
viable commercial option to install and operate renewable energy technologies for alumina refining 
without significant financial support (at least 50%).

The aluminium industry has undergone major changes over the last 15 years, notably the rise of China 
as the leading producer and investor in all segments of the value chain including alumina refining. The

jeopardizing European strategic autonomy in aluminium.  AAL operates in the 5% lowest carbon

would operate at the AAL’s carbon intensity of 0.51 tCO2/t level instead of global average, this would 
reduce carbon emissions by 104 million tonnes of CO 2 globally (Figure 2.3). This is a compelling 
argument to avoid carbon leakage as the carbon emissions globally would increase significantly if any 
alumina refinery  in Europe including AAL  is forced to shut down due to lack of carbon leakage 
protection.

3. How Fit for 55 can negatively affect or support AAL’s alumina production pathway towards 
climate neutrality? 
 
As alumina is a commodity product traded internationally and highly exposed to foreign competition, 
any additional costs related to carbon cannot be passed on to customers. The Fit for 55 package and 
particularly the revision of the ETS framework represent an opportunity to improve the existing carbon 
leakage tools currently in place to protect the alumina sector during this challenging transition period 
to significantly reduce its carbon footprint. 
 
3.1 AAL’s Investment plan for decarbonization 
 
a. Period 2005 - 2020 - Transition from fuel oil to natural gas and quick payback efficiency projects 
 
AAL has invested over  €200m since 2005 in reducing emissions and improving energy performance 
with the installation of high efficiency Combined Heat and Power plant (CHP) in 2006, conversion of 
the heavy fuel oil boilers (2014) and calcination (2012) to natural gas and implementation of many 
energy improvement projects  that have had short payback period s (less than 3 years ). These 
investments have reduced carbon emissions by 40%. For 2020, AAL has now the 4 th lowest alumina 
plant carbon footprint worldwide (source: Commodity Research Unit for 2020) at 0.51 tCO2/t versus 
a world average of 1.29 0 tCO2/t product and is committed to reducing carbon emissions to zero by 
2050 or earlier if possible (Figure 3.1).

b. Period 2021 - 2050 – Transition to renewable energy and long payback efficiency projects 
 
Most of the CO2 emissions from AAL come from natural gas , which is used on site. The transition to 
zero carbon for AAL will be centred around replacing natural gas, a reliable and continuous source of 
energy, with renewable intermittent energy source (wind in Ireland ) until green hydrogen is fully in 
place from offshore wind leading to hydrogen production, storage and supply to industry (likely from 
2030 onwards).  
 
However, there is no viable option with quick payback and no easy access to reliable renewable energy 
sources. AAL’s road map is focussing on three aspects: implement efficiency projects (Energy Efficiency 
First principle) that require s high capital investment and longer payback period s, process 
electrification for production of steam for alumina refining, and fuel switch to green hydrogen for CHP 
(steam and electricity) and alumina calcination operation (Figures 3.2 and 3.3).

3.2 Impact of ETS proposal on AAL’s operation – Level of Free Allocations 
 
The alumina refining sector (including AAL) is a very energy intensive and hard-to-abate sector due to 
the large quantity of energy continuously required. Figure 3.6 shows the high level of energy required 
for steam production and heat for calcination: this level of energy cannot be compared with most 
chemical plants in Europe.  The alumina sector is using fall -back benchmark values that are not 
representative of the complexity to abate carbon emissions from the sector. 
 
Figure 3.6 – Energy flows at AAL in 2020 
 
 
The benchmark values reduced by 24% between  ETS Phase III and first half of Phase IV (2021-2025) 
are expected to reduce by another 26% during the second half of the period (2 026-2030). This will 
lead to a 50% reduction compared to ETS Phase III. There is no alumina refinery in the world that has 
delivered such improvement in the past few years. This benchmark performance can only be done by 
moving to renewable energy source s which are not currently available in the scale required  for the 
alumina sector. It is unrealistic to propose such a reduction of benchmark values at this early stage of 
a transition to zero carbon emissions. The reduction of free allocation associated with the benchmark 
reduction of 24% and the additional reduction of 26% (as per current proposal) for 2026-2030 period 
will not incentivise the investment required to decarbonise the sector  that the free allowances help 
financing. The benchmark values should be assessed separately similar to what is done for primary 
aluminium and any new breakthrough technology implementation, as small technology pilots, should 
not impact the benchmark level for all existing plants within few years. 
 
Additionally, free allocations will be required until at least 2035 to ensure decarbonisation is complete 
in Ireland for the alumina refinery  as it is very unlikely that the availability of green hydrogen in 
quantity and pricing required will be in place prior to 2030 and this transition to renewable hydrogen 
will also require significant investment post 2030 at AAL. 
 
Adjusting the Market Stability Reserve (MSR), while remaining within the ETS cap, can be a way to 
avoid the Cross Sectoral Correction Factor (CSCF). This would support emission reduction target and 
reduce carbon leakage risk. The accumulated surplus because of the economic downturn due to the 
COVID crisis and the financial crisis must be protected from the invalidation clause. These E urope 
Union Allowances (EUAs) are a highly needed surplus to cope with economic recovery and industrial 
growth. More flexibility is required to the invalidation clause, to improve the MSR functioning as a 
better stability instrument. AAL and EAA propose that:

4. Our policy recommendations 
The EU policy must enable, support and reward progressive sustainable industry actors such as AAL.  
 
4.1 An ambitious emission reduction policy that avoids carbon leakage 
 
Rusal and AAL are committed to reducing its carbon emissions to zero by 2050. To do so, AAL needs a 
level playing field and financial support to ensure its investments are sustainable economically.  
 
Free allowances under the ETS should remain in place until at least 2035 to allow investment in 
innovation, mitigate carbon leakage concerns , and provide a level -playing field to develop  better 
climate products. Without this support, AAL cannot achieve its climate transition. The benchmark 
values should be assessed for the alumina product independently, similarly to aluminium product, to 
provide a more realistic benchmark and an adequate level of free allocation.   
 
The MSR should be optimised using the invalidation clause to avoid the introduction of the CSCF during 
phase IV. 
 
4.2 ETS Innovation fund and Environmental Aid to fund decarbonisation 
 
The Fit for 55 package must adopt  climate and energy policies that provide financial support to the 
most at risk of carbon leakage industry operators via ETS free allocation and financial support for the 
deployment of these innovative technologies during the transitional period (for examp le Innovation 
Fund and CCfDs).  
 
EU funding programs such as the Innovation Fund are critical for hard-to-abate sector s such as 
aluminium/alumina refining and must include efforts on process electrification, thermal energy 
storage use and development of renewable fuel such as biofuel and green hydrogen 
production/storage and distribution to decarbonise the alumina sector in Ireland and in Europe.  
 
To move forward with the implementation of AAL’s ambitious climate action plan, initial capital 
investment estimate in the region of €553 million is required to deliver a reduction of 340,000 tonnes 
of carbon from the alumina refining process  (35% reduction) with potential of 670,000 tonnes of 
carbon in carbon mode (70% reduction).  
 
The financial aid required to realise these ambitions must be at least 50% of the total investment cost 
to make it economically viable . AAL is seeking funding to support this transition through the 
Environmental Aid Scheme and ETS Innovation Fund. 
 
The Environmental Aid Scheme 2014 – 2020 has been extended until 3 1st December 2023 and will 
support initiatives to improve energy efficiency, to promote the uptake of energy from renewable 
sources and to encourage the carrying out of environmental studies. States aids guidelines and caps 
should be designed in a way that benefits to strategic industries exposed to carbon leakage. 
In summary, ETS free allocation must remain in place until at least 2035, to support the alumina sector 
and AAL’s decarbonisation plan using technologies that are currently not commercially viable. 
Additionally, the Innovation Fund and CCfDs will be required to support stand-alone alumina refineries 
such as AAL, introducing new renewable energy technologies that are compatible with our specific 
location in Ireland. 
 
If free allocation were to be phased out under ETS, it is mandatory that after 2030, the CBAM covers 
alumina product as well.

4.3 State Aid Guidelines for Climate, Environment Protection and Energy (CEEAG) 
 
As per our submission to EU commission on the 29th of July 2021, AAL welcomes the new revision of 
the State Aid Guidelines which are providing extended support for climate initiative. The Guidelines 
represent an opportunity to reflect the recent EU regulatory and policy developments towards a low 
carbon transition.  
 
The scope extension towards new decarbonization technologies, circularity and new supportive 
measures for energy intensive users will be critical to survival of the alumina sector in Europe. In 
particular, we strongly support:  
 
• The State Ai d Measure for the reduction and removal of greenhouse gas emissions including 
through support for renewable energy. 
• The State Aid Measure in the energy sector for infrastructure. 
• The preservation of existing provisions on aid schemes for Energy Intensive Users (EIUs) via the 
reduction of levies supporting Renewable Energy Systems (RES) as well as introducing the 
possibility of exemptions from new charges, including Combined Heat and Power (CHP).

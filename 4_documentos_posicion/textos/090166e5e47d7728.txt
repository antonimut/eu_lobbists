Introduction to the T&M Coalition 
 
The Test & Measurement Coalition represents an ad -hoc group of companies active in producing test & 
measurement industrial type products. The Coalition includes leading companies in the sector including 
Agilent Technologies, Fluke Corporation, Keithley Ins truments, Keysight Technologies, National 
Instruments, Thermo Fisher Scientific and Tektronix. We estimate the coalition membership represents 
over 60% of the global production  of industrial test and measurement products and other industrial 
equipment including chemical analysers.  
Products include a wide range of sophisticated electronic instruments such as signal generators, logic 
analysers, oscilloscopes, spectrum analysers, digital multimeters, electron microscopes, chemical and 
biological analysers, complex chromatography systems and their detectors, etc. The instruments are used 
in Research, Quality Control and Testing laboratories (including field testing) in Universities, Manufacturing 
facilities and by Governmental Agencies  (for conformance verification and environmental testing). They 
are essential to the good functioning of electronic communications networks, heavy industrial processes 
such as steel manufacturing, the testing of vehicles for compliance with emissions standards, and the 
monitoring of complex and critical systems of all types. 
Industrial Test & Measurement equipment is exclusively used in professional and industrial use, and end-
of-life instruments are collected through B2B waste schemes.  The whole of the monitorin g and control 
category of equipment represents by weight 1.8% of the total Electrical and Electronic Equipment (EEE) 
put on the EU market and 0.7% of the total Waste EEE (WEEE) collected in the EU . Industrial Test & 
Measurement instruments, a subset of mon itoring and control category, only represents in weight 0.2% 
of total EEE  put on the market in 2017 in the EU .1 See the Annex for more information on the Circular 
Economy model for the Test & Measurement Coalition. 
 
 Position on the revision of the CLP Regulation 
 
There are very strict reliability requirements for our products that result in extended lifetime performance. These 
are met thanks to the use of very small quantities of indispensable substances, allowed under the RoHS Directive 
through exempti ons. These substances, however are subject to regulatory initiatives under REACH (i.e. SVHC 
identification, authorization, restriction)  and consequently require S CIP notification . Prolonging the use phase 
significantly, the use of these substances contribu tes decisively to the circular economy. Moreover they have no 
impact on human health or on the environment thanks to their controlled professional and industrial use and 
collection of end-of-life instruments through B2B waste schemes.

Greater coherence between CLP and sector specific legislation, such as RoHS, is needed with regards to substance 
prioritisation for a ban, to ensure regulatory consistence,  efficiency and predictability.  
 
In the framework of the CLP revision, the Coalition is pro moting an overall legislative framework enabling 
transparency, coherence, predictability and legal certainty for companies , notably through: 
• In priority, the EU CLH should follow the GHS. Indeed, there should be no divergence between the EU CLH 
and international GHS. Labelling should be the same globally . This is particularly important for economic 
operators who place products on the market both in the EU and in third countries.  
 
• The generic approach to risk management should be refined:  while indeed discussions on the hazard 
identification, assessment and classification should take place in horizontal legislation such as REACH and 
CLP, risk management measures should not be automatically triggered in sectoral legislation based on 
hazard classification only. Instead, sectoral legislation should be used to adequately manage the risk in the 
relevant sectors taking into account exposure, specificities, proportionality and cost-efficiency analyses for 
the various sub-categories, as well as essential uses.  
 
• The example of the ongoing CLP classification of silver can be given. Any restriction on the use of silver in 
electronics would necessitate changes to virtually every component in the global supply chain in addition to 
product redesign and re -qualification that would involve Billions of Euros globally and take more than a 
decade to achieve.  T&M Coalition members are all subject to the RoHS Directive and similar material 
restriction legislation and manage portfolios accordingly. Substantial investment was necessary to transition 
our portfolios from using leaded solder to become RoHS -compliant while retaining the long-term reliability 
expected and required of our equipment. The RoHS ban on use of lead in solder caused product withdrawals 
from the EU market as late as 11 years after the initial introduction of a ban on lead in solder. This was made 
possible through the use of silver as a substit ute. Any similar ban on silver would be at least as complex as 
the RoHS ban on lead, with fewer viable alternative substitutes, so extended transition periods  would be 
required to convert the bulk of the portfolio. Member portfolios average in the thousand s of products and 
resources for redesign are not abundant due to the specialized nature of the sector.   
 
• Overlap of RoHS and REACH/CLP should be avoided: 
 
❖ Sector legislation, such as RoHS, should not create a separate stream of hazardous assessment 
The current methodology for identifying substances candidates for restriction under RoHS  maintains that 
“substances that are suspected of having such (hazardous) properties shall be included in the substance 
inventory. RoHS restriction process leads to substantial impacts on product design and market access of EEE. 
These are serious consequences and cannot be triggered by the simple suspicion of hazardous properties of 
the substance. RoHS should not create a separate stream of hazard assessment. This process exists in the 
context of REACH and CLP.  
 
❖ Substances subject to REACH CLP process should not be considered under RoHS before the REACH process 
is finalised and the classification is confirmed 
Substance evaluation does not always confirm suspec ted properties, therefore CORAP list, even CLP 
proposals as such, cannot be used as reference. Instead, the Commission should wait for the scientific 
output of the REACH evaluation process, at least, and ideally for the outcome of the CLP process before 
launching assessment of substance.

Substances which are considered carcinogenic, mutagenic, reproductive toxicants, and respiratory  
sensitizers as well as other substances (on a case -by-case basis) may undergo harmonization of their  
classification (CLH). Member State competent authorities, manufacturers, importers or downstream  users 
may propose the harmonized classification, which is then formally reviewed by ECHA in  conjunction with a 
public consultation. ECHA will then form an opin ion which is reviewed by the RCOM  and eventually voted 
upon by the EU Commission. In order to properly characterize the risk these substances create; they should 
not be considered until ECHA’s opinion has been drafted and not formally included into RoHS until after the 
CLH has been voted on by the EU Commission. This will  allow appropriate consideration of other means of 
control via harmonized (e.g., REACH) and sector  specific (e.g. Plant Protection, Biocides, Cosmetics, etc.) 
legislation.

Annex - Circular Economy model for T&M instruments  
The core loops of the Circular Economy model for technical materials are: maintain, reuse, refurbish and 
recycle. How are they translated for industrial test & measurement instruments? 
In regards to the C ircular Economy principles , industrial test & measurement instruments are very 
different from high-volume consumer products  which are frequently re -designed to follow consumer 
trends (every 1.5 year on average).  Industrial test & measurements instruments are  intentionally 
designed for long useful lifespans with high reliability . Test and measurement instruments (category 9 
under the RoHS Directive) are designed: exclusively for professional and industrial use; to meet high 
performance requireme nts in critical application s; and last up to 40 years ( 10 years typical first life). 
Redesign is not frequent and happens every 7 years on average  after which they typically enter a long -
term customer support period. 
 
The main focus of the test & measurement sector within Circular Economy is therefore on the use phase 
and how to prolong it. 
• MAINTAINING: Our instruments are expected to provide repeatable and accurate results 
throughout their lifetime. With a typical first use of ten years and an overall li fe of up to forty 
years; great care is taken during design and qualification to assure these stringent performance 
and rigorous reliability requirements can be met, as well as incorporate design for serviceability .  
To extend availability, instruments can be leased or rented; directly or through brokers; helping 
SMEs avoid capital expenditure and improve utilization. We provide periodic calibration services 
to assure the quality and traceability of instruments measurements are sustained year after year.  
 
• REPAIRING: While our instruments are designed for long term reliability, failures do occur  over 
such an extended use period . Our repair services help minimize any instrument down -time and 
customer disruption. Equipment is typically supported for a minimum of five years after market 
withdrawal. Towards the later stages of useful life, specific parts and sub -assemblies no longer 
available on the open market may be recovered from end-of-life equipment specifically to extend 
the support period.

• REFURBISHING: Technology does not stand still, and enhancements and upgrades are made 
available to extend the useful life of equipment. When a newer piece of equipment is finally 
required, substantial trade-in discounts are offered to customers to reflect the residual asset value 
of the instruments. This provides a steady supply chain of equipment for refurbishment. Extended 
life via resale is of great economic and environmental benefits.  Secondary markets 
(refurbishment and resale) are significant in this sector as they often account for 4-5% of producer 
turnover for test & measurement producers.  
 
• RECYCLING: Equipment only enters the waste stream many decades after being placed on the EU 
market.  Our contribution to the stream of waste electrical and electronic  equipment (WEEE) is 
insignificant (0.2% by weight of EU WEEE). WEEE is collected  exclusively through business to 
business – B2B schemes.  
 
Challenges linked to specificities 
The specificities of the test & measurement sector are also translated in specific challenges in the field of 
the Circular Economy, which need to be understood by policy -makers, especially in the framework of 
debates on the tracking of certain substances, or possible future restrictions of certain substances: 
• Feasibility challenges in tracking substances: 
o Traceability is challenging  given the extreme complexity of Test & Measurement 
products: 25 - 35% of the components used in Test & Measurement products are custom-
designed for instruments . Member companies of the Test and Measurement Coalition 
have supply chains that can exceed 100 000 suppliers for the quarter of a million parts 
used to produce our equipment; with portfolio scale of up to 5000 active products from 
any one producer. Traceability will therefore pose significant feasibility challenges for the 
sector. 
o As a result of their long product life, some instruments may only enter the was te stream 
up to 40 years after they are placed on the EU market. Taking into account the constant 
evolution of the list of substances of concern – especially over the course of several 
decades – this raises questions on the value of this information for the recyclers. 
 
• Need to assess the cost-effectiveness of restricting substances in products: 
o Test & measurement members proactively started to work on redesign before entering 
the scope of the RoHS Directive, and it took them 12 years to be RoHS -compliant. 
Substitution is very challenging  as test & measurement instruments have to guarantee 
very high long-term reliability criteria during an extended lifetime. 
o The impact on innovation for the users of test & measurement products would also need 
to be assessed. This includes SMEs which would be critically impacted in their ability to 
develop/ test new products if access to equipment is removed or availability of second -
hand / refurbished products is made more difficult. 
o After detailed risk assessments, a number of exemptions were granted to test & 
measurement companies to be able to use minimal quantities of certain substances of 
concern in very specific applications, under sectoral legislation. These exempted

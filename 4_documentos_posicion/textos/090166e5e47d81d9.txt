Re: Public consultation concerning revision of EU CLP regulation; request to omit biased 
questions regarding animal testing alternatives from analysis 
 
15 September 2021 
 
Dear Mr Sadauskas and Ms Schreiber, 
 
We are writing in regard to an ongoing public consultation by the European Commission 
concerning revision of Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008 on the classification, labelling and 
packaging of substances and mixtures (CLP).1 We object to the biased formulation of two 
questions and the multi-choices provided, which take advantage of citizens’ inevitable general 
lack of technical understanding of this complex subject matter. Specifically:  
 
Section 2- Testing chemicals on animals, Question 3: 
The foreseen introduction of new classes of hazards in CLP (such as endocrine disruptors) 
is likely to increase testing, including on animals, to assess if a chemical is safe or not for 
human health or the environment. Despite efforts made, there are not yet full alternatives to 
animal testing of chemicals for certain hazard classes. 
 
This means that to know if a chemical is harmful, and hence to be able to take the 
appropriate protective measures, tests will have to be done on some species of animals 
(mainly rats, mice, fishes and invertebrates). 
 
In order to balance the increased protection of human health and of the environment with 
animal welfare, do you think? (One single answer) 
- Animal testing is unacceptable for chemicals safety purposes and should stop now 
- Animal testing should be the last resort and used only when alternative tests are not 
available 
- No opinion

Firstly, this question misrepresents the role of CLP hazard criteria and conflates them with 
regulatory information requirements. In reality, the CLP is only a system for applying hazard 
data – not a means to require information to be generated via new testing simply because a 
hazard category exists.  
 
Second, the wording of this question suggests that human health and animal welfare are at 
odds or are mutually exclusive, when in fact, non-animal approaches have been shown to be 
more relevant and can be more protective of human health than animal-based testing. 
 
Third, this question is clearly intended to steer respondents to the second choice: “Animal 
testing should be the last resort and used only when alternative tests are not available” 
Consequently, the European Commission is likely to take this as evidence that the public is in 
favour of increased animal testing for safety. 
 
Fourth, the preamble demonstrates the false belief that animal testing is the only (and in this 
wording, the best) way to “know if a chemical is harmful” even though animal testing has been 
shown to be in general and across toxicities only moderately reflective of human responses and 
do not cover many adversities of concern.   
 
Section 2 – Classification, Question 34: 
To derive the correct classification of certain chemicals, the use of animal testing is still 
necessary. 
Would you be confident to classify (your) products on the basis of alternative methods 
only? 
- In the case the result of a test performed with an alternative method is positive, to 
classify (your) chemicals accordingly: Yes/No 
- In the case the result of a test performed with an alternative method is negative, not to 
classify (your) chemicals for that hazard class: Yes/No 
 
Neither of these choices is appropriate, and in fact the entire concept of this question 
demonstrates a misrepresentation of how non-animal methods are used in chemical safety 
assessment. It is not, nor should it be, the application of a single method to address a 
classification, but generally a combination of methods and other lines of evidence to draw a 
conclusion about a particular biological activity. The subjective nature of this question also fails 
to acknowledge the extensive scientific validation to which modern alternative methods are held, 
compared to the vast majority of animal tests, which are treated as a ‘gold standard’ despite 
having never been formally validated and have proven shortcomings. 
 
Since many of the GHS and CLP classifications are currently defined by the results of animal 
tests rather than by a particular hazard characteristic, again the assumption is that a non-animal 
approach must exactly mimic the outcome of an animal test, rather than providing a comparable 
level of protection. A more appropriate question to address would be how to revise the CLP 
 
 
 
 
 
 
(and GHS) classifications to better reflect the hazard of concern, and that would support 
classification using non-animal approaches.  
This question is clearly intended to further cement the use of animal tests for classification and 
labelling, regardless of whether they provide adequate or even relevant information for human 
and environmental safety.  
 
We consider the texts provided in the consultation unacceptable from a scientific, regulatory 
and ethical point of view. We therefore request that the Commission: 
 
 Disregards these two questions when analysing the results of this consultation 
 Issues a separate consultation regarding the appropriate use of non-animal 
approaches to chemical safety assessment that contains more informed preamble and 
unbiased questions 
 
Regrettably, the language of this public consultation reinforces our perception of a lack ambition 
by the European Commission to implement a 21st century chemicals strategy to maintain a high 
level of human and environmental safety while moving away from outdated animal testing and 
embracing human relevant science. In contrast, we remind the Commission that the US 
Environment Protection Agency has announced the abrogation of the requests for new animal 
tests on mammals by 2035. Surely the EU can at least follow this example, rather than moving 
in the opposite direction? 
 
Look forward to hearing from you.

 
Deployment of alternative fuels infrastructure 
The transport sector requires fundamental changes to be come more sustainable. 
Representing about a quarter of the EU greenhouse gas emissions, it is significantly 
lagging behind other sectors in emissions decline. Cutting emissions of the transport 
sector will be crucial to achieve net-zero. 
Clean Air Task Force (CATF) welcomes the European Commission’s proposal for a 
regulation on the deployment of alternative fuels infrastructure  (AFIR) that aims to 
enable a widespread uptake of low - and zero -emission vehicles in all transport 
modes. It is a major step in the right direction. We believe, however, that the proposal 
can be improved . We are particularly concerned that it promotes unsustainable 
options l ike liquefied natural gas (LNG) over true future -proof zero -carbon 
alternatives. 
 
KEY RECOMMENDATIONS 
• Remove the targets relating to LNG fuelling stations and infrastructure 
for both road transport and shipping 
• Include targets for hydrogen/ammonia bunkering infrastructure 
deployment in the EU core maritime ports 
 
The trouble with LNG 
Environmental and health benefits of LNG compared to conventional fuels  are 
contested in the scientific community.  Studies demonstrated that  the use of LNG 
leads to either limited improvement in GHG emissions or, worse, higher emissions1. 
LNG consists for the most part of methane, a greenhouse gas over 80 t imes more 
damaging than CO 2 in the first two decades after its release. To assess the true 
climate impact of LNG, we have to take into consideration the  methane emissions 
along the entire lifecycle , including upstream emissions from leakage during 
extraction, processing and transport, as well as downstream emissions.  When 
considering the full life cycle, environmental benefits of using LNG instead of 
conventional fuels are either very limited or non-existent2. 
Better alternatives exist 
For road tra nsport, electrification is the best cost -GHG benefit option already 
available. Transport emissions can be effectively reduced by shifting from oil and 
diesel to zero-carbon fuels (ZCF)3, notably hydrogen and ammonia for those sectors 
of transport where electrification is not feasible. 
The deployment of LNG does not bring any benefits that could not be achieved with 
electrification, and later hydrogen, and so the inclusion of LNG targets relating to 
road transport in the proposed regulation is moot.

When it comes to  maritime transport,  ammonia will be key in decarbonising the 
sector, especially for transoceanic voyages, provided that the hydrogen and nitrogen 
used are produced with little to no greenhouse gas. Ammonia could be ready to 
deploy as early as mid-decade and will be the lowest cost zero-carbon option out to 
the 2050s4. 
Waste of public investment 
The deployment of the LNG infrastructure would require major investments – at least 
$3bn and up to $22.2bn 5 – but would  not bring about an improvement in GHG 
emissions. These  valuable resources can therefore be put to  much better use  – 
towards true future-proof zero-carbon alternatives. 
Utilising LNG even as a transitional solution, until other technologies are ready for 
large-scale deployment, would risk engendering a lock-in effect. LNG infrastructure 
has a lifetime of about 25 to 40 years, which would incentivise its use well after zero-
carbon alternatives become available. In another scenario, the investments required 
to develop LNG infrastructure would  not be monetised or could end up in stranded 
assets. As such, deploying LNG would be a waste of public investment for no real 
climate benefits. Conversely, policy interventions can stimulate the demand for zero-
carbon fuels and so aid in their deployment. Additionally, including LNG targets does 
not send the right signals to the market for innovation to take place in th e 
development of zero-carbon fuels infrastructure and equipment.  
Room for improvement 
CATF welcomes the targets for hydrogen r efuelling infrastructure for road vehicles 
in the proposed regulation. We are however strongly opposed to the inclusion of 
LNG. The proposal itself states that LNG infrastructure should only be developed 
“unless the costs are disproportionate to the benefits, including the environmental 
ones”. There is literally no scenario in which this would be the case and so the entire 
category should be removed from the legislation. 
Similarly, on maritime transport, CATF considers that inv estments in LNG 
infrastructure are incompatible with the goal of decarbonising the shipping sector. 
Setting binding targets for LNG in the AFIR could create a lock-in effect and lead to 
gas being used in Europe for decades to come, despite it not representing a notable 
improvement in GHG emissions or even increasing them. 
Finally, FuelEU Maritime, a related legislative proposal aiming to increase the share 
of renewable and low-carbon fuels in the fuel mix of international maritime transport, 
will not work if the infrastructure needed for th ose fuels is not available. Therefore, 
we also urge that AFIR include targets to deploy hydrogen/ammonia bunkering 
infrastructure in the EU core maritime ports.

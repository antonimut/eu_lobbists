EUROPEAN COMMISSION PROPOSAL FOR THE 
ALTERNATIVE FUELS INFRASTRUCTURE 
REGULATION (AFIR) 
The European Automobile Manufacturers’ Association (ACEA) welcomes the 
European Commission’s proposal to amend Directive (2014/94/EU) on the 
deployment of infrastructure for alternative fuels. It is an important step for the roll-out 
of charging and refuelling stations suitable for the operation of low- and zero-
emission light- and heavy-duty vehicles. 
 
PART 1: SPECIFIC POINTS RELEVANT FOR 
LIGHT-DUTY VEHICLES 
GENERAL REMARKS 
• ACEA strongly welcomes the change in form from a Directive to a 
Regulation. This provides much higher legal certainty for both 
manufacturers and customers on future investments in infrastructure, as 
well as on the impact of the enforcement of other legal provisions laid out 
in the ‘Fit for 55’ package.  
• ACEA welcomes the introduction of mandatory targets for member states, 
as well as strengthened monitoring and enforcement measures, as well as 
a robust review process.  
• However, the AFIR proposal does not match the -55% CO2 reduction 
requirement for passenger cars by 2030 proposed under the CO2 
Regulation, and needs to be much more demanding. AFIR should match 
the CO2 targets for all steps to come and both regulations should be 
understood as a package. 
• There are also number of weak assumptions in the proposal, such as very 
optimistic energy consumption of cars, the underestimated share of public 
charging or low installed charging power in 2030.  
• The concentration of on-site charging in cities with limited access to 
charging at homes or offices, as well as measures to stimulate fast power 
charging are not sufficiently addressed. 
• Provisions to take into account the specificities of vans are also missing.  
 
www.acea.auto 2 
• Urban logistics is becoming increasingly important in connection with the 
rise of e-commerce business. Electrified transport is decisive in this field 
and is fundamentally dependent on the availability of a precisely tailored 
charging infrastructure. The category N2 (for larger-volume transport, 
between category N1 and the truck segment) also has a particular part to 
play, given the special demands relating to the amount of cargo space. 
Therefore, specific provisions with respect to the parking space for longer 
vehicles (especially N2) need to be ensured (longer lead and tailored 
space or charging). 
A huge number of commercial vehicles for example will need to be charged 
close to the drivers’ homes, which often do not have access to a charging 
connection. This use case is especially relevant for vehicles deployed in 
the craft trades, service fleets and delivery service providers (courier, 
express and parcel). 
It must be also noted that AFIR covers only publicly available charging points. It must 
be accompanied by an ambitious EPBD proposal to cover the remaining spectrum of 
charging events in private and semi-public/semi-private places. The number of public 
and private charging points must be interlinked; if one area is lagging behind, it must 
be compensated by the other. 
 
KEY ISSUES 
The overall ambition of AFIR is not sufficient to support the actual CO2 targets 
defined in the Fit for 55 package. For instance, based on data from IEA1, the 
ambition of AFIR seems to be even lower than the current AFID requirements.

This is largely due to the underlying assumptions in the AFIR proposal, which lead to 
an underestimation of the overall electric power and the number of charging points 
needed for 2030. Indeed, overall energy demand in 2030 will be significantly higher 
than stated by the Commission in its impact assessment – around 68TWh for battery 
electric vehicles (BEVs) and 18TWh for plug-in hybrid electric vehicles (PHEVs). 
According to ACEA calculations, around 90 and 27 TWh are needed, representing a 
much higher energy demand. 
This can be explained by the following factors: 
• The proportion of the energy delivered through public charging: ACEA 
considers that 60% of energy demand will be delivered through public 
charging. 
• Energy consumption of electrified vehicles: The overall trend for long-range 
BEVs requiring bigger and heavier batteries will lead to a significantly 
higher average energy consumption (0.2 kWh/km). 
• ACEA also estimates that the average power used, both for normal and 
fast charging, will be higher in future.  
 
ACEA PROPOSALS 
The following AFIR provisions should be changed or amended: 
1. The level of power needed for public charging (1kW parameter for BEV and 
0.66kW for PHEV) should be increased to 3kW for BEVs and 2kW for PHEVs. 
This change reflects different assumptions and models on energy used in 
vehicles in the future2, and leads to a significantly higher number of charging 
points required. Indeed, according to ACEA estimates (using the Commission 
calculations in the AFIR impact assessment), around 7 million charging points 
would be required by 2030, depending on the structure of the power 
distributed per charger. Such a build-up of charging points could be perceived 
as reliable and sufficient by customers.  
2. There should an alignment between requirements between TEN-T core and 
comprehensive networks and the implementation speed-up for 2025 and 2030 
for electricity.

3. The overall power installed in the charging points should be higher to raise 
consumer comfort and convenience. Therefore, the Article 3 point 2 should be 
modified and the values proposed for the TEN-T core and comprehensive 
networks doubled, both for 2025 and 2030.  
4. The density parameter needs to be added to sufficiently cover highly-
populated areas, which will have a huge concentration of demand in the 
future.  
5. The definition of publicly accessible charging points must clearly indicate 
unlimited and unconditional access to charging points on a 24/7 basis and 
allowing payment as specified in Article 5, point 2. A dedicated definition of 
semi-public charging is needed to cover charging points with certain access 
limitations. 
6. For hydrogen refuelling stations (compressed and liquified hydrogen), the 
maximum distance should be 100km instead of 150km, and targets should 
apply as of 2027 instead of 2030 on the TEN-T core and comprehensive 
network. This will be necessary to avoid long queuing times for customers.  
7. Incentives to stimulate fast charging development: It is clear that the higher 
share and power of fast chargers will lower the total amount of charging points 
that will be needed, helping member states to meet the proposed targets. 
Therefore, member states should be incentivised to invest in higher-power fast 
chargers to allow higher average power used.  
8. Standardisation and roaming must be supported within the proposal as well.  
9. The implementation on the national level will be based on the national policy 
frameworks. The speed of their implementation is a major concern for the 
industry, which calls for these plans to be assessed and notified by the 
Commission as quickly as possible and for the implementation dates to be 
shortened. Therefore, ACEA suggests relevant changes in the Articles 13 and 
15: 
Article 15, para 1 should read as follows: “By 1 January 2026 2025, the 
Commission shall assess the national policy framework notified…”.  
Consequently, Article 13, para 9 should read as follows: “By 1 January 2025 1 
July 2024, each Member State shall notify to the Commission its final national 
policy framework”.

PART 2: SPECIFIC POINTS RELEVANT FOR 
HEAVY-DUTY VEHICLES 
GENERAL REMARKS 
• ACEA has emphasised before that a dense network of charging and 
refuelling stations specifically dedicated to and respectful of the needs of 
heavy-duty vehicles is one of the indispensable preconditions for the 
transition of the road transport sector to carbon neutrality3. Without a 
sufficiently dense infrastructure network in place, transport operators will 
simply not invest in and start to transition to the new powertrain vehicles. 
Initiating the infrastructure roll-out for new powertrain technologies, namely 
battery electric and hydrogen‐powered vehicles as the key zero‐emission 
technologies, is therefore of utmost importance and urgency. 
• Commercial vehicle manufacturers are fully committed to the 
decarbonisation of the road transport sector and are rapidly ramping up the 
production of low- and zero-emission vehicles. According to industry’s (pre-
Green Deal) assessments, already by 2025 approximately 40,000 battery 
electric medium‐ and heavy‐duty vehicles will be in operation in Europe 
(EU27 + UK). By 2030 this figure will increase to approximately 270,000. 
The first hydrogen fuel‐cell electric vehicles are already being rolled out in 
Europe; from mid‐decade the offerings will increase significantly, with at 
least 60,000 hydrogen-powered trucks expected to be in operation by 
2030. In that respect, industry’s expectations of the ramp-up of zero-
emission vehicles significantly exceed the Commission’s expectations. The 
assumptions made in the Impact Assessment are not in line with other 
regulations, such as the HDV CO2 standards regulation ((EU) 2019/1242 
and the Commission’s proposal to amend the Effort Sharing Regulation 
(COM (2021) 555 final, 2021/0200 (COD)), and will likely fail to deliver the 
necessary emission reductions. 
• ACEA acknowledges that the AFIR shall (only) ensure the availability of a 
minimum infrastructure network across member states which allows the 
seamless and frictionless operation of vehicles. There is no doubt that 
medium- and long-term, the provision and operation of charging and 
refuelling stations will have to become a ‘business case’ just as the 
operation of conventional gas stations is today. However, ACEA would like

to highlight again the close correlation between available charging and 
refuelling opportunities and the market uptake of low- and zero-emission 
vehicles. Especially with respect to commercial transport operations, no 
operator will invest in any low- or zero-emission vehicle unless they can be 
sure that they are able to actually operate them according to their needs, 
which means that they are able to recharge or refuel them. Policymakers 
on European, national, regional and local levels play a pivotal role in kick-
starting the deployment of – not yet profitable – charging and refuelling 
stations. At least until the end of this decade, it is very likely that the level 
of available infrastructure will effectively determine (or rather limit) the 
number of low- and zero-emission vehicles that can be operated in Europe. 
In view of this, policymakers need to acknowledge that setting the AFIR 
targets now will effectively determine the CO2 emission reductions that can 
be expected from road freight and public road transport by the end of this 
decade. If they are set too low, vehicle manufacturers and the road freight 
and public road transport sector as a whole may well be severely 
constrained in their capabilities to contribute to the required emission 
reductions. 
• Despite the fact that the provision and operation of such a charging and 
refuelling network is not at the core of any manufacturer’s business, 
several manufacturers have publicly committed to supporting the 
infrastructure deployment by significant own investments (eg joint venture 
of TRATON Group, Daimler Trucks, Volvo Group4) to install and operate a 
high-performance public charging network for battery electric heavy-duty 
long-haul trucks and coaches across Europe; collaboration agreement of 
Nikola, Iveco and OGE5  to develop hydrogen fueling solutions, 
H2Accelerate – currently a collaboration of Daimler Truck, IVECO, Volvo 
Group, OMV, Shell, Total Energies and Linde6). The intention of all of 
these (and similar) commitments is to help address investment and 
operation risks which delay the market uptake of low- and zero-emission 
vehicles.  
• ACEA calls for all energy carriers and fuels used in road transport to be 
decarbonised quickly, not the least to ensure that the current vehicle fleet 
effectively contributes to the necessary emission reductions. In that 
respect, ACEA would welcome a clear statement from the European 
Commission on the development trajectory of non-fossil energy carriers

SPECIFIC ELEMENTS OF THE PROPOSAL WITH 
RESPECT TO HEAVY-DUTY VEHICLES 
1. Subject Matter (Article 1) 
• ACEA supports the European Commission’s ambition to enable a fast 
green transition by revising Directive 2014/94/EU of the European 
Parliament and of the Council on the deployment of alternative fuels 
infrastructure, and to make it a regulation so that the national targets for 
alternative fuel infrastructure in the Union will become mandatory. A 
prerequisite for achieving the set goals is furthermore that the various 
regulations, such as AFIR and state aid regulations (GBER), are consistent 
and well aligned. One example of the current lack of consistency is the 
definition of ‘publicly accessible’, where the definition in GEBR Article 36a 
is not aligned with the current AFIR proposal. 
2. Definitions (Article 2) 
• (38) ‘publicly accessible’ 
• ACEA appreciates the broader definition of ‘publicly accessible’. The 
proposed definition should ensure that an efficient, sustainable, and 
publicly accessible infrastructure for electric heavy-duty vehicles is now 
possible to establish. Again, coherent definitions with eg GBER is of 
utmost importance. ACEA suggests to amend the definition as follows: 
“‘Publicly accessible’ alternative fuels infrastructure, means an 
alternative fuels infrastructure which is located at a site or premise that 
is open to the general public, irrespective of whether the alternative 
fuels infrastructure is located on public or on private property, 
irrespective of limitations or conditions applying in terms of access to

3. Targets for electric recharging infrastructure dedicated to heavy-duty 
vehicles (Article 4) 
• ACEA fully supports the proposal in that it sets dedicated targets for a 
recharging infrastructure for heavy-duty vehicles. The technical 
specifications and requirements of battery electric heavy-duty vehicles are 
distinctly different to the infrastructure suitable for light-duty vehicles. This 
has to be considered with respect to locations of charging station, space 
requirements and most importantly required power levels. 
• In terms of total power output per recharging pool and power output per 
recharging station the proposal is insufficient to meet the rapidly growing 
number of electric heavy-duty vehicles that will become available already in 
the timeframe until 2025. The requirements for recharging pools/stations 
and the total power output should therefore be increased and the targets 
set earlier as explained below. 
• It is strongly advisable that the distance targets are understood as an 
average distance on the road network and that the exact locations shall be 
determined by local and/or regional conditions. While a sufficiently dense 
network will be required across Europe, it is reasonable to take local and 
regional conditions into account, especially in remote areas, to allow some 
flexibility with respect to the required distance between recharging pools. 
• On the TEN-T-core network (Art. 4.1 (a))  
• No later than 1 July* 20258, the recharging infrastructure shall offer at 
least 5,000 kW total power output per recharging pool. Each recharging 
pool shall include at least four recharging stations with an individual 
power output of at least 350 kW and four recharging stations with an 
individual power output of at least 800 kW9. 
• No later than 1 July 2030, the recharging infrastructure shall offer at 
least 6,500 kW total power output per recharging pool. Each recharging 
pool shall include at least four recharging stations with an individual 
power output capable of at least 1,200 kW.

• On the TEN-T comprehensive network (Art. 4.1 (b)) 
• No later than 1 July 2027, recharging pools shall be built every 100 km 
along the entire TEN-T comprehensive network. Each recharging pool 
shall offer at least 1,400 kW total power output. Each recharging pool

• No later than 1 July 2030, the recharging infrastructure shall offer at 
least 3,000 kW total power output per recharging pool. Each recharging 
pool shall include at least two recharging stations with a power output 
capable of at least 800 kW.  
• No later than 1 July 2035, the recharging infrastructure shall offer at 
least 5,000 kW total power output per recharging pool. Each recharging 
pool shall include at least two recharging stations with an individual 
power output capable of at least 1,200 kW.  
• Safe and secure truck parking areas (Art. 4.1) 
• The provision to equip safe and secure truck parking areas with 
recharging stations is welcome and necessary. However, the 
requirements should be increased to a minimum of at least four 
recharging stations, instead of just one, and already be implemented by 
2025, instead of 2030. 
• The Commission’s proposal would result in only 1,000 overnight 
chargers in 2030. ACEA recalls that battery electric trucks in long-haul 
operations will also heavily rely on overnight charging during their 
missions. The AFIR should therefore set targets for deploying public 
overnight chargers (100 kW) at truck parking areas along the highways, 
with at least 40,000 overnight public chargers available no later than 
2030. 
• Urban nodes (Art. 4.1 (e)) 
• The requirements for urban nodes should be increased so that by 1 
July 2030 at least 1,600 kW of total power output is deployed at each 
such location. Every recharging station shall provide at least 150 kW 
power output and there shall be at least two recharging stations with an 
individual power output of at least 350 kW. 
4. Recharging infrastructure (Art 5) 
• Charging in a ‘price per minute’ mode reduces transparency and 
comparability as it is not possible to calculate the recharging cost in 
 
www.acea.auto 10 
advance. How much energy an electric vehicle can receive during a certain 
period of time depends on several factors, eg outdoor temperature, battery 
temperature, battery state-of-charge etc. ACEA therefore suggests that 
operators of recharging points should not apply price per minute for ad hoc 
recharging as it cannot be considered clearly comparable as required in 
Article 5 (4). 
5. Targets for hydrogen refuelling infrastructure of road vehicles (Art 6) 
• Hydrogen-powered vehicles are not yet at the same development stage as 
battery-electric trucks and buses. However, the first fuel-cell electric trucks 
are already being rolled out already and will be followed by a wider offering 
from different manufacturers from 2024 onwards. It is expected that they 
will gain momentum, especially with a focus on heavy-duty vehicles with 
longer ranges and higher payloads. Following a conservative ramp-up 
trajectory it is expected that at least 60,000 fuel-cell electric vehicles will be 
in operation in Europe by the end of the decade. 
• In order to facilitate the roll-out of hydrogen-powered vehicles, the AFIR 
requirements should be set earlier than 2030. Moreover, this Regulation 
should properly consider the specific requirements of hydrogen-powered 
heavy-duty vehicles and their required refuelling infrastructure. ACEA 
proposes to set an intermediate target for 2025 for heavy-duty vehicles. 
The distance target should be understood as an average target for each 
direction with the exact location to be determined by local conditions. Also, 
the capacity requirement of 2t per day and per hydrogen refuelling station 
should be an average capacity to enable a combination of larger and 
smaller hydrogen refuelling stations in relation to local needs. 
• Liquid hydrogen shall be made available at publicly accessible hydrogen 
refuelling stations with a maximum distance of 300 km in between them by 
1 January 2027. 
6. Targets for LNG and CNG of road transport vehicles (Art 8) 
• In order to support efficient long‐haul and inter-regional transport 
operations, and to serve the growing number of low‐emission vehicles in 
Europe that can be powered by renewable and low- and zero-carbon fuels, 
a more comprehensive network of filling stations for compressed natural 
gas (CNG) and liquified natural gas (LNG) is needed as soon as possible. 
Member states shall ensure as soon as possible and until 1 July 2030 that 
an appropriate number of publicly accessible refuelling points are put in 
place to refuel alternatively-powered vehicles with low- and zero-carbon 
renewable in all member states.

7. Data provisions (Art 18) 
• ACEA considers that the information on public recharging and refuelling 
points (where relevant) should be supplemented with: 
• Price for parking (in addition to the recharging/ refuelling cost) 
• Time limit for parking 
• Payment (how to start the recharging/refuelling and available payment 
methods, key, app, RFID, credit card, etc) 
• Will there be a fixed connector for the recharging points or shall the 
user bring their own 
• Is a recharging point bookable 
• For recharging points: voltage (V) and possibly maximum current (A) 
• Heavy-duty vehicle accessibility: height, length and width capacity of 
recharging station 
• Are weather protections and other service functions available 
 
8. Common technical specifications (Art. 19 (7)) 
• It is important to update the technical specifications in Annex II according to 
the megawatt charging systems standards that are under development and 
expected to be ready by the end of 2024 at the latest.

Confindustria’s Position on the EU Commission 
proposal for a Directive (COM 2021/557 final) 
amending Directive (EU) 2018/2001 of the 
European Parliament and of the Council, as 
regards the promotion of energy from renewable 
sources 
 
 
September 2021 
 
 3 
Confindustria supports a comprehensive revision of the EU framework for renewables which 
should (i) lead to a more ambitious RES target for 2030, in line with the -55% GHG reduction target, 
(ii) strengthen the enabling conditions for the integration of renewables across all sectors, (iii) 
embrace the principle of sector integration, and (iv) harmonize RES policies with other regulatory 
instruments such as the ETS, the Effort sharing, the energy taxation, the State Aid guidelines 
(EEAG) and the measures promoting decarbonization in transport, as well as hydrogen and gas 
sectors. We welcome the introduction of sectoral targets for industry, construction and transport 
in order to reach the 40% RES target, but it will be necessary to facilitate the process of 
decarbonizing hard-to-abate sectors. 
 
It is important to provide regulatory stability as a fundament al prerequisite for mobilizing 
investment as uncertainties in policies increase the risks to the implementation of low -carbon 
projects. Any legislative changes should increase certainty for investors and not introduce 
disruptive elements to the long-term investment perspective provided by the current RED II. 
 
The achievement of the new 2030 ambition set out in the Directive proposal will require a 
fundamental acceleration of the  administrative procedures that still hinder the development of 
green energy projects, resulting in a higher risk for project delays and additional project 
development costs. The directive should be revised to have a further committed support from the 
EU and Member States in removing barriers in authorization processes. In this regard, it is pivotal 
the removal of permitting bottlenecks at national level, defining fast track processes aiming at (i) 
significantly decreasing permitting issuance times, (ii) providing certainty of 
permitting/administrative times and (iii) fosteri ng efficiency of authorization processes. Faster 
permitting procedures should facilitate even more repowering and revamping projects. The 
revised directive could raise its ambition on this point, as the existing plants can make the 
difference in achieving the 40% RES target while limiting the new soil consumption. The 
unprecedented efforts in planning, authorization and development of RES and grid investments 
required by the ecological transition must rely on an adequate legislative framework at EU level 
which should support the instruments implemented pursuant to the Recovery and Resilience Plan 
at national level. We believe the definition of a minimum set of clear and general rules would 
speed up the transposition process at national level and ease the ex -post monitoring of the 
measures adopted by Member States from the EC. In this regard, we consider necessary to 
integrate the RED III with obligations for MSs to provide clear indications at national level on the 
eligible areas for the installation of RES p lants, and on the conditions to be respected by RES 
investors for such instalment, in order to foresee simplified permitting procedure in those areas. 
It is important that the areas identified as "suitable" are as large as possible, and without stringent 
limits to the installable power, both (a) to enable operators to support the country's energy 
transition objectives in an effective  and efficient manner  as well as  according to principles of 
market competitiveness, and (b) to safeguard the development alrea dy underway, taking into 
account the expected updating of the PNIEC targets and the more ambitious objectives for 2050. 
Fast procedures should be introduced especially in specific cases, for instance for abandoned 
industrial areas, degraded agricultural areas, quarries and exhausted landfills. Together with those 
indications, Member States should also set benefits – in terms of real simplification of 
authorization procedures and of RES support mechanisms integrated with locational price signals 
– which effectively incentivize RES investors in locating the installations in those areas. European 
 4 
Commission should also supervise the application Clean Energy Package standards through the 
establishment of an annual benchmark on authorization processes, with the a im of sharing best 
practices at Community level. A specific Annex could be added to the Directive in order to illustrate 
these best practices. Simplification of permitting processes is key not only for RES, but also for the 
related grid infrastructure proj ects. For grid investments , which are essential for the ecological 
transition, the EC should integrate the RED III and other linked initiatives with obligations for MSs 
to provide fast -track procedures. Besides shortening maximum timings for those processes, it is 
necessary to fores ee measures that guarantee the effective respect of these envisaged timings 
(e.g., silent consent provision, promotion of the dialogue between the different parties involved 
in the authorization process). Permitting processes must be aligned with the EU de carbonization 
targets both in terms of issuance times and capacity expectations. A simplification of the 
authorization process would also be appropriate for the construction and operation of 
electrolysers and all the related infrastructures, making the process as clear as possible depending 
on the different configurations. Specifically, it will be appropriate to take into account, on the one 
hand, the average reference sizes of the electrolysers (according to the current and prospective 
technology development) and, on the other hand, the different possible configurations. 
 
In the power sector, support schemes for renewables should be designed in order not to distort 
wholesale markets. It will be necessary to explicitly allow Member States to adopt a market design 
potentially very different from the existing one, which was elaborated on the peculiarities of fossil 
fuels. EU energy Regulation should fully support market mechanisms providing long term price 
signals, which are necessary to promote capital intensive investments not only in RES, but also in 
assets, such as storage systems, that are essential for managing the system safely and reducing 
the risks of RES over generation and which would not be made if investors had to rely only on spot 
market price signals. RES support schemes should be improved with additional price signals aimed 
at targeting the geographical development of RES – including offshore RES – consistently with the 
local grid potentialities, so as to optimize transmission and distribution gri d management and 
developments.  In this respect, measures to address risks (State-backed guarantees) for PPAs could 
be arranged as part of programs to promote renewables penetration in industry. Guarantees are 
one of the main barriers for the development o f PPAs, the costs of which are particularly 
accentuated due to the time extension typical of PPAs and the potential range of price evolution 
and thus the "value" of the contracts themselves over a long -term horizon.  An effective solution 
consists in the i ntroduction of a centralized clearing management mechanism, in the hands of a 
neutral central counterparty which would be entrusted with the tasks of: (i) responding directly to 
the fulfillment of each operator; (ii) manage the guarantee s system; (iii) ove rsee the correct 
implementation of payment and delivery procedures. 
 
With regards to the gas sector, we welcome the extension to all sectors of the Guarantee of Origin 
(GO) for renewable gases, but we note the absence of both specific targets for renewable gases, 
which should be fixed taking into account the incremental availability of bio and renewable 
products related to specific incentives and supporting measures for the production, and regulatory 
links between GOs and the EU ETS. Besides  incrementing the use of renewable sources and 
carriers, the effort should also be focused on addressing the GHG emissions and providing a level-
playing field for low carbon gases. In fact, despite renewable gases des erve a stronger support, 
decarbonized gases can also play a role  due to their decarbonization potential. We underline the 
 5 
necessity to support the development of corporate long -term contracts for renewable energies 
other than electricity, such as biomethane or hydrogen. Regarding H2, the revision of the Directive 
could make the definition of renewable hydrogen more explicit and the requirements on 
additionality and spatial and temporal correlation for grid hydrogen more clear (delegated act). 
Those requirements are crucial to enable the hydrogen market uptake . Furthermore, we suggest 
leaving some space to the existing renewable plants, at least in an transitional phase. Finally, while 
generally agreeing with the need to promote clean hydrogen, for a more pre cise assessment of 
the minimum level of GHG saving , the calculation methodology used by the Commission  should 
be made public. 
 
In the H&C sector, the adoption of renewable energy in district heating and cooling can have a big 
impact in reducing GHG emission, especially in some urban areas. Therefore, the indicative target 
should be an annual average increase  of at least 2 ppt . For H&C sector , the Directive should 
promote the use of all the different solutions provided by the production pathways of bio and 
renewable gases, promoting the development and the penetration of these products, given also 
the possibility to directly implement bio and renewable gases in the existing infrastructure. 
 
The revision of sustainability criteria for biomass comes before they have even been fully 
transposed into national law, creating uncertainty in the market and delaying investments. The 
cascading use of biomass is a further measure to ensure sustainability, but we see difficulties in its 
implementation, with potential distortion effects. We remind that the 20MW exemption 
threshold is coherent with the EU ETS and adequate to cover 75% of the biomass used. Loweri ng 
the threshold to 5MW has a significant impact on small actors, with limited administrative capacity 
to prove compliance with the requirements.  
 
In the transport sector, all clean fuels shall be promoted, also through an obligation for the fuel 
suppliers in accordance with the availability of the products and the degree of development of the 
production chains. Electric mobility has a key role in the achievement of carbon neutrality by2050 
and the credit mechanism introduced by the proposal seems to go in the right direction as it eases 
the participation of supplying renewable electricity operators. We support the proposal contained 
in the revision of RED II to focus on the carbon intensity of fuels, eliminating the current 
coexistence of two overlapping mandates deriving from the obligations contained in the RED and 
the FQD. With the aim to achieve a very ambitious decarbonization of transport, the use of all 
renewable and low carbon fuels which meet the sustainability criteria  should be promoted . We 
also consider essential to swiftly extend the Annex IX of the Directive; eliminate the cap on raw 
materials in Part B of Annex IX (since the EU's centralized controls are certainly able to avoid 
misconduct on the part of operators) ; explicitly clarify that ther e is no ILUC risk for biofuels 
produced from residues and waste ; and remove the provision that attributes to non -advanced 
residues usable in the "food or feed market" the same GHG emissions applicable to their closest 
substitutes (Annex V) . The methodologies on the recognition of GHG Saving of recycled carbon 
fuels (RCF) and biofuels produced in co -processing should also be defined as soon as possible . If 
RCFs are produced from streams of liquid or solid waste of non -renewable origin that are not 
suitable for material recovery, the emission savings calculation methodology should consider GHG 
emissions reduction compared to applicable traditional treatment or disposal alternatives.

Confindustria’s Position on EU Commission proposal 
for a Regulation (COM 2021/556) amending the 
Regulation 2019/631 on CO2 emission performance 
standards for new passenger cars and new light 
commercial vehicles 
 
 
September 2021 
 
 7 
Confindustria welcomes the EU climate and environmental protection policy initiatives that foster 
the industrial competitiveness while reaching the 2050 climate neutral target. As for all  EU 
regulations, the proposed review of the CO2 fleet targets for cars and vans should comply with the 
‘Better Regulation’ guidelines to ensure that its provisions are based on and supported by sound 
scientific evidence . Furthermore, it should respect the principle of technological neutrality, as 
specified by the European Commission in its “Sustainable and Smart Mobility Strategy”. Despite in 
the last few years the EU Commission has been proposing a clear shift to electromobility and 
numerous car manufacturers are already investing to transform their productions, the new 
regulation should not indirectly lead to the ban of any technology. Electromobility - with 
renewable electricity and sustainable production of vehicles and batteries - will surely be pivotal 
to deliver the Green Deal ambitions, but it will take years to be fully accepted by customers, 
especially for specific market segments and for Light Commercial Vehicles.  
 
Moreover, CO 2 standards focused on tailpipe emissions fail to account the full environmental 
footprint of different powertrain technologies, considering only tank to wheel emissions (TTW). 
Without considering Well to tank emissions (WTT) for energy/fuels and the benefi ts in reducing 
CO2 emissions enabled by renewable and zero‐and‐low‐carbon fuels, as a complement to 
electrification, this approach distorts the competition both between powertrain technologies and 
between energy vectors. Furthermore, the new  proposal risks  to block all the investments in 
research and development of new production processes capable of decarbonizing existing fuels 
and, in particular, it eliminates any prospect of developing LCLF (differently from the proposal for 
a revision of the directive o n the promotion of renewable sources, which introduces increasing 
obligations, and the proposed new energy taxation directive, which provides a fiscal incentive for 
renewable and low-carbon fuels). In fact, the proposed definition of zero -emission vehicles itself 
is not consistent with a life-cycle emissions assessment.  
 
The CO2 standards could provide the critical boost for the uptake of renewable and low -carbon 
energy in transport, bringing together efforts of all obligated parties , from fuel suppliers to car 
manufacturer, for example via a crediting system which gives the right incentive for the uptake of 
better fuels . More ambitious CO 2 targets shall be linked to an adequate and proportionally 
distributed recharging infrastructure across all EU: this requirement, which should be mandatory 
for all EU Member States, is a key pre‐requisite for achieving 2050 climate neutrality target and 
the widespread deployment of new zero‐and‐low emission technologies. 
 
Finally, Confindustria recommends considering the social and industrial impacts of the proposal . 
Any reviewed CO2 target, including those beyond 2030, should be based on a detailed assessment 
of the potential impact on industr y and employment  along the entire automotive value ch ain. 
Particular attention should be placed also on Small Volume Manufacturers (SVMs) by keeping the 
current derogation and exemption scheme unchanged.

for Regulation (COM 2021/559) repealing the 
Directive 2014/94/EU on EU’s refueling/recharging 
infrastructure improvement 
 
 
September 2021 
 
 9 
Confindustria approves the EU Commission’s proposal setting binding targets for Member States 
concerning the deployment of recharging infrastructure (art. 3&4), agreeing that a critical effort is 
needed to enable electromobility (charging infrastructure is still limited: 220,000 charging points 
in EU - less than 15% fast charging - and more than 75% of them in just 4 Countries - DE, FR, NL, 
UK). Overall, the “ transposition” of the Directive into a Regulation will surely help ensuring the 
implementation and the enforcement of the new targets by all Member States. Likewise, we agree 
with the provision of binding targets regarding the use of electricity by ships stopping in port (cold 
ironing technology) as well as for the supply of electricity at the airport (art.9;10&12). We also 
agree with the proposal  to provide regular reports from each Member State to updating the 
implementation of their policy frameworks and the achievement of national targets and 
objectives. Such reporting will be crucial for the NRAs and the Commission to monitor the MSs 
efforts in achieving the targets set out in the Regulation and to request corrective actions when 
needed. 
 
Regarding the development of the recharging infrastructure for EVs, we welcome the effort 
towards greater harmonization and interoperability of recharging stations across the EU. It is 
important that the Regulation identifies Electric Vehicles charging at private charging points, as a 
provider of "services" and not a provider of electricity . We welcome the requirement that  all 
charging infrastructures must be equipped with smart charging technology, but this should  not 
be applied retroactively (art. 5.8). It is also welcomed the prescription of mandatory 
communication and physical interfaces standards developed by official sta ndardization 
organizations for all the charging points (art. 17). Finally, the Regulation should also focus on 
addressing VAT legislation on e -mobility, which is currently not mentioned in Art. 5. A diverging 
interpretation across MS on VAT treatment of EV charging services with regards to cross -border 
transactions is a significant problem that needs to be addressed. A harmonized VAT treatment at 
EU level is therefore necessary to create a predictable framework for investors. 
 
Furthermore, regarding the payment methods that should be used by operators of the charging 
service (Art. 5.2), it is important to stress that the use of technologies and payment systems made 
available to end customers, should be provided without any distinctions based  on the charging 
infrastructure capacity. In particular, it would be necessary to foresee also in the case of charging 
infrastructures with a power higher than 50 kW, the payment trough virtual pos, (currently 
foreseen only for infrastructures with a lower power  than 50  kw) which would allow the end 
customer to pay without a physical card, but through the simple and functional scanning of a Quick 
Response Code. 
 
Finally, it should be noted that with regard to the definition of the charging price (by the CPO and 
the MSP) this should be left to the free choice of market operators, since recharging is a competing 
service as defined by DAFI itself (Article 5.4 & 5.6). 
 
Nevertheless, in line with the  principle of technological neutrality , refueling and recharging 
infrastructure planning should be designed to promote vehicles using low or zero Well -To-Tank 
emissions fuels such as biofuels, hydrogen and synthetic fuels, along with – in the short and 
medium term - compressed and liquified natural gas, especially in hard to abate transport where 
 10 
direct electrification of transports is not possible. Knowing that the EU Commission impact 
assessment focuses almost all infrastructural investments (around 82 billion euros between 2021-
2030 and 373 billion euros between 2031 and 2050) on EV’s recharging points and stations, we 
believe that there is a significant potential for gaseous or liquid fuels with high renewable content 
and low carbon intensity to reduce the emissions of the existing vehicles fleet if their contribution 
is acknowledged and supported by an adequate infrastructure. As an example, the current 
proposal considers gaseous fuels as an option just during the transition period, without taking into 
account the act ual time needed to exploit the renewable alternatives overall contribution in 
transport decarbonization. In this regard, we believe it is useful to set not just generic but specific 
targets, with an adequate timeframe, for the deployment of CNG - LNG refueling stations.

Confindustria’s Position on the EU Commission 
proposal for a Regulation (COM 2021/562) on the 
use of renewable and low-carbon fuels in 
maritime transport 
 
 
September 2021 
 
 12 
The proposals regarding the use of sustainable and low carbon fuels in maritime (COM 2021/562) 
transport should promote the massive deployment of alternative and renewable fuels in a 
technologically neutral regulatory framework.  
 
Given the wide payback period  from the investments and burden, a simple, clear and stable 
regulatory framework is required in the long term. It will also be necessary to introduce support 
systems with economic incentives trough subsidies for research and industrialization. This is even 
more necessary in relation to the strong crisis affecting the port sector following the pandemic, a 
crisis that is expected to continue in the coming years in the cruise sector even after the healt h 
emergency has been overcome.  The whole Fit -For-55 package should be addressed coherently, 
avoiding the creation of a legislative framework which risks blocking investments in both 
traditional refining and biorefineries plants, jeopardizing the possibilit y of producing clean fuels 
for the decarbonization of the maritime sector. 
 
Confindustria also calls for a strengthen premium on renewable marine fuels, well beyond the 20% 
introduced with the multiplier of the RED, favoring the use in the initial stages. In this context it is 
a critical issue the decision not to confirm the multiplier in the proposal for a new RED directive. 
The development of the Sustainable Maritime Fuels (SMF) requires the introduction of measures 
to stimulate their research, industrialization and use, enabling their economic viability in line with 
the fossil alternative. 
 
We believe SMF may take the form of biofuels, renewable ammonia and methanol, hydrogen, e -
fuel or nonbiological origin fuels as well as LNG and Bio -LNG (LNG is the most mature because 
already available). We support the recognition of LNG as an alternative fuel and the affirmation of 
the principle of technological neutrality in the definition of the objectives of the regulation, but it 
should be noted that the emission evaluation parameters, contained in Annex II, place LNG almost 
on a par with the most polluting fossil fuels, undermining the attractiveness of investment in this 
technology. The impact of using the Well -to-Wake approach in setting emission reduction 
thresholds and targets in the sector should therefore be carefully assessed . Finally, we welcome 
the introduction of the obligation to use electricity supply systems for ships mooring in port (cold 
ironing), but we hope measures to support the adaptation of ships . However, we point out that 
for long-haul navigation, the approach should reward fuels ac cording to the improvement which 
they can bring to the containment of GHG emissions. It is therefore important to encourage long-
term investment in the use of clean fuels, such as renewable hydrogen and ammonia.

The proposals regarding the use of sustainable and low carbon fuels in air (COM 2021/561) 
transport should promote the massive deployment of alternative and renewable fuels in a 
technologically neutral regulatory framework.  
 
Considering the wideness of investments payback period and burden, a simple, clear and stable 
regulatory framework is required in the long term. It will also be necessary to introduce support 
systems with economic incentives both trough subsidies for resea rch and industrialization and 
differentiated levels of taxation between fossil products and renewable products. This is even 
more necessary in relation to the strong crisis affecting the airport sector following the pandemic, 
a crisis that is expected to continue in the coming years even after the health emergency has been 
overcome. The whole Fit-For-55 package should be addressed coherently, avoiding the creation 
of a legislative framework which risks blocking investments in both traditional refining and 
biorefineries plants, jeopardizing the opportunity to produce clean fuels for the decarbonization 
of the air transport sector. 
 
Confindustria also hopes for a strengthen premium on the renewable fuels for aviation, well 
beyond the 20% introduced with the multiplier of the RED, favoring the use in the initial stages. In 
this context it is a critical issue the decision not to confirm the multiplier in the proposal for a new 
RED directive. In addition, the proposal sets increasing limits to the SAF targets with  sub-targets 
for renewable fuels of non -biological origin – RFNBOs. The development of SAF requires the 
introduction of measures to stimulate their research, industrialization  and use, enabling their 
economic sustainability in line with the fossil alternative. 
 
The use of Sustainable Aviation Fuels (SAF), despite a current low consumption, will be essential 
to achieve the air transport sector's climate targets also because these products have now reached 
a level of technological maturity that makes them perfectly compatible with current aircraft 
engines. In order to increase the availability of green raw materials  for air transport, we suggest 
extending the definition of SAF to all sustainable biofuels with the exception of those considered 
to be at high indirect land use change (High ILUC) risk. A “Book & Claim” mechanism could also be 
introduced, flanked by a system of certificates, between fuel suppliers and airlines, useful f or 
overcoming the logistical/operational difficulties that could affect some airports and critical issues 
resulting from the end of the transitional period currently provided for by the proposal. We also 
believe that hydrogen will certainly be a valuable complement to the aviation decarbonization. 
 
EU policies, to promote their rapid deployment, must be included in the international legislation 
established by the ICAO, given the global dimension of air transport, without such harmonization 
there is a high r isk of market distortion with serious damage for the competitiveness of the 
European airline industry

From a broader perspective, the proposal to update the European Directive on energy efficiency 
seems to meet the main objective  of fostering the achievement of energy savings, while 
stimulating technological innovation, a widespread use of renewable sources, and social policies 
to combat energy poverty.  
The Energy Efficiency First principle remains one of the main pillars of the EU strategy to combat 
climate change and to deliver an effective and sustainable energy transition. Energy efficiency is 
one of the most cost -effective ways to achieve the EU's 20 50 climate neutrality target, ensuring 
security of supply and improving competitiveness. More specifically: 
▪ The new European target of reducing energy consumption by at least 9% in 2030 compared to 
the 2020 baseline Reference Scenario is welcome and seems to be in line with the broader 
GHG reduction target of 55%. However, the new baseline must not constitute a penalizing 
element for those Member States (MS) that have so far invested heavily in energy efficiency 
policies.  In addition, the mandatory nature of the target will drive Member States to put in 
place all the actions to fill the existing gap with respect to the current target. 
▪ The harmonized formula for calculating national contributions is a positive aspect of the EC 
proposal as it would provide greater transparency and feasibility, while allowing for easier 
monitoring of intermediate progress  and thus enabling the timely adoption  of corrective 
actions. In addition, the adoption of a n harmonized formula guarantees a level playing field 
among Member States and consistency with other provisions of the Fit for 55 Package (e.g. the 
revision of REDII and the Governance Regulation). 
▪ The increase in the annual energy saving target needs  to be carefully evaluated to avoid that 
it leads to potential market distortions generated by a mix of obligations and alternative 
measures implemented by M ember States. Furthermore, the increase in the energy saving 
target would entail a greater burden for the operators of those MSs that have already adopted 
obligation mechanisms. Conversely, to contribute to a just and inclusive transition , we agree 
with the plan to direct a part of energy savings to vulnerable customers or those in en ergy 
poverty.  
▪ In order to encourage Member States to invest their resources only in  "future-proof, 
sustainable technologies", the proposed revision of the Energy Efficiency Directive provides 
that from 1 January 2024 energy savings resulting from measures involving the direct use of 
fossil fuels will no longer be counted for meeting energy efficiency obligations. This could lead 
to the exclusion of energy efficiency interventions that involve the use of efficient technologies 
- such as cogeneration powered by natural gas  - from the recognition of Energy Efficiency 
Certificates (the Italian certificates are called “ Titoli di Efficienza Energetica -TEE”). In this 
regard, it is not clear why, if there are still margins of efficiency in the sectors in which fossil 
fuels are used, these savings cannot be realized and thus those “low-hanging fruits” should be 
wasted. However, this aspect, if confirmed, would make significantly harder for the EU to meet 
2030 targets. In fact, it would reduce incentives for MS that rely on coal and oil to take 
measures to switch away from those fuels. Therefore, it could significantly prolong the lifetime 
of those assets, in particular , where alternatives are not cost -effective. Moreover, it remains 
unclear how policy measures promoting renewable and low -carbon gaseous fuels in 
combination with direct gas combustion would be qualified. 
 17 
▪ Considering the huge energy consumption related to the Public Administration (PA) as well as 
the role model the latter must assume towards other  consumers, the specific targets 
attributed to the Public Administration are commendable, including those relating to the 
renewal of the building stock. At the same time, however, it is necessary to take into account 
the specific characteristics of the building heritage of the PA which, in the specific national 
case, is made up of historic buildings.  
▪ Compared to the current energy efficiency directive, the new proposal develops a particular 
focus on the issue of energy poverty: customers in a state of economic difficulty are identified 
as a priority target for energy efficiency interventions. More specificall y, it is envisaged that a 
share of the national energy efficiency target will be achieved through interventions that 
target customers in a state of economic difficulty. Although in principle, as stated before, 
directing a part of energy savings to vulnerab le customers or those in energy poverty is a 
reasonable goal, from an operational point of view, the possibility of producing complexity and 
less flexibility for those MS adopting energy efficiency obligation scheme based on Certificates 
should be equally considered and properly managed. This because, if strictly required to  
achieve a share of energy savings obligation among people affected by energy poverty, 
vulnerable customers or people living in social housing, obligated party may face an even more 
critical scarcity of certificates’ supply (given the usual illiquidity that characterize smaller sub -
segment of a market) and very high corresponding prices , thus unbalancing the energy 
efficiencies certificates scheme. 
▪ Although it seems reasonable that Member States shall ensure that also gas and electricity 
transmission and distribution network operators apply the energy efficiency first principle in 
their activities, the related  provisions for MS to ensure that DSOs and TSOs do not invest in 
stranded a ssets to contribute to climate change mitigation seems not well specified and 
detailed. In particular, only  security of supply and market integration concerns seems to be 
considered relevant in the evaluation of stranded assets, while investments to make the grid 
future-proof and to enable an evolutive use of the infrastructure (such as renewable gases and 
hydrogen’s injection, transport and distribution) and the uptake of innovative technology 
(such as, using the gas grid in order to send power-to-gas or power-to- hydrogen outputs along 
an integrated and coupled energy system) should be considered too. 
▪ The introduction of a further criterion for achieving high -efficiency cogeneration (270 
gCO2/kWh) is too challenging and incorrect from a technical point of view as it places the value 
of electric and thermal kWh on the same level. Assuming a need for 3 kWh terms and 1 kWh 
of electricity, to comply with that value the production of electricity in cogeneration must take 
place with an efficiency of almost 58% or with a specific emission of 346 gCO2 per kWh 
electricity, when the value allowed to access the capacity market is 550 gCO 2 / kWh or 
electrical efficiency of 36%. The request should therefore  be to make that value congruous 
with a combined production of electricity and heat (not only of heat as the proposal seems to 
be thought). A reasonable value could be 350 gCO 2/kWh, even though the thermoelectric 
performance would be already much improved. 
▪ The potential for energy saving lies mainly in buildings. From this point of view, the role of 
heating and cooling will be crucial. It is therefore absolutely necessary to ensure consistency 
between the proposals for a directive on renewable ener gy and energy efficiency and the 
proposal for the revision of the energy performance of buildings Directive scheduled for winter 
2021. 
 18 
▪ The role of demand-side response is positively represented, but only mentioned in the recitals. 
It is important to recall  the primary role of  aspects related to energy demand in energy 
efficiency, highlighting the contribution of end users - under consensus and against 
compensation - to the demand side flexibility. 
▪ The Commission's proposal welcomes the attention paid to MS' information requirements, 
also with regard to aspects of legal and financial support for all consumers. 
▪ Finally, the availability of qualification and certification schemes will allow to create an

Directive restructuring the Union framework for

(recast)

We believe that the proposal improves the legal clarity of the legislation  by providing for specific 
regulation of cases and products not covered by the current Directive or rather by establishing a 
comprehensive and up -to-date legal framework.  We share the general objective of reducing 
taxation for the most virtuous carriers (as for example  it is envisaged for biofuels). However, it 
should be noted that, in partial contradiction with this principle:  
• Natural gas and LNG are heavily penalized, although they represent solutions for 
decarbonization with the highest potential for heavy and maritime transport in the 
short to medium term.  
• Natural gas and LPG (recognized as alternative fuels) will be treated in the same 
way (with a minimum tax set at €10.7/GJ) of conventional fossil products, which we 
do not believe reflects their respective environmental impacts. 
• Taxation for natural gas is very high and does not take into account the level of 
efficiency when using fuels: it should be possible for Member States to reduce the 
minimum value for fuels used in high-efficiency cogeneration plants and therefore 
with high energy performance. 
• Among virtuous carriers, an important role is played by electricity for its 
contribution to decarbonization.  Proper allocation of taxes can make electricity an 
attractive option with its decarbonization potential to meet    2030 targets    at 
minimum social cost. 
• From a general perspective , the indexation of excise rates makes possible to 
maintain the consistency that in other tax fiel ds is ensured by rates linked to the 
values of the tax base. However, in some areas (such as gaseous products - LPG, 
CNG and LNG) the measure can be extremely impactful, since it is much higher than 
the values provided for in the current version of the Dir ective. It should also be 
stressed that the current structure of the Directive includes the possibility for 
Member States to derogate from the minimum levels defined at EU level for certain 
products (such as, for example, gaseous products), whereas the new  proposal 
would no longer include this option, thus amplifying de facto the negative impact 
of the increase of the minimum rate. 
• As regards the transitional period, within which pursuing a proportional increase in 
minimum rates, we believe that a period of 10 years may be too short and limiting. 
It would be desirable to foresee a possible prolongation, should regulatory, 
technical and/or market changes occur during the decade which would justify a 
revision of the time frame. 
• The proposal to keep th e rates applied by Member States within a structured grid 
with the proportions set for the minimum rates could be limiting for certain sectors, 
which would be severely disadvantaged. Having to apply a proportional deviation 
between the European minimum rate and the national rate to all energy products 
and considering that national taxation levels of traditional products are already 
much higher than the current EU minimum rates, alternative products – while 
having a reduced environmental impact - would be subject to such a higher taxation 
level than to the current one that their further deployment would be de facto 
hampered. 
 21 
• Many inconsistencies with other regulations exist. First of all, the new tax provides 
for a rate that includes a share on energy content  and a share on environmental 
performance. This means that sectors subject to ETS pay twice the environmental 
performance. There should be a double taxation mechanism for ETS sectors and 
thus the possibility of exemptions from the minimum values for these sectors.  
• An inconsistency emerges from a comparison with the provisions of the DAFI 
Directive (2014/94/EU on the development of alternative fuels infrastructure), 
where gaseous fuels (LPG, CNG and LNG) are identified as alternative fuels, namely 
among those product s on which the transport sector’s energy transition process 
should be based. On the contrary, the approach of the ETD proposal, instead of 
promoting the use of these products, seems to penalize the development of the 
relevant supply chains by making them subject, at the end of the transitional period, 
to the same minimum rates as traditional liquid fuels. 
• The tax rates proposed for natural gas may discourage the use of renewable gases 
that will be injected into the existing networks and mixed with natural g as to 
decarbonize the latter and to ensure a profitable use of the network infrastructure 
in the medium and long term. In fact, when a blend of natural and renewable gas is 
transported throughout the network, it will be extremely difficult to distinguish for 
tax purposes which percentage of the gas supplied and consumed by the end user 
comes from a fossil or a renewable source. 
• A further element of inconsistency can be seen when comparing the approach of 
the ETD proposal with the emission limits of pollutan ts that each Member State 
must respect to maintain and safeguard air quality. In fact, in the proposed 
directive, the definition of energy product, which identifies the products subject to 
excise duty, includes the quantities of woody biomass (CN 4401 and CN 4402) used 
as fuel only in plants with a thermal output of 5 MW or more, thus excluding all 
those volumes utili zed in smaller appliances intended for domestic heating. 
Numerous scientific studies have highlighted the emissive impact of solid biomass 
used in small appliances (35 kW) in the domestic heating sector, both in terms of 
dust emissions (with concentrations up to three orders of magnitude higher than in 
gas appliances) and of carcinogenic substances (not detectable in the case of gas 
appliances). 
• As reflected in the recitals of the ETD one main objective of this Directive is to 
ensure the proper functioning of the internal market. Following this approach, the 
legislator considered that a minimum harmonization of taxation levels was mainly 
justified when the product “is used as heating fuel or motor fuel” and state that “to 
that extent, it is in the nature and the logic of the tax system to exclude from the 
scope of the framework dual uses and non -fuel uses of energy products as well as 
mineralogical processes”. 
• In the revision of the ETD, the possibility of a process tax reduction for mineralogical 
processes is to be retained. A discontinuation would significantly impair the 
international competitiveness of European industries and make decar bonization 
more difficult.

General comments 
Confindustria supports the European Union's climate ambition, and the Italian industry is ready to 
take its share of responsibility , having different technological solutions and breakthrough 
innovations to provide. The new phase set by the Fit -for-55 package and the related legislative 
tools help to pave the way to achieve the EU's decarbon ization goal. This clarity is important to 
investment decisions by industry. 
 
However, the European Union will be exposed to important vulnerabilities, probably not deeply 
evaluated, both on economic and social side. The limited contribution of EU to globa l GHG 
emissions (around 8%) will strongly limit the actual visibility of the very significant EU efforts in 
global terms. EU ETS is the most important “tool” of  the EU climate policy. However, as long as a 
global agreement on carbon price will be adopted, we should consider the effect of the ETS on the 
European industry in light of its geographically limited scope. At the beginning, the attractiveness 
of such a market mechanism  was the lack of necessity of external “interventions” aimed at 
influencing the EUA price, but this is exactly what has been done (to get a high EUA price level), 
despite the fact that the established GHG emissions reduction is in any case “guaranteed” by the 
ETS framework, regardless of price interferences. 
 
The unilateral increase of ambition of the EU in the international framework requires strengthened 
carbon leakage protection for exposed sectors , therefore we support the creation of CBAM to 
ensure a full level-playing field between EU and non-EU suppliers in sectors where it is feasible to 
have a full imports traceability. In this respect, we note that the draft of the ETS Directive foresees 
the gradual phase -out of EUA free allocation s as CBAM is gradually phased -in: this proposal 
weakens the carbon leakage protection when such protection is mostly needed due to the 
increasing carbon price and the required massive investments in low carbon technologies. In fact, 
our companies are already damaged by heavy imports from non-EU countries and therefore they 
need to be accordingly protected. We also wish to underline that CBAM and EUA free allocations 
are not strictly comparable and interchangeable measures.  
 
In addition, we want to emphasize the importance of having both ETS Directive and the CBAM 
Regulation to be examined in parallel by the EU institutions, given the obvious connections 
between these pieces of legislation, in particular, on the phase-out of free allocation.  
 
1. Specific comments on ETS revision proposal 
 
To get to a 55% overall emissions reduction target by 2030  - a 15% increase from our previous 
target of 40% by 2030 - the Commission considers necessary that the ETS sectors reduction target 
by 2030 should change from -43% to -61% emissions. 
Our preliminary comments on ETS Directive revision proposal are the following: 
 
➢ The higher 2030 target should be achieved in the most cost-efficient way: the rebasing 
of the cap (through one -off cancellation of allowances), as well as the doubling of the 
intake rate of the MSR and the new threshold for cancelling allowances in the reserve, 
reduce significantly the budget of allowances available during the trading period (i.e. the 
trajectory until 2030) without impacting the 2030 target (i.e. the end point of the 
 24 
trajectory). Such measures are not strictly needed for the achievement of the new 2030 
target, and therefore they should be withdrawn in order to avoid unnecessary additional 
costs for industry and European society. All these measures increase artificially the carbon 
costs (for EU industry but also for households through higher electricity prices) for the 
same level of 2030 ambition. Furthermore, the revision of the legislation needs to address 
the increasing role of financial speculation in the EU carbon market and avoid its impacts 
on compliance operators exposed to international competition. 
➢ An increase of the ambition of th e EU in the international framework require 
strengthened carbon leakage protection for exposed sectors. Higher unilateral costs for 
EU industry, with no comparable carbon constraint foreseen till 2030 for extra EU 
competitors, requires strengthened carbon leakage protection. Full benchmark based 
free allocation and indirect costs compensation need to remain fully effective also for 
CBAM sectors at least until 2030 to allow companies focusing on low carbon investment 
and to assess the effectiveness of the CB AM.  The proposal to phase out free allocation 
for CBAM sectors as of 2026 weakens the carbon leakage protection when the 
effectiveness of the CBAM will not have been assessed yet (since the financial costs will 
be applied to imports only as of 2026), removes financial resources from companies that 
need to invest urgently and massively in low carbon technologies, and undermines the 
competitiveness of EU exports (since no export rebate is proposed).  
➢ Benchmark: existing rules on benchmark definitions and ben chmark reduction rates 
need to remain until 2030 to ensure predictability and carbon leakage protection.  The 
combined impact of the increase of the maximum annual reduction rate of BM (from 1.6% 
to 2.5%) and the inclusion of new low carbon technologies in the BM boundaries without 
careful consideration of their maturity and applicability, will produce a sharp decrease in 
free allowances for carbon leakage sectors. When such technologies represent still a very 
little percentage of the market or even are not generally applicable in all processes or 
sectors (i.e. fuel and heat benchmark), this will reduce prematurely carbon leakage 
protection and increase the risk of higher imports from third countries more than 
incentivizing such technologies in the EU.  Benchmark rules, which have been revised very 
recently, need to take into account that the transition to new technologies, which is highly 
dependent on externalities (e.g. energy, H2, input materials, logistics and storage, etc.) 
will be gradual and will requir e sufficient time. Furthermore, the proposals to modify 
product benchmarks in secondary legislation create major uncertainty, since they would 
be set very shortly before the beginning of the second subperiod (2026) making 
impossible any investment planning. 
➢ Free allocation should not become conditional to energy efficiency investments to avoid 
double regulation. Free allocation is already subject to very strict rules, since it is set at 
the level of the average of the best 10% of installations, making the lower performances 
- compared to benchmark - very costly. Secondly, making free allocation conditional to 
further investments is not consistent with its essential objective (namely, avoiding carbon 
leakage). If now free allocation is made conditional to additional measures to be taken by 
the company (i.e. investments in energy efficiency), de facto it is not anymore a (partial) 
mitigation of unilateral carbon costs because it require s additional expenditure to the 
company. Thirdly this measure will penalized early movers and companies which have 
already invested in energy efficiency reaching high performances. Finally, a consistent 
 25 
regulatory framework should avoid any overlapping bet ween different pieces of 
legislation, such as the EU ETS and the Energy Efficiency Directives. If conditionalities will 
be kept, it is important to ensure coordination among all policies where these 
requirements are included (i.e. ETS free allocation Guidelines, CEEAG Guidelines, etc). For 
example, conditionality of allocated free allowances,  which requires the realization of 
investments coming from energy audit recommendations, with a payback time (PT) until 
5 years is inconsistent with the 3 years mention ed on par. 55 of ETS state aid guidelines 
(COM (2020) 6400 final), that should be adopted. Moreover, we believe that when an 
audit system is requested only one audit should be sufficient for all pieces of legislation. 
A term for the realization of the investment (for example 5 years) should also be foreseen. 
Furthermore, to take into account the possible unavailability of the necessary financial 
resources, in addition to those required for the enterprise core business projects, and/or 
with projects with a shorter PT, the obligation to realize the investments (that obviously 
are in the operator’s interest) should be coupled with the possibility of access to external 
financial resources. 
 
➢ The EU ETS should support investments in low-carbon technologies through innovative 
financing mechanisms. We strongly believe that the upcoming review of the EU ETS is a 
key opportunity to support the early deployment of breakthrough technologies, such as 
CCUS. In this respect, we welcome the inclusion of Carbon Contracts for Difference (CCDs) 
into the Directive, the reinforcement of the ETS innovation fund, as well as the 
requirement for Member State to use ETS revenues primarily for climate purposes. We 
note that the draft of the ETS Directive suggests that the free allocatio n no longer 
provided to the CBAM sectors will be auctioned and that the revenues will be allocated 
to the Innovation Fund, where “special attention should be given to projects in CBAM 
sectors”. Such provisions should be reinforced. The proposed ETS Directive endangers this 
business case through a very narrow definition of the “exemption from the obligation to 
surrender allowances”, which would be restricted to (i) permanent geological storage and 
(ii) the CO 2 chemically bound in a product. In addition, the omission of the requirement 
that CO 2 should be “released in the atmosphere” when defining an “emission” risks 
opening the gateway to CO2 accounting with the captur e plant. If this is the intention, 
such approach raises doubts on the financial viability of carbon capture and use projects. 
It is clear that some CO 2 utilization projects – such as the use of CO 2 in synthetic fuels – 
need a serious debate on carbon accoun ting, since the captured CO 2 can eventually be 
released when used (for instance, by the airplane when consuming the synthetic fuel). 
However, we are concerned that the legislative ground for carbon capture and utilization 
(CCUS) in the proposed Directive is too  fragile. Based on the above, we would like to 
suggest that the revised ETS Directive reconfirms that there is an emission of CO 2 only if 
and at the point of release into the atmosphere. A transfer of CO2 to a third operator does 
not result in an emission for the transferring plant. The ultimate use of CO2 should not be 
the determining factor in allocating the CO2. The European Commission proposes (Art. 10 
a para 8) to make available allowances for Innovation Fund, using 365 M EUA from FA and 
85 M EUA from auctioned EUA: It is proposed to invert the 2 figures.  
➢ Indirect costs compensation  (Art. 10a (6)). The term “should” leaves the possib ility for 
MSs of not providing indirect costs compensation, with consequent distortion of internal 
 26 
competitiveness. The Indirect cost compensation should remain in force at least until 
2030 and it should include as well CBAM sectors. 
➢ EU ETS system and GOs system should be linked: enabling the creation of a liquid GOs 
market requires the introduction of provisions allowing for the offsetting of EUA through 
GOs. That would be a key driver for the development of low carbon and renewable gases 
at scale.  
➢ Hydrogen production should be included in the free allowance mechanism. The limit set 
by the legislation enables the inclusion only of plants that produce at least 25t/day. It 
should be provided also to include smaller green hydrogen production plants. 
➢ The ETS proposal in the transport and building sectors don’t’ provide the definitions of 
the subject in charge of the obligations  (DSO, TSO, supplier), as they will be defined in a 
following act.   
 
2. Specific comments on CBAM proposal 
 
An increased level of climate ambition in Europe requires stronger carbon leakage protection.  
We do not believe that the CBAM could effectively replace the existing carbon leakage measures 
(especially indirect cost compensation), and a CBAM should only be implemented i f this can be 
done in a way that is complementary to the existing measures.  
Our preliminary comments on carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM) are the following: 
 
➢ Need to find a solution on export.  Unfortunately, the Commission proposal does not 
provide a solution on “export rebates” and this represents a major weakness of the CBAM 
proposal. The EU’s specific carbon costs will have to be offset somehow adopting specific 
solutions which are crucial to maintain exports competitiveness.  
➢ CBAM introduction timing. Considering the high exposure of some MS markets to 
imports from non-EU countries, a well-designed CBAM able to strengthen carbon leakage 
protection should be enforced even before 2026, but the mechanism shouldn’t be 
activated before having insured the effectiveness of its implementation. However, carbon 
leakage measures have to be maintained. 
➢ The carbon content of embedded goods should closely match the methods used in the EU 
ETS, and importers should be incentivized to use verified emissions. 
➢ CBAM should avoid any form of ‘double protection’ -take into account the carbon pricing 
scheme developed in other countries - and this should be done through a strict equivalency 
regime. The European industry has repeatedly argued in favor of a CBAM that is as 
transparent as possible and avoids any form of ‘double protection’ for EU suppliers, 
provided that a full level playing field is ensured. A strict CBAM monitoring system should 
be put in place as CBAM is implemented. In addition, we believe that a proper monitoring 
system for CBAM should be put in place, given the untested nature of the instrument. We 
therefore support the creation of an EU-level body (e.g. ‘EU CBAM authority’) in charge of 
monitoring and supporting the implementation of CBAM. Its mandate should be defined 
as to ensure a CO2 cost equivalence between EU and non-EU suppliers.   
➢ Indirect emissions should be included in the CBAM, and due consideration should be given 
to transport emissions.  Including indirect emissions in the CBAM is crucial to create a level-
 27 
playing field, as these will become even more important as energy -intensive sectors 
progressively decarbonize their production processes. However, it is important to 
guarantee that the in clusion of indirect emissions is done following a robust assessment 
thus to not create  distortion between EU and extra EU producers. Urgency in the 
introduction of indirect costs must be placed on energy -intensive sectors that are not 
eligible, according to the EU guidelines, for the compensation of indirect emission costs. 
➢ CBAM revenues should support the decarbonization of hard to abate industries . The 
revenues generated by CBAM should be used to unlock key breakthrough technologies in 
the sectors covered . We support this provision  and suggest strengthening it, further 
clarifying that the revenues generated by CBAM will also be used to support the 
decarbonization of the energy-intensive sectors covered by this instrument.  
➢ Need to prevent products circumvention. Art. 27 of the proposal looks at circumvention, 
however the Commission’s proposal seems to consider that the only risk of circumvention 
would reside in the introduction of “slightly modified products” to avoid CBAM obligations. 
Whilst this risk is r eal, it is indispensable that “resource shuffling” is also considered as a 
motive for circumvention. We see “resource shuffling” as a phenomenon whereby 
exporting countries would use their cleanest industrial plants to export to the EU and keep 
more polluting installations for their domestic market. Moreover, enforcement measures 
and penalties for attempts at circumvention should be implemented including the option 
of withdrawing the import authorization.

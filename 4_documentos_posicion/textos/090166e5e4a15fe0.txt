WKÖ supports the European Green Deal and stands by the climate neutrality goal by 2050. 
Now a concrete set of measures for a sustainable, ecological change in the EU, in line with 
a new growth strategy, is necessary. WKÖ will contribute to a legislative package that meets 
the requirements of effective climate protection and at the same time is practicable and 
compatible with economic prosperity. 
On the way to climate neutrality, the transformation of our energy system plays an 
essential role. Energy efficiency and the expansion of renewable energy sources will 
definitely contribute to that goal. It is essential to adopt concrete, effective and well-
coordinated measures that actually lead to the achievement of the goals. When working on 
such measures, the challenge of dramatically rising energy prices must also be taken into 
account. 
In addition to the sustainability of our energy sources, security of supply and 
competitiveness - at the national as well as European and global level - are essential 
prerequisites for the economy. In order to take a holistic approach, the following 
principles must be taken into account: 
Security of supply and availability of renewable energy are a top priority 
in a decarbonised energy system. This makes it all the more important to 
create the necessary framework conditions for the increased use of 
renewable energies. Besides improving energy efficiency and the 
availability of renewable electricity, back-up systems as well as climate-
neutral gaseous and liquid energy sources are necessary, in order to 
compensate for the increased volatility in our energy system. Especially in 
the transition phase to climate neutrality by 2050, all alternatives should 
be permissible as long as they contribute to a reduction of greenhouse gas 
emissions. The principle of technological openness should lead the way 
towards climate neutrality.  
To ensure that sufficient renewable energy is available for the 
decarbonization, both inner-European energy cooperations must be 
strengthened and partnerships with third countries established. North 
Africa has large unused areas available, a high solar radiation and is 
geographical close to Europe. These factors would, for example, enable 
the cost-effectively establishment of large-scale industrial photovoltaic 
plants or wind farms. The resulting renewable electricity could be used to 
both cover local demand and to generate hydrogen by means of inserts 
from Power-to-X technologies for export to Europe. Once it is upgraded or 
converted, existing pipeline infrastructure can be used for transport. At 
the same time, the EU is called upon to place a stronger focus on energy 
diplomacy and on finding international partners. 
Produced energy must reach the end consumer in a reliable and 
economically competitive way. Essential framework conditions for the 
expansion of necessary infrastructure must be established rapidly – this 
includes in particular the acceleration of approval procedures. It is 
further essential to maintain the existing gas infrastructure and to 
prepare it for the feed-in of green gas.

Digitalization and technological advances allow for an integrated, 
decarbonized energy system. Cross-sectoral measures are needed to make 
optimal use of the potential for cost-effective renewable energy as well 
as energy efficiency in all sectors of the energy system. Alternative 
gaseous and liquid forms of energy must be taken into account. Instead of 
focusing on technical measures that will be exhausted in the medium to 
long term, it is also important to approach the path towards climate 
neutrality systemically. Accordingly, the feed-in of waste heat, e.g., from 
industry, can constitute a necessary step towards both more energy 
efficiency and the use of renewable energy. Either way, it is important to 
consider both the economic and technical practicability.  
An integrated and consistent approach must also be followed in the 
preparation of legal acts and in the setting of objectives. This way, 
contradictory objectives and double burdens for the economy can be 
avoided. The ultimate goal must remain climate neutrality by 2050. Until 
then, Member States need flexibility in order to make optimal and 
targeted use of the available potentials. The economy needs sector-
specific impact assessments, accurate funding instruments such as e.g., 
incentives for innovation, swift scaling, and implementation of new 
technologies (e.g., CCU/CCS) as well as a supportive, new state aid 
framework. Innovative approaches and funding instruments must be 
encouraged instead of blocked. 
 
Focus: Review of the Renewable Energy Directive (RED III) 
 
The rapid expansion of renewable energy is undisputedly necessary on the way to a 
climate-neutral energy system. With the "Fit for 55" package and the revision of the 
Renewable Energy Directive (RED III), the necessary, appropriate framework conditions and 
concrete measures must be defined.1 
The increase of the European renewable target from the current 32 % to 
40 % by 2030 is a necessary step to achieve climate neutrality by 2050, 
but not enough to effectively promote the expansion of renewables. 
Detailed sector targets – as now provided for in the European 
Commission's proposal (Art. 15a, 22a, 23-24, 25-27) – do not necessarily 
lead to improvements in the individual areas but inhibit the flexibility of 
the Member States. Conflicting requirements must be solved. Thus, 
consistency has to be taken into account when achieving the objectives.  
In this context, the international and European competitiveness, 
affordable energy prices and the availability of renewable energy must be 
guaranteed. Essential thereby is the rapid expansion of renewable energy 
plants, grid, and storage infrastructure and thus the acceleration of 
approval procedures. We very much regret that no adjustments of the 
Art. 16 are provided for in the proposal.

Renewable electricity will definitely make a significant contribution to 
climate neutrality. The direct use of energy without conversion steps is 
indisputably the most efficient. However, it has to be kept in mind that 
renewable electricity is a volatile energy source and that storage 
technologies are currently only available to a limited extent, mostly very 
costly. This makes it all the more important that other climate-neutral 
forms of energy (gaseous and liquid) are also comprehensively taken into 
account in RED III for security of supply. Clear definitions and clear 
certification requirements are necessary, which must be defined 
uniformly across all legal acts of the "Fit for 55" package (Art. 2). In the 
medium to long term, Europe will also be dependent on renewable energy 
from third countries. To this end, it must be ensured that a uniform origin 
and certification system for all climate-neutral energy sources is the basis 
for. 
We welcome the fact that renewable fuels of non-biological origin 
(RFNBOs) should play a stronger role in the future. Additionally, to the 
sub-target in the transport sector (Art. 25) a sub-target for industry is 
added (Art. 22a) – for energetic and non-energetic use.  
We are critical of the fact that in the necessary phase of the market 
ramp-up the principle of additionality is tightened (Art. 27). Accordingly, 
only renewable hydrogen may be counted towards the target if the 
necessary renewable electricity was produced with a newly built and 
never subsidized plant. These requirements will become the stumbling 
block of renewable hydrogen expansion and therefore is strictly rejected. 
Example from Austria for H2-production: In Austria – in contrast to e.g., 
to Spain – large PV or wind turbines are not yet economically installable 
and are therefore being promoted. H2 generated from this electricity 
would not be counted towards RED III. This would result in an enormous 
locational disadvantage for Austria during the H2 market ramp-up. 
However, in order to make electrolysis cheaper, these plants would have 
to be built in adequate sizes. 
Example from Austria on H2-consumption: Non-energy hydrogen 
consumption is very high in Member States such as Austria - with a strong 
chemical and refining industry - compared to energy use. On the one 
hand, a market for renewable hydrogen must first be established. On the 
other hand, there is a risk that individual companies would need so much 
renewable hydrogen for non-energy purposes, which would overwhelm 
and distort the market – which must also include energy use and 
transport. All energy sources and technologies that can achieve the goal 
of CO2-reduction should therefore be approved. Stricter targets without 
the availability of energy at competitive prices, combined with the 
principle of additionality, are blocking the market ramp-up of climate-
neutral hydrogen. Appropriate and innovative support mechanisms are 
therefore needed. 
Only with a wide range of technologies and energy forms in all sectors – 
buildings, heating and cooling, transport, and industry - the necessary 
change must be created. The reduction of CO2-intensity in total must be 
the focus, not just a single technology alternative. We demand, e.g., in 
the building sector, for the option of a “fuel switch”, instead of generally

For years (since RED I), European law has allowed Member States to 
cooperate more closely in the promotion of renewable energy sources, 
either in the form of statistical transfers, joint projects, or common 
support schemes (Art. 9). So far, however, this possibility has only been 
used sporadically – and not at all by Austria. Administrative 
simplifications in European legislation could encourage Member States to 
enter such cooperation and to make use of the opportunities already 
available. In principle, we welcome the fact that Member States must 
agree at least one cross-border joint project with another Member State 
by 2025. However, this is not enough and the present proposal falls far 
short of the necessary requirements. The international and European 
cooperation must be strengthened. It needs in addition partnerships 
beyond electricity cooperations.

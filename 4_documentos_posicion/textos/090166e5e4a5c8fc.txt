Introduction

worldwide, including many EU fintechs such as Adyen, Bunq, Getaround, Nickel, Revolut, Wise 
and Monese.  
 
Our leading biometric and AI technology enables clients to p rove that their customers are 
who they claim to be, enabling them to comply with regulatory obligations including AML and 
KYC requirements in the EU. Onfido works by scanning a user’s photo ID to detect whether 
it’s genuine and compares this to a selfie or  a short video taken by the user to check facial 
biometrics match those on the photo ID and ensure the person is the live and genuine owner. 
We use a hybrid model that combines machine learning with human expert oversight, which 
delivers best-in-class speed, consistency, and accuracy.

Overall assessment 
 
Remote identity proofing services such as biometrics, ID/document verification and database 
checks are proven methods of establishing genuine identity to a high level of assurance. Such 
checks can establish that the user requesting the service: 
 
● is truly there without the need of a witness (with further advances, this will also 
capture if the person is being forced); 
● is a genuine user with non-fraudulent documents; and 
● is not a politically exposed/sanctioned individual. 
 
These are key factors in facilitating compliance with “know your customer” and associated 
AML customer due diligence requirements. Furthermore, remote identity verification and 
remote id entity authentication services have the flexibility to add additional safeguards 
where needed, allowing these methods to adapt to stay ahead of bad actors. It is crucial to 
the success of AML and associated fraud measures that EU regulation facilitates the adoption 
of such services without national barriers.  
 
The AML Regulation provides an opportunity to further harmonise the existing ruleset and 
thus facilitate a more  unified, highly robust, and standardised framework which reduces 
Internal Market barriers, promotes security, and more effectively prevents money laundering 
and terrorist financing. 
 
We welcome the decision to shift private sector AML obligations from a Directive to a 
Regulation. This change is long overdue, and we hope and expect it will lead to a significantly 
more harmonised implementation and enforcement of European AML rules.  
 
It is also important to recognise the global nature of the issues addressed in these proposals. 
It is vitally important that standards and rules set in the EU have interoperability at their heart, 
so firms and associated third parties helping them to comply are able to sca le efficiently and 
without barriers.  
 
Scope of the AML Regulation 
 
We welcome the widened scope envisaged by the AML Regulation. This will help to provide 
a greater level of certainty to end consumers as well as businesses owing to the increased 
transparency that will accompany the application of AML rules on the entities that were 
previously out of scope. We consider that any perceived increase in administrative and 
financial costs of AML compliance due to the broader scope are to a large extent offset by  
technological developments that have reduced overall costs. Products such as our own 
identity proofing tools have allowed businesses to complete their Customer Due Diligence

requirements at a faster pace, and a lower cost than was previously available. Th e universal 
use of such technologies across all Member States should therefore be a key aim because it 
will further drive costs down, improve user experience, reduce fraud, increase competition, 
and allow obliged entities to complete necessary checks in the most efficient manner.  
 
More specifically on the crypto sector, the expansion in scope – now encompassing all crypto-
service providers – was much needed, in a market which is still in its infancy, and is 
characterised by anonymity and reduced transparency.  
 
The main concern that we have with regards to the scope is the exemption of gambling 
services. The ability for national governments to exempt certain gambling services 
domestically from the application of AML rules such as customer due diligence obli gations 
leads to a fragmented approach towards the sector, and different requirements for the same 
gambling services in different Member States. Given the aim of the package itself is to further 
harmonise AML rules, national exemptions such as these should  not remain in force. It runs 
counter to the aspiration of a Digital Single Market and will maintain friction and complexity 
in the market without justification.  
 
Customer Due Diligence (CDD) 
 
Identity verification requirements 
We strongly welcome the fact that the proposed AML Regulation sets out explicitly the 
specific information to be obtained as part of the identification process. This provides much-
needed clarity and certainty for the industry. 
 
We agree with the proposal to reduce the threshold f or applying customer due diligence for 
occasional transactions from 15,000 Euros to 10,000 Euros. This will act to further protect 
customers and businesses from the possibility of fraud and is to be welcomed. 
 
Relationship with eIDAS and the inclusion of remote identity proofing services 
Under the previous AML Directive, as well as the proposed AML Regulation, there is a direct 
read-through to the eIDAS Regulation for the identification and verification of customers 
requirement (Know Your Customer rules). A rticle 18(4)(b) of the proposed AML Regulation 
(Article 13 in the current AML Directive), specifically references qualified trust services under 
eIDAS as reliable and independent sources for documents, data, and information.  
 
Nevertheless, the implementation of the eIDAS Regulation has been fragmented up to now, 
and remote identity verification as well as authentication services have not, as of yet, been 
included as qualified trust services under the Regulation. This is why we consider that a 
reference to such services within Article 18, separate to the reference to qualified trust 
services, needs to be made.  
 
4 
 
 
Currently, such services fall within the scope of “any other secure, remote or electronic 
identification process regulated, recognised, approved or accepted by the relevant national 
authorities” in Article 13 of the AML Directive, which means that there is no harmonisation. 
Such service providers are effectively blocked from servicing customers on a cross -border 
basis unless they are recognised in each Member State they operate in. The requirements for 
a service provider to be recognised in each Member State differ, reducing the ability of such 
providers to scale. In turn, this also reduces the scalability of European Fintechs, which cannot 
use a singl e remote identity verification method as they operate and expand cross -border. 
Some examples of this patchwork are provided below.  
 
Country Video call requirements for eKYC service providers 
Austria Video-based electronic procedure is required as of 2017 with a series of 
conditions that need to be upheld1. 
Germany Video call requirement with a list of security requirements2. 
Luxembourg Video conference call required with limits on outsourcing outside of the 
country, and requirements including a trained employee for the calls3. 
Netherlands No specific requirements yet 
 
 
This raises high unnecessary barriers to entry, prevents regulatory certainty, increases costs 
and complexity, and degrades the user experience.  Instead of creating a harmonised regime 
for such services, the Commission has opted to not include t he referen ce to such other 
processes within the new AML Regulation, which could potentially exclude such services 
completely. Such an approach would freeze innovation in the trust services sector, as it is 
through this clause that new technologies, not yet included in the scope of eIDAS, can be 
certified and used for the purposes of eKYC. 
 
To keep such services within scope and successfully harmonise rules for identity proofing 
service providers, the reference to “any other secure, remote or electronic identification  
process regulated, recognised, approved or accepted by the relevant national authorities” in 
Article 13 of the AML Directive should remain as part of Article 18(4)(b) of the AML 
Regulation, and be accompanied by a mutual recognition clause, allowing recog nised 
processes in one Member State to be used seamlessly across all the other Member States.

We would suggest that Article 18(4)(b) should therefore read as follows:  
 
“(b) the use of electronic identification means and relevant trust services as set out  in

Outsourcing arrangements 
Outsourcing rules are currently not uniformly applied across the bloc, with national 
competent authorities only having the EBA’s outsourcing  guidelinesfrom 2019 to base their 
ruleset on. As seen by the EBA’s  compliance table on these guidelines, a significant amount 
of EU Member States are still non-compliant (with a few not intending to comply at all with a 
number of the points raised in the guidance).  
 
Outsourcing rules listed in the AML Regulation are therefore a welcome addition, setting out 
more detail on how outsourcing for customer due diligence can occur, and providing certainty 
to market participants. This is essential to ensure that obliged entities can benefit from the 
efficiencies and end user benefits that can be realised by outsourcing to expert third parties, 
without compromising on security or privacy.   
 
Clarity on outsourcing checks 
Clarity is still required under Article 40(3). The Article sets out that obliged entities will have 
to complete ‘regular checks’ on the outsourced entity, and the frequency of such checks will 
depend on how ‘critical’ the t ask being outsourced is. The text should clarify what activities 
are considered critical and what activities it considers less so, plus the nature of the checks 
themselves.  
 
This can be achieved either directly in text, or through a technical standard to be drafted by 
the newly formed AMLA, which would set out the frequency of checks required for each CDD 
activity being outsourced. As things stand, the current wording may lead to differing 
interpretations amongst national competent authorities, and therefo re different frequency 
of checks being required in different Member States for the same outsourced activity. 
 
Prohibition of outsourcing activities 
Further to the above, the  decision to prohibit certain activities from being outsourced as it 
currently stands is somewhat arbitrary and disproportionate. In particular, the attribution of 
risk profiles, that will be prohibited under Article 40(2)(d), is something that CDD service 
providers could very well provide without increasing any risk parameters for obliged entities. 
On the contrary, CDD service providers that are well -versed in identifying and monitoring

risks, may be even better suited to provide such a service on behalf of obliged entities. Such 
providers are specialised in said activities and would the refore allow obliged entities to 
complete this task to a higher standard. This is especially the case for smaller obliged entities, 
that do not necessarily have the financial capabilities to invest in their own risk profiling 
framework. As with other outso urced activities, the obliged entity would still remain fully 
liable. Instead of completely prohibiting the outsourcing therefore, a more proportionate 
measure, would be a ‘sign-off’ requirement on the outsourced entity’s process for providing 
said task. 
 
Harmonisation of sub-outsourcing 
Overall, we do still believe that Article 40 of the AML Regulation will improve the current 
landscape and lead to a more harmonised application of outsourcing rules. Nevertheless, 
Article 40 also does not encompass sub -outsourcing. To effectively harmonise the EU 
outsourcing regime and create further certainty for CDD service providers, a common 
approach to sub-outsourcing should also be included within the AML Regulation’s Article 40. 
  
This is especially important as tech businesses/startups in the AML space tend to specialise 
on specific elements of the CDD process. To effectively compete with Big Tech and established 
players, such businesses need to be able to cooperate with other businesses to provide one 
joint solution. In the area of eKYC and CDD this is achieved in many instances through sub -
outsourcing of the services which an outsourced company is not specialised in. This allows for 
businesses to remain specialised and to develop higher -end solutions, whilst remainin g 
competitive in the wider CDD marketplace by being sub-outsourced. 
  
We would suggest the below wording to be added in Article 40 of the AML Regulation to 
achieve complete harmonisation around sub-outsourcing in the EU: 
 
“(5) Outsourced entities shall be able to sub -outsource activities, as long as the 
outsourced entities: 
 
(a) have laid  such sub-outsourcing out in their written agreement with the obliged 
entity; and 
(b) are able to  report on the effective implementation of measures by the sub -
outsourced entities to the obliged entity. 
 
The obliged entity remains fully liable for any actions of the sub-outsourced entity, and 
the tasks undertaken by the sub -outsourced entity shall als o not be undertaken 
in such way as to impair materially the quality of the obliged entity’s measures and 
procedures to comply with the requirements of this Regulation and of Regulation 
[please insert reference – proposal for a recast of Regulation (EU) 201 5/847 - 
COM/2021/422 final].”

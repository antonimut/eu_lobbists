International Council on Clean Transportation consultation response on Proposal for a Regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council on ensuring a level playing field for sustainable air transport   November 18, 2021 This consultation response is submitted by the International Council on Clean Transportation (ICCT). International Council on Clean Transportation (ICCT) welcomes the opportunity to comment on the European Commission’s Proposal for a Regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council on ensuring a level playing field for sustainable air transport (ReFuel EU Aviation). The ICCT is an independent non-profit organization founded to provide first-rate, unbiased research and technical and scientific analysis to environmental regulators. Our mission is to improve the environmental performance and energy efficiency of road, marine, and air transportation, in order to benefit public health and mitigate climate change. We promote best practices and comprehensive solutions to increase vehicle efficiency, increase the sustainability of alternative fuels, reduce pollution from the in-use fleet, and curtail emissions of local air pollutants and greenhouse gases (GHG) from international goods movement.  The ICCT welcomes the opportunity to provide comments on the Commission’s proposed ReFuel EU Aviation regulation. We commend the Commission for its continuing efforts to promote a cleaner, lower-carbon transportation sector that uses less petroleum-based fuels. This proposal builds upon the impressive steps the Commission has undertaken to promote low-carbon biofuels. The comments below offer a number of technical observations and recommendations for the Commission to consider in its continued efforts to strengthen the regulation and maximize its benefits in mitigating the risks of climate change and reducing petroleum use. Staff may contact Nikita Pavlenko (n.pavlenko@theicct.org) or Dr. Stephanie Searle (stephanie@theicct.org)  with any questions.  Stephanie Searle, PhD

Cap the contribution of Annex IX List B biofuels at 1.7% of aviation fuel  We commend the Commission for its proposal for ReFuel EU Aviation to solely incentivize advanced biofuels and synthetic fuels (i.e., synthetic aviation fuels); this decision will allow the aviation sector to focus on those fuels capable of offering the highest possible GHG savings and avoiding many of the sustainability risks of food-based biofuels. However, the inclusion of Annex IX List B biofuels (i.e., waste oil-derived biofuels) in ReFuel EU poses risks to the long-term success of the policy—therefore, we recommend capping their contribution to ReFuel EU to 1.7% of aviation fuels, consistent with their overall cap of 1.7% within the Renewable Energy Directive (RED II).   A SAF blending mandate open to waste oils risks diverting them from their existing uses in the road sector, providing questionable net climate benefits. In a previous techno-economic analysis, we found that the 1.2x multiplier for aviation fuels in the REDII may be enough to incentivize greater production of jet fuel compared to road diesel from hydroprocessing.1 However, there is little to be gained by diverting used cooking oil and animal fats from the road sector to the aviation sector.   Hydrotreating lipids to produce drop-in biofuels is already a fully commercialized technology, with over one billion liters of capacity in the EU, with much it produced from waste oils.2 Though most of this capacity is dedicated to renewable diesel production, it can be reconfigured to produce a greater share of SAF.3 Optimizing the hydrotreating process to generate a greater share of SAF requires additional hydrogen and decreases the overall yield of liquid fuels from the process; using the Argonne National Laboratory’s Greenhouse Gases, Regulated Emissions, and Energy Use in Technologies (GREET) Model, we estimate that the direct life-cycle emissions for SAF produced from hydrotreated soybean oil increases to 32.8 gCO2e/MJ when the process is jet-optimized, compared to 25.6 gCO2e/MJ when the process is distillate optimized—an increase of 28%.4   1 https://theicct.org/publications/cost-supporting-alternative-jet-fuels-european-union 2 https://apps.fas.usda.gov/newgainapi/api/Report/DownloadReportByFileName?fileName=Biofuels%20Annual_The%20Hague_European%20Union_06-18-2021.pdf  3 Pearlson, Matthew, Christoph Wollersheim, and James Hileman. “A Techno-Economic Review of Hydroprocessed Renewable Esters and Fatty Acids for Jet Fuel Production.” Biofuels, Bioproducts and Biorefining 7, no. 1 (January 2013): 89–96. https://doi.org/10.1002/bbb.1378. 4 https://greet.es.anl.gov/greet_1_series  
 A comparative LCA for UCO in the EU estimates that UCO-derived jet fuel has 60% higher life-cycle GHG emissions than UCO-derived renewable diesel.5 That analysis also estimates that diverting waste oils away from existing fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) biodiesel is inefficient; the lower overall yield of jet-optimized UCO conversion reduces the net benefit of displacing fossil fuels by 12% relative to UCO use in FAME.6 Overall, this suggests that an incentive for using used cooking oil and animal fats in aviation will largely support an existing, commercialized process—further, this risks diversion from existing these waste oils from existing use in the road sector, while actually delivering reduced net carbon savings.   Though the overall contribution of Annex IX List B biofuels is capped at 1.7% in the RED II, the lack of a corresponding cap for aviation suggests that they may contribute far in excess of 1.7% within the sector. Based on 2019 consumption levels, the EU aviation sector (fuel consumption for domestic + international departing flights) consumed approximately 2,000 PJ of energy from kerosene, relative to approximately 13,300 PJ consumed in the EU transport sector when including road + aviation fuels.7 On an energy-equivalent basis, the 1.7% cap when applied to transport-wide energy consumption would be equivalent to approximately 11% of aviation sector energy consumption. So in theory, the entire 5% SAF target in 2030 (except for the 0.7% synthetic fuels subtarget) could be met with UCO- and animal fat-derived jet fuel, without the contribution of more advanced technologies such as cellulosic biofuels. In effect, this could crowd out the contributions of synthetic fuels and Annex IX List A biofuels through the first 10 to 15 years of the ReFuel EU mandate.   The cost of hydrotreated used cooking oil and animal fats is less than €1 per liter, significantly cheaper than lignocellulosic biofuels or synthetic fuels; therefore, these fuels are likely to be the primary mode of compliance other than the quantity of synthetic fuels required under the sub-target.8 Without a cap on the contribution of waste oils, UCO and animal fat-derived jet fuel could in theory be used to meet the entire SAF target (apart from the synthetic fuels subtarget), including the 20% target in 2035, if UCO imports are increased. By capping their contribution at 1.7%, consistent with the RED II, ReFuel EU could provide an earlier, stronger signal for investment in the synthetic fuels and advanced biofuels necessary to meet the higher targets of the later years of the policy.   If the contribution of used cooking oils and animal fats is uncapped and their usage expanded, it may exacerbate existing sustainability risks posed by waste oil fraud. Increase the sub-target for synthetic fuels used in aviation to 2.5% in 2030  The ICCT commends the Commission for the inclusion of a separate sub-target for synthetic fuels (i.e., synthetic jet fuel) in the overall SAF mandate; these fuels are likely to be more expensive than other SAFs in the near term and ensuring their long-term success requires investment in the early stages of ReFuel EU without being crowded out by the other SAFs.12 However, the proposed sub-target of 0.7% in 2030 is out of proportion with the sub-target for renewable fuels of non-biological origin (RFNBOs) in the proposed revision to the Renewable Energy Directive (RED II) of 2.6% of 2030 transport fuel supply (on an energy basis). The RFNBO sub-target, open to both synthetic fuels used in transport as well as hydrogen used in upstream fuel production, includes synthetic fuels used in aviation for ReFuel EU; further, aviation fuels benefit from a 1.2x multiplier for the purposes of compliance with the RED II.13   The Fischer Tropsch synthesis process used to produce synthetic fuels generates both a diesel and jet share of fuel; a future producer may adjust the yield of their process to either maximize middle distillates (i.e., diesel maximized) or jet fuel. Though it is slightly more efficient to produce diesel than jet fuel, the 1.2x multiplier for aviation fuels may reduce this gap.14 A recent economic optimization model analysis estimates that the RFNBO target is stronger than the synthetic fuel subtarget in ReFuel EU. The optimization model, which balances the separate incentives for the RED II and ReFuel EU, fuel production costs, and life-cycle GHG intensities for alternative fuels, estimates the most cost-effective mix of fuels to comply simultaneously with the RED II and ReFuel EU. The modeling estimates that electrofuel SAFs would supply 2.6% of aviation fuel by 2030 (exceeding the ReFuel EU target) largely due to the multiplier and RED II RFNBO sub-target, even when assuming that half the RED II RFNBO subtarget (1.3%) will be met

Consider a longer transition period or credit trading system for Union airport obligation  The proposed Regulation obligates Union airports to make aviation fuel available to aircraft operators that contains a minimum share of SAFs and synthetic fuels consistent with the ReFuel EU mandate, based on the physical quantity of fuel supplied. Though the Regulation provides a 5-year transition period through 2029, wherein fuel suppliers may report their SAF content as a weighted average across union airports supplied, Union airports will be required to report quantities as small as 0.7% synthetic fuels in their fuel mix in 2030. The impact assessment argues that this will not penalize airports by ensuring that fuel suppliers will have an incentive to spread their distribution to a wider network; however, for fuel pathways and technologies with small production volumes and potentially uneven distribution, this may pose a burden on compliance and increase the complexity of ReFuel EU implementation. Therefore, the Commission may wish to consider either extending the transition period to 2035 or allowing for the trading of SAF compliance credits across airports to provide flexibility in cases where fuel distribution is uneven.

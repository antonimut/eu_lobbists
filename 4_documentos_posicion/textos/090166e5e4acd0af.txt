European Fermentation Group’s views on the review 
of the Renewable Energy Directive 
The European Fermentation Group welcomes the ongoing revision of the Renewable Energy Di-
rective (RED II). This provides an opportunity to ensure a level playing field between the different 
uses of biomass, allowing – biofuel and bio-based chemicals users alike – to provide valuable con-
tributions to the climate neutrality ambition of the EU Green Deal  and the circular economy. We 
are pleased to see the maintenance of the 7% cap on crop -based biofuels and the additional 
measures Member States may take to further restrict the use of crop-based biofuels. Further, we 
call on the European Commission and the co-legislators to not include molasses into Annex IX Part 
A or B. 
 
EFG’s Key Messages 
We call on the European Commission and the co-legislators to rule out any inclusion of molasses in Annex 
IX part A or B  and to not designate them as feedstocks for sustainable biofuels for any mode of 
transport.  
• Actively incentivising the use of molasses and/or low-green syrup 2 for biofuels would have a 
negative impact on the competitiveness of the European fermentation industry.  
• Molasses and low -green syrup 2 are valuable co-products of the sugar production with a high 
nutritional and financial value. EFG members rely on sugar and sugar co -products to produce 
citric acid, lactic acid, vitamins, amino acids for animal feed and penicillin for antibiotics from 
molasses. Producers require yeast for various fermenting pr ocesses. Yeast is also needed for 
bread and bakery goods, wine and beer.  
• The availability of EU -sourced molasses at food grade quality  is the success of our industry. 
Sourcing non-EU molasses is already necessary for the EU’s Fermentation Industry but is neither 
a cost-effective nor viable solution due to quality concerns.  
• In 2018, the co-legislators prevented the inclusion of molasses in Annex IX to avoid its diversion 
from historic uses. As such, molasses and low-green syrup 2 are not listed in Annex IX part A and 
B and are not considered as feedstocks for sustainable biofuels. 
• Excluding all food- and feed-based biofuels (including bio-ethanol based on molasses made from 
sugar beets), as well as intermediate crops, from the RED II transport target would result in GHG 
savings of 66 million tonnes of CO 2 equivalent in 2030 and an average GHG abatement cost re-
duction of 64%.1  
• As a well-established sector of the European Bioeconomy and enabler of circular economy, EFG 
members contribute to the objectives of the European Green Deal by keeping valuable co-prod-
ucts such as molasses in the cycle and creating jobs in Europe.

The fermentation industry has been enabling the European Bioeconomy and sustainable chemicals produc-
tion for centuries in Europe. There is a well-established high-value market for molasses and low -green 
syrup 2 in human (yeast, bread, confectionery, beer , and citric acid) and animal nutrition. By using co-
products of the sugar production, our industry has contributed to a robust and sustainable circular econ-
omy in Europe for decades. Preserving and encouraging bio-based chemical industries in Europe is in line 
with the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability. 
When reviewing RED II and to ensure a level playing field, we ask policymakers to consider the following:  
• Molasses are neither waste nor residues, 
but high value nutritional co-products of the 
sugar industry when transforming sugar 
beets into sugar.  
o This is recognised both by the sugar and 
the molasses industries. Molasses have a 
high financial value. Molasses prices are 
based on raw sugar content, the result-
ing prices are nearly equivalent to sugar 
prices in relation to raw sugar content.   
o Classified as  feed materials, 2 molasses 
are subject to technical regulations to 
guarantee food safety. Adding molasses 
and/or low-green syrup  2 to Annex IX 
would be contrary to the principle of the 
Waste Hierarchy.3  
• Molasses are scarce: The availability of EU-sourced molasses at food grade quality is key to the suc-
cess of the European fermentation industry.  
o The European yeast and fermentation industry heavily relies on molasses to manufacture food and 
feed products. There are not enough molasses available in EU. The EU needs to import more than 
1.8 million tons of molasses every year, resulting in a structural deficit in the supply of molasses.4 
o Molasses in Europe are produced from sugar beets, their production is vulnerable to climatic and 
other environmental factors such as pests, which further increase their scarcity. 
o Any added competition for European high-quality molasses or low green syrup 2 from the biofuel 
industry would be detrimental to the European fermentation industry. 
• High-value food and feed uses should be prioritised over low-value energy uses especially when dis-
placing historic uses. Prioritisation of uses needs to be in line with circular economy, resource effi-
ciency principles and the intention of the Renewable Energy Directive.  
o During the previous revision of the Directive (RED II), the co-legislators jointly agreed that molasses 
should not be added to Annex IX of the Directive. This would divert molasses from their historic 
uses rather than creating and incentivising new, unused feedstocks for biofuels.5

When refining sugar beets into sugar one of the two 
high-value co-products results, based on a decision 
made by the producer for a given year: 
Low-green syrup 2 results when the crystallization 
process is deliberately stopped after the second refin-
ing round. It contains ca. 56 % sugar. In this case no 
molasses are produced.  
Molasses are a co-product resulting when three 
rounds of crystallization are carried out. The thick and 
sticky syrup has a high nutritional value. It contains on 
average 50% of sugar and minerals such as iron, cal-
cium, magnesium and potassium.

• Molasses create sustainable jobs in the European Union.  
o Our Members have 19 industrial plants in 11  European countries (7,065 direct jobs and around 
25,000 direct and indirect jobs).  The inclusion of molasses in Annex IX would result in increased 
imports of products, such as yeast and citric acid, from third countries, and thus less investment 
and jobs in Europe. 
• The inclusion of molasses in Annex IX would likely create noteworthy indirect greenhouse gas emis-
sions, while excluding food- and feed-based biofuels would reduce GHG emissions.  
o Indirect emissions are caused by the associated need of the f ermentation, bakery, brewery, and 
animal feed industries to replace, when possible 7, molasses by other feedstocks .8 This creates in-
direct land-use change related emissions through increased the sugar production in third countries 
to satisfy the growing European needs. 
o Excluding all food- and feed-based biofuels (including bio -ethanol based on molasses made from 
sugar beets), as well as intermediate crops, from the RED  II transport target would result in GHG 
savings of 66 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent in 2030 and an average GHG abatement cost reduc-
tion of 64%.9  
 
Promoting or providing incentives for the use of molasses for biofuels, through their potential inclusion in 
Annex IX, would divert them from well-established food and animal feed applications. This would put at 
risk the existence of traditional sectors of the European bioeconomy. Therefore, we call on the European 
Commission and the co -legislators to support the European Fermentat ion Industry by protecting its sus-
tainable business model and its continued access 
to raw materials. Thus, the European fermentation 
industry strongly recommend s that molasses 
should remain excluded from Annex IX, today and 
in all future reviews.

About the European Fermentation Group (EFG) 
EFG is the voice of the European fermentation industry vis-
à-vis EU institutions, national governments, and civil soci-
ety. The main objective of the EFG is to ensure that EU pol-
icies promote the competitiveness of the fermentation in-
dustry contributing to the development of a sustainable 
European bioeconomy. Our companies mainly produce cit-
ric acid, lactic acid, vitamins, amino acids for animal feed 
and penicillin for antibiotics.

Fertilizers Europe’s reply to public consultation on the European 
Commission revision of the Renewable Energy Directive 
 
Fertilizers Europe welcomes the possibility to share its views  on the revision of the  European 
Commission revision of the Renewable Energy Directive. 
The European fertilizer industry supports the European Green Deal ambition of climate neutrality by 
2050. Already today the main European fertilizer products have a climate footprint that is typically 
half of the global average.  
The EU fertilizer industry  welcomes that the revision of the Directive recognizes the significant 
potential of R enewable Fuels of Non-Biological Origin (RFNBOs). In addition to the critical role of 
hydrogen, it is important to note that another RFNBO, ammonia, can be transported more easily and 
cost-effectively and that it also has tremendous potential as a true zero-carbon fuel. The revision of 
the Renewable Energy Directive has the potential to enable the development of a hydrogen economy 
and support the decarbonization of hard-to-abate-industries, notably the fertilizer sector. However, 
to reach such goals, Fertilizers Europe would like to raise the following strategic priorities: 
 
1. Ensure that the industry target for renewable hydrogen use is realistic, technology-neutral 
and that its design supports industrial decarbonization.  
a. CAPEX and OPEX s upportive mechanisms must be made explicit and  should be 
considered as prerequisites to reaching the target. These should be set at EU level.  
b. In a number of cases, notably ammonia production, the industry target on hydrogen 
use has shifted the pressure of higher production cost to the middle of the value chain. 
There needs to be coherence with downstream markets.

c. Access to renewable energy in the relevant Member States will be critical to reaching 
such a target. We ask to link the production of renewable in Member States to the 
RFNBO industry target. Furthermore, w hile Fertilizers Europe supports the principle 
of additionality, it raises the concern that this will be, alongside access to funding, the 
most significant determining factor in reaching such a target. We call on a relaxation 
of additionality criteria in the initial stage of the hydrogen economy development . 
Furthermore, an RFNBO definition cannot be left to a separate delegated act as the 
target is meaningless without a definition. 
d. The varied context between Member States’ hydrogen industrial landscapes  and 
future production of renewable hydrogen (due in large part to access to renewable  
energy) must be considered and flexibility must be given to achieve the renewable 
hydrogen industry target. This notably includes the ability for companies and Member 
States to choose the best technological pathway rather than have it forced top-down.  
 
2. A thorough economic impact assessment on the sector’s impacted by the new target on 
hydrogen use in industry is needed.  
 
3. We invite the Commission to actively recognize industry -led existing or in development 
RFNBO certification schemes when developing its methodology.  
 
In this regard, Fertilizers Europe would like to share the following recommendations on the revision 
of the Renewable Energy Directive. 
• We support the increased renewable energy targets, which are in line with the 55% reduction 
targets. Regarding the introduction of new specific targets for industry, specifically the 50% 
share of hydrogen used in industry to come from RFNBOs, we accept its premise as a driver

of the development of the hydrogen economy. However, for such a specific target to b e 
successful at any level, a number of issues must be addressed: 
o Availability of renewable electricity: while the Fertilizer Industry is committed to the 
decarbonization process, the current proposal does not adequately address critical 
points necessary for the development of the hydrogen economy. First and foremost, 
the availability of renewable energy and the rules under which it can be allocated 
towards RFNBO production are critical elements in realizing any renewable hydrogen 
industry target. It is unlikely that in the short term any given Member State will have 
sufficient renewable hydrogen production to export to other Member States. 
Therefore, we believe that as the share of renewable hydrogen that can be  used in 
industry will be closely linked to the share of renewable energy in a Member State, 
that this should be reflected in the size of the target. Secondly, while not in the scope 
of the RED II revision, the rules that will be set in the upcoming delega ted act on 
renewable electricity for RFNBOs will be critical in reaching such a target. While 
Fertilizers Europe supports the principle of additionality (the idea that additional 
renewable electricity consumption must always be covered by additional renewa ble 
capacity), there are several concerns regarding its practical application that must be 
addressed. The European Commission should take into consideration the significant 
challenges posed by such requirement. Until there will be sufficient and affordable  
access to new renewables, the additionality requirement would entail additional 
barriers to the promotion of investment for projects green hydrogen and green 
ammonia. Furthermore, such restrictions will only apply to hydrogen producers and 
not all users o f renewable electricity, which will lead to a discriminatory market.  We 
call for a relaxation of additionality criteria in the short term to ensure the sector can 
take off the ground.  
RFNBO definition cannot be left to a later delegated act  as the target is meaningless 
without a definition. 
 
4 
 
o A technology neutral approach must be adopted : We cannot accept that such a 
critical target forgoes the principle of technology neutrality, even more so when it is 
set at such an ambitious level. This is particular critical in the case of renewable 
hydrogen use in industry for two reasons. First, the availability of renewable electricity 
in different Member States varies dramatically, with s ome not likely to have any 
significant production in both the short and long term due to a lack of wind and sun. 
Second, even in the most optimistic scenarios, the cost of renewable hydrogen will be 
too steep for industries to be commercially viable, and this will be further exacerbated 
by the inherent intermittent nature of renewable energy.  
Enabling ‘bridging’ low -carbon technologies, such as the use of Carbon Capture and 
Storage, would support the industry’s decarbonization in a transition phase, and the 
option to generate hydrogen via nuclear would help palliate the issue of intermittency 
by enabling access to a low carbon electricity grid in times of need. There also exist a 
wide array of low -carbon alternatives for low-carbon hydrogen production (e.g.  
biogas, waste recycling, pyrolysis, etc.), which should all have a role to play depending 
on the industrial context.  
It is understood that low -carbon and CCS -derived hydrogen will be delt with in the 
upcoming so-called ‘Gas Package’, however the link between these two legislations is 
unclear and unlikely to sufficiently guarantee technology neutrality therefore leading 
to a lot of uncertainty. Member States and companies should have the opportunity to 
choose the best most suitable decarbonization route based on their specific contexts.  
o Such a target must be extended to the downstream value chain : ammonia 
production is located in the middle of the value chain. While it is understandable that 
such an industry target would support hydrogen producers in scaling up production 
by ensuring they have a market, this merely passes on the burden on the next step in 
the value chain as any resulting product will necessaril y be more expensive to 
produce. There needs to be coherence with downstream markets.

o Support scheme : the sectors concerned by this target have not been subject to a 
proper economic assessment and do not have any guarantee of a downstream 
renewable produc t market. While the ammonia industry will strive to maximize its 
existing assets, plants will require significant modifications to be able to start both 
producing and using renewable hydrogen. The industry is therefore faced with high 
CAPEX and OPEX alongside no guarantees on downstream markets. There is therefore 
a critical need for such an industry target to be specifically linked to clear funding 
mechanisms. We do not accept that funding mechanisms are solely mentioned in the 
recitals as this provides no  concrete commitment. We ask for the inclusion, in the 
Directive, of clear support schemes for projects shifting their hydrogen use towards 
RFNBO. This could be in the form of Contracts for Difference, which should be 
harmonized at EU level but also take i nto account the significant regional differences 
that exist.  
• We are very critical of the accompanying Impact Assessment, which is both vague and unclear 
on several critical aspects. This is particularly concerning in the context of the 50% RFNBO 
industry target.  
o There is no detailed and clear explanation of how the European Commission decided 
on the 50% value and on its feasibility, apart from references to the hydrogen strategy 
and other non-public data. It is however clear that the main part of the burden will be 
on the ammonia sector. We call for a more detailed explanation on the 2030 scenario 
for industrial use of hydrogen.  
o In setting this target, the impact assessment does not give a thorough assessment of 
the economic impact it wil l have on the most concerned sectors , notably fertilizers 
(ammonia production). Due to the exclusion of a significant part of the hydrogen used 
in refineries from the industry target, ammonia production for industrial purposes will 
still represent a significant part  of the industry hydrogen use in 2030. Its current  
production is based on natural gas and is an extremely integrated process. Therefore, 
regardless of the availability of the renewable hydrogen, the retrofitting of ammonia

production to accommoda te a high proportion of hydrogen from a different source 
will be both extremely costly and complex.  A thorough economic impact assessment 
is therefore needed, including on downstream markets.  
• We support the introduction of a certification methodology to i dentify which products are 
made via total or partial renewable fuels. This is critical as such products will be more 
expensive to produce and will thus require a premium. We invite the Commission to actively 
recognize industry-led existing or in development certification schemes when developing its 
methodology.  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
About Fertilizers Europe 
Fertilizers Europe represents the interests of the majority of mineral fertilizer manufacturers in the 
European Union. The association’s membership comprises 16 fertilizer manufacturers from countries 
across the Union and eight national fertilizer associations. The association communicates with a wide 
variety of stakeholders,  institutions, European and national policy -makers and members of the 
general public who seek information on fertilizer products and application technology, and topics 
relating to today’s agricultural, environmental and economic challenges.

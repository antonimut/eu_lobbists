EUROFER Key Messages 
• The Energy Efficiency Directive (EED) should remain focused on its primary objective of 
promoting energy efficiency and refrain from double regulation with other legislation. 
• The energy efficiency target should be revised to avoid a cap on economic growth and 
barriers to decarbonisation technologies  
• The Energy Efficiency First Principle should be implemented in such a way as to not 
impose regulatory burdens on industrial energy consumers. 
• Cost-efficient solutions should be promoted through energy savings  that reflect the 
saving potential of different sectors and technologies. Energy savings deriving from the 
EU Emission Trading Schemes (EU ETS) should be fully accounted for. 
• Low-carbon energy solutions should be fostered primarily in those sectors with the 
highest saving potential, such as the heating and cooling sector s, avoiding additional 
constraints on energy-intensive industries. 
Introduction 
Today, high energy prices affect the competitiveness of European energy -intensive industries 
(EIIs), and in particular the steel industry vis-a-vis competitors in third countries. A coherent EU 
energy policy framework should aim at ensuring affordable energy prices, so to boost industrial 
competitiveness and avoid losing further market shares at a global level. 
Due to the high proportion of energy costs in total production costs, energy efficiency is 
perceived by steel companies as a key element for preserving the ir competitiveness. European 
steel producers  operate processes that are already very close to thermodynamical limits in 
terms of energy consumption. Deeper emissions reductions are only possible with the 
deployment and rollout of breakthrough technologies that require, among others, access to 
abundant and competitive low carbon energy sources , including hydrogen and electricity. This 
is why minimizing the impact of regulatory costs related to the implementation of the EED should 
be a priority when it comes to EIIs and the steel industry. 
Steel companies are already subject to the obligation to perform energy audits under the EED.

The revision of the Energy Efficiency Directive shall focus on those sectors with the highest 
potential in terms of savings, such as the building sector while avoiding setting rules that may cap 
industrial growth and result in double regulation. In particular, the following elements should be 
taken into account: 
1. Avoiding double regulation for industry 
Industrial sectors such as steel are already subject to other pieces of legislation aimed at reducing 
energy consumption and e missions levels, such as the Emissions Trading System (ETS) , the 
Industrial Emissions Directive (IED) , and other national or regional measures. By extending the 
scope of energy audits to renewable energy consumption and production to industry (new Annex 
VI), the EED’s scope would overlap with existing legislation and create double regulation. 
The D irective should instead focus on promoting energy efficiency , leaving issues such as 
support to renewable energy or limitation of fossil fuels’ use to other legislation.   
Steel producers are already subject to the obligation to perform energy audits and shall be 
safeguarded against additional regulatory burdens and obligation s. To avoid competition 
distortions, ins tallations shall be entitled to take part in national energy -saving schemes – 
where they exist - only on a voluntary basis. The EED should target those sectors with the highest 
untapped potential in terms of energy efficiency, such as buildings. 
2. Avoiding a cap on economic growth and decarbonisation technologies through adequately 
formulated energy efficiency targets (art. 3 and 4) 
The energy efficiency target is expressed as a linear and absolute reduction of the primary and/or 
final energy consumption. Ho wever, a reduction in energy consumption does not necessarily 
lead to increased energy efficiency . On the contrary , such a definition could impose a cap on 
future growth and create the wrong incentive to produce less. The roll-out of breakthrough 
technologies at industrial scale, which is indispensable for achieving the decarbonisation target, 
will lead to an increase in energy use in steel and other energy-intensive industries (EIIs). At the 
same time, the electrification of other sectors and the shift to low carbon energy sources such 
as biomass will further contribute to the increase of global energy consumption. The increasing 
share of intermittent renewable s in the energy mix will require also flexibility from large 
industrial energy consumers, with  an impact on the energy efficiency of industrial processes. 
These dynamics must be reflected in the design of the EU energy efficiency targets. 
For these reasons, the indicative nature of the target should be maintained. The target should 
be calculated as an actual energy efficiency indicator , to reflect the technical and economic 
potential rather than as a target on  absolute energy reduction. The Member States shall retain 
the possibility of expressing the efficiency target either on primary or on final energy 
consumption.

3. Minimising regulatory burden stemming from the implementation of the Energy Efficiency 
First Principle on industrial energy consumers (art. 3, 25 and 11, Annexes VI and XII) 
Member States ' efforts (art. 4{2}) to wards collective energy-saving targets  or contributions  
should remain indicative, leaving room for governments to tailor their policies according to local 
conditions such as their energy mix and to introduce certain technologies instead of others. The 
latter possibility might lead to varying energy needs and impacts on energy efficiency.  
In this regard, as long as the energy efficiency indicator is expressed in terms of energy 
consumption, we oppose the obligation for the Member States to take into account in t heir 
planning, in their  policy, and their major investment decisions, energy efficiency solutions 
concerning non-energy sectors, which would include industries. This would  result in an 
increased burden for diversified sectors that have limited space for improvement (art. 3 and 25 
combined).  
Prioritising Energy Management Systems in energy end -use sectors - according to art. 11(1) – is 
a welcomed proposal. However, it must be guaranteed that audits, permitting requirements, and 
tariff setting will not pose additional regulatory burden s on steelmakers. Henceforth , the 
minimum “criteria” for energy audits should remain “guidelines” (Annex VI) and, with regard 
to the scope of energy audits  (point c), these should investigate cost -potential solutions for 
“efficient” energy consumption rather than solutions to “decrease energy consumption”.  
Furthermore, the obligation for companies to publish the results of energy efficiency audits and 
the information on implemented measures, in the company’s annual activity reports should be 
removed, since this would entail the disclosure of confidential information, particularly crucial 
for companies such as steelmakers exposed to global markets and international competition (art. 
11(2) and 11(3)). 
4. Promoting cost -efficient solutions  through energy savings obligations reflecting the 
potential of sectors and technologies (art. 8 and 9, Annex V) 
Once the climate and energy targets are set, the regulatory  framework shall ensure these are 
achieved in the most cost -efficient way. This requires reliability and sufficient flexibility in the 
rules. In this context, the annual energy saving obligations schemes foreseen in the new article 
9 do not take into accou nt that the saving potential varies across sectors and technologies , 
Member States , and over  time. Therefore, any measures need to be based on an EU -wide 
holistic, transparent , and reliable planning taking international competitiveness fully into 
account. Alternative measures should be prioriti sed over any obligation schemes  as means to 
achieve energy savings  while according flexibility is necessary to ensure that measures are set 
when and where those are most cost-efficient. Where obligation schemes are in place, industrial 
installations shall not be subject to direct nor indirect costs of such measures. From an industrial

5. Promoting and enabling low-carbon energy solutions in the heating and cooling sector (Art. 
24 and Annex III) 
The European Commissio n’s proposals both for the Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency 
Directives go in the right direction concerns the promotion of  an efficient heating and cooling 
sector and sectoral-integration; policy solutions such as, inter alia, the extension of Third-Party 
Access should apply  to industrial providers  of waste heat to District Heating and Cooling 
Systems operators (DHC). The relative amendment under the RED II is , therefore, a welcomed 
proposal. Nonetheless, the role of steelmakers as providers of low -carbon heat to civil and 
economic sectors should be  preserved feasibly; any restriction for the use of industrial waste 
heat is should be avoided under the EED and REDII.  
Against this back ground, the Commission proposed a direct CO2 emissions target value - 270 
gCO2 per 1 kWh  - which implies that only gas -fueled high-efficiency cogeneration installations 
will meet the criteria of Annex III. Other conventional fuels and liquid or gaseous residual fuels 
used in steel production processes will be out of the scope of this directive since these do not 
meet the prescribed criteria for high -efficiency cogeneration. The use of waste heat from steel 
plants (e.g., on-site CHP units) would also be drastically restricted and the use of such a key CO2 
mitigating technology for the industry precluded.

EUROFER Key Messages 
• The achievement of targets on the integration of renewable and low carbon energ y in the 
European industrial sector requires inevitably the deployment of a considerable amount of 
renewable and low carbon electricity and hydrogen at large scale, making them available 
at cost-competitive prices, and realising the needed infrastructure (i.e., for all gasses and 
electricity).  
• Any measure  should be set via holistic, transparent , and realistic assessments of the 
demand, supply , and infrastructure needs  of the targeted sectors . The i nternational 
competitiveness of industrial energy users  needs to be taken into account  among other 
determining criteria. 
• The target for promoting the consumption of renewable energies and renewable fuels of 
non-biological origin (RFNBOs) in industry should be assessed together with reform of the 
EU Gas Framework since the legal framework for a hydrogen market and infrastructure is 
yet to be defined. Such a holistic approach to impact assessments should also apply to 
other relevant legal provisions.  
• Any mandate, whether indicative or binding, at all levels, on RFNBOs and RES consumption 
should be applied only if the necessary market and regulatory conditions and dedicated 
support measures (i.e., CEEAG, Carbon Contracts for Difference, IPCEI, etc) are in place. 
This is crucial for maintaining the international competitiveness of the sectors involved.  
• A complete and clear definition of renewable hydrogen should be provided in the text of 
the Renewable Energy Directive and should not be left to secon dary legislation (i.e., 
delegated acts), to allow economic operators the necessary certainty to conduct 
investments in clean and breakthrough technologies. 
• The application of the additionality principle for both industry RES consumption and the 
production of RFNBOs must be commensurate enough to optimise the currently available 
and future supply of renewable and low carbon energy.  
• Access to renewable Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs ) for energy-intensive industries 
shall be strengthened and supported.

Introduction  
The transition of the European steel industry towards climate neutrality will build on the 
deployment of new technologies and will be dependent on the reliable availability of low carbon 
energy (mainly electricity and hydrogen) at economically viable  and affordable prices. It is 
estimated that the European steel industry alone will require approximately 400TWh of CO2-free 
electricity every year by 2050 (including for the production and use of hydrogen), supported by 
adequate transport infrastructures guaranteeing a stable, continu ous, and secured supply of 
energy1.  
A supportive EU regulatory framework is henceforth a key prerequisite. In this sense, the revision 
of the Renewable Energy Directive (RED) shall aim at fostering the uptake  of renewables by 
energy-intensive industries (EIIs) and thus accelerate the transition towards climate neutrality by 
2050. This must occur cost-effectively due to the exposure of energy -intensive industries to 
international competitiveness and related risk of carbon leakage. Low bargaining power vis-à-
vis energy producers must be carefully addressed and mitigated as the lack thereof would 
hamper the access to renewables by energy-intensive industries (EIIs). Any measure within the 
Renewable Energy Directive  cannot be set withou t undergoing a  holistic assessment of real 
energy demands across various sectors of the Union , including the existence of the necessary 
market and economic conditions, and on the possible support measures to be designed for large 
energy consumers, enabling both access and use of renewable and low carbon energy sources at 
affordable prices. Significant differences between the Member States and regions in terms of 
energy mixes and availability of energy from renewable sources  persist and  these must be 
considered carefully when setting measures to promote the use of renewable and low carbon 
energies in the European industry. 
Industrial sectors such as steel are already subject to legislations that aim at reducing energy 
consumption and emissions levels, such as the ETS and the EED, along with other national or 
regional measures.  
Any new provision of the revised RED should ensure the alignment with the ETS and EED and 
guarantee that EIIs requirement of access to cost-affordable low carbon energy, availability of 
adequate volumes, and security of supply  is properly taken into account  while striving infant 
clean technologies will unavoidably increase energy consumption . In this regard,  EIIs and the

In this context, the European steel industry puts forward the following policy recommendations 
for the revision of the Renewable Energy Directive:  
1. Setting the RES targets at EU and sectorial level  based on  a holistic, transparent , and 
realistic assessment of demand, supply, and infrastructure needs (art. 3) 
Targets aimed at increasing the absorption of renewables in the EU shall be set based on  a 
transparent and holistic assessment, focusing also on industrial energy consumption. Such an 
assessment should take into account E U industries’ international competitiveness and carbon 
leakage risk, the global level of GHG emissions, the administrative hurdles , and procedures 
connected with the deployment of renewable generation units at the national level, investment 
predictability, the status of the EU electricity grid, including the level of development of  smart 
grids and Member States differences in RES availability across the EU.   
Such assessment needs to be done in line and coherently with the reform of the Gas and 
Hydrogen Dec arbonisation Package  (Q4, 2021) , as this will be the  essential legislative pillar 
establishing the EU approach on the  nascent hydrogen market, setting rules on fundamental 
elements of the low carbon and renewable gasses value chain. 
2. Setting up the adequate regulatory framework supporting the uptake of low carbon 
energies in industry (new art. 22a [I])

4. Providing a clear and complete legal definition of renewable hydrogen within the text of 
the Renewable Energy Directive (RED) in line with the structure of art. 2  
Substantial reduction of CO 2 emissions within the European steel industry can only be achieved 
through considerable investments in clean and breakthrough technologies which will require 
sizeable volumes of low carbon energies, in particular, fossil-free and renewable electricity and 
hydrogen supplied both via on-site generating assets (i.e., power plants located in the industrial 
installations), other national suppliers and/or imported, within and outside the EU.

Commission draft proposal and is left to be determined only through technical legislation in the 
RED framework (i.e., through the delegated act on Renewable Fuels of Non -Biological Origin 
[RFNBOs], pursuant art. 29a[3]).  
The lack of such a comprehensive and harmonised notion of renewable hydrogen within the RED 
text (as opposed to technical legislation) obstructs legal clarity, which is a necessary precondition 
for investment decisions from the steel industry in clean and breakthrough technologies, and for

5. Ensuring a commensurate application of the additionality principle for energy -intensive 
industries (art.3[4a]) 
The additionality principle  was primarily developed as sustainability criteria for the production 
and accounting of low-carbon and renewable fuels in the transport sector (art. 27). The proposal 
of the Commission extends the principle as well on industry sector, adding up additional 
conditions on what type of electricity can be considered as renewable.  
In 2050, the European steel industry  will need annually about 162 TWh of external electricity 
for steel production processes and about 234 TWh of electricity for the production of ~5.5 Mt 
of hydrogen. This corresponds to a total electricity need of about 400 TWh, 7 times higher than 
its actual consumption. As highlighted by a study from the Vrije Universiteit Brussel [VUB] 
commissioned on behalf of the Alliance of Energy Intensive Industries, the elec tricity needs of 
the energy-intensive sectors combined, is estimated to be about 3.000 and 4.000 TWh in 2050 4, 
against existing challenges around the EU grid’s stability vis-à-vis renewables integration and by 
the absence of efficient RES storage capacities.  
The demand for electricity from different energy-intensive sectors by 2030 and 2050 to meet 
the RED and in line with the GHG emissions reductions targets, requires the application of the 
additionality principle in a commensurate way  to facilitate the integration of RES in industry. 
The principle should unlock  the potential of all RES generating capacity in the European Union, 
both from existing assets and from planned ones.  
As currently formulated  (i.e., new electricity demand must be met by new RES g eneration 
capacity additional to a business -as-usual scenario)  and for the reasons specified below , the 
generic application of the additionality principle would risk slowing down investments in clean 
and breakthrough technologies , key for the reduction of GHG emissions of industry . Since the

An additional challenge faced by industry regarding additionality relates to  the integration of 
new renewable fluctuating electricity and the need for steel production to have a stable intake 
of energy flow. By evaluating and combining the additionality principle with the wording of new 
art. 22a in the case of intermittent supply, steel production and in particular of low carbon steel 
would be negatively affected  both from a technical and economic standpoint: Once the  
interruption of renewable electricity flow s materialises, for the characteristics of steel 
production, the energy void created needs to be promptly met with alternative and immediately 
available (therefore already existing) electricity, thus exposing the installation to factors well 
beyond its sphere of control. 
Therefore, also to guarantee the security of supply compatibly with any measure and/or targets 
in cases of  intermittency, the additionality principle should be applied with the related 
flexibility to never preclude industry’s ability to access the low carbon energy needed, including 
imports of electricity and hydrogen from third countries.

6. Reinforcing access to low -carbon energy sources for energy -intensive industries through 
open and improved access to renewable PPAs (art. 15[8]) 
Renewable Power Purchase Agreements ( RE-PPAs) would represent  an effective and viable 
solution for the uptake of renewable electricity in the EU and  the partial electrification of large 
industrial energy users. As corporate sourcing tools, PPAs allow companies to acquire power at a 
known price, reducing the risk of exposure to price volatility (market rules of law and demand) , 
ensuring the security of supply , and ultimately achieving environmental and sustainability 
ambitions.  
These aspects make RE-PPAs a valuable option for the iron and steel sector to complete its 
decarbonisation p athway. However, access to such power contracting  has been not uniform 
across industries and economic sectors whereby regulatory barriers (e.g., as renewable PPAs 
need several years before being concretely implemented while the electricity buyer is bound t o 
sign-off the contract well before the PPA -related project is constructed by the seller), economic  
obstacles (i.e., elevated prices deriving from stabilising intermittent electricity supply also known 
as firming/shaping costs) and financial challenges (i.e., insufficient-credit rating) barriers existed 
and are persisting. 
Henceforth, we see the necessity to make access to renewable PPAs as equal and open as 
possible by removing any existing barrier for energy-intensive industries and by providing the 
widest range possible of policy solutions  accordingly, exploring options including the 
establishment of an EU public fund window providing  credit risks guarantees for industrial off -
takers.  
4. Fostering energy system integration compatibly with energy -intensive industries 
decarbonisation pathway and need for security of energy supply (art. 20a) 
Transmission System Operators (TSO) and Distribution System Operators (DSO) have been 
playing and will keep playing a crucial role in delivering stable and reliable electricity to end-users. 
Energy-intensive industries in particular, given the centrality of energy for their production 
processes, require high standards of security of supply and stability of the grid, and therefore 
these must be guaranteed against the inherent challenges that the intermittency of renewable 
energy sources (solar and wind particularly) imposes.

7. Phasing out renewable schemes (art. 3{4a}) 
Due to the highly distortive impact of renewable schemes and their effects on the ramp-up of 
corporate sourcing tools (i.e., the much-preferred subsidised support for RES project developers 
versus private financed projects [corporate RES PPAs])5, these should be quickly phased out. As 
long as they are in place, EU-wide compulsory exemptions for energy-intensive sectors exposed 
to international competition such as steel should be maintained or put in place where they have 
not been implemented already. Moreover, where support schemes are maintained, these 
should be targeted only on immature technologies f or a limited time and be subject to 
competitive bidding based on  a technology -neutral approach  with a preference to pure 
investment support to minimise their distortive impact. 
8. Valorising the re-use of carbon as a means to reduce emissions through a technology push 
approach (art. 29a) 
The ambition to reduce GHG emissions  in the steel industry builds upon two main coexisting 
pathways, one aimed at re -using CO 2/CO rich gasses stemming from residual off -gasses of 
internal combustion processes (i.e., circular use of carbon) to produce low carbon added value 
products [industrial CCU/s applications] called Smart Carbon Usage and the other one aimed at 
directly avoiding emissions by shifting to renewable electricity and hydrogen-based steelmaking, 
called Carbon Direct Avoidance.  
Alternative fuels and feedstocks derived from industrial process gases represent a key enabler 
for achieving the energy and climate targets in Europe  for both pathways while maintaining the 
competitiveness of energy -intensive industries and enhancing the energy security of the EU 
economy. In many industries, there are large volumes of residual gases containing energy carriers 
like CO or H2 being produced in different processes. By applying ap propriate technologies and 
by bringing gases from different industries together, they can be converted into more valuable 
chemicals and fuels, thus reducing the direct carbon footprint and avoiding additional emissions. 
This technology can speed up decarbo nization in the steel industry parallel to the ramp -up of

9. Setting up a  supportive regulatory framework within the RED fostering co-processing 
technologies across industry sectors 
Developed initially in refining procedures, co-processing applications (i.e., procedure with which 
biomass feedstock is treated together with fossil feedstocks for the production of low -carbon 
fuels) are becoming one of the enablers of CO2 emissions reduction in the steel sector where a 
circular carbon feedstock is used both for primary steelmaking and as a basis for the production 
low-carbon products, fostering the sector decarbonising efforts in line with the Smart Carbon 
Usage technological pathway. 
The Renewable Energy Directive requires the Commission to adopt a delegated act determining 
the methods for allowing the quantification of the biogenic component in co -processes 
fuels/products. With a view to stimulate investments in such technologies and to appreciate their 
CO2 reduction potential, we deem it essential to apply a scheme of free -choice attribution of 
renewable carbon to the output stream of choice , according to the mass balance principle as 
the most effective way to unlock the opportunity for investments and by creating the appropriate 
regulatory conditions. 
10. Mitigating any regulatory and economic impacts of the certification schemes and 
obligations for energy users of RFNBOs and RCFs (art. 30) 
The current formulation of art. 30 does not allow for unequivocal identification of economic 
operators upon which the obligation to certify the RFNBOs and RCFs produced, used, and sold is 
placed upon, which increases the regulatory uncertainty for industrial energy users  and 
eventually also for the certification of low carbon and/or green steel. Furthermore, t he cost -
competitiveness of such schemes for end-users must be safeguarded, in respect of the final price 
the energy consumer has to pay for.

11. Promoting the role of industries as third-party providers of low carbon heat in the heating 
& cooling sector (art. 24) 
The use of heat and cold produced from renewables and waste  represents an important 
opportunity to contribute to the climate and energy targets. We welcome the higher targets in 
district heating and cooling systems which include recoverable w aste heat and the extension of 
the Third -Party Access obligation to DHC system operators for waste heat suppliers, which 
expressively recognizes industr ies such as the steel sector and tertiary sectors as potential 
providers of low carbon heat . However, we regret the option chosen by the European 
Commission under the revision of the Energy Efficiency Directive to establish a direct CO 2 
emissions threshold at 270gCO2/kWh of energy output for combined heat and power generation 
units. These are relevant on -site energy generating assets the steel industry resort to for both

New rules for the private sector (AMLR)  
Proposal for regulation COM(2021)420 
 
General remarks 
Finance Denmark supports the Commission’s Anti-money laundering and coun-
tering the financing of terrorism legislative package and a new and revised regu-
latory framework and supervision in the EU, and we strongly agree that increased 
harmonisation both in the regulation and in the supervision will strengthen the 
joint efforts in the area. Furthermore, we value the increased coordination be-
tween authorities and the focus on generally ensuring a level playing field in in 
the AML-framework.  
 
Finance Denmark finds it very positive that the majority of the directive will be 
transferred to a directly applicable regulation to ensure the harmonisation of the 
rules across the EU. A higher degree of detail and clarity i.e., in the rules on cus-
tomer due diligence (CDD) and beneficial ownership, will ensure a more uniform 
approach in the EU, which will benefit both the obliged entities and the custom-
ers. However, it is important to supplement this with an operational guidance for 
the rules and maintaining the risk-based approach. In this context Finance Den-
mark would like to highlight that the creation of a true single rulebooks precludes 
additional national rules and that the possibility to uphold such rules should be 
limited to the furthest extend possible.    
 
A high priority area for Finance Danmark is the opportunity for increased infor-
mation sharing and public-private cooperation. 
 
The ability to share data is an important tool in fighting money laundering and fi-
nancing of terrorism as combined data sources give a better basis to detect 
more criminals – and networks of criminal transactions - as well as detecting crim-
inals at an earlier stage, more sound and substantiated reports to the authorities 
on suspected money laundering and financing of terrorism and better secure-
ment of evidence.    
However, the Commission's proposal does neither provide a clear basis for infor-
mation sharing and increased sectoral cooperation – nor a basis for public pri-
vate partnerships. We believe that it is crucial and necessary to establish a clear 
legal basis for information sharing to strengthen the financial system against 
money laundering and terrorist financing.  
 
Another important focus for Finance Denmark is the rules on governance and the 
compliance functions. We see great value in harmonising the rules in this area –

Specific comments on the proposal 
Art.  2 
Finance Denmark suggests including a definition on ‘transaction’, which would 
be very helpful.  
 
Finance Denmark finds that a clarification on ‘senior management’ - which refers 
to both the board of directors and an officer/employee and on the other hand 
rec. 48, which seems somewhat unclear – is needed.  
 
We propose to clarify ‘Targeted financial sanctions”. The connection between 
AML/CTF and ‘evasion of targeted financial sanctions’ must be clarified, and an 
impact assessment be performed. The scope of ‘targeted financial sanctions’ is 
unclear – in some sections it is it linked to ‘proliferation financing’, but not in all. It 
is unclear what is meant by ‘measures should also be put in place to mitigate 
any risk of non-implementation or evasion of targeted financial sanctions’ (rec. 
7). Also unclear to what extent this will be covered by the mandate of AMLA.  
 
Art. 3 
We believe, traders in good should remain an obliged entity covered by the AML 
regulation to ensure a level playing field. The EU wide cash limit would not ap-
pear to serve a sufficient mitigating measure, leaving the area and sectors in-
volved unregulated and highly exposed in an AML context. The scope of obliged 
entities should at least include luxury goods of high value, such as clothes, bags, 
watches, and the like.

Art. 7-8 
Finance Denmark proposes to clarify the role of internal audit in accordance 
with the normal function hereof: “7(1)(c) an independent audit function to eval-
uate whether test the internal policies, controls and procedures referred to in 
point (a) are operating effectively”. 
 
Finance Denmark notes that art. 7 includes ‘proliferation financing targeted 
sanctions’, but clarification should be provided if this includes all ‘targeted finan-
cial sanctions’ issued by EU under art. 29 Treaty on EU and regulations under art. 
215 Treaty on functioning of European Union, see definition of ‘targeted financial 
sanctions’. If so, this would go beyond FATF rec. 1 and 7, which aim at countering 
proliferation of weapons of mass destruction in relation to Iran and DPRK. 
 
Furthermore, art. 7.b.a-f apply also in relation to sanctions, but sanctions are not 
explicitly referred to. Hence, the expectations for the sanctions are unclear. All 
banks must have processes in place for handling sanctions risks and compliance, 
but to the extent this is also required under the AMLR, there is a risk that banks 
need to apply parallel internal policies in the sanctions area. Such setup might 
conflict with the general ambition of many banks to have a common set of inter-
nal sanctions standards including all sanctions relevant for the bank’s operations, 
rather than a fragmented set of standards. International sanctions may only be 
connected to AML and CTF risks to the extent it makes sense from an ML and TF 
risk perspective. It should furthermore be mentioned that supervision may be 
fragmented and inefficient to the extent parts of the sanction’s compliance are 
subject to AMLR and hence potentially the supervision of AMLA, whilst other 
sanctions are not. Further analysis is much welcomed on this topic.  
 
Regarding art. 8 there are no risk factors or risk variables connected to sanctions 
in the annexes, which is much needed for obliged entities to understand risks 
connected to sanctions. Further guidance in terms of risks connected to prolifera-
tion financing-related targeted financial sanctions must be provided both on an 
EU-level in the supranational risk assessment and by member states in their na-
tional risk assessments. 
 
Art. 9 
The proposed role and responsibilities for the compliance manager falls outside 
the scope of the distribution of roles and responsibilities in Nordic corporate law 
within the Board of directors (BoD) and between the BoD and daily manage-
ment, hence, we suggest amending the article to accommodate this.

We suggest aligning the definition of: “management body” to mach point (7) of 
Article 3(1) Directive 2013/36/EU. The definition in Directive 2013/36/EU takes into 
account the different company law governance structures in EU member states 
where strategic decision-making and effective direction can be placed in the 
same one body or different bodies. 
 
As a general remark we therefore want to point out the need for clarity about 
the two compliance roles in relation to national company law and current guide-
lines on governance. Furthermore, we ask for clarity on the distinction between 
the compliance manager and the compliance officer, incl. where operational 
and business-related task and responsibilities may sit.  
Finally, we suggest to clarify that the compliance manager and compliance of-
ficer may delegate responsibilities in the performance of the listed responsibilities.  
 
Art. 11 
Art. 11 (2) may be understood as an obligation for employees to develop and 
maintain a list of all existing and potential connections between the employees 
and customers and potential customers, which seems disproportionate, also con-
sidering that banks are already subject to conflict of interest rules. 
 
We suggest a clarification on the meaning of repute and how this is to be as-
sessed and on what is meant by “private or professional relationship” in this con-
text. 
 
Art. 15 
The current rule in AMLD5 art. 11(B) only covers ‘carrying out’ transactions. It is un-
clear what added value the proposed wording regarding ‘involved in’ gives in 
relation to AML/CFT why we suggest to delete the latter.    
   
Art. 16 
‘Persons purporting to act on behalf of’: We find that the rule would benefit from 
being formulated in a more risk-based manner. Clarification is needed whether 
this includes all persons acting on behalf of the customer, e.g. initiates transac-
tions, with disposal rights to accounts etc., or only those representing the cus-
tomer in relation to the business relationship. We would understand the term to 
include primarily the person(s) de facto representing the customer, i.e. initiating 
or extending the business relationship, deciding terms and conditions of agree-
ments, rather than the customer’s employees acting in accordance with an em-
ployee mandate.

Art. 18  
Regarding’ legal representatives’ and’ person action on their behalf’: We find 
the rule would benefit from being formulated in a more risk-based manner.  
This requirement potentially entails a huge number of names to register without 
much reason from a risk perspective, i.e., authorised representatives not de facto 
representing the customer. We find that clarification should be made specifically 
regarding agents (in Danish: ‘stillingsfuldmagt’). 
 
Regarding senior managing officials 
Senior managing officials’: Clarification of scope and factors to assess who to 
consider beneficial owner(s) is crucial to avoid individual interpretations and 
fragmented approach.  
 
Regarding information on ‘place of birth’ 
We believe, information on place of birth will not increase the value of the CDD. 
Requirements for ‘place of birth’ and ‘nationality’ also have the consequence 
that obliged entities will only be able to use passports as the identification docu-
ment. This could lead to difficulties with citizens – e.g., elderly citizens – whose 
passport has expired. If the obligation to collect information on place of birth is 
maintained, there is a need for clarification on the extent – would it be country, 
city or both? Further, any due diligence innovations such as this should be subject 
to a transitional period in terms of implementation, so that such information may 
be updated as part of ongoing due diligence. 
 
Regarding the national identifications number 
The national identification number is the single most important information when 
identifying customers why we suggest changing the wording from a possibility to 
leave this information out when this is ‘not applicable’ (meaning cases where the 
customers do not have such a number) and change it to an obligation to obtain 
an equivalent number (e.g., passport number).    
 
Article 21 
As for ‘frequency of updating of customer information shall in any case not ex-
ceed five years’ please clarify whether ODD has to be finalized within 5 years, or 
merely be started up within 5 years. 
 
Additionally, we suggest that the frequency-period could be extended for low-
risk customers. E.g., in Denmark, we have mortgage customers who only have 
mortgage products where a longer period of frequency for updating customer 
information would be reasonable.

Art. 30 
We believe it does not seem reasonable from a risk-based perspective to always 
perform enhanced due diligence measures, incl. the specific enhanced due dili-
gence measures set out in art. 30, where two institutions have a principal-to-prin-
cipal relationship with no provision of products or services to underlying custom-
ers of the counterparty. The specific EDD measures are typically not adapted to 
these kinds of relationships, and it is unclear whether the definition or art. 30 leave 
room for a risk-based approach. 
 
Art. 32 
Finance Denmark welcomes a risk-based approach in relation to customers or 
beneficial owners being political exposed persons where the actual ML/TF risks 
do not motivate enhanced due diligence measures. Examples could be where a 
business relationship with no or very few transactions or where the PEP’s political 
position does not influence the customer relationship, i.e. no link between the po-
litical position and the person’s functions with the customer.  
 
We also highly welcome a more risk-based approach for state-owned compa-
nies, which should not as a customer become subject to enhanced PEP 
measures per default as a result of PEP identification.  
 
We suggest clarifying the principle outlined in art. 32 (2) (b) so that it will be ex-
tended to all business relationships. 
 
Furthermore, it is unclear form the wording what the legal consequences of hav-
ing a customer with a beneficial owner that is a politically exposed person. We 
suggest for this to be clarified throughout the provision.  
 
Art. 40 
Banks are subject to very extensive regulation on outsourcing, therefore any ad-
ditional regulation specifically for the AML-area seems unfounded, unnecessarily 
complicating, and burdensome. We contest any limitations made compared to 
AMLD4.  
 
As an overall remark, it is important to emphasise that there is a need to be able 
to outsource the tasks according to the AMLR, except for the compliance func-
tions and internal audit as well as the ultimate responsibility for compliance with 
the rules.

Specifically, Finance Denmark suggests clarifying this by redrafting the proposed 
art. 40(1) as follows: 1. Obliged entities may outsource tasks deriving from require-
ments under this Regulation to an agent or external service provider, whether a 
natural or legal person, with the exception of natural or legal persons residing or 
established in third countries identified pursuant to Section 2 of this Chapter. 
 
Art. 42  
Finance Denmark proposes further clarity on this provision, specifically how “at 
least one of the following” in (a)-(d) should be understood and how such assess-
ment is meant to be performed?  
 
The scope and range of art. 42 (1) (d) seems to have been inspired by the regu-
lation on mergers. In an AML context this provision will take the identification of 
beneficial owners to an extent and uncertainty which could jeopardize the ef-
forts to provide further clarity and harmonise with the regulation. 
 
Finance Denmark suggests changing the provision or expanding it with examples 
on how to delimit the scope and range. 
 
Art. 44  
Finance Denmark welcomes the standardization of data to be collected by 
obliged entities for establishing beneficial ownership. However, the 14-calendar 
day requirement for providing beneficial ownership information is too short, espe-
cially in cases of complex corporate set-ups. 
 
Beneficial ownership information should for the sake of transparency and opera-
tional reasons contain information on status as political exposed person or family 
member or close associates hereto. 
 
Finally, Finance Denmark suggest that obliged entities should be able to place 
full reliance on the national beneficial owner registers and as such use them as a 
single source for identifying and verifying beneficial owners.  
   
Art. 50 
Finance Denmark suggests clarifying the obligation in regard to ‘directly’. 
Obliged entities should only support during weekdays and regular office hours. 
Generally, the deadlines proposed are too short to manage in practice.    
   
Article 54 
To strengthen the fight against money laundering and terrorist financing, we see 
a great need for stronger and better cooperation in the financial sector.

The ability to share data is an important tool in fighting money laundering and fi-
nancing of terrorism as combined data sources give a better basis to identify sus-
picious transactions and to detect more criminals.  
  
Based on the wording of article 54(5), we suggest that the Commission should 
broaden the scope of the possibility to share information from “the same cus-
tomer and the same transaction involving two or more credit institutions” to “the 
same customer or same transaction involving two or more credit institutions”.  
 
Article 56 
This provision gives rise to discussions in Denmark including the FATF's rec. 11. 
Therefore, we have certain reservations in making remarks on this provision. On 
the one hand, it is beneficial to have a clear rule and some institutions with tech-
nically advanced monitoring capabilities make use of stored information 
throughout the customer relationship. On the other hand, it creates practical 
challenges in relation to storage, just as there are privacy and data minimisation 
considerations that speak against it.

US Industrial Pellet Association (USIPA) position on RED III 
 
Sustainable biomass is currently the largest renewable energy source in Europe and has proven to 
be a fast and reliable way to decarbonise the economy. Sustainable biomass can directly displace 
coal while providing stable, on -demand, renewable power that supports the deployment of other 
renewables, such as wind and solar , whilst protecting jobs. Sustainable biomass also has a key role 
to play in security of supply a nd is one of the only alternatives to fossil fuels in Combined Heat and 
Power plants. Further, this solution is  available today making it vital to achieving  the 2030 carbon 
reduction targets. As regards 2050 Sustainable biomass will be increasingly sought in the industrial 
sector to displace fossil-fuel to produce steel, cement and chemicals. Furthermore, Bioenergy with 
CCS (BECCS) is now a proven technology that is in the process of being scaled up, and poten tially 
rolled out across Europe, enabling the delivery of the negative emissions and low carbon industry 
that will be critical to achieving global climate targets.  
 
USIPA strongly welcomes legislation that ensures that only sustainably sourced biomass can be used, 
but we urge legislators to ensure that unintended consequences do not cause a legislative burden 
that could be detrimental to carbon reduction and the health of our forest ecosystems. We call on 
the Commission to follow the evidence and ensure that legislation is implementable on the ground. 
Likewise, it is critical that amending the legislation is not formulated in a way that restricts 
investment into removing fossil fuels from  the energy system or impeding investment in BECCS.  
 
Below are our priority areas of feedback.  
Established forestry principles already ensure efficient use of feedstocks and best carbon 
outcomes (Article 3) 
 
The forest products sector has highly sophisticated supply chains and markets.  Woody material that 
stores carbon longer, such as ‘quality roundwood’, is priced  considerably higher (Fig 1) as it is used 
in long-life products such as furniture or construction, not to bioenergy. The market provides its own 
natural economic cascading force, ensuring that the biomass sector only uses low-quality wood fiber 
that does not meet the specification for higher-value industries or is undersold in the local market.  
The existing economic cascading principle ensures this is the case.  
 
Additionally, forest markets and conditions vary across countries and across regions, and it will not 
be feasible to develop and implement one delegated act that could successfully apply globally.  
Forestry markets are highly complex and are impacted by a multitude of factors, including housing 
starts, yearly rainfall, weather events, local agriculture markets, commercial development activity, 
etc. Tree species, tree size, stumpage prices, volume of available wood fiber, and competition levels 
- which all i mpact value - also vary from market to market. Unnecessary intervention into such a 
complex environment carries significant risk. We are concerned that such an action could present 
artificial barriers that reduce the availability of sustainable biomass, but provide no benefit to forest 
sustainability and biodiversity   
    
As noted in the JRC report from January 2021, on the use of woody biomass translating cascading 
principles “into norms proved to be difficult.” Previous attempts at developing legislation have found 
that, “given the wide variety of situations across Member States, it was difficult to univocally define 
and meaningfully implement such restrictions in an EU legislation.”  The report concluded “the risk 
would have been to complicate compliance without necessarily fostering further sustainability or 
biodiversity conservation”.

Forests around the world vary enormously, and legislation should allow for local specificities 
(Article 29, 6b, iv) 
 
Whilst European regulation can set frameworks for sustainability, detailed and prescriptive 
provisions regulating the specifics of on-the-ground sustainable forest management should not be 
included in European energy legislation, but rather in accordance with the principle of subsidiarity  
and be addressed by national, regional, or local authorities -including those of third countries. For 
example, minimising negative impacts on biodiversity and soil quality are legitimate objectives, but 
requirements must work for all the highly diverse range of global forest types, irrespective of where 
in the world the biomass originates. The tools to achieve them should be detailed at the national, 
regional, or local level to ensure the effectiveness of policy actions.  
 
Similarly, deadwood removal and clear -cuts can have positive benefits for particular forests and 
sustainable forest management systems.  The most appropriate  practices vary by region and forest 
type.  In most cases, sustainable biomass exists as a smaller part of the larger forest products 
industry.  If left as is, these restrictions on feedstock for   biomass  would not have an impact on 
harvesting decisions  as these decisions are driven by the high-value forest products industry and not 
by biomass.   
Restrictions on biomass power only installations could inadvertently extend the life of fossil 
fuels (Art 3.3b) 
 
Article 3 states that no subsidies should be granted for forest biomass power only installations after 
2026 unless in a Just Transition Area or is accompanied by Bioenergy and CCS (BECCS). We believe 
this could have negative unintended consequences if left as is proposed: 
 This Directive will not be implemented by 2026 , which is  too early to make decisions and 
secure investment to get coal off the grid to make Europe Fit for 55 by 2030. 
 BECCS is still in its infancy in the EU and for the technology to be scaled up to the levels 
required, there will need to be greater transitional support. According to the JRC analysis of 
net-zero scenarios the equivalent of 50GW of BECCS power capacity will be needed to get to 
Net Zero by 2050. The capital cost saving for retrofitting CCS to an existing bioenergy power 
plant is considerable – with a retrofit having a 30% lower levelized cost of energy. If funding 
is only provided to plants with operational  BECCS solutions already in place, it will make it 
difficult for existing plants that could feasibly use BECCS at a later date than 2027. BECCS is 
non-negotiable for reaching EU and global climate targets and cutting off funding to existing

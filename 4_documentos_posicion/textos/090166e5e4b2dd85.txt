 
 
 
Assogestioni views on the proposal for a  Regulation of the European 
Parliament and of the Council on the prevention of the use of the financial 
system for the purposes of money laundering or terrorist financing.   
 
18 November 2021 
 
1. General remarks  
 
Assogestioni, the Italian fund and asset management Association, is pleased to have 
the opportunity to share views on the Package of legislative proposals to strengthen 
the EU’s anti-money laundering and countering the financing of terrorism (AML/CFT) 
rules. 
 
We support the European Commission's approach of ensuring harmonised rules 
throughout the internal market with the proposed Regulation (AML Regulation). 
 
The lack of a harmonised regulatory framework has led to significant diffe rences in 
the application of AML/CTF obligations and has created uneven conditions of 
competition by giving an advantage to obligated entities established in Member States 
with less strict rules (
1).  
 
For that reason, we welcome the European Commission's proposals for greater 
harmonisation of Customer Due Diligence (CDD) measures, including more detailed 
rules to identify the beneficial owner(s) of corporate and other legal entities. A clearer 
set of rules, including directly applicable provis ions, will ensure more consistent 
application of the framework. 
 
Assogestioni also agrees with the approach of giving AMLA the task of issuing RTS, 
ITS and guidelines to detail CDD, third-party performance and outsourcing measures. 
  
In this regard, we bel ieve that for the appropriate application of the AML rule s, it is 
crucial that the delegated acts on CDD and third-party performance should take into 
account the characteristics and distribution models of investment funds, in order to 
achieve a clear and proportionate regulatory framework with respect to the level of 
risk of these products. 
 
The proposed AML Regulation introduces new and strict provisions  on compliance 
functions that could  lead to significant changes  to the governance of the asset 
managers In this regard, we encourage the European Institutions to keep in mind the 
differences between banks and other financial institutions before providing measures 
that may have an impact on the governance of obliged entities.  
 
Finally, we would like to remember that the European Council in its Conclusions 
adopted on 4 November 2020 noted that “the clarification and harmonisation of the

2. 
Key issues and proposals 
 
2.1. AML Regulation  
 
a) Nominee obligations: The AML Regulation sets out that nominee shareholders 
and nominee directors of corporate or other legal entities shall maintain 
adequate, accurate and current information on the identity of their nominator 
and the nominator’s beneficial owners. This information, a nd the status of 
nominators, is also required to be reported to the beneficial ownership 
registers as well as to obliged entities when they are taking customer due 
diligence measures.  
 
While we understand the rationale behind these provisions, we believe it is 
important to point out the impacts that this approach could have on the 
investment funds sector.  
 
Nominee agreements are frequently used to make the subscription of 
investment funds and their distribution more efficient  and not to conceal or 
obscure the beneficial ownership . An intermediary (nominee) may act on the 
account of hundreds of nominators (investors) and the application of nominee 
obligations, as provided in article 47 of the propo sed AML Regulation, would 
lead to inefficiencies in the asset management industry. 
 
We believe that when units or shares of an investment fund are purchased 
through a nominee, the fund manager should apply CDD measures only with 
respect to the intermediary (nominee) and should not verify the identity of the 
underlying investors.  In this regard, i t is worth emphasising that the 
intermediary acting as nominee and/or the other intermediaries involved in the 
distribution of the funds are obliged entities under AML/CTF directive and, as 
a result, they must apply CDD requirements to the investors. 
 
The AML/CTF framework should be driven by the risk-based approach and the 
CDD measures should not result in a “box -ticking exercise”. For that reason , 
we are of the view that duplication of checks on the same investors should be 
avoided, unless high risk is involved.

Moreover, it is crucial establishing clear and harmonised rules  regarding the 
nominee obligations in the investment funds sector , in order to avoid 
regulatory divergences that generates additional costs and burdens for asset 
managers established in Member States with stricter rules.  
 
The EBA guidelines on customer du e diligence ( 3) already state  specific rules

underlying investors, although Member States may decide the degree to which 
measures should be applied.  
 
In some countries ( i.e. Italy) the fund manager must obtain the identification 
data of the underlying investors while in other countries this is not required 
and the fund manager applies due diligence only to the nominee. 
 
Also for that reason, it is vital ensure a harmonised approach to the nominee 
disclosure in the investment fund sector, avoiding duplication of checks on the 
same investors.  
 
b) Compliance functions. Article 9 of the proposed AML Regulation establishes 
that obliged entities shall appoint: 
- one executive member of their board of directors who shall be responsible 
for the implementation of measures to ensure compliance with the 
AML/CTF framework (compliance manager); 
- a compliance officer. 
 
These measures take up the advice  of the EBA contained in its Report on the 
future AML/CTF framework in the EU (
4), which originates from the findings of 
the EBA implementing review (5).  
 
The paragraph 53 (d) of the Report on the future AML/CTF framework states 
that “The EBA’s implementation reviews found that AML/CFT supervisors in 
some Member States do not interact with financial institutions’ senior 
management. This is because there is no legal or reg ulatory requirement in 
those Member States to appoint an AML/CFT compliance officer at a level that 
is sufficiently senior to report to the financial institution’s senior management 
body”.  
 
Starting from this point, the EBA has advised the European Commission “ to 
allocate to a member of the management  body ultimate responsibility for the 
financial institution’s AML/CFT systems and controls, including, where 
applicable, group AML/CFT policies and procedures, and to appoint a

The AML/CTF implementation review  is an assessment of competent 
authorities to the AML/CTF supervision of banks.  The (public) results of the 
assessment do not refer to other obliged entities, such as asset managers.  
 
We disagree with this approach to introduce such severe requirements for all 
obliged entities on the basis of a specific assessment focused only on the bank 
sector.  
 
The size of the asset managers is not comparable to that of the banks as well 
as the overall risk profile (6) and, as a result, we believe that the appointment 
of the compliance manager is disproportionate to the governance of asset 
managers.  
 
We also note that the introduction of a compliance manager is not consistent 
with: 
-  the regulatory fram ework applicable to the asset managers which , in 
general, establishes that the management body is colle ctively responsible 
for ensuring compliance with all laws and regulations (
7); 
 
- the model of “the three lines of defences” such as developed in the sectorial 
rules applicable to the asset managers. 
 
In addition , the principle of proportionality in Article 9(6), by referring 
generically to the size of the obliged entity, is too vague and could not lead to 
a harmonised approach. 
 
For these reasons,  it is crucial  to amend Article 9 removing  the mandatory 
appointment of the compliance manager. We strongly believe that: 
- the responsibility for compliance with AML/CTF obligations should be 
assigned collectively to the board of directors or to an equivalent governing 
body; 
- the compliance manager should be appointed, where applicable, only by 
larger obliged entities with a significant overall risk profile; 
- the responsibility for implementing the obliged entity’s policies, controls 
and procedures should be assigned to the  management body in its 
management function.

In line with the above, we also suggest reviewing the duties of the AML/CTF 
compliance officer according to his/her role as “second line of defence”.  
 
In this regard, it is crucial to establish that the AML/CTF compliance officer: 
- has to be appointed at management level;  
- must be placed at the same level as the other control functions; 
- has the responsibility to monitor and to assess the adequacy and 
effectiveness of the measures, policies and procedures put in place in 
accordance with the AML/CTF framework. In this respect, we note that is 
not in line with the role of second line of defence the task of being “in 
charge of the day-to-day operation of obliged entity’s AML/CTF policies” as 
referred to article 9 (3) of the proposed AML Regulation;  
- must be independent and may not have direct responsibility for operational 
units; 
- shall submit at least once a year to the governing body a report on the 
implementation of the obliged entity’s internal policies, controls and 
procedures.  
 
In addition, we also believe that the compliance officer should not be directly 
responsible for reporting suspicious transactions to the FIU. This responsibility 
could be assigned to the management body and, where applicable, it could be 
delegated to the AML/CTF compliance officer .  In this respect, it should be 
noted that the AML/CTF compliance  function can usually be outsourced, 
whereas the reporting of suspicious activities cannot be outsourced under any 
circumstances. Also for this reason, it should be avoided that the compliance 
officer is directly responsible for reporting of suspicious activities.  
 
Finally, we suggest assigning AMLA the task of issuing Regulatory Technical 
Standards to establish more specific and detailed measures on the role and 
responsibilities of the compliance officer according to the principle of 
proportionality and considering the different governance models of the obliged 
entities. 
 
In this regard, it should be noted that the EBA has published a consultation 
paper on policies and procedures in relation to compliance management and 
the role and responsibilities of the AML/CTF compliance officer. In order to 
avoid obliged entities having to change their internal rules frequently, we 
suggest coordinating the proposed AML Regulation with the EBA guidelines.

schemes within the scope of Directive (EU) 2016/2341(IORP II Directive) (

disproportionate to impose AML/CFT requirements on them. For the same

reasons, it is crucial to clarify that this exclusion also applies when the obliged 
entities referred to in Article 3 of the AML Regulation provide an IORP.  
 
Italian asset managers can promote and manage pension funds called “Open -
ended Pension Funds” (OPFs) falling within the scope of the IORP II Directive 
when they offer occupational plans. The occupational plans are set up through 
collective agreements between employer and employees, including those 
signed at company level.  
 
Contributions are made by way of deduction from wa ges and by employer’s 
contribution. In any case, the employee can contribute additional funds. The 
decumulation phase can only start when the member reaches retirement age, 
and early withdrawal is possible only in cases provided by law. 
 
We strongly believe that OPFs (as IORPs) are outside the scope of AML 
Regulation despite being promoted and managed by asset managers.  
   
The absence of clear rules in this respect has led to uncertainty as to whether 
the AML obligations should be applied to members of OPF s, and if so how to 
execute them.  
  
For all these reasons, it is crucial that the new AML Regulation clearly states 
that in any case IORPs are outside the scope of anti -money laundering 
legislation.  
 
 
d) Senior managing officials/beneficial ownership /PEP:  the proposed AML 
Regulation requires obliged entities to identify the natural person(s) holding 
the position(s) of senior managing official(s)  in the corporate or other legal 
entity where no natural person is identified as beneficial owner, or where there 
is any doubt that the person(s) identified is/are the beneficial owner(s).  
 
Although the provision is carried over from the IV AMLD, additional details are 
needed to overcome the uncertainties caused by this rule. 
 
The identification of the senior managing official as a beneficial owner is a 
“residual option” that allows for a beneficial owner in all cases. However, it is 
often challenging to assert that the senior manager's role in a corporate  or 
other legal entity is consistent with the definition of beneficial owner proposed 
by the AML Regulation. 
 
This is particularly the case where the senior managing official of a  credit or 
financial institution is identified as beneficial owner.  
 
That being said, we believe that the AML Regulation should establish that when 
the senior manag ing official  is identified as beneficial owner  of a  credit or

be collected and the intensity of scrutiny.  
 
For example, if the senior managing official of a credit or financial institution

to the aforementioned reasons, the application of EDD  should not be

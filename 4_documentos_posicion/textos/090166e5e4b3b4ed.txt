As part of the European Green Deal (EGD) - specifically with the “Fit for 55” package 
presented in the summer - the EU's energy and climate legislation is being comprehensively 
revised. The EU climate law provides the political and legal framework to achieve the 
overarching goal of climate neutrality in 2050 or CO2 reductions of at least -55% by 2030. 
The "Fit for 55" package shows the way of implementation. The Austrian Federal Economic 
Chamber (WKÖ) wants to help ensure that the end result is a package that fulfils the 
climate protection purpose and is practicable, compatible and as stimulating as possible 
for the economy. 
 
I. General position about the “Fit for 55“ package 
 
The dossiers presented in the "Fit for 55" package aim to reduce CO2 emissions by 55% by 
2030 and thereafter to follow the path towards achieving the goals of the Paris Agreement, 
in particular towards climate neutrality from 2050. The Austrian Federal Economic 
Chamber stands behind these objectives. The goal of climate neutrality has been set; it is 
a matter of shaping the transition in such a way that growth is generated and losses are 
avoided.  
 
Given the dimension of the challenge, all technologies under consideration will have to 
make their contributions. For example, the conversion of CO2 into a raw material, more 
rail capacity for freight transport, hydropower as well as wind power and photovoltaics not 
only on roofs are needed. The approval procedures for the necessary energy and transport 
infrastructure projects must become much faster. These building blocks are missing from 
the “Fit for 55” package. 
 
The question of the availability of non-fossil energy sources in sufficient quantities would 
have to be addressed quickly by the EC. This requires a long-lasting huge investment push. 
The EU Commission is avoiding the problem of the long approval periods. Obviously, the 
long procedural durations also have to do with Union law, as the public interest in the 
energy transition is not always sufficiently weighted in approval decisions, even for high-
value and urgent projects. 
 
In the sense of the "climate protection through growth" orientation set by the highest 
bodies of the EU, the goal of international competitiveness of EU industry must be given a 
high, equal priority. The EU's climate and energy policy must not be a stumbling block for 
Europe as a business location and for its companies but must always be formulated with a 
view to maintaining international competitiveness. Economic success is the best way to 
ensure that Europe can actually fulfil the envisaged role of a pacesetter in global climate 
protection and that other countries and economic areas follow this path. 
 
II. Position about the proposal 
 
For the WKÖ, the following principles are essential: ensure economic mobility for all 
sectors, technology-neutral mobility turnaround, push domestic value creation, electricity 
grid as critical element for e-charging stations. Mandatory targets are rejected; financial

incentives and reduction of bureaucratic obstacles are prefered. Infrastructure deployment 
must correlate with the number of vehicles on the market. 
 
Austria has already reached the target of 1 kW charging capacity per battery-electric 
passenger car or light commercial vehicle and 0.66 kW per plug-in hybrid. However, the 
distance requirement on the TEN-T network is not yet met. 
 
The fuels natural gas (CNG, LNG) and liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) will only be taken into 
account in the infrastructure expansion on a transitional basis. This is incomprehensible for 
reasons of technological diversity, that is necessary for the transformation. 
 
Consider truck- and bus-specific infrastructure network: There is a lack of infrastructure 
requirements in connection with heavy goods vehicles, buses and coaches. It is also 
important to set up targeted charging pools in the vicinity of multimodality hubs such as 
railway stations.  
 
Impacts on the electricity grid is not addressed: There is hardly any mention of the 
impact on the electricity grid. Only in recitals (20) to (22) reference to the need for grid 
stability, which is to be achieved with smart charging (!). 
 
Article 2: There are far-reaching consequences of the new definition of "public 
accessibility". The introduction of an EU-wide uniform definition is welcomed in principle, 
but the current proposal, however, is critical, as semi-public charging/fuelling facilities 
(e.g. in underground car parks) will also be included in future. However, technical 
requirements for these two groups differ considerably. It is still unclear whether the 
existing semi-public infrastructure can meet these requirements. 
 
Article 8: Technology openness is not given; there is a one-sided focus on e-mobility; 
existing CO2 reduction potentials through the use of gaseous energy sources are massively 
misjudged. Especially in the long-distance segment, there is a need for powerful and 
immediately available solutions such as LNG technology. CNG and LNG are seen merely as 
transitional solutions; but these are already mature today and can be operated 100% 
climate-neutrally without technical adaptation by using biomethane.  
 
Article 17 "User information": Raising awareness is certainly important, but the decision 
to buy a vehicle has already been made during the refuelling/charging process - the 
benefit is of the requirement is therefore questionable. 
 
Article 18 "Provision of data": Static and dynamic data must be provided by the operator 
free of charge. For static data, this is technically feasible and is therefore seen as positive 
in principle; for dynamic data, on the other hand, it may entail some major technical 
challenges.

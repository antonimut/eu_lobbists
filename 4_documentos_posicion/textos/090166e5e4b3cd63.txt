As part of the European Green Deal (EGD) - specifically with the “Fit for 55” package 
presented in the summer - the EU's energy and climate legislation is being comprehensively 
revised. The EU climate law provides the political and legal framework to achieve the 
overarching goal of climate neutrality in 2050 or CO2 reductions of at least -55% by 2030. 
The "Fit for 55" package shows the way of implementation. The Austrian Federal Economic 
Chamber (WKÖ) wants to help ensure that the end result is a package that fulfils the 
climate protection purpose and is practicable, compatible and as stimulating as possible 
for the economy. 
 
I. General position about the “Fit for 55“ package 
 
The dossiers presented in the "Fit for 55" package aim to reduce CO2 emissions by 55% by 
2030 and thereafter to follow the path towards achieving the goals of the Paris Agreement, 
in particular towards climate neutrality from 2050. The Austrian Federal Economic 
Chamber stands behind these objectives. The goal of climate neutrality has been set; it is 
a matter of shaping the transition in such a way that growth is generated and losses are 
avoided.  
 
Given the dimension of the challenge, all technologies under consideration will have to 
make their contributions. For example, the conversion of CO2 into a raw material, more 
rail capacity for freight transport, hydropower as well as wind power and photovoltaics not 
only on roofs are needed. The approval procedures for the necessary energy and transport 
infrastructure projects must become much faster. These building blocks are missing from 
the “Fit for 55” package. 
 
The question of the availability of non-fossil energy sources in sufficient quantities would 
have to be addressed quickly by the EC. This requires a long-lasting huge investment push. 
The EU Commission is avoiding the problem of the long approval periods. Obviously, the 
long procedural durations also have to do with Union law, as the public interest in the 
energy transition is not always sufficiently weighted in approval decisions, even for high-
value and urgent projects. 
 
In the sense of the "climate protection through growth" orientation set by the highest 
bodies of the EU, the goal of international competitiveness of EU industry must be given a 
high, equal priority. The EU's climate and energy policy must not be a stumbling block for 
Europe as a business location and for its companies but must always be formulated with a 
view to maintaining international competitiveness. Economic success is the best way to 
ensure that Europe can actually fulfil the envisaged role of a pacesetter in global climate 
protection and that other countries and economic areas follow this path. 
 
II. General position about the proposal 
 
The planned regulation stipulates that suppliers of aviation fuel will have to blend a 
certain proportion of so-called sustainable aviation fuels (SAF) from 1 January 2025. 
  3/5 
European airlines will be obliged to refuel at least 90% of their annual requirements in the 
EU. Airports will have to provide the necessary infrastructure. 
 
Sustainable aviation fuels are a suitable means of reducing emissions from aviation. They 
can be blended with paraffin in high percentages without requiring modifications to the 
aircraft engine. As the share of sustainable aviation fuels in paraffin is currently very low 
(more than 99% comes from fossil sources), the potential for GHG reduction in aviation 
through blending SAF is high. 
 
The blending rates for 2030 and 2050 are in line with the targets formulated in the 
"Destination 2050" programme, with a SAF supply potential of 6% for 2030 and 66% for 
2050. "Destination 2050" is a programme of the European aviation industry that shows the 
way to CO2-neutral aviation ("net zero CO2 emissions") in Europe. SAFs play a central role 
in this. 
 
The introduction of specific sub-targets for synthetic fuels is positive, as these fuels have 
the highest sustainability potential when produced from climate neutral hydrogen and 
electricity, and do not have the same feedstock availability constraints as biofuels. At the 
same time, a massive expansion in sustainable energy production is needed to provide 
sufficient climate neutral hydrogen or electricity. 
 
Due to the crucial role of SAF in the decarbonisation of aviation, all possibilities should be 
exhausted to accelerate and scale up the production and roll-out of SAF. Therefore, those 
taxes and levies that are specifically collected from aviation should be reinvested in 
sustainable aviation fuels. In Austria, in particular the funds from the aviation tax and the 
national ETS funds should be used and earmarked for this purpose. 
 
Making the use of SAF more attractive through reduced tax rates in aviation (as provided 
for in the draft amendment to the Energy Tax Directive) is also a sensible accompanying 
measure. 
 
Even if sustainable aviation fuels are to be promoted in air transport, this must not happen 
at the expense of the competitiveness of the transport sectors concerned, especially 
airlines and airports.  
 
There is still some need for clarification regarding the obligations of airports with regard to 
the infrastructure for SAF: Airports are obliged to provide airlines with the necessary 
infrastructure for the delivery, storage and refuelling of SAF (Article 6). This obligation is 
unclear in that paraffin will already be delivered in mixed form (i.e. with drop-in SAF) as 
Jet A-1. A separate infrastructure for SAF is therefore not necessary. 
 
The fact that only European airlines are obliged to refuel at least 90% of their annual 
requirements in the EU puts them at a considerable competitive disadvantage compared to 
non-European airlines. We cannot support such a requirement. It would be better to 
  4/5 
provide incentives (tax advantages, subsidies) instead of imposing a (refuelling) obligation 
only for European airlines. 
 
The refuelling obligation for EU airlines is all the more critical as the aviation industry is 
already confronted with considerable multiple burdens due to other measures provided for 
in the “Fit for 55” package (such as additional costs from the stricter EU ETS, higher 
paraffin tax, etc.). 
 
This provision in Article 5 aims to limit the risk of tankering by aircraft operators. 
Tankering would take place if aircraft operators avoid refuelling at airports covered by the 
SAF mandate where potentially higher fuel prices prevail. 
 
Tankering must be avoided as far as possible so as not to generate side effects that run 
counter to sustainability objectives. At the same time, a competitive disadvantage of EU 
airlines compared to third-country carriers must be kept as low as possible. The 
regulations in this regard (especially in Article 5) should therefore be regularly reviewed 
for their practical success and adjusted if necessary. 
 
European airlines see themselves at a particular disadvantage vis-à-vis third-country 
carriers, since competitors from the Gulf States, for example, are not covered by the 
obligation to refuel with SAF in the EU for feeder flights to their hubs and thus enjoy a 
competitive advantage. A weakening of the European hubs and, as a consequence, "hub 
switching" appear to be pre-programmed as a negative effect, because it becomes more 
attractive to use non-European airports for international long-distance traffic, such as 
Istanbul or other hubs in the Near and Middle East. Connections to Eastern and South-
Eastern Europe are likely to be particularly affected, which would harm Vienna in a special 
way.  
 
Rising ticket prices and lower demand are to be expected. Therefore, it should also be 
clearly communicated to aviation customers that these requirements will bring massive 
price increases in aviation. 
 
All these factors weaken connectivity and thus also the position of Vienna and the whole of 
Austria as a business location as well as the position of Vienna Airport as a hub. 
 
Overall, European aviation will be confronted with a multitude of new, regulatory burdens 
in the coming years. At the same time, the industry must master the recovery after the 
COVID19-related crisis (keyword: covid recovery). Against this background, the 
consequences of the “Fit for 55” package on the industry need to be further examined, as 
a weakening of the sector vis-à-vis other world regions is highly likely and this will damage 
the export-oriented Austrian economy and the tourism location. 
 
Finally, we note that the targets for blending quotas are very ambitious. It is therefore 
imperative to ensure that SAF is available in sufficient quantities within the specified 
  5/5 
period and that penalty payments are avoided. The concept must also be designed to be 
more economically compatible for the European aviation industry in order to protect it 
from competitive disadvantages vis-à-vis competitors from third countries.

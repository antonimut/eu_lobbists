EC’s consultation Position paper on amended REDII 
 
 
 
EDF’s position paper on the amended REDII  
European Commission ex post consultation  
EDF welcomes:  
 the increased RES target of 40% at EU level by 2030, in line with the new 2030 climate objective (Art. 3);  
 the new provisions on e-mobility: bidirectional charging stations and data sharing on batteries to develop 
flexibility and storage solutions for the growing share of RES (Art. 20a) and obligation to introduce a credit 
mechanism to ensure that electricity can be properly accounted in RES-T target (Art. 25);  
 
We draw attention to:  
 the need to issue guidance for Member States on good practices to accelerate renewables’ permitting 
procedures and support the implementation of existing requirement (Art. 16);  
 the complexity of the proposal with the multiplication of sub -targets for end -use sectors and for specific 
technologies which may not give sufficient flexibility to Member States to deliver a cost-effective transition 
and could delay the adoption and transposition of the directive;  
 the necessity to add a clear definition for PPA and clarify the modification on rules about GOs (Art. 19)  
 the extension of RFNBOs to all sectors which risks imposing overly restrictive criteria for hydrogen to be 
qualified as renewable through the RED II upcoming delegated act (Art. 27) and we underline the need to 
leverage the potential of low carbon electrolytic hydrogen to contribute to ambitious sectoral targets;  
 the need to extend the possibility for MS to derogate to the RED II sustainability criteria for the use of 
bioliquids in non-interconnected areas following the example of biomass fuels (Art. 29); 
 the need to perform a cost -benefit analysis and assess the technical feasibility on a case by case basis 
rather than imposing third party accessed for district heating (Art. 24).  
 
 
Overall ambition and targets  
EDF welcomes the revision of the Renewable Energy Directive to align the ambition with the 2030 Climate Target 
Plan and endorses the increased overall union RES target at 40%. The proposal should reinforce the role of direct 
electrification as the primary driver for  the decarbonization in Europe and promote efficient systems such as heat  
pumps for heating and EVs for road transport.  
 
We draw attention to the complexity of the proposal with the multiplication of sub -targets for end-use sectors and 
for specific technologies which may not give sufficient flexibility to Member States to deliver a cost -effective 
transition and could delay t he adoption and transposition of the directive. Some clear indications are missing – 
especially in new articles 15a for buildings and 20a for industry – to ensure that RES from the network and heat 
recovery can be properly counted towards meeting theses targets. Following the example of the transport sector, 
the demonstration of RES consumption in these targets should be based on ‘physical’ calculation and not rely on 
commercial flows using for example GOs.  
 
Permitting issues 
Raising the RES targets wil l remain theoretical if permitting bottlenecks are not quickly addressed. Procedures i n 
Europe today are too slow. T he binding permitting deadlines in Article 16 of the directive are not met most of the 
time because of burdensome authorization process and a lack of human resources at nation level to process a 
growing number of applications. We call on the Commission to issue guidelines to share best practices regarding 
permitting, help the Member States to address their challenges and ensure continuous monitoring of progress.   
PPA 
We would like to underline that a clear definition of Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) is missing in the Renewable 
Energy Directive. It must be clear that PPA should be an alternative way of developing RES production 
complementing n ational support schemes.  In particular, access to any form of government -backed credit risk 
guarantee - set up to promote corporate PPAs - should not be granted to projects already receiving FiT or CfD. 
Guarantees of origins  
The revision for the rules around GOs in article 19 should be clarified. While we support the possibility to issue GOs 
for every RES MWh (under support scheme or unsupported), it should still be possible for the Member State to 
auction the GOs for RES assets under support schemes.  This methodology guarantees an access to GOs for the 
various actors while revealing a reference price – which may otherwise be difficult to estimate.  Moreover, the 
Commission should assess the potential downwards impact on the ongoing GO market.

GO system should remain an instrument dedicated solely to customer disclosure and could be further strengthened 
to improve transparency and credibility. Member states should keep the possibility to issue GOs for non RES 
production and mandatory monthly matching should already be implemented at EU level in the current revision of 
the REDII to ensure a standardized approach across all Members States. It should already be possible to register 
GO with information on the time of generation at a more granular level (hourly). Finally, a new specific category of 
GOs, separated from the one used for gas,  should be dedicated to hydrogen because of its specificities as both 
energy carrier and feedstock.  
 
Renewable fuels of non-biological origin 
The simultaneous timing for the publication of the RFNBO delegated act on rules of calculation of the renewable 
share of hydrogen in relation to targets and the revision of REDII brings significant uncertainty for the hydrogen 
industry. EDF welcomes the re moval of the additionality requirement for electricity in the transport sector (art. 27) 
and does not support the extension of additionality r equirements for hydrogen in all the sectors of the economy. 
Additionality should not be a burden for hydrogen producers but should rather be looked at Member States’ level to 
foster system integration. Indeed, the demand for electricity triggered by the deployment of electrolytic hydrogen, 
like that of any other use of electricity, can only be assessed from a glob al perspective including all existing and 
future renewable and low carbon electricity plants. With this approach,  existing renewable assets – such a s 
hydropower or wind energy - should not be discriminated against and should be used to produce renewable 
hydrogen and synthetic fuels  
 
The introduction of end-uses targets for renewable hydrogen in the transport and the industry sectors goes in the 
right direction to help scaling up the production of renewable electrolytic hydrogen. Nevertheless, these targets 
could be really difficult to achieve depending on the additionality as well as geographical/temporal correlation criteria 
to be defined in the upcoming delegated act for RFNBOS. Most importantly, low-carbon hydrogen produced with 
grid-connected electrolysers should be allowed to contribute to the targets  as in some countries it is already an 
effective option to produce hydrogen below the -70% GHG reduction threshold measured according to a fact-based 
methodology at an hourly step. Indeed low carbon electrolytic hydrogen and synthetic fuels contribute to enhancing 
the deployment of renewable hydrogen and should therefore be able to be accounted for in the end -uses targets. 
The 50% clean hydrogen target by 2030, which solely focused on renewable hydrogen, is a strong constraint which 
may restrict the Member States' own energy policy choices  and increase the cost to decarbonize hydrogen uses 
for industry.  
 
Transport sector  
EDF welcomes the new provisions for electro-mobility in Article 20a as both access to battery data and smart/V2G 
charging stations are key to ensure flexibility and storage solutions for the growing share of RES. EDF endorses 
the introduction of a mandatory credit mechanism to ensure that electricity can be properly accounted in RES -T 
targets but it should not be limited to public charging and should be extended to private charging as well as other 
types of transport such as rail.  
 
EDF is open to the GHG intensity based target of -13% by 2030 as long as the methodology sufficiently incentivize 
electrification of transport. The removal of multiplier should in return be carefully considered as it may slow down 
the necessary uptake of E Vs. In addition, expressing a target in GHG intensity reduction could be used as a first 
step towards technological neutrality. Low carbon electricity and hydrogen could be counted towards this target in 
addition to renewable fuels as a quick and already available solution to decarbonize the transport sector.   
 
Biomass 
While we support the need to strengthen the protection of forest, the new provisions of biomass should be carefully 
designed to give sufficient time for the market to adjust. We propose therefore in case of solid biomass fuels, in 
installations producing e lectricity, heating and cooling to lower the threshold for the applicability of sustainability 
criteria from the current 20 MW to 10 MW – instead of the 5 MW proposed by the European Commission (Art.29).  
 
Bioliquids 
As stated in recital (64) the specific situation of outermost region regarding the energy transition should be fully 
considered. While some provisions already apply for biomass fuels (solid and gaseous fuel) with a specific carbon 
reference in Annex VI and the possibility for Member State to derogate to REDII sustainability criteria, nothing can 
be apply to the use of bioliquids. For non-interconnected areas (as defined in article 2 of the directive 2009/72/CE), 
the conversion of oil -fired power plants to b ioliquids is one of the most effective ways to decarbonize the energy 
mix, to reduce GHG and pollutant emissions and to increase the overall share of RES, especially intermittent RES 
– which need flexible power plants to maintain the grid stability such as bioenergy plants.

To better reflect the specificity of non-interconnected areas, EDF believes that a fuel reference carbon value of 245 
gCO2eq/MJ would be more suitable regarding the energy mix of those areas - this value corresponds to the 
reference carbon for the reference fossil primary energy indicated in the RED II directive (94 gCO2eq / MJ) divided 
by the engine efficiency specified in the LCP BREF (38.3%). This specific fossil fuel comparator for outermost region 
should be added in annex V for the use of bioliquid as well as the possibility to derogate for a limited period of time 
in Article 29. To minimize administrative burden, exemptions should also be apply to the use of bioliquids in small 
power plants (P< 20MWth).  
 
Finally the wording should be clarified to ensure that biofuels used for electricity production in non -interconnected 
areas should not be included in the 7% market share ceiling in the transport (Art.26).  
 
 
District heating  
Third party access is a way to  enhance the energy transition in district heating/cooling solutions. However, due to 
technical and economical specificities of this kind of networks, no “one fits all” solutions is possible. Hence, a 
considered cost-benefits analyze should be done before allowing the integration of a third party  (Art.24). The final 
decision on the access of this third party should be in the hands of the network operator basing its decisions on the 
aforementioned considered analyze.

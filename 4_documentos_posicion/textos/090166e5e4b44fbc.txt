From: Svemin – Swedish Association of Mines, Mineral and Metal Producers 
Matter: Public consultation – updating the Renewable Energy Directive (RED III) 
Date: 2021.11.18 
Comments from Svemin on the Feedback period of 
the Commission adoption of updating the Renewable 
Energy Directive (RED) 
Svemin (Association of Mines, Mineral and Metal Producers in Sweden) is the national business 
association for mines, mineral and metal producers in Sweden. The number of member companies 
amounts to more than 50. The member activities occur throughout the country, of which the mines 
are mainly located in northern Sweden and Bergslagen.  
Summary 
• Many proposed changes in the Renewables Directive in general are too detailed and risks 
restricting the sovereignty of Member States in choosing their energy mix. The EU should 
encourage the use of fossil-free energy, not regulate Member States’ energy mix, as 
regulating the energy mix would risk resulting in considerable challenges to security of 
supply and reduce the cost-effectiveness of transition. 
• EU regulations should have a technology-neutral fossil free starting point. All fossil-free 
energy resources will be needed to meet increased demand for electricity and it is 
therefore unwise to create different conditions for different types of fossil-free energy.  
• The system perspective and security of supply, as well as cost-effectiveness, the 
functioning of the market, and the effects on greenhouse gas emissions are important 
aspects that are missing in the Directive. The Commission does not take into account the 
role of fossil-free electricity in security of supply, low climate emissions and the ability to 
produce the hydrogen that the climate transition will demand. 
• Biofuels are an important component in the transition to climate neutrality for Swedish 
industry, especially in the short term in replacing fossil-based fuels where electrification or 
other measures are not (yet) possible. The proposal’s reduced opportunities to use 
bioenergy is worrying and risks undermining business’ scope to transition.  
• Main elements of legislation directly affecting industry should be written into the Directive - 
not be subject to delegated acts 
Ambitious climate targets dramatically increase demand for metals and minerals 
for climate technologies  
Svemin supports the Paris Agreement's targets and the EU's ambitious net-zero climate targets by 
2050.  Ambitious climate targets dramatically increase demand for metals and minerals for climate 
technologies such as renewable energy and e-mobility – up to six times overall and up to 42 times 
for individual metals and minerals, according to the IEA. Maximized recycling only reduces demand 
for primary metals by about 15-30% by 2050, according to the World Bank. The EU is today up to 
100% dependent on imports of many of the metals and minerals critical to the climate tra nsition 
from countries such as China, Congo and Indonesia, where sustainability and social requirements 
are low to say the least. Together with Finland, Sweden is the EU's leading mining nation and is

maintained competitiveness of the mining, metal and mineral industries in Europe. 
Security of supply and cost efficiency need more focus in the Directive 
It is undesirable for the EU to regulate member states’ energy mix beyond encouraging the  
transition to fossil-free energy. This is because national energy systems are important tools in the 
transition away from fossil fuels, but the systems differ considerably between member states, 
regarding in how and for what purposes different energy sources are used, and the extent to which 
infrastructure needs to change or be expanded. Member states have very different capacities as 
well in terms of producing and distributing renewable energy. Also, there will be differences 
between member states as to what is the most cost-effective way to achieve climate neutrality. 
Member states themselves have the best knowledge of where in their energy systems  different 
fossil-free energy sources can be used most cost-effectively. 
There is a considerable risk concerning the security of supply of electricity systems in Europe, if the 
Renewable Energy Directive is pushing all Member States towards the same energy mix.  The 
transition of the electricity system to using a larger share of renewable electricity  production entails 
enormous, unprecedented change. It is therefore vital that all changes that affect, for example, the 
operation of the electricity system and security of supply, are carefully reviewed at national and EU 
level. 
One-sided focus on RFNBO risks becoming counterproductive to industry's 
climate transition 
The new definition "Renewable Fuels of Non Biological Origin" (RFNBO) covers hydrogen 
produced from electricity from renewable sources. It is imperative that the industry's climate 
transition is not hindered. The use of hydrogen will increase dramatically, and the importance of 
being able to produce hydrogen cost efficiently in an electricity system with minimum climate 
impact cannot be underestimated. The definition, requirements and traceability of hydrogen should 
be included in the revision of the Renewable Energy Directive and not remain in a delegated act. 
The contents of the delegated act to be proposed by the Commission should be revised within the 
revision of RED III. 
Member States possibilities to produce hydrogen from fossil-free electricity should be encouraged. 
The use of hydrogen will have an important role in reducing emissions in transport and industry and 
therefore the Directive should promote hydrogen produced from fossil-free electricity. 
Additionality and industry sector targets for hydrogen use risks driving costs and 
limiting the availability of hydrogen 
In the proposal, sector requirements for the use of renewable energy are set in a new way.

1.1% per year. In addition, Member States shall ensure that the industry's use of hydrogen shall

The requirement of additionality as well as a binding target for industrial use for hydrogen is 
counterproductive for industry transition. Building additional energy production without grid access 
serves no purpose. Having a target for hydrogen use in industry is positive, but the target should 
not be binding for Member States that already use hydrogen and not be applicable to Member 
States that don´t, so the proposed target risks to distort the internal market. 
The impact on the hydrogen market in terms of needs and different opportunities  in industry needs 
to be thoroughly assessed. There is a risk that sectoral targets set in this way could drive costs and 
limit the availability of hydrogen, which would lead to delays in action in the part of the industry that 
is the most in need of investments. Measures to increase the share of renewable energy should 
rather focus on facilitating on the producer side. 
The strong governance in favour of the use of PPAs risks sub-optimisation in the 
electricity system  
Svemin questions the strong governance in favour of the use of Producer Purchase Agreement 
(PPA). This type of proposal affects member states’ energy policies and disrupts the electricity 
market in general. The requirements risk leading to unjustifiably increased costs for users because 
they risk displacing types of production with system-critical properties that are needed to cost-
effectively maintain the necessary operational reliability in the electrical system.  The electricity use 
in Sweden is predicted to at least double by 2045, mainly driven by climate targets since 
electrification is the main tool for Swedish industry but also other sectors to become climate 
neutral/fossil free.  
Similar developments are underway in other member states. This trend places considerable 
demands on electricity systems to provide electricity when and where it is needed at a competitive 
price. To meet this demand with good quality electricity, a cost-effective electricity mix of several 
different fossil-free energy sources is needed. It is therefore unwise to create different conditions 
for different types of fossil-free fuels. EU regulations should have a fossil-free technology-neutral 
starting point. 
The proposed requirements favoring PPAs risk leading to unjustifiably increased costs for users 
because they risk displacing types of production with system-critical properties that are needed to 
cost-effectively maintain the necessary operational reliability in the electrical system.  
Instead, the requirements should continue to be placed directly on producers and linked to 
demands for reduced greenhouse gas emissions. This would provide a clearer link to measures, 
reduce administration and give development better conditions to take place in relation to the 
system function. 
New requirements for frameworks and governance of renewable energy 
deployment risk encroaching on national sovereignty to determine their energy mix 
The new frameworks and governance of renewable energy deployment go in a direction that risks 
encroaching on the national competence to determine its electricity and energy mix based on 
market conditions and cost efficiency. 
The reference to additionality, i.e. that the additional need for electricity for the production of 
RFNBO (green hydrogen via electrolysis) should come from new renewable electricity production,  
is an unreasonable limitation for the industrial climate transition. Limiting emissions from electricity 
production is already controlled via the EU ETS, and for Sweden it is necessary that no fossil-free 
electricity is disadvantaged in efforts to electrify industry and make it climate neutral. Requiring

The proposed limitations on bioenergy risks undermining industry’s short term 
climate transition 
Biofuels are an important component in the transition to climate neutrality for Swedish industry, 
especially in the short term in replacing fossil-based fuels where electrification or other measures 
are not (yet) possible. The proposal entails reduced opportunities to use bioenergy and the use that 
remains possible will be more expensive, thereby undermining business’ scope to transition.  
Main elements of legislation directly affecting industry should be written into the 
Directive - not be subject to delegated acts 
All main elements of legislation directly affecting industry should be written into the Directive and 
should not be subject to management in delegated acts. As these criteria and definitions will have a 
major impact on national energy policy and the electricity system, Member States' positions on 
them should govern the rules that will ultimately be laid down.

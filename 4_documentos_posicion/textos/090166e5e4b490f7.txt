RED II Proposal – ambitious, but not ambitious enough 
 
The Renewable Energy Directive II (RED) revision proposal brings the EU one step closer to becoming 
a global leader in renewable hydrogen development by adding to EU’s ambition on renewable energy 
in the form of electricity and hydrogen. We welcome the approach of focusing solely on renewable 
hydrogen in this framework and underline a level playing field between all clean technologies needs 
to be ensured. In addition, we look forward to the Hydrogen and decarbonised gas package to address 
all other forms of clean hydrogen.  
 
Ensuring increased renewable energy and hydrogen specific targets are not watered down is crucial 
for the development of an internal hydrogen market. This will place the EU in competitive terms with 
other areas that are also developing hydrogen in order to increase the ir overall energy system 
efficiency and facilitate renewables integration. Hydrogen Europe welcomes the increased EU target 
for renewable energy to 40% by 2030.  Furthermore, we consider this target could be even more 
ambitious. This increase is an important driver for 1/ much needed additional renewable electricity 
capacity, 2/ the decarbonisation of the power sector, and 3/ boosting investments into clean energy 
projects. We note the different stages of Member States economi c development,  and as such the 
need to ensure the use of all available clean energy solutions driving decarbonisation.  
 
Hydrogen Europe welcomes the proposed binding target of 50% for renewable fuels of non-biological 
origin (RFNBO) used as feedstock or a s an energy carrier in industry. Whilst discussions in Member 
State capitals and in the European Parliament are ongoing whether the target is overly ambitious, 
Hydrogen Europe advises against watering it down. Rather, we consider the target should be 
strengthened by improving permitting procedures for new renewable projects to ensure rapid build -
out, developing financing mechanisms such as Carbon Contract for Difference, introducing pragmatic 
additionality criteria and reforming state aid rules to ensure they fit the purpose of reaching net zero 
by 2050.  
 
We also note the specific target of 2.6% for RFNBO and the increased target on reducing GHG 
emissions by 13% by 2030 in transport. We consider the specific target is a first step towards boosting 
the uptake of clean hydrogen in mobility, however, we believe the approach could have benefited 
from more ambition. The 2,6% specific RFNBO target is estimated to generate demand for 2,34MT of 
renewable hydrogen by 2030. Depending on the amount of electricity expec ted to be consumed in 
transport in 2030, very limited demand for H2 is expected to be generated from the 13% GHG emission 
reduction target, as almost all the emission savings could be achieved through a combination of (i) the 
specific targets, (ii) first generation biofuels and (iii) renewable electricity supplied to road transport. 
 
Hydrogen Europe also emphasises that meeting the renewable h ydrogen targets is highly contingent 
on electrolyser manufacturers scaling up manufacturing capacities. Alongside th e cost of renewable 
electricity, capex costs are an important factor in bringing down the cost of renewable h ydrogen. 
Electrolyser manufacturers and the industry would benefit from clear and coherent policy signals via 
these demand-side measures, helping m anufacturers to develop economies of scale. The cost of 
equipment will drop dramatically by undertaking a so-called “scale-up-cost-down”. 
 
We also draw your attention to the absence of long-term policies going beyond 2030 describing the 
future ramp-up on the path to climate neutrality. This is all the more important since the amortisation 
periods for renewable energy assets as well as H2 proj ects are lasting much longer than 2030 and 
investors need planning security.

A pragmatic approach to additionality criteria  cannot be underestimated. This will be the main 
variable determining whether a hydrogen market will develop. M eeting the above- mentioned 
targets is dependent on the Delegated Act on additionality, temporal and geographic correlation. We 
fully recognise the imp ortance and support the principle of additionality, namely the idea that 
additional renewable electricity consumption must always be covered by additional renewable 
capacity. However, given the lack of clarity, we raise concern over the possible extension of these 
criteria to other sectors, and its subsequent impact on the deployment of renewable hydrogen, 
meeting the targets set by the EU Hydrogen strategy and ultimately the long-term EU climate 
objectives. In addition, we underline that holding hydrogen producers responsible for such elaborate 
reporting in absence for such requirements for other energy producers and consumers, is immensely 
discriminatory.  
 
- Consider providing an exemption from proving additionality to RFNBO producers until 2025. 
- In 2025, the Commission and relevant stakeholders should assess progress towards meeting 
the 2024 6GW and 2030 40GW target of the H2 strategy. 
- Member States should bear responsibility for providing additional renewable electricity 
capacity by setting dedicated RE targets to be used for RFNBO production. 
- RFNBO producers should be allowed to produce renewable hydrogen from curtailed 
renewable electricity. 
- Require Guarantees of Origin alongside Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) to prove the 
renewable character of the electricity used in hydrogen production. 
- Acknowledge that renewable hydrogen creates demand exclusively for renewable energy, not 
for fossil -based power. All renewable hydrogen producers need to prove the origin of 
renewable sources, therefore, it is misleading to claim that renewable hydrogen production 
boosts fossil usage.  
 
Different energy carriers require separate systems of Guarantees of Origin
 (GOs). The RED II revision 
proposal does not sufficiently take into account the urgent need to rehaul the GO system. Whilst we 
note the positive development on including provisions obliging Member States to issue GOs to a 
producer that receives financial support, more needs to be done.  
 
- The provisions on the Union Database (UD) are a step forward towards avoiding misleading 
claims and increasing transparency and traceability. At the same time, we consider all carriers 
should be included in the UD by applying existing data sources, e.g. on  energy statistics, 
beyond liquid and gaseous renewable fuels and recycled carbon fuels.  
- In addition, we raise questions regarding the method of proving the renewable character 
towards the end consumer if the GO is cancelled before being recorded in the UD. In case the 
renewable character would be included as part of the recorded transaction, this needs to be 
clarified. In addition, such a mechanism for GO cancellation could potentially reduce the 
trading of GOs, therefore reducing their value. 
- We are also concerned that the UD functioning is conceived in a manner that set s barriers to 
the trading of RFNBOs across Member States. It is of high importance that RED compliant fuels 
can be transported through appropriate grid networks and can be traded across the EU. 
- We also raise concern on the functioning and design of the UD.  
- We would also li ke to enquire how will the UD interact with future imported hydrogen. 
Considering the large volumes of clean hydrogen needed in the future, it is important to 
develop clear administrative procedures to process third country data and certification. 
Hydrogen Europe underlines the need to develop mutually recognisable sustainability criteria 
and certification in order to develop supply hubs such as those foreseen in the H2 Strategy 
e.g. Ukraine, Maghreb. The GO system applied should therefore have a global scope and needs 
to be part of international negotiations.

- Member States should be obliged to issue GOs for low carbon hydrogen and other forms of 
clean hydrogen that shall be addressed and defined in the Hydrogen and gas decarbonisation 
package.  
 
Clear guidance is needed on GHG saving criteria for RFNBOs. Article 1(19) inserts a new Article 29a 
indicating that energy from RFNBOs can only be counted towards the targets set in the RED II revision 
if corresponding GHG emissions savings are at least 70%. In addit ion, energy from recycled carbon 
fuels can only be counted towards the transport target if its GHG emissions savings are at least 70%. 
We would welcome further details as per the methodology and the impact on how the GHG avoidance 
reference are used to cal culate the 70% GHG reduction. We also raise concern on the absence of a 
deadline to finalise this methodology. Moreover, projects are not able to account for specific 
requirements while building their business case on the sale of RED compliant products. Th e absence 
of a clear methodology puts at risk project developments just as it is the case with the uncertainty on 
the Delegated Act for the renewable electricity sourcing. 
 
Please find below comments on specific aspects of the Directive. 
 
- Art 7 rejects  ‘hydrogen’ as separate carriers and reads “gas, electricity and hydrogen from 
renewable sources shall be considered only once for the  purposes of calculating the share of 
gross final consumption of energy from renewable sources. Energy produced  from renewable 
fuels of non biological origin shall be accounted in the sector electricity, heating and cooling 
or transport where it is consumed.” 
o  We consider it paramount to include hydrogen as a separate energy carrier. As such, 
art 19 should make a clear distinction between GOs certifying gas and GOs certifying 
hydrogen.  
- There is no definition of renewable hydrogen for the purpose of Art 22 (a) Mainstreaming 
renewable energy in industry. For legal certainty this should be defined in the RED. 
- Art 25 on GHG intensity reduction in the transport sector from the use of renewable energy 
reads ‘When setting the obligation on fuel suppliers, Member States may exempt fuel suppliers 
supplying electricity or  renewable liquid and gaseous transport fuels of non bio logical origin 
from the requirement to comply with the minimum share of advanced biofuels and biogas (…)’. 
o We suggest amending ‘may’ to ‘shall’. This minor change in wording could help RFNBO 
producers tremendously by decreasing the administrative burden.  
- Article 25 (2) on credit mechanism for the supply of electricity provides that “Economic 
operators that supply renewable electricity to electric vehicles through public recharging 
stations shall receive credits, irrespectively of whether the economic opera tors are subject to 
the obligation set by the Member State on fuel suppliers, and may sell those credits to fuel 
suppliers, which shall be allowed to use the credits to fulfil the obligation set out in paragraph 
1, first subparagraph”. 
o Hydrogen and RFNBOs could be supplied to vehicles by economic operators other 
than fuel suppliers subject to the fuel supplier obligation. The way in which the credit 
mechanism is defined does not allow such operators to generate and exchange credits 
resulting from the sale o f renewable hydrogen or RFNBOs contributing towards the 
targets dedicated to fuel suppliers.  
o We consider further consideration is merited as it is perceived discriminatory by 
market participants and distorts the market . additionally, we suggest reviewing the 
mechanism to ensure a level playing field and a similar mechanism for economic 
operators providing RFNBOs to vehicles, irrespectively of whether the economic 
operators are subject to the obligation set by the Member State on fuel suppliers.  
- Article 27: “For the calculation of the GHG intensity reduction referred to in Article 25(1), first 
subparagraph, point (a), the following rules shall apply:

 (i) for biofuel and biogas, by multiplying the amount of these fuels supplied 
to all transport modes by their emissions savings determined in accordance 
with Article 31; 
 (ii) for renewable fuels of non-biological origin and recycled carbon fuels, by 
multiplying the amount of these fuels that is supplied to all transport modes 
by their emissions savings determined in accordance with delegated acts 
adopted pursuant to Article 29a(3); 
 (iii) for renewable electricity, by multiplying the amount of renewable 
electricity that is supplied to all transport modes by the fossil fuel comparator 
ECF(e) set out in in Annex V;” 
o While the fossil fuel comparator EF(t) is set in Annex V is 94 g CO2eq/MJ, it appears 
that the Directive intends to apply a different comparator when calculating the 
contribution of renewable electricity towards the target. ECF(e), in Annex V is used in 
relation to the production of electricity and set at the level of 183 g CO2eq/MJ in that 
Annex. 
o Using a value of 183 g CO2eq/MJ to calculate the GHG emissions saving from the use 
of renewable electricity represents an overestimation of the GHG emission saving of 
electricity used in transport (where it replaces gasoline, as opposed to replacing 
electricity consumption from coal and other polluting sources  for the production of 
electricity) which undermines the effectiveness of this target. Because electricity 
replaces gasoline / diesel in transport, an adequate comparator should be the EF(t) 
set in Annex V 94 g CO2eq/MJ also in relation to the GHG emission savings from the 
use of electricity. 
o In fact, due to the manner in which the target is defined, the use of electricity towards 
the 13% target would already benefit from a hidden multiplier (for each MJ of 
electricity added to the numerator, approximately 4 MJ of gasoline will be taken out 
of the denominator. If the comparator is set at 183 g CO2eq/MJ, it would give 
electricity an additional multiplier towards the calculation of the target. 
o Art 27,  point (d) states: “the greenhouse gas intensity reduction f rom the use of 
renewable energy is determined by dividing the greenhouse gas emissions saving 
from the use of biofuels, biogas and renewable electricity supplied to all transport 
modes by the baseline”. 
o This article appears to have a typo, having excluded the contribution of RFNBOs from 
the numerator. 
o Hydrogen Europe recommends that  Art 27 (a) point (iii) is amended to establish the 
fossil fuel comparator EF(t) as set in Annex V (94 g CO2eq/MJ) as the adequate 
benchmark for calculating the GHG emissions from the use of renewable electricity. 
o Article 27 (d), RFNBO should be added to the enumeration of energy products which 
provided GHG intensity reduction for the purpose of the target (article 27, point (d)).  
- Art 27 (3) extending additionality criteria to all sectors. We consider it is central to first finalise 
the Delegated Act on RFNBOs and conduct a holistic, transparent impact assessment taking 
international competitiveness fully into account before extending the criteria to any further 
sectors.  
- Art 31 (a) Par 4 The term “renewable gases” is not defined. For legal certainty this should be 
defined in the RED. 
- Art 30 (a) point 2 GOs should not have to be cancelled if RFNBOs are counted towards Art 3 
targets only (how else could you show consumers renewable character).

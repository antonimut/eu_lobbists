Introduction 
Business and Science Poland welcomes the possibility to comment on the proposed amendment of the 
Directive (EU) 2018/2001 on the promotion of  the energy from renewable sources  (RED II). Our 
organization represents entities operating in refinery, chemical, energy, fertilizers, metals and mining, 
postal services, transport and financial sector, employing over 100 thousand employees in Poland, other 
EU Member States, as well as outside the European Union.  
We would like to highlight several important issues identified in the analysis of the proposed solutions.  
 
‘Greenhouse gas intensity reduction target’ in transport 
The draft revision of the RED II Directive provides that Member States set an obligation on fuel suppliers 
to achieve a minimum 13% greenhouse gas intensity reduction target . This approach is a substantive 
change as compared to Directive  (EU) 2018/200 1. U nder the applicable regulations , the transport 
subsectors contribut e its share to the climate target that Member States are required to deliver . 
Considering that the E uropean Commission's analyses indicate that  a new and more ambitious 13% 
target should be achieved in 1/3 through the increased use of (renewable-based) electricity, an 
obligation imposed only on fuel suppliers seems unjustified. Furthermore, fuel suppliers have moderate 
influence on y he pace of development of the electric vehicle fleet,  as well as on the development of 
charging infrastructure 
We propose to: 
▪ maintain the current model, where a Member State, after an analysis of its individual situation, 
decides in which transport subsectors the target will be the most optimal to achieve and thus 
allow to share the obligation between the entities operating in those subsectors, 
▪ increase the role of the RCF (Recycled Carbon Fibres) as a solution in automobile industry that: 
−  supports the path towards efficient circular economy by reclaiming the carbon fibers 
and reusing them for further applications and 
contributes to the reduction of emissions in transport with insufficient development of the  BEV and 
FCEV in CEE countriesObligations and implementing tools  
Under the current legislation, fuel suppliers are committed to fulfill two parallel obligations regarding 
the reduction of CO 2 emissions in transport – (1) indirectly, through ensuring a minimum share of 
renewable fuels (RED II) and (2) directly, through the obligation to meet a 6% reduction target in fuel 
life cycle emissions (FQD1). These obligations are largely carried out with the same tools, which call into 
question the legitimacy of their coexistence. 
▪ In this context, the introduction of one ‘greenhouse gas intensity reduction target’ instead of 
two tools is assessed positively.

▪ Nevertheless, attention should be given to the very ambitious level of 13% reduction target.  
In this context it should be noted,  that in 2020 barely any EU entity was able to fulfil the requirement 
of 6% greenhouse gas intensity reduction  target set in Fuel Quality Directive , while the new RED 
proposal scales it up to 13 % by 2030. Moreover, it is important to stress that the new system withdraws 
the possibility to use UER certificates (Upstream Emission Reduction certificates) , which were an 
important instrument for fuel suppliers towards the 6% reduction target under FQD, eg. Lotos Group 
(BSP Member) counted that without the purchase of UER certificates, the overall fuel life cycle emission 
reduction in 2020 would estimate a bit more than 3%.  
We propose to place a new mechanism instead of the UER, where certificates would be issued on the 
basis of  investments in decarbonising the production of conventional fuels (eg . CCS, electrolysers, 
biogas plants, biorefineries). 
Decarbonizing transport through electrification  
The European Commission proposes d ecarbonization of transport mainly through  electrification. For 
Member States with the dominant share of fossil fuels in the energy mix, the electrification of the fleet 
will not bring the desired target. Under Polish Regulation of the Minister for Energy of 12 June 2017 on 
the methodology for calculating greenhouse gas emissions, determining their emission factors and their 
net calorific value for individual fuels and the energy value of electricity 2, emissions from the electricity 
grid accounts for 221,7 gCO2eq/MJ. The application of the correction factor for electric engines 
determined at the level of 0.4, results in the electricity emissions from transport at the level of 88.68 
gCO2eq /MJ. This allows to achieve 5.8 % reduction in emissions. 
▪ Consequently, this means that with the current energy mix, even the electrification of the 
entire fleet will not be sufficient to achieve the 13% reduction target.  
▪ We are of the opinion that PPAs and certificates of origin should be included to 
demonstrate renewable energy in transport. 
▪ A diametrical increase of obligations in 2030 will significantly raise fuel prices. Furthermore, 
it will constitute an additional pro -inflationary stimulus (as transport costs are included in 
all products’ prices) and it will hardly hit the poorest social groups. Therefore, it is necessary 
to consider such ambitious targets in terms of social costs of, as well as realistic capabilities 
of each Member State. With this in mind, the end-user compensations should be addressed 
under the Just Transition Fund, while the EU ETS auction revenues should be reinvested and 
directly dedicated to finance the costs of both: large-scale and prosumer investments. 
It is not possible at this stage to fully assess the proposed RED amendment as the proposal forsees that 
the methodology of calculating emission reductions - a very important element - will be regulated by 
future delegated acts. Bearing in mind the drafted 2030 objectives, the implementation of delegated 
acts in 2023/2024 timeline seems to be too late. It creates a huge strategic and investment risk and also 
contributes to higher producer’s transformation costs.

▪ The requirement to ensure the use of 50% of 'green' hydrogen in industry by 2030 for energy 
and non-energy purposes seems to be nonrealistic. In our opinion, a level of 10% share would 
be more likely to meet. Alternatively, the RFNBO target should be set as a factor individually

assessed by each industry sector in order to reflect decarbonization capability in the most

▪ Regarding fuel suppliers’ obligation to set 50% share of RFNBO in the industry, it is necessary to 
establish a calculation methodology for integrated plants (raf inery + petrochemistry) to meet 
this target, because flows and mass balances at such plants are more complicated.  At the 
moment, only one type of RFNBO seems to be available to be used on a large scale in the 
horizon of 2030, which is hydrogen produced in the electrolysis process using renewable 
energy.  
▪ Regarding an absence of a hydrogen-powered vehicles, one of the tools for achieving this 
target is to incorporate hydrogen produced from electrolysis into traditional fuels – a method 
allowed by the draft revision of the RED. However, it will not solve the problem.  
Preliminary estimations of LOTOS Group, which is one of the most m odern refineries in Europe and 
capable to incorporate relatively large amounts of hydrogen into traditional fuels, indicate that the 
replacement of its all currently used hydrogen into RFNBO (i.e.  hydrogen from electrolysis) would 
ensure about 2.35% of RFNBO's share in transport. That means that the target will not be achieved.  
▪ Therefore, it is not possible to ensure both objectives at the same time, i.e. 50% of RFNBO in 
industry and 2.6% of RFNBO in transport. 
Hydrogen production based on electrolysis accounts for approx. 1 -2% of the annual H2 production in 
the world. Additionally, to meet the objective of Art. 25 together with the purpose of Art. 22a, a giant 
expansion of additional renewable energy capacity is required regarding the electrolysis process, as well 
as an appropriate amount of network infrastructure and a coverage of water demand. The biggest RES 
hydrogen electrolyser capacity in Europe is only 10 MW3. The commissioning one of the largest green 
hydrogen production projects in Poland - ‘Green H2’ with a capacity of 100 MW electrolyser - will allow 
to convert only 10-12% of the hydrogen used so far in the refinery into "green" hydrogen. 
In this context, the scale of challenges  should be taken into account when considering the proposed 
targets.  
▪ Therefore, we propose to extend the definition of RFNBO and include every available zero and 
low-emission hydrogen, not only hydrogen produced through electrolysis using renewable 
energy sources (see the chapter below “Definition of RFNBO”). 
 
Definition of RFNBO 
We propose to broaden the definition of non-biological renewable fuels (RNFBO) with the aim to meet 
the drafted objectives. There is a need to take into account  and support all possible technologies that 
can contribute to cost-efficient adaptation to the proposed regulations, with regard to the specificity of 
a given country, in particular the potential of renewable energy sources and the condition of the power 
grid. 
We propose to include: 
▪ hydrogen production from biomethane in the steam reforming process  (hydrogen from 
SMR+CCS) as this method was recognized ‘renewable’ by the EU Hydrogen Strategy (A hydrogen 
strategy for a climate -neutral Europe)  published on 17 September 2020. We recognize this 
technology as an important emission reduction potential and crucial for countries lik e Poland 
with high agricultural potential and at the same time low share of RES in energy production. In 
addition, it should be noted that the combination of this technology with CO 2 capture (CCS / 
CCU) would achieve negative emissions throughout the production cycle

Hydrogen (WtH) technologies).Comments to Part B of Annex IX –- feedstock for advanced biofuels 
▪ A limited feedstock is one of the main barriers for the development of advanced biofuels, thus 
it is worth to clarify whether the European Commission has already analyzed the availability of 
feedstock in the context of the proposed 3.5% target for advanced biofuels in 2030.  
▪ It is recommended to introduce the broadest  possible group of feedstock listed in Part B of 
Annex IX that would increase chances for the achievement of the objective.  
▪ Under the current system the energy content from the feedstock listed in Part B of Annex IX 
(UCO, animal fats) is eligible to be double counted, which the new proposal does not provide. 
With this in mind and due to the withdrawal of the multiplier system, the share of biofuels and 
biogas produced from feedstock in Annex IX, Part B, in our opinion should be set at the level of 
3.4% of the energy content of transport fuels supplied for consumption or use on the market 
instead of proposed 1.7% level.  
 
EU Emission Trading Scheme for transport 
A lot of ambiguity is related to the proposal for establishing a new emissions trading scheme for road 
transport and buildings. It is not clear yet whether new EU ETS provisions for transport would generate 
double charging of fuels. The refining sector is covered by EU ETS and already bears the emission costs 
associated with the production of fuels. The introduction of an additional emissions trading scheme for 
transport carries the risk of double cost for the same emissions. 
The draft ETS revision does not specify whether the emissions trading system for transport will be 
general (European) or separate for each Member State. The first solution, following the EC's 
assumptions that the main source of r eduction will be electricity in transport and comparing energy 
mixes Polish and other EU countries, would result in the need for Polish fuel suppliers to purchase 
additional emission reductions from countries with a higher share of RES.  
In addition, it is important to acknowledge that reducing emissions in the buildings and transport sectors 
can vary significantly in cost, which could lead to sub -optimal allocations for decarbonization if both 
sectors are included in a single trading scheme.

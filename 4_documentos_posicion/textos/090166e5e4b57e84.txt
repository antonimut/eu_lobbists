Reaching climate neutrality will not be possible without reshaping the energy system in which the majority of energy 
will come from renewable energy sources by 2050. Energy infrastructure is key to a reliable, affordable and sustain-
able, integrated energy system. Together with all European Transmission System Operators (‘ TSOs’) for electricity, 
Amprion ensures that not only an increasing share of renewable energy is integrated into the electricity system, but 
also that (offshore) renewable energy sources  are connected to customers, demand centres and green hydrogen 
production, while at the same time maintaining system security 24/7. 
Amprion therefore appreciates the review of the revision of the Renewable Energy Directive (‘RED  II’) as it is a key 
instrument for the further integration of renewables and for achieving the decarbonisation objective by 2030. 
The revision of the RED II (‘RED III’) should set the framework that enables and incentivises the increase of the share 
of renewables in all sectors, be it through electrification or use of renewable gases. The inter-sectoral objective should 
be to achieve CO 2 reductions as efficiently as possible. The direct use of electricity with consequent reduction of 
primary energy needs, t ogether with the renewable energy deployment, must be promoted wherever this is techni-
cally and economically efficient and sustainable. At the same time, indirect electrification will be necessary in certain 
sectors where decarbonisation is not technically possible. Therefore, sector integration must be adequately promoted 
through legislation. 
With a view to the climate neutrality objective, it is also important to create planning certainty beyond 2030 by provid-
ing a timely outlook on the further renewable energy deployment and the necessary grid development between 2030 
and 2050. 
KEY ISSUES 
-  Joint projects between Member States  | Joint offshore energy planning per sea basin : TSOs should be 
closely involved into the joint planning process of the bordering states of the European sea basins. A holistic 
planning process of the power infrastructure together with the renewable energy build-out is key to allow for a 
timely and cost-efficient development. Anticipatory investment is key to allow for a smooth process which reduces 
the interferences of space for the public as well as for species on- and offshore. 
- Administrative procedures, regulations and codes | Permitting: The simplification and acceleration of plan-
ning and authorisation procedures as well as the reduction of red tape is not only crucial for the deployment of 
renewables, but also for the necessary grid expansion. This includes shortening the maximum timings as well as 
adapting the European requirements of nature conservation and species protection law with the aim of streamlin-
ing and accelerating permitting processes. 
-  Guarantees of Origin/Calculation in the transport sector | RFNBOs and additionality: Electricity from renew-
able energy sources must be used as efficiently as possible to achieve a maximum greenhouse gas emissions 
reduction at minimum cost for consumers and economy  (Energy E fficiency First principle) . Only if renewable 
energy cannot be integrated into the electricity system it should be made available for sector coupling and used 
by means of electrolysers before it is curtailed. In this context, the additionality criterion serves to ensure that the 
use of renewable fuels of non-biological origin (‘RFNBOs’) actually contributes to the reduction of greenhouse gas 
emissions and that the increasing demand for electricity from renewable energy sources  is met by additional 
renewable energy generation capacities. Guarantees of origin having a finer time ((sub-)hourly) and space gran-
ularity would further strengthen the role of hydrogen in the energy transition.  
-  Facilitating system integration of renewable electricity | Information obligation for TSOs: An obligation for 
TSOs to calculate and report the greenhouse gas emissions content close to real-time would impose a significant 
and disproportionate additional burden that would not be matched by an equivalent benefit. In addition, further 
clarification of the level of detail and accuracy required for the provision of information on the share of renewable 
electricity under the new Art. 20a
 
(1) is needed to ensure that the requirements are feasible and proportionate to 
the actual objective. This also includes a clarification of liability issues as well as the question of data ownership.

ARTICLE 9 | JOINT PROJECTS BETWEEN MEMBER STATES  
The European Commission proposes in its Offshores Renewables Energy Strategy that Member States bordering a 
sea basin shall cooperate to jointly define the amount of offshore renewable energy they plan to produce in that sea 
basin by 2030, 2040 and 2050, taking into account among others the importance of ensuring the associated inte-
grated grid planning (Art. 9(1)).  
Especially when new hybrid projects are considered, the grid development and upfront planning of possible routes 
and their integration into the existing power systems is key to deliver these projects in a timely manner.   
Therefore, we would like to strengthen the need of an integrated planning of renewable energy development 
and grid planning. With the huge investments ahead, TSOs should be allowed to do anticipatory investment in 
due time. This applies for the grid connection to shore but also making offshore infrastructure hybrid ready. Such 
measures can safe time, reduce the environmental impacts and disturbances of the public infrastructure and allow 
for cost reduction as synergies can be raised. Risks of stranded assets are very much un realistic looking at the 
installed offshore capacity today and the target figures in the decades to come.  
Cross-border cooperation and planning also allows for more technical standardisation of cross-border interfaces 
of offshore connection technology which states another potential for significant saving of costs. TSOs would like to 
further work on a more harmonised framework and support the relevant fora in this context.  
Offshore renewable energy deployment needs to be done in consideration of the power system development poten-
tial. As a consequence, TSOs must have a pivotal role in renewable energy planning for each sea basin. Con-
sistency with the need for a holistic on - and offshore approach must be ensured so offsh ore renewable energy 
can be transported to shore and even further to where the power is needed. In this light, achieving the offshore wind 
potential for each basin must be established in strong coordination with the electricity TSOs in the framework of the  
regional groups (e.g. North Seas Energy Cooperation (NSEC), Baltic Energy Market Interconnection Plan (BEMIP)).  
ARTICLES 15, 16 | ADMINISTRATIVE PROCEDURES, REGULATIONS AND CODES 
In order to accelerate the development of renewable energy and the necessary grid infrastructure, there is an urgent 
need for provisions to simplify and accelerate planning and permit procedures , which represent one of the 
biggest obstacles to renewable energy and grid development. Although the provisions from the RED II have provided 
some impetus, the challenges remain and should be addressed with urgency. We therefore appreciate the European 
Commission's announcement to issue a guidance on accelerating permitting processes  for renewable energy pro-
jects in 2022.  
For network investments, which are key to the environmental transition, the European Commission should link 
RED III and other related initiatives with an obligation for Member States to offer fast -track procedures. In 
addition to shortening the maximum timings for obtaining authorisation, measures must be provided for both, renew-
able energy and grids, to ensure effective compliance with the timings provided (e.g. silent consent provision, pro-
motion of the dialogue between the different parties involved in the authori sation process). Moreover, the maximum 
timings for the authorisation procedure provided for in Art. 16 of RED II should not only apply to power plants, but also 
to the assets required for their connection and integration into the grid. 
Furthermore, it should be ensured that these timings are taken into account when determining the timing and planning 
of support schemes, in particular by organising and planning the tendering procedures in a timely  manner. It is also 
crucial to ensure coordination between the authorisation procedures for renewable energy installations and the nec-
essary transmission infrastructure projects, which can help to reduce the time lag between the authorisation of grid 
and renewable energy projects. 
However, the problem of long project planning and procedure durations cannot be solved by setting tighter and tighter 
deadlines for an ever-increasing scope of assessment. Instead, the scope of the assessment must be streamlined 
and simplified. A significant contribution has to be made by the European requirements of nature conservation and 
species protection law, which should be adapted with the aim of facilitating and accelerating approval procedures for 
renewable energy installations and the necessary grid development. To this end, corresponding provisions for the 
application of European nature conservation and species protection regulations  should be included in the 
RED III, e.g.: 
 Stronger and uniform anchoring of the population approach instead of individual protection in investment pro-
jects for application in the Habitat Directive (92/43/EEC, ‘FFH Directive’)) and the Bird Directive (2009/147/EC) 
also aside from the derogation.

Moreover, the participation of citizens and associations in the authorisation procedures  is a high value. How-

longer be effectively raised in the further authorisation procedure or any subsequent court cases. 
ARTICLE 19 AND 27(3) | GUARANTEES OF ORIGINS /CALCULATION IN THE T RANSPORT 
SECTOR AND WITH REGARD TO RFNBOS REGARDLESS OF THEIR END USE 
The 2050 climate neutrality objective can only be achieved if all available decarbonisation options in all sectors are 
used in a wise manner in terms of an efficient CO2 reduction. Therefore, the RED III should also provide the necessary 
framework for the ramp -up of renewable gases. In this context, the provisions should be set up  to make the CO 2 
reductions as economically and energetically efficient as possible  (Energy Efficiency First principle). This in-
cludes, that in the context of the consideration of energy chains, the energy conversions are limited to the necessary 
minimum in order to minimise energy losses and that, as a consequence, renewable energies are used as efficiently 
as possible. However, the contribution of renewable energy sources to the economy’s total end consumption is and 
will remain limited, at least in the short and medium term. Electricity generated from renewable energy sources 
has to be used as efficiently as possible  if a maximum reduction in greenhouse gas emissions is to be obtained 
at minimum cost to the economy. It is therefore better to use electricity directly first for existing applications in the 
electricity system and new applications like electric vehicles and heat pumps. That is because transport and conver-
sion losses are low, while the efficiency of electricity end applications is high. The potential for one kilowatt hour of 
‘green electricity’ to contribute to the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions is greatest in the electricity system itself.  
Only if renewable energy  cannot be integrated into the electricity system it should be made available for sector 
coupling, such as for hydrogen  and PtL. An important prerequisite is that this electricity canno t be integrated 
into the electricity system and is used by means of electrolysers before it is curtailed. In principle, we support the 
considerations at EU level to use the additionality criterion to ensure that the use of renewable fuels of non-biological 
origin (‘RFNBOs’) actually contributes to reducing the greenhouse gas emissions and that the increase in demand 
for electricity from renewable energy sources  is met with additional generation capacity from renewable energy 
sources. 
While the RED III has removed the framework for additionality of electricity in the transport sector (Art.  27(3)), it does 
not indicate the same principle for hydrogen. As the production of hydrogen entails a less efficient use of electricity 
compared to the direct use of the en ergy carrier for final consumption, setting hydrogen objectives would result in 
more renewable energy being installed than required to meet the EU objectives if the same electricity were used for 
direct final use. This must be carefully considered by the legislator when setting the rules for determining the renew-
able origin of hydrogen. 
Extending the same principles for granular certificates to renewable fuels based on a temporal (hourly or sub-hourly) 
and local reconciliation (geographic resolution of guarantees of origin limited to bidding zones) would meanwhile 
reinforce the role of hydrogen in the energy transition. In view of the fundamental changes in an energy system with 
increasingly renewable -based and fluctuating energy generation, the certification of renewable energy should 
take into account real energy flows . It is therefore important that guarantees of origin have a finer granularity

in time and space . In order to ensure that the RED  III provisions (Art.  19) on renewable energy certification are as

 In principle, it should apply that renewable electricity generated from a direct line connection must be matched

 The actual consumption of electricity needs to be matched on a time -granular basis with renewable energy pro-

energy and consumed energy. The aim should be an even finer granularity that matches the most frequent 
settlement period on the wholesale market, i.e. 15 minutes, which will be a key step towards enhancing the way 
in better aligning the renewable energy remuneration with system needs. 
ARTICLE 20a | FACILITATING SYSTEM INTEGRATION OF RENEWABLE ELECTRICITY 
REAL-TIME DATA PROVISION ON RENEWABLE ELECTRICITY SHARE AND GREENHOUSE GAS EMISSIONS 
CONTENT (ARTICLE 20a (1)) 
Paragraph 1 introduces new obligations for electricity TSOs to provide the share of renewable electricity and green-
house gas emissions content delivered in each bidding zone close to real-time (less than one hour), with forecasting 
where available, would provide a signal to consumers and market participants in response to the share of electricity 
from renewable energy sources. 
In principle, the creation of transparency with regard to the availability of renewable energy and the disclosure of 
emissions is to be supported in the sense of strengthening sector coupling, as this strives to make efficient use of as 
much renewable energy as possible and to further decarbonise the energy system.  
Furthermore, the provision of estimated renewable energy  generation da ta is already widespread among TSOs. 
However, the proposed "close to real -time" data provision would lead to a significantly increased effort  for elec-
tricity grid operators, compared to which no equivalent benefit is currently apparent. It is not clear wha t added value 
the proposed provision of data is supposed to generate. Nor is it clear what degree of liability this information should 
have, which might result in conflicts under liability law. 
Apart from the liability issues, there is also no clarity regarding the question of data ownership. The TSOs do not own 
the data to be published. Moreover, it would require a huge effort to obtain all the data required.  
In addition, the technical feasibility needs to be examined from various points of view, including: 1) the possibility 
of publishing such information on an hourly basis; 2) the difficulty of disclosing the percentage of renewables when 
generation is not directly connected to the distribution grid; 3) the use of balancing technologies (e.g. storage) in the 
grid, which further complicates this data provision.  
If the determination of renewable electricity shares is comparatively simple from a methodological stance, but not 
within the time frame required, the calculation of the greenhouse gas content, on the other hand, is very complex. 
The calculation would have to be power plant -specific. In order to avoid market distortion, the determination of the 
greenhouse gas emissions  content needs to follow an objective method ology, e.g. ETS methodology. This would 
require very high measurement and, if necessary, certification costs.  
In sum, this amendment means that considerable and disproportionate additional effort would be incurred from a 
transparency perspective. Therefore, an obligation to calculate and report the greenhouse gas emissions con-
tent should be disregarded. Moreover, further clarification on the level of detail and accuracy required by the 
new Article 20a for the provision of information on the renewable electricity share is needed to ensure the 
requirements are feasible and proportionate to the actual objective.

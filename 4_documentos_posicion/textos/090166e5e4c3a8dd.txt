1. Summary 
Solar Heat Europe welcomes the European Commission’s proposal for the recast of the 
Energy Efficiency Directive (EED)  and supports the European Commission goal to align it 
with the Union's climate objectives. 
Energy efficiency offers multiple benefits  such as greenhouse gas emission reductions, 
lower dependency on energy imports, job creation, alleviating energy poverty and 
improved health. However, the current trends indicate that efficiency investments are not 
happening at the required pace to achieve the necessary energy savings and reduce energy 
consumption.  
Renewable (RES) technologies, like solar thermal heating and cooling, allow for a reduction 
on energy consumption from fossil fuels and benefit consumers. Solar thermal faces similar 
barriers as energy efficiency, including lack of financial support to address the upfront 
investment, low awareness levels and lack of information on why and how to save energy. 
Furthermore, the proposal indicates a clear interest towards heat decarbonisation, 
including concrete measures to address the heating and cooling demand. As such , we 
support the increased ambition reflected in the recast of the EED  and would like to 
additional improvements to the proposal, by proposing some amendments. 
Clear and ambitious policies are needed to demonstrate the serious interest in a societal 
and eco nomical change. With a clear signal, new investment decisions will happen , 
bringing in turn additional investors and financers, creating a virtuous cycle.  
 
EED sets several positive measures although it still presents weaknesses, such as the lack 
of national binding targets, lagging on commitment. To convince and lead a real change, 
the signal must be clear: stronger targets and measures are required to implement a faster 
energy transition, promoting the combination of energy efficiency and decentralized RES 
solutions, in particular renewable heat.  
 
The recast of the Energy Efficiency Directive is an opportunity for a comprehensive and 
reinforced policy framework that addresses in a coherent way both demand and supply.

2. Positive clarification of the Energy Efficiency First principle  
We support the clarification of the Energy Efficiency First principle included in  the 
preamble, and reflected  in  (the new) Article 3: 
The energy efficiency first principle should be applied taking primarily the system efficiency 
approach and societal perspective into consideration. Consequently, it should help increase 
the efficiency of individual end-use sectors and of the whole energy system. 
The definition in Article 3 would benefit from a reference to  the energy efficiency first 
principle being applied across the entire value  chain (generation, distribution, 
transmission, and consumption). 
The application of the “Energy efficiency first” principle  on the planning, operation and 
expansion of networks should avoid investment in potential stranded assets and/or fossil 
fuels lock-in. This also applicable to the expansion of the grid, in the sense that there needs 
to be caution regarding wide electrification to assure that a too fast -paced electrification 
process does not lock-in non-renewable electricity generation, while disrupting the further 
deployment of existing RES heat sources, such as solar thermal.  
Additionally, this principle does not mean to prioritise only energy efficiency measures but 
rather to combine in an optimised manner energy efficiency measures and renewables 
deployment (particularly renewable heat), ensuring the best results in terms of costs and 
sustainability. 
Overall, the inclusion of the “Energy efficiency first” definition helps to avoid 
misinterpretation of the principle for instance in relation to the building  or the industrial 
sectors, where the combination of energy efficiency measures and renewable heat ing 
technologies are very much in line with energy efficiency first principle from a system 
efficiency and societal perspective. Nevertheless, the  
 SHE’s position:  
- support the proposed definition of “Energy Efficiency First principle laid out in 
Article 3, proposing the following  revision: 
Art.3.2. Member States shall ensure that the application of the energy efficiency first 
principle is applied across the entire value chain (generation, distribution, 
transmission, and consumption) and t hat it is verified  by the relevant entities where 
policy, planning and investment decisions are subject to approval and monitoring

3. Higher ambition reflected in national binding targets 
 
The increased level of ambition, including that of the energy efficiency target, will have to 
be complemented by strong measures to underpin its delivery. It will require a profound 
transformation of all sectors of the European economy and energy system, in line with the 
objectives of the Paris Agreement. Increased ambition and binding national targets are 
needed, as well as promoting the interaction with flanking measures at national level, such 
as carbon pricing. As such, provisions in Art. 23, Art. 6 or Art 8 can be streamlined to 
provide more consistency and ambition. 
 
Clear and ambitious policies are needed, to demonstrate the serious interest in a societal 
and economical change. With a clear signal, new investment decisions will happen , 
bringing in turn additional investors and financers, creating a vi rtuous cycle . EED sets 
several positive measures, namely the increased ambition regarding the EU binding target.  
 
Although it still presents weaknesses, such as the lack of binding national targets which 
falls short in terms of commitment and bring many regions to still consider energy 
transition as a second order issue. To convince and lead a real change, the signal must be 
clear: stronger targets and measures are needed to implement a faster energy transition, 
promoting the com bination of energy efficiency and decentralized RES solutions, in 
particular renewable heat.  
 
SHE’s position:  
- Proposal to include national binding targets (Art.4)  and streamline provisions in 
Art. 23, Art. 6 or Art 8 to provide more consistency and ambition.

4. Support the decarbonisation of heating and cooling  
 
Energy efficiency measures and renewable solutions should go hand in hand , as they are 
complementary and equally reinforcing their targets. This is particularly true in the case of 
decentralised RES, since energy efficiency facilitates the integration of renewable heating 
and cooling sources like solar thermal, jointly contributing to the increase of EU’s climate 
ambition. A well -insulated building will have lower space heating needs, lower 
temperatures requirements and smoother variations in demand, which helps renewable 
heat solutions to be more competitive and easier to integrate.  
 
Similarly, with respect in particular to industrial heat requirements, replacing old processes 
(that use heat cascading) by systems that scale up temperature up to the needs of each 
process, will facilitate the use of RES heat in different segments. By reducing final energy 
consumption through energy efficiency measures, it is easier also to increase the ambition 
in relation to renewable energy targets, in particular for renewable heating and cooling. At 
the same time higher penetration of decentralised renewable energy solutions (namely 
heat) makes the energy system overall more efficient.  
 
Therefore, it is natural that the EED, the Renewable Energy Directive and EPBD are 
intertwined, notably around the issue of the heating and cooling sector. As such, it , it is 
logical to include a definition of “renewable heat” in Article 2, including solutions such 
as solar heat, ambient heat, geothermal or sustainable biomass. 
 
Furthermore, provisions such as the energy savings obligation will help to accelerate access 
to financing for the implementation of energy efficiency measures in buildings, including 
deep renovations, while the EPBD sets the rules on how to improve the energy 
performance of buildings. Removing indirect and direct fossil fuel subsidies is now critical 
as these are a significant competitive distortion which is not compatible with the climate 
targets or the Renovation Wave  and hinders the deployment of energy efficiency 
measures or renewable technologies like solar thermal heat. The interconnection between 
EED, EPBD and RED II should not be forgotten when it comes to the accounting of targets. 
 
The recast of the EED is an opportunity to strongly promote fuel switch towards renewable 
sources both in residential, industrial and district heating systems. In this context, a better 
definition of “efficient district heating” will be needed since the aim of article 24 is to 
increase the share of renewables in district heating networks. Therefore, natural gas cannot 
have a preferential role in the recast and renewable heat must be the preferred solution 
for new and substantially refurbished systems.

Finally, the provision on Heating and cooling assessment and planning are an essential 
element of the proposed recast (A rt. 23). According to this article, member states must 
include in their National Energy and Climate Plans (NECP) a comprehensive assessment on 
their heating and cooling demand, including a cost-benefit analysis. This exercise is critical 
for the decarboniza tion of heating and cooling, since the value of the comprehensive 
assessment (CA) to local authorities in designing their decarbonisation plans is 
paramount.  
 
It is essential that CAs are thorough enough, including renewable heating and cooling  
demand in households (like solar thermal for space and water heating, cooking, cooling), 
in commercial buildings and industry  (solar industrial process heat, SHIP). The recast of 
the EED could also ensure a successful Renovation Wave , going beyond the usual 
improvements on the building envelop or lighting systems, and take a comprehensive 
approach to the topic, pushing also for the modernisation of heating and cooling. 
 
The revised EED Article 23 states that the CA is required for municipalities with over 50 
000 in habitants, as a tool to prepare local heating and cooling plans. The urgency of 
achieving significant reductions on CO2 emissions related to heating and cooling require 
more ambition and as such the threshold should be set at a lower level, such as 20 000 
inhabitants. This requirement needs to be complemented by support measures to 
municipalities, in particular the smaller ones. These measures shall include financing and 
technical support to the development and implementation of their local heating and 
cooling plans. 
  
SHE’s position:  
- Stress the need to: 
o Include in the comprehensive assessments  (Art. 23 and Annex IX), data on 
renewable heating and cooling  demand in households, in commercial 
buildings and industry.  
o Include definition of renewable heat in Article 2. 
o Stress the need to remove indirect and direct fossil fuel subsidies. 
o Extend requirement for  local heating and cooling plans  to municipalities 
over 20 000 inhabitants. 
o Address the need of setting up financial and technical support for 
municipalities to develop and implement their local heating and cooling 
plans.

5. Promote planned replacement of old heating systems 
Consumers will play a key role in the energy transition , by investing in energy efficiency 
measures, by decarbonising their consumption and future proofing their businesses and 
homes. Replacing a broken space and/or water heater is often a stressful time. It is urgent; 
it needs to be available locally, installed quickly and not be too expensive. For that to 
happen, consumers need options, be it through a utility or self -consumption to find the 
right choice that meets their needs. To accomplish this means raising awareness of 
renewables at a local level and, most importantly, ensure that options are compatible with 
a 2050 carbon neutral society, which means that we need to ensure that all solutions for 
heating and cooling installed after 2030 are decarbonised.  
 
This is a critical decade for this transition and the European Union and Member Sates need 
to set up cleat measures to encourage consumers to replace Europe’s 100 million 
inefficient boilers by providing replacement schemes which encourages “planned 
replacement” with more efficient and renewable heating systems. 
 
The recast of the EED is an opportunity to step up the renovation and retrofit of old system 
with efficient and renewable heating systems. As such, SHE supports the inclusion of a 6% 
yearly target for the replacement of old and inefficient heating equipment.  
 
SHE’s position:  
- Proposal to include: 
o a 6% yearly target for the replacement of old and inefficient heating 
equipment. 
o in Article 21 and Article 23, references to the need for measures addressing 
the planned replacement of old and inefficient heating equipment.

6. Mitigate energy poverty 
 
To address climate targets and, simultaneously, to tackle energy poverty require an effort 
to make our homes future proof< This implies particular care about heating and cooling, as 
space heating accounts for 65% of energy consumption in homes. In addition, water 
heating represents a similar level of energy consumption as power appliances do (15%), 
which adds to the relevance of tackling both water and space heating. 
The combination of efficiency and renewable energy should be optimised to provide the 
consumers with sustainable options while maintaining lower overall heating costs. One of 
the options is to support the transition from old and inefficient heating systems (mostly 
fossil fuel based) with efficient and renewable heating systems.  
 
Renewable heating systems, notably solar thermal, require higher upfront investment by 
have  low operating costs. They have a competitive levelised cost of heat and lower 
lifecycle costs than other heating solutions, both fossil-fuel based or even renewable. 
They provide important reductions on heating costs, simultaneously providing saving on 
greenhouse gas emissions and contributing to the improvement of air quality in urban 
areas. 
 
Therefore, we applaud the additional attention given to measures focused on vulnerable 
consumer groups and stress the need for effective measures, including financial support  
and addressing both energy efficiency measures and renewable heat solutions, to 
alleviate energy poverty. 
 
SHE’s position:  
- Welcome additional references and measures tackling energy poverty 
- Stress the importance of setting fin ancial support applicable to energy efficiency 
measures and renewable heat solutions.

7. Improve Primary Energy Factor 
SHE welcomes the clarity that is provided with the additional references to the Primary 
Energy Factor in Art. 29. T he primary energy factor (PEF) is particularly relevant in the 
context of energy labelling and ecodesign regulations for space and water heaters. The 
improvements in data collection regarding the energy system shall allow for more 
Members States to have th eir own estimations, avoiding the use of a default value at 
European level that does not reflect properly the national context. 
 
Furthermore, the Primary Energy Factor should be dynamic (seasonal) and regional. Data 
are available to improve the method, increasing transparency. An example is the impact of 
the use of electricity for heating. In most countries the PEF (and emissions) increase during 
winter, depending also on the national/regional level on which sources are used. Hence  a 
unique PEF does not reflect changes in the context. 
 
SHE’s position:  
- Stress the need to promote national/regional PEF, which is dynamic/seasonal.

MedTech Europe welcomes th e opportunity of this public consultation to provide input  on the upcoming 
revision of the CLP Regulation. We urge the Commission to consider sectoral legislation already in place 
when revising the CLP Regulation  in order to adopt the appropriate level of regulatory consistency. As far 
as possible, alignment with the international UN GHS system should be ensured.  
 
CLP and sectorial legislation applying to medical technologies  
 
Sectorial legislation applies to med ical devices (MD Regulation (EU) 2017/745  - MDR) and in vitro  
diagnostic medical devices (IVD Regulation (EU) 2017/746  - IVDR). The CLP Regulation is referenced in 
each of these regulations under their General Safety and Performance Requirements (see Appendix I).  
 
Under the MDR:  
 
• As a result, changes to the hazard classifications of substances which are carcinogenic, mutagenic 
or toxic to reproduction (CMRs) of category 1 A or 1B and the classification of substances with 
endocrine disrupting (ED) properties can have downstream implications for the medical technology 
sector. 
 
• Specific provisions apply to certain medical devices when containing CMR  1A/1B or endocrine 
disrupting substances. Devices shall be designed and manufactured in such a way as t o reduce as 
far as possible the risks posed by substances or particles, that may be released from the device.  
(see Appendix I: MDR Annex I, Chapter II, Section 10.4.1; IVDR Annex I, Chapter II, Section 10.3). 
 
• Labelling of those substances (see Appendix I: MDR Annex I, Chapter II, Section 10.4 .5) and a risk 
justification for their use (see Appendix I: MDR Annex I, Chapter II, Section 10.4 .2) is required 
where these substances are present above a threshold of 0,1% weight by weight in certain medical 
devices. Such substances can only be used in medical devices where the benefit -risk assessment 
supports the continued use of these substances. 
 
• The recent up-classification of Cobalt metal as Carcinogenic 1B and Reprotoxic 1B has resulted in 
significant impacts and challenges for the medical technologies industry, requiring labelling and risk 
analysis of devices containing Cobalt -based alloys such as CoCr and ma ny grades of stainless 
steel. Under the current CLP  regulation, metal alloys are considered as mixtures that require 
classification using information related to the classification of the substances (such as Cobalt) that 
they contain. However, the CLP Regulat ion also recognises the status of metal alloys as special 
mixtures, that have a different hazard profile to the constituent metals (ref. Appendix III). The 
special case of cobalt illustrates the conflict that exists between CLP and MDR where the use of 
cobalt-based alloys that are biocompatible and benefit from a long history of safe clinical use in 
medical devices are required to be labelled as indicating the presence of a CMR substance.  
 
For these reasons, MedTech Europe proposes that the European Commission addresses the need 
for further guidance on this aspect in the context of this CLP revision. The classification of metal 
alloys under CLP should take into account the special properties of alloys, which could also be 
applied under downstream legislation  such as MDR. This is relevant for Cobalt and potentially 
other metals used in alloy form in medical devices.

Under the IVDR:  
 
• The CLP Regulation excludes IVDs from its scope . The IVD Regulation requires that CLP 
Regulation requirements apply for the IVD  label with a specific derogation:  where IVDs contain 
substances or mixtures which may be considered as being dangerous, the label must follow the 
CLP Regulation (IVDR Annex I, 20.1i). Because many IVDs can be small yet must fulfil a 
considerable list of IVD -specific labelling requirements, derogation is given to place most CLP 
elements in the instructions for use where there is insufficient space on the IVD label for including 
all CLP elements. It should be considered that the label requirements set out in the IVD Regulation 
are specific for IVDs and communicate information which is essential to the IVD user in supporting 
the safety, performance and traceability of the IVD.  
 
• Other IVDR General Safety and Performance Requirements cover the design and manufacturing of 
the device as well as warnings and precautions, taking into account hazardous substances 
including those which are CMR, endocrine disrupting or which could result in  sensitisation or an 
allergic reaction by the patient or user. S afe disposal of waste is also covered,  hence introducing 
the notion of environmental hazard into the device design, information and lifecycle.  
 
New categories of hazard classes for Endocrine Disruptors 
 
MedTech Europe supports the European Commission’s objective to establish a horizontal, legally binding 
mechanism to identify endocrine disruptors (EDs) based on the WHO definition. That said, CLP is primarily 
designed to classify adverse effect s, whereas endocrine disruption – as per the WHO definition – happens 
via an endocrine mode of action that is causally linked to an adverse effect.  
 
In the case of medical devices, due to the cross -reference to CLP in the MDR, the identification as a 
hazardous substance under CLP automatically triggers downstream user obligations. A CLP classification 
can therefore have a significant impact which is not considered in CLP decision -making. Hence, 
substances deemed suitable for use in medical device based on a  positive benefit-risk assessment could 
still face restrictions or be subject to authorization under REACH. We believe that the REACH SVHC 
process (i.e., Article 57f) provides for the best transparent and predictable framework for EDs, as proposed 
by the European Commission in its communication on the EU’s Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability.  
 
EDs in medical devices are already adequately identified and regulated —including relevant information on 
safe use—under 10.4 and other sections of MDR (see “Exempt ion from CLP Regulations” below, for more 
information), ultimately driven by REACH Article 59. We ask the European Commission not to limit policy 
options for regulating EDs to the CLP regulation only. Furthermore, a classification of a substance as 
having endocrine disrupting properties should only happen based on sufficient scientific evidence and not 
for suspected properties, as this can create legal uncertainty on the related legislative requirements.   
 
Critically, the identification as an endocrine disruptor should benefit from an acceptable transition timeline 
before taking effect. This would allow to assess impact on downstream uses and to have sufficient time to 
update risk analysis and labelling.

Exemption from CLP Regulation 
 
Currently, MDs are partially exempted and IVDs are entirely exempted from labelling obligations under the 
CLP Regulation (Article 1(5). These exemptions should be maintained for the following reasons: 
 
- The sectoral legislation requires more specific labelling obl igations for IVDs and medical devices  
than the CLP Regulation (see Appendix I : MDR Annex I, Section 10.4.5. and Section 23.2 and 
Section 23.4(u)), with an overarching principle to provide clear and legible information to allow safe 
use of the device. More  information on the labelling would dilute further the necessary and crucial 
information, like patient safety, performance and traceability  information. In the case of the IVD 
label, a hierarchy of information has been clearly set out including how to include CLP labelling 
requirements.   
 
- MedTech Europe recommends that if for exempted devices any environmental hazards remain , 
that those are communicated to users through the labelling regulated by sectorial legislation.  
 
- Provision of information for safe handling and use is sufficiently ensured for these devices by 
existing sectoral legislation. This covers human health as well as environment al aspects, like 
advice on adequate and safe waste disposal (see Appendix II : MDR Annex I, Chapter II, Section 
14.7) and the consideration of potential damage to the environment as part of the risk management 
for medical devices as part of the “harm” definition (see Appendix IV, Clause 3.3 of ISO 14971). 
 
 
Transition times 
 
As our specific, downstream legislations make reference to horizontal legislation (REACH and CLP), any 
change to the horizontal legislation (such as the introduction of a new hazard clas s for endocrine 
disruptors) impacts the sectorial legislations implying identification of risks. Depending on the change, a 
sufficiently long transitional period will be required . Risk management of medical devices and IVDs are 
strictly regulated by the MDR and IVDR and requires significant effort and time for approval.  
The same applies for any change of scope, withdrawal of exemptions which would affect all exempted 
medical devices and in vitro  diagnostic medical devices and require their change of strictly regulated 
labelling.

Harmonisation with the UN Globally Harmonised System 
 
MedTech Europe asks the European Commission to keep the Classification, Labelling and Packaging of 
substances and mixtures in line with the UN Globally Harmoni sed System level (UN GHS)  as far as 
possible. Many medical devices and IVD manufacturers  cover the whole internation al market. A global 
harmonised system should therefore remain in place to favor global trade policy, facilitate export and import 
to the EU, strengthen the EU competitiveness at global scale  and avoid administrative  burdensome 
procedures and additional related costs for the medical technologies sector.

IV.5.6.1 Classification of alloys and complex metal containing materials  
Metal alloys, or alloy manufacturing products are not simple mixtures of metals or metal compounds, since the alloy has clear ly 
distinctive properties compared to a classical mixture  of its metal components. Justified by their intrinsic properties, the solubility 
properties can differ substantially from what is observed for each individual constituent in that alloy (eg the rate and exte nd of metals 
release from pure metals are differe nt from the ones from alloys). The rate and extend to which the ingredient of the alloy react with 
the media to transform to water soluble forms can be measured in the same way as with metals (by using the OECD 
Transformation/Dissolution test (Annex 10 to UN GHS)). However, alloys often react slowly and to a very limited extent, making the 
application of the T/D protocol more complex. Special care should be taken in this respect to the detection limit and the acc urate 
determination of the measured surface. Initial testing of alloys, using the T/D protocol, shows that this can be useful but further 
additional guidance on this aspect is recommended.

Appendix IV: ISO 14971 Medical devices — Application of risk management to medical devices 
 
Clause 3.3 of ISO 14971 in its section on Terms and Definitions defines “harm” as an “injury or damage to the health of 
people, or damage to property or the environment”. 
The rational e of the requirement (Annex A.2.3 Terms and definitions) elaborates that “Damage to property and the 
environment” is undesirable and the associated risks need to be considered as well, for example those related to hazardous wa ste 
materials created by the use or disposal of the medical device.”

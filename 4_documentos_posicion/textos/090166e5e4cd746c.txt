We have decided not to respond to the survey itself but instead to provide written comments, because 
we are concerned about the tone and content of many of the questions and doubt that answering 
them directly will provide the Commission with useful information or allow us to properly express our 
opinions. The survey appears biased with q uestions engineered to garner public support for the 
introduction of endocrine disruptor classes in CLP . It  starts from the assumption that endocrine 
disruption is a troubling hazard, rather than a mechanism by which harm could potentia lly come 
about. It a lso assumes that additional testing on animals will be required to determine if a  known 
hazardous substance causes harm via an endocrine-disrupting mechanism, whereas that hazard may 
be adequately controlled by risk management measures that are already in place. 
We are additionally concerned about the following questions:  
 The Section 2 preamble  states that “there are not yet full alternatives to animal testing of 
chemicals” and “ that to know if a chemical is harmful … tests will have to be done on some 
species of animals” but fails to mention that there is not a full suite of validated animal tests 
for detecting and characterising Endocrine Disrupting Chemicals (EDCs) either, and that many 
of the animal tests are unreliable and unpredictive of human outcomes. For example, in 26% 
of chemicals , repeats of the Uterotrophic Bioassay (developed in the 1930s) produced 
different classifications.1 Using animal tests to detect and characterise EDCs is not a necessity 
but a policy decision, and the Commission needs to be clear about this – and about the 
weaknesses of animal tests – to avoid misleading people. 
 Question 3 (“In order to balance the increased protection of human health and of the 
environment with animal welfare …”) creates a false dichotomy by  presenting protection of 
human health and the environment at the expense of animal welfare, and vice versa, as the 
only two options. The question is incredibly leading  and makes it impossible for someone to 
select the first answer without suggesting that they support reduced protection for human 
health and environment. In reality, agreeing that “animal testing is unacceptable for chemicals 
safety purposes and should sto p now ” is completely compatible with wanting increased 
protection of human health and the environment, because making best use of existing data, 
generating new data only through non-animal approaches  and implementing  the 
precautionary principle can offer that protection.

 Questions 10 and 46 do not allow expression of a nuanced view. We support classification and 
labelling of environmental hazards for the product types listed, but only if this does not involve 
conducting new animal tests. This is particularly important for cosmetics, where there is huge 
public and political support for protecting and strengthening the animal testing bans to ensure 
that cosmetics tests on animals are not conducted for any purpose. 
 Question 14 (“Are you in favour of a sub -categorisation for chemicals with a high level of 
certainty on their endocrine disrupting properties, as for mutagenic chemicals (e.g. Categories 
1A and 1B)? ”) does not allow explanation of why this may, or may no t, be favoured. This is 
important; in our view,  the existence of a  “Category 2 ” classification could bring serious 
ramifications regarding the impact on animals used in new tests, depending on how the 
criteria are set (see our comments below). 
 It is inappropriate to seek opinion on Question 26 (“To what extent do you think that the data 
currently available on chemicals are sufficient to perform an assessment for the foreseen 
hazard classes...?”), especially without giving the option to provide evidence to support the 
answer. If the Commission does  not know the answer to this crucial question, it needs to 
conduct a study  to find out ; requiring new animal tests when existing data is sufficient  for 
hazard assessment goes against the last resort requirement. 
 Question 34 again falsely presents what is a policy decision (“ To derive the correct 
classification of certain chemicals, the use of animal testing is still necessary”) as an objective 
necessity. The question is very leading as it does not allow respondents to answer “yes”  to 
either part  without implying that they support incorrectly classifying certain chemicals.  
Whether non-animal approaches are able to correctly classify chemicals or – more relevantly 
– sufficiently protect human health and the environment is not a matter of opin ion; the 
Commission needs to decide on this point.  
We urge the Commission to disregard the responses to Questions 3, 26 and 34 for the reasons stated 
above. 
Though CLP does not set information requirements, it determines the form of information generated 
under REACH and other sector -specific regulations that is used to classify and label . For example, if 
classification according to CLP for a given hazard requires animal test data, the information 
requirement under REACH and other relevant regulations will stipulate use of an animal test and the 
regulator will reject an attempt to meet the information req uirement without using an animal test .2 
When widening the scope of CLP to new hazard classes or product types, the Commission must 
therefore take care to ensure  that criteria are constructed to allow  non-animal methods and 
approaches to be used for classification. This will minimise the impact on animals used in new tests of 
widening the scope of CLP and will allow NAMs to be fully implemented as they become available. In 
particular, widening the scope of CLP to cover cosmetics must not be allowed to undermine the ban 
on conducting new animal tests on cosmetics and their ingredients to assess safety for humans.

We oppose the introduction of hazard classes for endocrine disruptors in CLP. 
International harmonisation is of the utmost importance to avoid duplication of testing and divergent 
classification outcomes across different jurisdictions. If new ED hazard classes are set in CLP before 
UN GHS, global harmonisation may ultimately become more difficult due to the increasingly different 
ED testing and hazard identification systems across different jurisdictions, thus creating a barrier to 
the eventual replacement of all animal test methods in the EU, as per the goal described by Directive 
2010/63/EU. Rushing to introduce new CLP hazard classes for ED that do not exist under GHS does not 
support the principle of global harmonisation and should be avoided. 
Introducing hazard classes for ED in CLP is premised on endocrine disruption being a troubling hazard 
in itself, rather than a mechanism by which harm could potentially come about. This then supports 
testing on animals to specifically determine if a substance causes harm via an endocrine -disrupting 
mechanism, whereas if a substance is already classified as, for example, carcinogenic or toxic to 
reproduction, there may be no practical purpose in chasing an EDC classification as it will not lead to 
any more stringent risk management measures . We acknowledge that identification of endocrine 
active substances is an important component of identifying EDCs , but we object to new tests on 
animals being performed specifically and solely for the purposes of determining that a substance is 
endocrine active for those substances where significant adverse effect(s) have already been identified 
and controlled for. Mechanisti c in vivo tests aimed at addressing the endocrine activity criterion for 
endocrine disruptor identification, but which will not give rise to any change in risk management 
measures, are unnecessary and must be avoided.  
While CLP informs on classification and labelling duties, rather than detailing legal requirements to 
generate new data, the creation of ED hazard classes nevertheless influences the type and extent of  
testing that occurs for the purposes of endocrine disruptor identification; this is acknowledged in the 
survey itself  (“The foreseen introduction of new classes of hazards in CLP (such as endocrine 
disruptors) is likely to increase testing, including on animals, to assess if a  chemical is safe or not for 
human health or the environment ”). To illustrate the scale of the potential impact on animals we 
estimate that between 220,000 and 350,000 rats, up to 330,000 fish, and between 1,700,000 and 
2,700,000 frogs and tadpoles – potentially over 3 million protected animals – could be used in new in 
vivo mechanistic tests for REACH registered substances with the aim of determining if substances are 
endocrine active, regardless of whether a potentiall y ED -relevant adverse effect has already been 
identified for that substance.3

We do not see how introducing standalone hazard classes or criteria for immunotoxicity and 
neurotoxicity offers any benefit over the current mechanism of immunotoxicants and neurotoxicants 
being classified as toxic to reproduction or specific organs (repeated exposure). Does the Commission 
believe that the current mechanism offers inadequate protection to human health and, if so, can it 
substantiate that claim with evidence? Additionally, it is unclear what the rationale is for singling out 
these hazards; why introduce specific classes or criteria for neurotoxicity and immunotoxicity but not, 
for example, cardiotoxicity? We believe that introducing separate immunotoxicity and neurotoxicity 
hazard classes or criteria in CLP could have the negative impact of increasing testing on animals (in the 
same way that introducing the endocrine disruptor hazard classes will), whilst failing to offer any 
additional protection to human health. 
Regardless of whether the Commission pushes ahead with developing standalone hazard classes or 
criteria for immunotoxicity and neurotoxicity, it is crucial going forward that these hazards can be 
classified on the basis of NAMs data, accommodating developments including  the outcome of the 
ongoing OECD project developing a  battery of in vitro  tests for identifying and characterising  
developmental neurotoxicants.6 
We fully support the introduction of clear statements supporting the use of data generated by non-
animal methods to conclude on the criteria related to toxicity and bioaccumulation; we agree it is 
important to provide a strong signal on the importance of non -animal data for the classification of 
substances as PBT, vPvB and PMT.   
Instruction should also be given so that the criteria (P, M, B or T)  which can be wholly addressed 
without requiring generation of any new in vivo test data, or, if that is not applicable, using the least 
amount of new in vivo test data, should be evaluated first. In this way, the need to generate new in 
vivo data for the remaining criteria may be avoided. In practise, this may equate to the P or M criterion 
(neither are likely to involve any in vivo test data) being evaluated in the first instance, followed next 
by the B criterion or possibly the T criterion (both of which may potentially be addressed using new 
non-animal data, or by using existing in vivo data).

Specific recommendations for classifying endocrine disruptors 
It must be made clear that there is always the option to apply a weight of evidence (WoE) approach 
to establish whether there is evidence for adversity and/or endocrine activity, which may 
incorporate non-animal species-relevant data. If the type of data that could be used to identify 
substances causing adversity or eliciting endocrine activity, i.e. human data, animal test data, or non-
animal data (in vitro, in silico etc.), is to be explicitly described in the CLP text then all possible types 
should be included, noting that the strength of evidence understood to be offered by each type can 
change as the state of the art evolves (in any case this should always be addressed in up-to-date 
guidance). The CLP text must make it clear that a WoE determination can be used as strong or 
moderate evidence that a substance causes adversity and/or elicits endocrine activity. 
As stated above, we support use of the phrase “biologically plausible link” in the criteria for ED 
identification;7 the CLP text and/or associated guidance should describe what constitutes a strong, 
moderate or weak basis for a biologically plausible link, making it clear that such considerations do 
not need to incorporate substance-specific test data and that performing new tests to that end is 
not required. 
The option to apply a WoE approach to establish whether there is evidence for adversity and/or 
endocrine activity, which may incorporate non-animal species-relevant data, should apply to 
Category 1 and Category 2 equally. 
We do not support Category 2 classification unless it is properly defined to ensure only substances 
with a well-founded concern for both adversity and endocrine activity are classified, otherwise 
Category 2 classification would be disproportionate, could lead to public uncertainty, and could 
result in excessive additional animal testing in attempts to definitively prove that a substance should 
actually be Not Classified. In any case, Category 2 must not be a catch-all bin for many substances 
where there is insufficient cause for concern regarding adversity or endocrine activity. Substances 
where there is weak or no evidence indicating adverse effect or endocrine activity, and it is deemed 
that “sufficient investigation” has not yet been performed, for whatever reason, should not be 
classified, i.e. they should not be assigned Category 2 classification by default.  
This survey will not provide the information required to sufficiently address the impact on animals 
used in new tests of the proposed changes to CLP. 
It is crucial to consider in the impact assessment the indirect effects that widening the scope of CLP – 
both to new hazard classes and additional product categories – may have on information

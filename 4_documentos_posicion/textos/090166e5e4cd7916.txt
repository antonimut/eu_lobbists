 
 
Unilever attachment to the CLP consultation  
The revision of the CLP regulation  represents an opportunity to upgrade CLP labelling to make the 
information relevant, understandable and actionable  for the end user . The digitalisation of labelling 
can help to meet th is objective while sectorial legislation – also under revision - remains the ideal 
framework to provide consumer-focused information about the safety of consumer products.  
This exercise also represents a timely opportunity to  update the official guidelines to maximize the 
use of non-animal methods for existing hazard categories in line with article 7.1 of the CLP legislation. 
The EU has the potential to play a leading role globally in developi ng, evaluating, and implementing 
hazard classification criteria based on New Approach Methodologies (NAMs), rather than animal 
studies. 
Consumer focus  
Over the years hazard labelling of chemicals and mixtures in the European Union has shown its value 
in informing the professional end-user on their safe use and precautionary measures while handling 
these. However, when it comes to the use of the same labelling for consumer products, several pieces 
of research1 have shown that consumers struggle to understand CLP pictograms, and the amount of 
text associated with these.  
Furthermore, some information can lead to consumer confusion as to what is the more sustainable 
choice, or how to effectively and sustainably use or dispose of the product . A clear example is the 
environmental classification of detergents linked to the presence of (readily biodegradable) 
surfactants. The more concentrated products may become classified as ‘ Harmful to aquatic life with 
long term effects’ with the advice to ‘ Avoid release to the environment’. Yet more diluted products - 
that use more packaging and require more energy in transport  -, would not have to carry this label, 
despite releasing a similar amount of the surfactant down the drain due to their higher dosage per 
wash. 
We would therefore favour the revision of the CLP labelling requirements for products aimed at the 
general public, making the information consumer relevant, understandable and actionable.   Sector 
specific legislation (e.g. the Detergent Regulation and Cosmetics Products Regulation) would be the 
ideal vehicle to govern this more consumer-focused safety labelling. 
Digitalisation 
We believe that the development of digital technologies is an oppo rtunity to provide tailored, 
actionable, and detailed information to consumers. Not only can it help in preventing overload of 
information on pack, it can also be instrumental to meet the demands of specific consumers that are 
unable to read what is on pack or need dedicated information quickly (e.g. on allergens). 
Moreover, future sustainable consumption modes designed to reduce packaging like in-store re-filling 
and at home refills may even more rely on digital information to correctly inform consumers. As ways 
of doing shopping continue to evolve, the digitalisation of information is becoming increasingly 
important. For example, for e -commerce, consumers are not necessarily best informed if all

Scope of CLP 
The consultation suggests expanding the scope of the CLP Regulation to sectors that already have a 
very well-functioning sectoral legislation such as Cosmetics. We would object to applying CLP labelling 
to Cosmetic products as w e believe that such labelling would not deliver any clear and eas y to 
understand information on to how to use these products effectively , safely  and in a sustainable 
manner.  
Several studies, including ECHA’s, show the limited benefits of generic hazard labels compared to 
specific use -relevant instructions and warnings, such as those required in the Cosmetic Products 
Regulation for each cosmetic product placed on the market2.  For this reason, we strongly believe that 
any specific labelling instructions, including related to the environment, should be covered in sector-
specific regulations. 
Animal testing 
Ending animal testing under CLP and REACH  is essential for meeting ever-increasing societal 
expectations (75% of EU consumers say that they don’t  want to buy consumer products associated 
with animal testing3) and therefore essential for enabling businesses to remain competitive in Europe.  
We are highly concerned that new hazard classes under the CLP regulation  could increase the need 
for new data based on animal testing, with no benefit for protecting human health or the 
environment. It is paramount to make safety decisions on ingredients u sing the best science, 
considering all available existing (eco)toxicology data without generating new data requiring animal 
testing.  
The revision of CLP is an opportunity to review and update the existing official guidelines to maximize 
the use of non-animal methods for the existing hazard classes, to respect the mandate of CLP article 
7.1 that tests on animals shall be undertaken only where no other alternatives which provide adequate 
reliability and quality of data are possible4.  
We believe that through the revision of CLP the EU can take the lead at UN level on the proposal of 
hazard classification criteria that are based on New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) rather than 
animal studies.  
New hazard classes 
The introduction of new hazard classes to the CLP regulation would need to be rooted in sound 
science, avoid duplication of existing ones (e.g. Endocrine disruption vs Reproductive toxicity) and be 
aligned with existing global initiatives (e.g. WHO definition of endocrine disruption) to enable future 
global harmonisation with the United Nations’ "Globally Harmonized System of Classification and 
Labelling of Chemicals" (GHS). 
Safeguarding upfront alignment of CLP with the GHS would avoid complexities and barriers to trade 
(i.e. for exports to and fr om the EU) and protect Europe’s competitiveness without compromising 
consumer or environmental benefits.

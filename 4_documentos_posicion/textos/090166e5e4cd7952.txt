ROCKWOOL position on the CLP revision – aligning the CLP with the objectives of 
the Green Deal 
ROCKWOOL Group is a manufacturer of stone wool operating in the EU and beyond. Our 
man-made vitreous (silicate) fibres (MMVF) are exempted from classification under Note Q 
in Annex VI of the CLP and are registered under REACH. We welcome the Commission’s 
revision of the CLP, and recommend that the Commission takes a holistic approach to this 
revision in consideration of the framework for regulation of chemicals at the EU and global 
level.   
The CLP as the centre piece for hazard assessment in the EU 
The EU’s Green Deal provides the backdrop for the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability 
(CSS), and we support the Commission’s objective of having the CLP as the starting point 
for any hazard assessment and classification of a substance. In particular, we note that the 
Commission intends to further align related downstream legislation with the CLP, and 
would welcome some clarity on how the Commission will ensure that hazards are 
assessed in a harmonised manner. This should include concrete guidance on how the 
‘one substance one assessment’ approach will be implemented in practice by the 
regulators. We also encourage the Commission to ensure that the involved 
representatives continue to work within their area of expertise (whether this is CLH, value 
setting under workers protection legislation etc.) to ensure that any recommendations and 
opinions from RAC and Member States are based on sound science and relevant data. 
ROCKWOOL Group takes note of the intentions to group substances of concern as 
outlined in the CSS and the draft Restrictions Roadmap.1 To ensure that the objectives of 
ensuring the highest protection of human health and the environment are met, we support 
such an approach. This could for example be when there is sound, unequivocal evidence 
and data that a group of substances are suspected of having harmful properties which will 
make such an assessment more fit-for-purpose than regulating the individual substances 
in the group one by one.   
 
The link between circular economy, the Green Deal and chemicals legislation 
The Circular Economy Action Plan2 underlines the importance of stone wool and other 
types of insulation materials in meeting waste management objectives, and the 
Commission particularly committed itself to that ‘As part of the revision of the recovery 
targets for construction and demolition waste, the Commission will pay special attention to 
insulation materials, which generate a growing waste stream.’

The ROCKWOOL Group is recovering, reusing and recycling our stone wool insulation in a 
number of EU Member States and would be pleased to share further information with the 
Commission about these recycling systems. A full uptake of these initiatives is, however, 
limited by the lack of coherence and consistency between classifications and related 
legislation on waste. We encourage the Commission to consider the knock-on effects of 
the CLP on waste legislation, and to take a harmonised approach to ensure that all 
stakeholders can benefit from practical solutions when handling stone wool waste.         
 
Alignment of the CLP with the global framework 
In 2001, the WHO’s International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) concluded that 
MMVF are ‘not classifiable’ as carcinogenic to humans.3 The IARC conclusions were 
based on human evidence from epidemiological studies; evidence which is described in 
CLP Annex I, point 3.6.2.2.3 as ‘Sufficient human evidence’ providing the background for 
classification or non-classification of a substance. Such links between IARC and CLP 
classifications4 should be retained and improved in the revised CLP. 
The cornerstone for classification and labelling of chemicals at global level is the UN’s 
Globally Harmonised System, GHS. The Commission is strongly recommended to take the 
GHS as the starting point when proposing to introduce new categories for hazard classes; 
and not divert from this system. Discrepancies between the CLP and the GHS risk creating 
obstacles to global classification and trade, and would jeopardise a clear and consistent 
application of rules which regulators and stakeholders depend upon.

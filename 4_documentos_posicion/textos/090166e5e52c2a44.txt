FRENCH BANKING FEDERATION RESPONSE  
TO THE EUROPEAN COMMISSION CONSULTATION ON AMLAR 
29th November 2021 
 
The French Banking Federation (FBF) is the professional organization that represents all banks 
operating in France. This means that it is the voice of  337 banks, including 117 branches or 
subsidiaries of banks with non-French parent undertaking.  
 
The FBF welcomes the opportunity to comment on the proposal for a Regulation establishing the 
Authority for Anti -Money Laundering and Countering the Financing of Terrorism and amending 
Regulations (EU) No 1093/2010, (EU) 1094/2010, (EU) 1095/2010  (AMLAR). This proposal , 
together with the proposal for the 6 th AML directive, are a historic opportunity to enhance the 
EU supervision of AML/CFT  that will probably be scrutinized by many observers and AML/CFT 
experts. An efficient EU AML/CFT supervision is a collective challenge for the integrity of the EU 
economy and financial system and the security of its citizens. We fully support the European 
Commission ambition and acknowledge the complexity of the exercise. The FBF is grateful to be 
able to comment on the proposal for AMLAR and to  be able to  encourage the European 
Commission to go further to achieve a united AML/CFT supervisory framework rather than a 
collection of national AML/CFT supervisors.  
 
Cases of alleged money laundering involving EU credit institutions have shown shortcomings in 
the EU framework with responsibilities on both sides, public and private sectors. The report of the 
European Banking Authority (EBA) on approaches of competent authorities to AML/CFT 
supervision confirmed that the methods to identify risks and to apply the risk -based approach to 
supervision diverge1. As AML Authority will have the capacity to directly supervise a small number 
of entities, AML/CFT supervisory practices convergence is not enough . We should target 
similar and unique supervisory practices to achieve the very best supervision by national 
AML/CFT supervisor and dedicate the action of the AML Authority to the riskiest obliged entities. 
 
In order to achieve such objective, it is unavoidable to reach the maximum level of harmonisation 
in all the components of the AML/CFT regulatory and institutional framework.  
 
We fully support the harmonisation of the AML/CFT legislation through European reg ulation. 
However, we noted that, within 2 years, after the entry into force of the AML Regulation , th is 
regulation will be completed by several technical regulatory standards  (RTS) on fundamental 
issues such as , for example : (i)  the minimum set of information to inter into a new business 
relationship with customers , or (ii) assess ongoing ones , or (iii) the rules on simplified and 
enhanced due diligence measures. Based on the assumption that the AML Authority will start its 
operational activities other than direct supervision in 2024 and that we expect that the future RTSs 
will be submitted to public consultation, we believe it will be very difficult that all the necessary 
RTS will be adopted at the time the AML Regulation will be applied (probably 2026). This will 
result in difficulties for banks to understand how to apply the provisions already applicable and will 
potentially lead to a lack of consistency in interpretations of the legislation until full harmonisation. 
For these reasons, we urge the European Commission, either: (i) to provide more details in the 
AML Regulation on particularly relevant Articles,  or (ii) provide that the AML Regulation 
will apply on the date on which t he last RTS envisaged by the AML Regulation will be 
adopted.

Full harmonisation of legislation should also apply to guidelines or other instruments of “soft law” 
edited by national AML/CFT authorities . In  other words, all “soft law” edited by the AML 
Authority should prevail on and lead to the invalidation of existing “soft law” edited by 
national AML/CFT, or by EBA on the same topic. Otherwise, the regulatory and supervisory 
framework will remain fragmented, and the ambition of the European Commission, which 
we understand is intended to provide a single rule book and a single supervisory practice, 
will not be reached2.  
 
Another prerequisite to full harmonisation  is to clarify the interplay between AML Authority, 
and indirectly the AML/CFT national authorities, and the European Central Bank. Indeed, 
AML/CFT considerations have been introduced in the SREP 3. Although w e understand the 
objective, consistency, and efficiency command however that all evaluations related to AML/CFT 
risks, processes or policies be made by the technical competent authority. That is why we believe 
that the AMLAR should clarify that the competent AML/CFT supervisor (whether the AML 
Authority for directly supervised entities or the AML/CFT national supervisors ), provides 
all the analysis or evaluation needed by the European Central Bank to conduct its SREP . 
Otherwise, banks would face contradictory requirements from different superviso rs and 
supervision will remain fragmented.  
 
A harmonised framework in terms  of legislation and supervisory practices impl ies that the AML 
Authority positions, recommendations, orientations, analysis should be imposed to any other 
supervisor or authority: EBA, ECB, national supervisor or authority.  
 
Riskiest obliged entities are not necessarily the large cross-border entities as defined in 
the proposal for AMLAR.  
 
We believe that the criteria that are currently proposed to establish a direct AMLA 
supervision do not target entities that could potentially present a high level of ML/ CFT 
risks, such as: (i) small banks with a low level of cross-border activities or carried out using the 
freedom to provide services, (ii) banks with a large cross -border activity without establishments 
outside their country, (iii) entities whose parent entity is established in a third country and having 
a significant presence in the European Union (through establishments / branches) or a large cross-
border activity; iv) entities that are within the financial sector but are not banks , or (v) entities 
outside the financial sector. 
 
Indeed, all cross-border activities do not present the same level of ML/FT risk and a cross-border 
activity is not always the indicator of a ML/FT scheme. The ML/FT risk varies according to (i) the 
type of customers, (ii) the country of destination or origin of the transaction, (iii) the services offered 
and (iv) the type of transactions. Furthermore, a distinction should be made between cross-border 
activities within the EU and those with a third countries  as well as between intragroup activities 
and activities with third parties . Distinction should also be made whether cross -border activities 
are carried out by entities established in one or more Member States or by those using the freedom 
to provide services. It is important to note that if the cases of alleged money laundering involving 
credit institutions revealed in 2018 were all cross -border nature, only one case involved a large 
banking group.  
 
We believe that the criteria related to the establishment in several Member States for the 
assessment of obliged entities and the sole consideration of ML/FT inherent risks as provided by 
articles 12 and 13 of AMLAR will lead, on the one hand, to submit mainly large banks to the AML 
Authority supervision and, on the other hand, to exclude de facto some obliged entities from the 
assessment of the new AML Authority while presenting a high level of ML/FT risks.

Moreover, by setting a criterion only by reference t o inherent ML/FT risk, the AML Authority will 
not be able to consider the residual ML/FT risk by taking into account the quality of internal risks 
mitigation framework as part of the assessment process and hence, would select entities that have 
good risks mitigation policies and processes, and which residual ML/FT risk is correctly managed. 
We do not think that this methodology will prevent the EU from new cases of alleged ML/FT.  
 
We strongly believe that another way compatible with the constraints of the AML Authority, 
notably budgetary, with the FAFT guidance on risk-based supervision4 , and with option 3 
of the European Commission impact assessment5 is possible. This option would imply to 
rely, under certain conditions, on the national AML/CFT authorities and to implement the 
“integrated AML/CFT supervisory system”  that the European Commission is willing to 
build.  
 
We understand that the AML Authority will have to develop drafts of regulatory technical standards 
and submit them for adoption to the European Commission with the aim to set out the benchmarks 
and methodology for national supervisors assessing and classifying the inherent and residual risk 
profile of obliged entities, as well as the frequency at which such profile shall be reviewed6. These 
benchmarks and methodology should also be applied to select obliged entities to AML Authority 
supervision to avoid discrepancies between the latter evaluation and those of the national 
supervisors. In these conditions, AML Authority should select the obliged entities subject to 
its supervision by relying on and overseeing national assessments of obliged entities. It 
being provided that AML Authority may not, at its discretion, rely on such national 
assessment should its  standards being not taken into account in the assessments . 
Although the lack of harmonisation of national assessments may lead to some bias in the early 
stages (i.e., until AML Authority has published the regulatory technical standards setting out the 
methodology), this solution to rely on national supervisor assessment of residual ML/ FT risks is, 
in our opinion, the only one valid in a long-term perspective. 
 
AML Authority should also b e more demanding regarding the assessment of the state of 
supervisory convergence and not be satisfied by a compliance on the basis of a “best efforts” 
principle. AML Authority should, if need be, in conjunction with the European Commission, 
be granted with efficient and quick constraints powers toward s AML/CFT national 
supervisors of the financial sector that do not apply its standards , guidelines or 
recommendations. Otherwise, supervisory convergence may never be reached . Hence, the 
AMLA should be given the authority to require the harmonisation of national supervisory 
practices and the consistency of such practices with its own.  
 
As a consequence , the perimeter of obliged entities should be  enlarged, notably to new 
industries with significant ML/ FT risks, without additional constraints for the AML 
Authority, which will concentrate its efforts on the riskiest entities whatever their statu s and not 
only on large banks . As explained above, to identify these entities, we believe that it is also 
unavoidable to supplement the inherent  ML/FT risks criteria, to add residual risks ML/ FT 
criteria and to take into account  the role of the AML/CFT supervisory colleges which is a 
form of EU supervision on which we should capitalise7.

Last but not least, to be consistent with the tasks of the AML Authority with respect to selected 
obliged entities which will concern individual and group -wide compliance with the requirements 
laid down in EU AML/CFT legislation, we propose to clarify that where selected obliged 
entities are identifie d regardless whether they are parent companies  of groups or a 
subsidiaries thereof, the AML Authority supervision will apply at the highest level of 
prudential consolidation and to all the undertakings of the same group in order to avoid 
duplication of supervision (by the national autho rities on one side and by AML Authority 
on the other side)8. 
 
Regarding the procedural rules for taking supervisory measures and imposing administrative 
pecuniary sanctions, the proposal for AMLAR does not provide for a clear distinction between the 
prosecutorial, investigative and sanctioning functions of the AML Au thority which are the 
guarantees of independence and impartiality of administrative and pecuniary sanctions  
required by Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights and several European Court 
on Human Rights judgments 9. We believe that the creation of a sanctions committee is 
essential. Furthermore, we think it would be more appropriate that only maximum amounts 
of sanctions be provided for in the AMLAR and that the minimum levels of sanctions 
currently envisaged be removed. 
 
Indirect supervision of non-financial sector will cover a large range of sectors which everyone 
has, however, an important role to play in the AML/CFT. We understand the constraints of the 
AML Authority but, they should be balanced by coercive powers towards non-financial supervisors, 
if need be, in conjunction with European Commission, to ensure an efficient and quick compliance 
with standards, recommendations and guidelines of the AML Authority.10 
 
In terms of controls and supervisory practices , we believe that in order to achieve  a 
harmonised and effective AML/CFT framework, emphasis should be laid on less mature 
sectors, including entities belonging to the financial sector that are not banks or entities outside 
the financial sector. 
 
Finally, the burden of the AML Authority budget, currently supported 75% by the financial sector 
and 25% by the public sector, should be more balanced , particularly regarding the non -financial 
sector which does not contribute while indirectly supervised by the AML Authority.

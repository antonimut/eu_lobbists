KEY MESSAGES 
 
The EU’s role on STR:  
❖ Given that housing policies are at the core of Member States retained prerogatives, we 
do not deem neither necessary nor desirable to have an EU legislative initiative which 
would harmonize in a precise way the STR-sector.  
❖ Instead, a clarity effort  at EU level  should be made in relation to the  limits of 
competence between EU and Member States, i.e. the regulatory powers that national 
public authorities retain while remaining compliant to the Services and the E -
Commerce Directives. 
 
The importance of national registration schemes: 
❖ While we are supportive of pushing forward national mandatory registration systems, 
we nonetheless think that the possibility of a ‘common registration scheme’ should be 
excluded, as Member States should retain the freedom to choose the most appropriate 
way to regulate such schemes and an eventual EU framework should mainly consist of 
guidelines, templates suggestions and collection of best practices. 
 
On-line platforms’ clear prerogatives:  
❖ We believe that on -line platforms should only display registered STRs and should be 
required to take reasonable steps to verify the validity of the registration number 
provided by the STR host. 
❖ We also deem necessary for these platform s to d elist properties after receiving 
effective knowledge of illegality from appropriate enforcement authorities. 
 
The necessary distinction between peers and professional service providers:  
❖ Even though we support the Commission’s intention to clarify the applicable legal 
framework for both peers and professional service providers and to ensure 
proportionally differentiated regulatory approaches,  we deem that Member States 
should be granted a certain level of flexibility in deciding the thresholds and the specific 
rules applicable to the two categories.  
 
A restrictive approach on data sharing: 
❖ The SRT initiative should have a complementary approach on the data issue. The EU 
legislator, before imposing additional data sharing obligations and disproportionate 
and unnecessary requirements on platforms, building owners and hosts, should first 
identify whether there are any gaps in the existing legal framework. 
 
 
 
UIPI position on the short-term rental initiative  
  
 
 
2 
 
 
Short-term accommodation rental (‘STR’) services are an important part of the EU tourism 
ecosystem.  
As a fast-growing sector, STR brings substantial benefits to the society. It also boosts and create 
new forms of tourism, which – thanks to this – has become more accessible and affordable. It 
contributes to the economic and social development of many cities and communities all over 
Europe. It creates new business prospect as well as new sources of income for EU citizens.  
Yet, these new opportunities are no t without effect on the local housing market, direct 
neighbourhood, local communities and co -owners. They can have some impacts on housing 
availability and affordability , by  leading in turn to stricter limitations on the traditional rental 
markets. Moreover, doubts have been expressed on the sustainability of an unregulated 
expansion. STR market players, for their part, have repeatedly raised the existence of obstacles 
deriving from fragmented, burdensome and restrictive rules. 
As representatives fro m European homeowners, co -owners and private landlords, we consider 
important the Commission’s initiative to tackle these problems and to propose new solutions in 
order to make the STR ecosystem more harmonised, fair and transparent , and we wish to 
contribute to the discussion.  
 
 
DETAILED OBSERVATIONS  
 
 
The EU’s role on STR: 
Quite often STR rules  prove to be unclear, disproportionate, fragmented and non-transparent. 
Many are under frequent review, not published in a central website  and introduce different 
obligations for different categories of service providers . Sometimes it is difficult to understand 
their g eographical applicability , while sudden changes in local rules impose disproportionate 
restrictions and an effective enforcement is not always guaranteed. In this context , the 
intervention of the EU legislator could focus on  increasing legal certainty and help reduce 
regulatory fragmentation. While national authorities, together with regional and local authorities, 
keep their prerogative  to legislate according to their competences and local situations, the EU 
should help to set a consistent framework that provides clarity and transparency. 
Therefore, a clarity effort should be made in relation to the regulatory powers that national public 
authorities retain while remaining compliant to the Services and the E -Commerce Directives 1. 
Legal clar ity is essential to support the development of the  STR se ctor and resolv e the legal 
disputes that increasingly appear in various countries between authorities and many STR 
platforms and actors. In this sense, the ECJ Cali Apartments ruling2 has already provided a strong

However, the EU effort to clarify the rules around the STR sector should not completely override 
the fact that  this variation in regulatory approaches to STRs, which is  perceived as mostly 
problematic in the Inception Im pact Assessment, reflects in fact an underlying reality of great 
variation in housing markets and in the touristic use of public and private space across the EU. The 
same can be said for the different societal and social impact of STR, which often var ies not only 
from country to country, but also from region to region and even from city to city. Consequently, 
we would like to express some doubts about the fact that an EU legislative initiative which would 
harmonize in a  precise way the STR -sector will be at the same time respectful of the needed 
balancing of the different public interests that are at stake  in all the different levels mentioned.  
Similar concerns relate to the existence of an EU competence to harmonize in detail such aspects, 
that constitute the core of Member States’ prerogatives on housing policies. 
 
The importance of national registration schemes 
We acknowledge the need for  public authorities to put in place proportionate 
registration/authorisation schemes on STR providers . Any required authorisation or registra tion 
should be available online for all accommodation service providers, while public authorities should 
be required to provide a decision within a reasonably short time frame. We warn however against 
complex rules and registration requirements as well as possible lengthy authorisation feedback 
and additional red tape that might disqualify individuals (either homeowners willing to rent their 
home or individual non -professional landlords willing to put o ne or two dwellings on the STR 
market).  
In this sense, we believe that an EU framework for registration/authorisation schemes would be 
beneficial in providing legal clarity on the setting up of such schemes both for authorities and 
hosts. Ensuring simple, clear and digital processes for hosts to register/obtain authorisation should

Therefore, we are  supportive of pushing forward n ational mandatory registration systems, 
whereby STR providers would be obliged to register their activity and ensure compliance with

guarantee that all STR actors can operate in full transparency and security. However, we think that

retain the freedom to choose the most appropriate way to regulate such schemes. 
 
On-line platforms’ clear prerogatives 
Clarifying the role on -line platforms can play in supporting enforcement of STR rules and 
registration schemes will be key for the future development of the ST R sector. To this end, we 
believe that on -line platforms should only display registered STRs . In addition, platforms should 
be required to take reasonable steps to verify the validity of the registration number provided by 
the STR host . Automated technical solutions allowing for real -time verification of a registration 
number could represent a good compromise which does not require much effort  on their part.  
Automated solutions would benefit both public authorities’ enforcement of registration schemes 
and platform reliability and reputation on the market. 
In line with what already happens in many countries , we also support the delisting of properties 
from the platforms after receiving effective knowledge of illegality from appropriate enforcement 
authorities. This aspect is very important to ensure that the STR market is composed by actors 
who have all the necessary requirements to operate in this sector. In particular, tight controls are 
necessary to tackle unlawful conducts such as the ones of tenants subletting their accommodation 
and advertising it on such platforms without the owner’s express permission. 
 
The necessary distinction between peers and professional service providers 
We recognise the need to clarify the different types of providers offering STRs  and the nature of 
their economic activity , together with an understanding of the differentiation of rules that can 
apply to different categories of providers. In particular, we believe that regulatory approaches to 
STR should differentiate between peer (amateur) providers and professional operators.  This 
aspect was also addressed in the European Commission’s Communication on the collaborative 
economy (2016)5 and in three Studies concerning the monitoring of the collaborative economy in 
the EU that the Commission published in 2018 6. These studies have shown that creating a clear 
distinction between occasional peer providers and professional services providers often avoids the 
imposition of disproportionate requirements for those who wish to rent only very occasionally. 
Reasonable t hresholds can be a m eans to facilitate such distinction (e.g. number of nights a 
property is rented out per year or  whether or not the STR activity is the main occupation of the 
host), and Member States that are considered to perform best in this market are the ones that

A restrictive approach on data sharing 
UIPI acknowledges the importance of data sharing in the STR sector. Having greater legal clarity, 
especially around which data are relevant for public authorities, and proportional for STR 
platforms to collect, is a key aspect. In addition, property owners and host should not be required 
to disclose their data beyond what is necessary. In this context, a distinction should be made  
between aggregated data for public authorities relative to occupancy rates and gu est numbers, 
and data requested by tax authorities for taxation purposes.  
For the first category, we recognise that aggregated data might be relevant  to analyse the STR 
positive as well as sometimes negative impact on local areas and for the designing of public policies 
to regulate this market in a proportiona te and effective  way. In this regard , the 2019 Eurostat 
agreement with STR platforms8 was a good starting point for providing aggregate information on 
occupancy rates and number of room nights, and it could represent a good basis to look at when 
thinking about systematising the data collection  procedure. In particular , by focusing on 
aggregated data, this agreement ensures that the privacy of citizens, including guests and hosts, 
is protected in line with applicable EU legislation. Thus, data will not allow individual citizens or 
property owners to be identified.  In view of further agreements or regulations,  while we agree 
that a more systematic approach might be beneficial, the importance of the Eurostat agreement 
cannot be overlooked, as public authorities already have access to a large pool of data on short -
term rentals and we do not see why and which further specific data would be needed. Any future 
solution to ameliorate the collection of data and their sharing between platforms and public 
authorities will need to consider the specific type of data already held by STR platforms, and what 
is proportional for the purpose for which the data are intended. 
For the second category of data, we believe that the recently adopted DAC79 already sets  a 
sufficient legal framework to regulate the exchange of information between online platforms and 
tax authorities . Hence, to a void double regulation , confusion and further burdens on both 
platforms and property owners , any obligations relating to data for tax authorities should refer

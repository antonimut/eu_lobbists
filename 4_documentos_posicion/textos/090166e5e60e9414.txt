Introduction 
Short-term rental (STR) platforms, such as AirBnB, have changed the usage and character of 
many urban centres and towns in Europe, especially in tourist hotspots. This can create both 
positive and negative outcomes. For example, the OECD has noted that the presence of these 
online platforms “has contributed to a disruption of the local real-estate market. Impacts include 
inflated real-estate prices, unfair competition for licenced accommodation providers, and 
gentrification of tourism hotspots and inner-city areas, sometimes to the point of pushing locals 
out of the area”1.  
It is also the case that the use of a home as a STR on a full-time basis (in other words, on a 
‘commercial’ basis) is illegal in most cities. For example, Paris estimates that 60% of AirBnB 
listings in the City are illegal, while in Berlin 80% of listings do not include a legally mandated 
registration number2. However, platforms actively thwart laws designed to prevent illegal listings 
on their sites, claiming that it is the person making a listing who should be required to comply 
with the law, not them. 
The counter argument is typically that STR allows to maximise the use of buildings, for example 
via the letting of a spare room, or by allowing a household the flexibility of temporarily vacating 
their home without being impeded by rent or mortgage payments. STRs can also provide 
additional income for households who might otherwise struggle financially. STRs may also 
support the tourism sector, which can help to create additional employment. 
However, there is now significant evidence that in many European cities STRs are primarily full 
housing units rented out on a full-time basis by increasingly professionalised landlords3; 
supporting the aforementioned conclusions of the OECD.  
Of course, it must be noted that the COVID pandemic has reduced the stock of STRs in many 
cities, with some of these properties returning to the long-term rental stock. However, it remains 
to be seen if this is only a temporary situation, and if in a post-pandemic world if providers of 
STRs will simply pick up where they left off. 
Impact on affordable housing providers 
When it comes to the impact on public, cooperative and social housing providers the main two 
issues that are cited are: 
 
1. STR platforms, like AirBnB, are making the urban housing shortage worse and pushing up 
prices. This in turn creates additional demand on providers of affordable housing 
2. STR platforms enable the illegal subletting of public, cooperative or social housing

STR impact on prices and availability

platforms, such as AirBnB, put upward pressure on house and rental prices, which could in turn 
drive housing affordability issues or housing exclusion. Some recent research on this topic is 
outlined below.

STR platforms and the illegal subletting of affordable housing in the EU 
In relation to the claim of illegal subletting, this is known to be the case in several member states. 
However, there are currently few tools available to tackle this problem, as platforms cannot or will 
not be compelled to take measures to prevent it. 
Information below shows the current situation when it comes to subletting of social housing in a host 
of countries and regions in the EU. It shows that subletting of social housing is prohibited in almost all 
cases, and that in situations in which it can be allowed under some circumstances, the flexibility 
withing the system is often abused.

permitted Notes 
Austria X 
• The subletting of social housing in Austria is strictly prohibited 
• Despite this, illegal subletting of social housing does still happen 
• Social providers have had to take rental agreement infringement proceedings 
against tenants, including some cases of the termination of the rental contract 
Belgium   
(Brussels) X 
• There have been recorded cases of social tenants illegally subletting their homes on 
AirBnB in recent years 
• When social housing agencies discover that one of their tenants is subletting their

Belgium 
(Flanders) X 
• Extra people may be added to a household after the signing of a rental agreement 
(e.g., additional children or family members), however this must be agreed with the 
social provider and the rent will be adapted accordingly — however these are long-
term additions, not short-term ‘visitors’ or ‘guests’ 
• There are so far few reported cases of illegal subletting in the Flemish social housing

• In the event that illegal subletting is detected, there are sanctions for the social

Denmark — 
• Social tenants have a right to sub-let for a period of up to two years, provided this is 
due to illness, temporary relocation for work, studying abroad or other similar 
temporary absences 
• The tenant is obligated to inform the landlord/housing provider about the sub-
letting 
• Subletting that falls outside the rules is a problem in Denmark 
• There are frequent cases of tenants “forgetting” to inform housing providers that 
they are vacating their home, which in some instances is sublet to several different 
households for short period of time — this gives parts of the social stock the 
appearance of being more like a hotel than a home  
Finland — 
• Tenants have the right to sub-lease up to half of their apartment to another person 
“for residential purposes”, but only if it doesn’t cause the landlord significant 
inconvenience or disturbance — thus sublets are allowed only in cases where the 
designated tenant remains living in the property full-time 
• Because tenants can only sublet half of their apartment, it means studio apartments 
cannot be sublet 
• The total rent paid (designated tenant + subletter) cannot be more than the cost-
based rent agreed with the social provider — thus, the designated tenant cannot 
make a profit from sub-letting, they can only use it to reduce their own costs 
• Social tenants may also assign their apartment to another person for up to two years 
if there is a reason for the tenant to reside in another location, e.g., for reasons of 
employment, study, or illness 
• Especially in the Helsinki metropolitan area, there are regularly cases where tenants 
have been sub-letting their apartments illegally, using for example AirBnB.

France X 
• Subletting is totally forbidden for social tenants in France 
• Illegal subletting is grounds to terminate a rental contract 
• The rise of STRs in many urban areas is reducing the stock of ordinary rental housing, 
which in turn puts more pressure on social housing providers to meet the needs of 
those who find themselves excluded from the housing market 
Germany X 
• Subletting can be facilitated under exceptional circumstances, provided the owner of 
the home agrees. However, this is uncommon and the landlord can refuse to give 
their consent 
• If subletting is permitted, this must be on a long-term basis, in order to avoid a 
constant turnover of tenants. Thus, letting on STR platforms is not possible in almost 
all cases 
• Many German regions (landër) now have strong legal controls on STR. Thus, in 
instances in which tenants are subletting their home on STR platforms, this is both a 
violation of their lease agreement and of regional law — despite this, illegal 
subletting in Germany is still a noted problem 
Ireland X • The subletting of social housing in Ireland is strictly prohibited 
Luxembourg X 
• Subletting is not permitted within the social housing stock 
• Illegal subletting of social housing is a problem in Luxembourg 
• Measures have been taken to identify social tenants who engage in illegal subletting 
• The size of the social housing provided is always aligned with the size of the 
household. Therefore, illegal subletting can mean that houses become overcrowded, 
with cases of children sleeping in the living room or sharing a room with their 
parents 
Netherlands X 
• The subletting of social housing in the Netherlands is strictly prohibited; though in 
very rare circumstances, a social landlord can give their agreement for this, though 
not via STR platforms 
• Illegal subletting has become increasingly common in recent years, especially in 
major tourists cities, where the potential returns from subletting are large 
• The cost to social providers of detecting illegal subletting can be significant, and is a 
drain on resources that should be used for social purposes 
• Social providers have been pushing for a long time for local authorities to be enabled 
to adequately monitor the illegal subletting of social houses — for this, local 
authorities must have access to the relevant data in order to properly regulate the 
STR market. However, this is not yet the case. 
Portugal                  
(Lisbon Region) X 
• Subletting in the social housing stock in Lisbon is prohibited 
• The proliferation of STR has put pressure on the housing market in Lisbon, 
contributing to a reduction in the stock of permanent housing, while at the same 
time contributing to an increase in the price of private rentals — this in turn can put 
pressure on social housing providers 
• In 2020 a financial package of €17.4 million was put in place to manage the 
conversion of properties allocated to short term rental units to build a pool of 
dwellings in the scope of affordable rent - Secure Rent Programme. 
Slovenia X 
• Affordable rental housing provided by the Housing Funds of the Republic of Slovenia 
are not allowed to be sublet 
• At present, the illegal subletting of affordable public housing is not seen as a 
problem in Slovenia 
Spain X 
• Subletting in the public rental stock is not allowed 
• The proliferation of STRs in tourist centres is putting pressure on local property 
markets 
• It also impacts on social housing because many social housing providers in Spain 
have agreements with private owners in order to make their dwellings available for 
disadvantaged groups — STRs can reduce this supply 
• Overall, the illegal subletting of public rental housing is not yet considered a major 
issue in Spain 
Sweden X 
• Only those named on the rental agreement with a provider of public housing have a 
right to live in a given home 
• Leases do not allow for subletting, short-term or otherwise

Key asks from the providers of affordable housing 
Based on conversations with providers of affordable housing from across the EU, Housing Europe is 
putting forwards the following asks with regard to the regulation of short-term letting platforms. 
1. Activities that are illegal in the real world should also be illegal on short-term letting platforms: 
If the subletting of a category of housing, for example social housing, is considered to be an 
illegal activity, then STR sites should not be able to facilitate such lettings either directly or 
indirectly, e.g., as a result of taking a passive stance on the verification and monitoring of the 
compliance of rentals on its platform with local legislation and regulations.  
A simple solution to this problem would be for STR platforms to be legally required to ask all of 
those offering rentals through their platform to make a legally binding declaration that the 
home is not social housing, or is not otherwise forbidden to be rented as STR. GDPR regulations 
mean that simply providing STR platforms with a database of social housing properties is not 
possible. 
2. Ensure that the regulation of short term rentals preserve the ability of public authorities to 
regulate such activities, with a view to maintaining the affordability of living for permanent 
residents: In many instances, cities have introduced regulations to better control STR platforms, 
with a view to finding a balance between the aforementioned positives and negatives of such 
rental activities. However, they have local authorities have also all too often been thwarted by 
the non-cooperation or delaying tactics of the platform operators10. This is clearly not 
acceptable, and runs contrary to the fulfilment of democratically supported political mandates. 
Thus, public authorities need to be supported, rather than hindered by EU rules when it comes 
to regulating and monitoring the activities of STR platforms. This should include: 
o Facilitate a mandatory registration system: It should be possible for public authorities to 
demand that anyone listing on a STR platform can prove that they hold the correct licence or 
documentation to do so. Early adopters of such a system — like Amsterdam, Berlin and 
Barcelona — have struggled to make platforms like AirBnB comply with the new rules   
o Data disclosure: Public authorities must have the means to compel STR platforms to share 
data with them on their rental activities, e.g., the location of properties, the number of 
nights they are rented for, and whether or not they have provided a valid registration licence 
or declaration that their home is not social housing

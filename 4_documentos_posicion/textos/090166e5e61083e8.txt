TACKLING AIR POLLUTION 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
AIR QUALITY IS ESSENTIAL TO EUROPEAN CITIZENS’ QUALITY OF 
LIFE 
Air pollution is causing a severe impact on the quality of life of European citizens. In 2019, across the 
European Union, 307,000 premature deaths were attributed to chronic exposure to fine particulate matter 
and 40,400 premature deaths were attributed to chronic nitrogen dioxide exposure1. Furthermore, over 90% 
of the world’s population live in areas where air pollution exceeds safe levels.2 
In the European Union, 97% of the urban population is exposed to levels of fine particulate matter above 
the latest guideline levels set by the World  Health Organization. To this day, various Member States have 
failed to comply with European regulations on air quality, including the Ambient Air Quality Directives.  
MASSIVELY REDUCING POLLUTION FROM TRANSPORT WILL BE 
KEY 
Transport represents almost a quarter of Europe's greenhouse gas emissions and remains the main cause 
of air pollution in cities. According to the European Commission, emissions from the transport sector remain 
higher than in 1990. Within the sector, road transport is by far the biggest emitter accounting for more than 
70% of all GHG emissions from transport in 2014  3. In 2019, road transport was the principal source of 
nitrogen oxides, responsible for 39% of emissions. 3 
People in bigger cities tend to be exposed to higher concentrations of nitrogen dioxide because of emissions 
from traffic. The trend caused by the COVID-19 health crisis of flight from urban centres will aggravate this 
tendency. The weakness of the alternatives to the private car generates large flows of vehicles on the  
periphery-centre links and saturates the road networks, causing congestion and pollution.

THE ROLE OF PUBLIC TRANSPORT IN TACKLING AIR POLLUTION 
 
European policies must support investment in public transport 
This is particularly relevant for the update of air quality plans provided by the Ambient Air Quality Directives. 
Given the significant impact of air pollution, the European Union should act accordingly and better include 
the role of public transport in its policies to fight air pollution. A significant part of the health solution would 
involve equipping cities and their inhabitants with integrated public transport, reducing risks from traffic 
injuries, obesity, air pollution and noise. Those would benefit all and reduce the social inequalities in fr ont 
of these hazards. 
Moreover, according to the European Environment Agency, public transport is four times more efficient per 
passenger-km than private cars. Every kilometre travelled on public transport saves 95 grams of GHG 
emissions and 19 grams of NOx compared to motorised private transport.  
REORIENTING THE EUROPEAN UNION'S POLICY TOWARDS THE 
DAILY MOBILITY OF EUROPEANS 
Achieving the objective of a 55% reduction in emissions by 2030 implies reducing either the distances 
travelled or unit emissions or increasing the modal shift so that more people can be transported by vehicle. 
This will not be possible with the sole switch to elect ric vehicles, whose deployment will be too slow: a car 
bought today will still be around in 2035. If we manage to reduce unit emissions by 20% by 2030, which 
seems very optimistic given the market share of the electric vehicle, modal shift and distance reduction will 
have to be responsible for around 55% of the emissions reduction: this step is very important if we want to 
be ready in time for 2030. Urban planning policy to reduce distances for local travel coupled with a modal 
shift from the car to public transport for home-to-work journeys will be the key to achieving the EU's GHG 
reduction objectives. 
This modal shift must focus on the links between cities and their peripheries and between cities, acting on 
three pillars: 
• Transport offer (high frequency express bus services, rail services in urban nodes and peri urban 
areas) 
• Multimodal infrastructures: upgrading of the railway, dedicated lanes on road arteries for public 
transport and car sharing, with park -and-ride facilities, multimodal hubs. Cycling pol icy must be 
designed with these public transport solutions in mind, since although the bicycle is an ideal tool for 
daily mobility, it cannot handle excessively long distances. 
• Multimodal digital solutions providing fluid access to all mobility services via a single medium.

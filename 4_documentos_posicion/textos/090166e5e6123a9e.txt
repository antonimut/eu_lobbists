EUROFER position paper on the work to assess the impacts of 
a possible revision of the Ambient Air Quality Directives 
 
EUROFER welcomes the possibility to contribute to the ongoing impact assessment carried out 
in view of a possible revision of the Ambient Air Quality Directives (AAQD). The steel industry 
attaches great importance to the issues relating to emissions to air. It has made major 
investments to reduce emissions per tonne of steel and will continue to do so in the future. 
With this position paper, EUROFER would like to stress several points, which should be duly 
considered during the impact assessment. 
• The AAQDs are broadly fit for purpose and, if they have not reached all their objectives, 
it is mostly due to lack of implementation at Member State level 
• Any update of AAQ standards should better target population exposure and account for 
natural background concentrations 
• All sectors should contribute to an improved air quality in a balanced way, to the extent 
of their technical-economic capabilities 
• The steel industry in Europe is already committed to high air quality standards through 
the implementation of ambitious source legislation and sectors’ transition roadmaps will 
likely bring those standards to new levels of excellence 
• Improved information towards the general public, in particular vulnerable people, 
would greatly mitigate health risks incurred by air pollution 
 
Introduction 
Despite drastic reduction efforts for emissions of most air pollutants, air pollution remains a 
significant issue, responsible for about 400,000 premature deaths in the EU every year, 
according to the Commission’s Second Clean Air Outlook. 
While any figure above zero will always be too high, it should not cover up the massive efforts 
carried out by the steel industry to reduce its emissions of air pollutants which contributed to 
the increasingly reduced impacts on health in the last 20 years. Ever greater emission 
reductions are likely to occur if the right conditions are set to implement the pathways 
described in EUROFER’s Low Carbon Roadmap. 
EU industry creates jobs and wealth which, in turns, provides EU Member States with the ability 
to maintain a high level of services, infrastructures, etc. To safeguard EU industry’s 
competitiveness and avoid its relocation to less-regulated third countries, any future policy 
framework for air quality shall carefully balance out social, economic and environmental 
considerations.

1. AAQDs are broadly fit for purpose 
EUROFER believes that the AAQDs are broadly fit for purpose and that addressing poor 
implementation by Member States and improving information towards the public are 
paramount to further protect citizens from air pollution. 
EUROFER also supports a gradual increase in action and ambition to tackle the harmful effects 
of air pollution in view of the alignment, as far as technically and economically feasible, of EU 
standards with the standards set out in the World Health Organisation’s (WHO) recently 
published Air Quality Guidelines. When revised EU air quality standards should be achieved 
depends on the policy framework to facilitate the implementation of transition plans, 
roadmaps, etc. that will drive emission reductions. 
Besides air pollutants already addressed by the AAQDs, EUROFER also supports efforts to 
streamline the monitoring of concentrations of ultrafine particles (UFPs) to better understand 
their effects on the health of citizens. 
However, any level of ambition that the Commission will seek to achieve must be reconciled 
with the availability of technologies that will allow, on the one hand, to quantify the pollutants 
at low levels and, on the other hand, to mitigate pollution to levels that are no longer harmful 
to human health and natural ecosystems. 
 
2. Better target population exposure and account for natural background concentrations 
To address air pollution most efficiently, it is important to consider where the risk to suffer 
from its consequences is highest. Therefore, better targeting locations representative of the 
exposure to air pollutants of the general population is necessary. To this end, more detailed 
modelling can help identify locations where exposure to air pollutants is high. To ensure 
quality/comparability of the data, but also to minimise costs/administrative burden, the 
modelling should be carried out by a centralised body – and not by private undertakings 
themselves as is sometimes the case – with the data provided by stakeholders and using a 
common methodology, e.g., set out at EU level.

4. Align AAQD on sectoral legislation and duly consider sectors’ transition agendas 
The steel industry is engaged in a long-term transition process that could lead to CO2 emissions 
cut of 80-95% in 2050 compared to 1990 with positive knock-on effects on air quality. This 
transition must take place gradually and in an orderly manner, notably by refraining to force 
the implementation of regrettable measures going beyond what is considered a ‘Best Available 
Technique’ (BAT). 
BATs consider the technical and economic feasibility of pollution prevention and mitigation 
measures and BAT requirements are set out following an ‘integrated approach’ to pollution, 
which entails that all industrial emissions need to be considered to avoid the side effect that 
the reduction of one parameter can have on others. These requirements are reviewed 
following a frequency which is aligned, as far as possible, on investment cycles. 
Forcing industry to go beyond the requirements set out under BATs and the IED by setting more 
ambitious air quality standards and/or by more frequently updating them risks undermining the 
delicate balances achieved in the frame of the Seville process. In addition, whenever an 
industrial installation complies with its permit requirements, it should not be sanctioned (e.g. 
via the imposition of stricter requirements) when local exceedances occur. 
 
5. Strengthen information towards the general public 
Availability of simple and practical information towards the public is not available in a 
systematic way and is therefore an area for improvement, although it would greatly help raise 
awareness of the risks incurred by air pollution and mitigate them. 
Access to near-real-time air quality information is already possible, among other things via the 
EU Air Quality Index. Better accessibility to real-time modelling and forecasting of air quality 
and implementation of alert systems, in particular for the benefit of the most vulnerable 
citizens, would dramatically increase the level of protection and allow citizens to remain in safe 
places or take appropriate measures during air pollution peaks.

ECF welcomes the Commission’s CountEmissions EU initiative. In order to give a complete picture of sustainable transport and mobility options, it 
is important that active mobility modes like walking and cycling be included in the assessment  and compared to other modes of transport and 
mobility. This will help to achieve the objectives set by the Commission in its recently released European Urban Mobility Framework to put a clear 
focus on active mobility in order to reach the emissions reduction goals of the Union’s 2030 Climate Target Plan , and to improve data collection 
for all modes of transport . It will also help users to choose the most sustainable transport and mobility options by including active mobility in the 
selection. 
 
In order to help with the inclusion of active mobility in the CountEmissions EU initiative, we would like to give some leads on existing resear ch on 
how to calculate greenhouse gas emissions from cycling.

There are several studies estimating CO2 emissions from cycling. Many papers use a study carried out by ECF in 2011, with the following reference 
values for cycling and electric cycling: 
 
• ca. 21 g CO2 equivalents per passenger kilometre travelled (5 g/CO 2 e for production and maintenance (96 kg CO2 e per vehicle), and 
16 g/CO2 e for additional metabolic energy produced by the cyclist’s body) for conventional cycling 
• ca. 22 g CO2 equivalents per passenger kilometre travelled (7 g CO2 e for production and maintenance (ca. 140 kg CO2 e per vehicle), 
10 g CO2 e electricity consumption, and 6 g CO2 e for additional metabolic energy produced by the cyclist’s body) for electric cycling. 
 
The estimations for electric bicycles are based on earlier work by Hendriksen et al, 200 8, which put the figures for production and maintenance 
at 5-9 g CO2 equivalents per passenger kilometre travelled, and for electricity consumption at 10 g CO2 per passenger kilometre. 
 
A more recent study led by researchers from the University of Oxford in the framework of the EU research project PASTA (Physical Activity Through 
Sustainable Transport Approaches) used similar values for production of bicycles: 100 kg CO2 equivalents per vehicle for conventional bicycles 
(4.9 g CO 2 e per passenger km)  and 190 kg CO 2 equivalents per vehicle for electric bicycles ( 9.3 g CO 2 e per passenger km).  For electricity 
consumption of electric bicycles, the researchers calculated individual values for each city in the stu dy based on local electricity production 
characteristics, with values reaching from 0.04 g CO2 e for Zürich to 5.2 g CO 2 e for London. They did not take into account emissions linked to 
metabolic energy produced by cyclists’ bodies. 
 
Several companies active in the bicycle sector have also commissioned studies on the CO2 emissions impact of cycling. According to a study carried 
out by TÜV Rheinland  Energy for Bosch eBike Systems  following international standard ISO14067 , production, maintenance, transport and 
recycling account for emissions of ca. 12 g CO 2 e per passenger km for electric bicycles, and electricity consumption for 2-5 g CO 2 e per km, 
depending on the electricity mix. Trek, an American producer of bicycles, put the figures  for production per vehicle at 116 kg CO 2 e for a high-
volume, standard conventional bicycle, and at 229 kg CO2 e for an electric bicycle. 
 
Bicycles also have a high emissions savings potential when it comes to freight transport, and should be included in comparisons between different 
modes there. According to a recent study by UK-based cyclelogistics service provider Pedal Me, lifecycle emissions for electric cargo bicycles are 
at 30.5 g CO 2 e per km , composed of 4 g CO 2 e per km for manufacturing (280 kg CO 2 e per vehicle) , 4.5 g CO 2 e per km for electricity 
production (based on UK electricity mix), and 22 g CO2 e linked to metabolic energy produced by cargo bike riders’ bodies.

These figures show that CO2 emissions for cycling calculated in different studies are largely in the same order of magnitude of ca. 5 g CO2 e per 
km for conventional cycling, ca. 15 g CO2 e per km for electric cycling, and ca. 10 g CO2 e per km for cyclelogistics (not taking into account 
metabolic energy consumption). The main difference in the aggregated values lies in the inclusion or not of metabolic energy produced by 
cyclists’ bodies, which is debatable. There is certainly room for more research on this topic and for more consolidated figures, but the numbers 
we have already show that it is possible to count emissions from cycling and to include them in comparisons with other modes of transport. 
Therefore, we would like to see cycling included in the Commission’s CountEmissions EU initiative, both for passenger mobility and freight 
transport. We will be happy to work with the Commission on refining the methodology for that.

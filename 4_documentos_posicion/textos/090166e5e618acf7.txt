Recommendations supporting our response to the Open Public Consultation on the Revision of the Ambient Air Quality Directives (2008/50/EC1 and 2004/107/EC2)   Eurometaux welcomes the review of the Ambient Air Quality Directives and the opportunities provided to stakeholders to contribute. This document provides the background rationale and detailed explanation of our answers to the questionnaire of the open public consultation. With this document, Eurometaux aims to provide recommendations from the non-ferrous metal sector that should be duly considered during the impact assessment.   Introduction. The objectives of the EU Air Quality Directives review carried out in the context of the EU Zero Pollution Action Plan must be coherent with the climate objectives of the European Green Deal to enable the transition to a green, digital and sustainable society and economy. The non-ferrous metal (NFM) sector is strongly committed to continuing lowering emissions of air pollutants for the benefit of human health and the environment. However, the sector calls for improvements in air quality to be achieved in the most cost-effective approach possible, without disproportionate costs for industry. To develop efficient and proportionate air quality policies, it is crucial to increase the understanding of the different sources of emissions and their impacts, to be able to design effective actions.  Consider the links between the various policies and their objectives. The NFM sector complies with a wide range of rigorous environmental pieces of legislation. It is important to consider the links between the various policies and their environmental objectives as increased efforts to reach one target (e.g., reduction in the emissions of certain pollutants) can impact the achievement of other targets (e.g., reduction of energy consumption and CO2 emissions). For example, the Renewable Energy Directive calls for an increased use of biomass (considered as reference energy source in the update of the ETS fallback benchmarks), but the biomass combustion process has a negative impact on air quality. Ultimately, air pollution and climate change should be tackled jointly with policies and measures developed through an integrated approach ensuring coherent EU strategies.   Promote synergies and coherence with other Green Deal initiatives. Metals are materials that Europe needs for its Green Deal to succeed. They are naturally occurring, interdependent, and infinitely recyclable. Europe’s energy and digital transitions will require increasing amounts3 of metals in batteries, electric vehicles, grid infrastructure, and renewable energy technologies. Our sector has the ambition to be a steward for managing their impacts and minimising harmful pollution or exposure across the lifecycle. We are a front runner in waste management and recycling, and in the application of Best Available Techniques (BATs) to control pollutants. There is an obvious tension between the need to produce more metals to achieve our climate goals, and the need to reduce emissions of pollutants to achieve our zero pollution goals. This is the reality we must face in a constructive manner. Our proposal for a holistic approach to all Green Deal initiatives, including its Zero Pollution ambition (and with it, the review of the Ambient Air Quality Directives), is to strike the right balance between the “3Cs”: Climate, Circularity and Chemicals management.   1Directive 2008/50/EC on ambient air quality and cleaner air for Europe including the following elements: the merging of most of existing legislation into a single directive (except for the Fourth Daughter Directive) with no change to existing air quality objectives; new air quality objectives for PM2.5 (fine particles) including the limit value and exposure-related objectives; the possibility to discount natural sources of pollution when assessing compliance against limit values; the possibility for time extensions of three years (PM10) or up to five years (NO2, benzene) for complying with limit values. 2Directive 2004/107/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council relating to arsenic, cadmium, mercury, nickel and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in ambient air (Fourth Daughter Directive). 3 IEA (2021), The Role of Critical Minerals in Clean Energy Transitions, https://www.iea.org/reports/the-role-of-critical-minerals-in-clean-energy-transitions; World Bank (2020), Minerals for Climate Action: The Mineral Intensity of the Clean Energy Transition, https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2020/05/11/mineral-production-to-soar-as-demand-for-clean-energy-increases

A new air quality policy should not lead to harder achievable standards. The NFM sector, to date one of the most electrified industries and a leading example of circular economy in action, is committed to continuing lowering emissions of air pollutants and supporting the gradual alignment of EU standards with the latest scientific evidence. However, the policy level of ambition must take into consideration the technical and economic feasibility aspects of aligning EU legislation with the standards set out in the World Health Organization Air Quality Guidelines. The current air quality standards are already difficult to meet for many EU Member States, which are struggling to implement effective measures and already facing a number of infringement procedures. This must be taken into account when proposing more ambitious standards.   Improvements in air quality must be achieved in the most cost-effective approach possible, without disproportionate costs for industry. To substantially reduce air pollution, it is important to target those activities where there is the biggest potential for further reductions at the minimum cost. Further relative reductions will be hardly achievable for sectors already meeting low values, compared to the past and others. Industry should not be subject of further restrictions, if reductions from non-industrial sectors are not achieved: other sectors and activities, such as transport, energy production, construction, shipping, residential commercial and institutional heating, agriculture, waste, etc. should equally aim to further reduce their emissions.  The IED shall remain the main tool to consider the environmental impact of industrial activities. The IED and the use of Best Available Techniques (BAT) have proved instrumental in achieving results in terms of environmental performance of industrial installations in Europe, including the reduction of air pollutants arising from NFM activities. Potential for further reduction of emissions (in particular of SO2, dusts, metals) from the NFM sector is technically limited and would require significant and potentially disproportionate investments. Furthermore, the current target values for metals are already very strict and shall be kept as they are. The NFM sector is striving towards the goals of the EU Zero Pollution Action Plan, with continuous investments to meet the BAT standards of the NFM BREF4 and cannot be expected to face potential regrettable measures going beyond what is considered best available technology today.   Data collection, modelling and monitoring. To develop effective and proportionate air quality policies, it is crucial to improve data collection and modelling, in order to better understand the nature of emissions and thus identify the main contributions. Most importantly, the current framework is missing a relation between source/sector emissions and population attributable exposure fraction and health impact. Strengthening the science/methodology/harmonization behind air quality monitoring, modelling and plans is essential to improve address air pollution most efficiently.   Consider the specificities of Metals. In the case of metals, contributions from natural sources shall be considered when assessing compliance with air quality limit values and target values, as they have a considerable impact. Local settings, geographical characteristics, climate circumstances, annual weather conditions, etc. must be considered. Natural occurrences and the co-existence of other activities contributing to air pollution must be taken into careful consideration. For example, some metals released into air cannot be traced back to NFM industrial operations, but are released from other sources such as transport or fossil fuel burning.

According to the report Air Quality in Europe 2020, the manufacturing and extractive industry sector is the sector with the largest decoupling of emissions from gross value added (GVA), together with energy5. A recent report from the European Environmental Agency (EEA) showed that the aggregated costs of damage over the period 2008-2017 caused by emissions reported from E-PRTR facilities decreased by 54% over the period (from 415 to 749 billion € in 2008 and from 277 to 433 billion € in 2017)6. The NFM sector has largely contributed to improving air quality in Europe with dramatic emissions reductions for the most critical air pollutants. For example, in the past 30 years the reduction rate of emissions was of 71% for SOX, 55% for TSP, 55% for PM2.5, 51% for PM10 and 37% for NOX, as shown in Figure 1. Similarly, the reduction rate of emissions for selected metallic pollutants was of 87% for Arsenic, of 80% for Ni, of the 63% for Cd, and of 62% for Hg, as shown in Figure 2.

 Scientific articles to be considered  The European Environmental Agency 9 found that 24 out of the top-30 polluting facilities are thermal power stations (the majority use coal, some combine gas or oil) and from externalised health costs ~2-4% were related to metals (As, Cd, Cr, Hg, Ni, Pb) and rest to other pollutants. It is also good to note that most of the metal emissions are coming from burning fossil fuels. Anenberg et al. (2019)10 showed that transportation-attributable fractions (TAF) of combined PM2.5 and ozone deaths in 2015 in EU & EFTA was attributable of the 24% of respective air pollutant deaths (see also Goodkind et al. 2019)11. Tschofen et al. (2019)12 showed how to estimate the added value by industry groups comparing gross external damage and gross domestic product.   Policy making should place emphasis in addition of sector-specific emissions to source/sector specific:  • Population attributable exposure fractions (which sectors are causing the highest exposures)  • Health impact fractions (which sectors are causing the highest health impacts) • Individual high emissions sources • Added value for economy/society (e.g., NFMs are key component in green technology)  Without these key elements, it is not possible to make an efficient air policy.

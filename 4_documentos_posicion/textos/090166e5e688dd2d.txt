CITIZEN’S RECOMMENDATIONS ON THE MEDIA FREEDOM IN EU 
Safeguarding media freedom and pluralism in the internal market (Media Freedom Act 
 
Europe’s People’s Forum (www.europespeoplesforum.eu) aims to strengthen democracy 
between elections and at European, national and regional level, by giving all citizens a say on 
policy making and decisions. We prioritize web-based engagement and face-to-face meetings 
to develop citizen participation and deliberative dialogues across the EU and member states, 
including processes like the Conference on the Future of Europe. 
Europe’s People’s Forum is a network of more than 30 technical partner organisations and 
institutions from all 27 member states plus more than 50 civil society organisations and social 
partners within the European Union. All national technical partners are experienced in 
facilitating citizens participation in policy making or decision making. Some partners are 
researchers specialised within this field whereas others have expertise within design and 
implementation.   
 
Citizens’ concerns for independent and pluralistic media 
Independent media and freedom of speech were among the top priorities, when Panel 2 on 
European Democracy/Values and Rights, Rule of Law, Security finalised its 39 
recommendations to the Plenary of the Conference on the Future of Europe.  
Recommendation 5 reads: “In the actual context of many fake news, we recommend to 
promote more independent, objective and balanced media coverage by: 1. Developing at EU 
level a minimum standards directive for media independence. 2. Promoting at EU level the 
development of media competences for every citizen”.  
  
Justification: The EU must produce a directive to ensure the independence of the media and 
freedom of speech.  
 
Recommendation 12 reads: “We recommend that the EU enforces its competition rules in 
the media sector more strictly to ensure that media pluralism is protected in all Member 
States. The EU should prevent large media monopolies and political appointment processes

for media outlet boards. We also recommend that the upcoming EU Media Freedom act 
entails rules on preventing politicians from owning media outlets or having a strong influence 
on their content”.   
  
Justification: We recommend this because enforcing EU competition rules fosters a pluralist 
media landscape where citizens have a choice. Since the Commission is currently developing 
a law (Media Freedom Act) for the integrity of the EU media market, this law should also 
reflect that media outlets fshould not be owned or influenced by politicians.  
 
Recommendation 24 reads: “We recommend that the EU invests in countering 
disinformation swiftly, by supporting existing organisations and initiatives, such as the Code of 
Practice on Disinformation and the European Digital Media Observatory, and similar initiatives 
in the Member States. The counter-measures could include fact-checking, creating awareness 
about disinformation, providing easily accessible statistics, appropriately sanctioning those 
who spread disinformation based on a legal framework, and tackling the sources of 
disinformation”.   
  
Justification: This recommendation is important because misinformation and disinformation, 
coming from within and outside of the EU, create conflicts among EU citizens, polarise the 
society, put democracy at risk and damage the economy. Given the complexity of the topic, 
significant human and financial resources are needed.  
 
Media is central to guarantee a transparent democracy and accountable authorities that are 
trusted by the citizens. Media also offers people the information that is necessary for citizens 
to make decisions for themselves and give them a qualified say in and between the elections. 
From several participatory processes we know that many citizens feel badly informed and see 
the media they use as biased and partial in their coverage. We also know that most media 
outlets do not pay much attention to democratic dialogues, which involve citizens from all parts 
of society in line with the Charter on EU Fundamental Rights. 
Citizens typically cite issues like lack of independence, lack of pluralism and that media outlets 
are biased due to economic or political interests of their owners.  
Despite these legitimate concerns, a Media Freedom Act may establish necessary guidance 
and minimum standards for a multitude of formal and informal processes that influence the 
content, and the extent of independence and pluralism of the media market. 
In the consultation process for the Media Freedom Act Europe’s People’s Forum considers 
itself as a key stakeholder for the citizens’ perspective on the independence of the media as 
well as the freedom of expression for citizens exercised through offline and online media in the 
internal market.   
 
Methodological considerations 
This feedback paper from Europe’s People’s Forum identifies key problems and possible 
solutions in the media sector based on our experience as citizens and as professionals.  
3 
 
The paper makes use of the data provided through The Media for Democracy Monitor 2021 
(MDM 2021), the Euromedia Research Group and the Media Pluralism Monitor 2021 (MPM 
2021), Centre for Media Pluralism and Media Freedom, EUI.  
This paper does not attempt to specify local details with a documentation of problems and 
possible solutions. Nor does it intend to document the problems with legal references or seeks 
to formulate possible directions and solutions in legal terms. 
Europe’s People’s Forum acknowledges that the key objectives of the initiative Safeguarding 
media freedom and pluralism in the internal market (Media Freedom Act) will be to:   
• ensure that media companies can operate in the internal market subject to consistent 
regulatory standards, including standards concerning media freedom and pluralism;   
• ensure that EU citizens have access to a wide and varied online and offline media offer;  
• safeguard the editorial independence and independent management of the media, which 
is a precondition of media freedom and of the integrity of the internal market;   
• foster undistorted competition between media companies by ensuring a transparent and 
fair allocation of state resources.   
Within these objectives Europe’s People’s Forum examines how citizens may be duly included 
in a diverse media coverage as well as in the governance, complaints and regulatory 
structures of the offline and online public and private media sector. 
 
Issues to be solved 
 
1. Protection mechanisms against political and economic interference 
According to MPM 2021 (p.66) 7 member states score high, 6 member states low and the 
remaining 14 member states score medium risk for political influence on the media through 
ownership or through indirect political control from parties, government, or from 
businessmen.  
Public Service Media 
Political influence on public service media is primarily linked to the following issues: 
• Election procedures for the board of directors of the public service media often allow 
for the government or political majority undue influence on the election of board 
members.  
Instead, the majority of the board members should ideally be nominated by civil society 
institutions and organisations whose choice should subsequently be confirmed by the 
parliament. Or the majority could be elected by all political parties in the parliament. In 
both cases the members could neither be candidates nor members of political bodies, 
but collectively live up to the criteria of a professional board of directors. 
• Members of the board or external political and economic decision makers do not 
restrict themselves to the strategic level but interfere directly in the production and 
distribution of daily content. 
4 
 
To minimise possible political interference the Media Freedom Act must explicitly state 
that the responsibility for the daily content production and distribution lies with the top 
managing director or with the editor-in-chief, whose independence should be further 
protected by being hired on a 5-year irrevocable contract that does not conform with 
the period of national elections. 
• Political appointments within top and middle management of public service media may 
leave space for the generation of biased news and other content. 
Merit based appointments of top and middle management by the independent board of 
directors encourages balanced and pluralistic media coverage. 
• Distribution of state advertisement to media outlets may be used to put the individual 
media outlets including public service media under pressure. 
The Media Freedom Act must specify the principle for the distribution of advertisement 
money from state and public authorities by a strictly independent authority and ensure 
that advertisements are distributed according to professional commercial calculations. 
• Funding of public service media varies from country to country and is often one of 
three models: License fees may be collected by the public service media, a specific 
media fee may be collected by the tax authorities, or allocations from the state budget 
may be transferred directly to the media. The latter model is not transparent to the tax-
paying citizen. 
The Media Freedom Act must describe models forthe funding of public service media, 
which emphasise that the citizens are owners and beneficiaries of the public service 
media. Each model must demonstrate to the individual citizen that he or she 
contributes to the funding of the public service media. The size of the license fee or the 
media fee collected by the tax authorities should be determined by the Parliament for a 
period of 5 years to allow the public service media to plan ahead.    
 
Private media: 
• Lack of transparent ownership and regulation allow owners, their relatives or allies to 
influence and control the editorial line of private media – and often concentration in 
large media networks.  
Non-profit community media often operate in an opaque framework of CSO-agendas 
combined with idealistic citizens’ participatory local frameworks.  
Clear regulation of to enhance transparent ownership of media and avoid media 
concentration should be included in the Media Freedom Act. It should also ban political 
candidates and elected politicians from being direct or indirect media owners.  
To minimise possible political interference the Media Freedom Act must explicitly state 
that the responsibility for the daily content production and distribution lies with the top 
managing director or with the editor-in-chief, who should be given a 5-year irrevocable 
contract that does not conform with the period of national elections. 
5 
 
The Media Freedom Act must specify the principle for the distribution of advertisement 
from state and public authorities by a strictly independent authority according to 
professional commercial calculations. 
The Media Freedom Act should preserve the best characteristics of non-profit 
community media by allowing different organisational models and emphasize inclusive 
governance and content. 
 
2. Mechanisms for pluralistic and inclusive content 
According to MPM 2021 (p89), 4 member states score high risk for non-inclusiveness, 19 
medium risk and only 4 countries low risk concerning Social Inclusiveness. This is based 
on the indicators: Access to media for minorities ; Access to media for local/regional 
communities and community media; Access to media for women; Media literacy; Protection 
against illegal and harmful speech.  
Public Service Media:  
• Public service media do not always fulfill its core mandate to the public as an 
independent and unifying forum for pluralistic democratic dialogue across different 
cultural and linguistic groups, national and regional communities, while also keeping 
the political authorities accountable.  
A European mandate for public service media that fulfills the overall requirements of 
the Media Freedom Act should be developed by public service media themselves to 
establish the minimum standards for the EU directive, based on a principle of best 
practices.   
• Editors-in-Chiefs may choose the easy way, allowing political bias or may neglect to 
include population groups including women, minorities, as well as marginalised and 
vulnerable socio-economic groups.  
The editors should produce a European code of ethics to defining the minimum 
standard for the EU directive and based on best practices.  
• Prior to regional, national or European elections reporting and coverage of the political 
parties and candidates risk being biased and partial.  
As part of the Media Freedom Act, a common election code for the public service 
media should be developed, defining the minimum standards for equal and fair 
treatment. 
• Complaint mechanisms inside the media outlets do not always exist within the public 
service media and Public service media are not always represented  in existing self-
regulatory mechanisms, and the independent, media-owned complaint mechanism that 
exists in many countries.  
All public service mediamust be active members in the independent national self-
regulatory council, which will treat complaints. Depending on best practices also in-
house ombudsmen for the citizens should be considered. 
 
6 
 
Private media: 
• Private, commercial media are usually established to generate a profit to their owners. 
Nevertheless, they are vital to democracy because they  contribute to media pluralism 
by adding other journalistic perspectives.Given the fact that advertisement is a key 
income for  online and offline media alike the content will tend to address an audience 
with large purchase capability which will frequently be reflected in the choice of experts 
and citizens who are given a voice in the media content. 
Private media should be covered by the European code of ethics for editor-in-chiefs 
produced by the editors themselves as a document defining the standard as part of the 
EU directive. 
The license to a private media should carry the obligation to be member of the 
independent national self-regulatory council that will treat complaints about media 
content.  
 
3. Citizens’ involvement in governance and in content 
MDM 2021 (p.-40) emphasizes that the more news media allow citizens to voice their 
views and reactions to news stories the higher is their chance to reflect pluralistic opinions 
and interests. However, “mainstream news organisations do not really fulfil the promises 
they make of citizen participation”, and “entirely new models may be required “rather than 
simply further opening of existing models”. Currently, mainsteam analogue and digital 
news media use the internet for comments and criticism. New online media outlets 
provide new types of citizen participation accessible to groups with sufficient skills and 
with only few gatekeepers. Professional mainstream media with their gatekeeper functions 
are no longer solely responsible for the communicative power. 
 
Public Service Media: 
• Public service media are neither privately owned nor government-run. They may be 
state owned, but they must be managed by the public, serve the public and be 
accountable to citizens that ultimately constitute the public.  
With a professional board of directors, as well as a professional director/editor-in-chief 
responsible for the daily production and distribution of content, countries frequently fail 
to provide instruments for direct oversight and inputs from the public. 
Each public service media should establish a citizens’ media user panel. Citizens 
should be encouraged to candidate, and all citizens in the area covered by the public 
service media should be able to cast their vote in online elections. The citizens’ media 
user panel should convene frequently to advise and guide management and editors of 
public service media with regard to the distributed content and suggest new content.  
• The distance between the public service mediand its citizens should be reduced while 
respecting editorial independence. Public ownership to the public service media may 
be emphasised by strengthening the relationship between the citizens and public 
service media.  
7 
 
Each public service media should have a public interest editor, appointed by the 
citizens’ media user panel. The public interest editor should be independent and treat 
feedback from citizens on media content and direct principal, ethical and editorial 
questions to public service media editorial staff in a dialogue that is transparent to the 
citizens. 
• Public service media may fail to provide for social inclusiveness of women, minorities, 
un-educated, and marginalised groups or citizens outside major urban areas. All 
population groups should be invited to express their needs, visions and opinions in the 
media in dialogues with other citizens and with decision makers so media reflects the 
pluralism of society.  
The Media Freedom Act should include recommendations on the role of public service 
media so that it may be a forum for dialogue between population groupswhere all 
citizens express and discuss their concerns and goals with others. The development of 
new analogue and online content formats should be encouraged. 
• A significant part of the population uses social media to network with others. Many 
citizens also get most of their news through the social media, which presents users to 
an automatised selection of content that includes fake and incorrect news. The reach 
and citizens’ use of social media is very high compared to mainstream media and 
poses important questions about whether they shall seek to involve the social media 
audience in their own edited formats, or the mainstream media primarily shall protect 
themselves from such unfair competition. 
In addition to the agreed self-regulatory “Code of practise on Disinformation” the 
European Commission has proposed a Digital Services Act to regulate the internal 
market for digital services, while ensuring the users’ fundamental rights, which should 
be signed by social media platforms, advertisers and other relevant stakeholders, the 
public and private professional online media covered by the Media Freedom Act must 
carry full editorial responsibility for the information they collect and distribute from 
social media platforms. 
 
Private media: 
• Private media may not consider themselves accountable to the public, but to the 
market. Nevertheless, all media serve as a democratic instrument that allow citizens to 
make decisions for their own life and for the society that are a part of.     
The Media Freedom Act could consider ensuring competition on equal terms that also 
private media establish a citizens’ media user panel, a public interest editor or similar 
measures to strengthen the citizens’ influence and interests.  
 
4. Media Self Regulation 
MDM 2021 examines how much journalists are involved in the creation of codes of 
conduct and guidelines concerning the use of social media content. The MDM aims to 
document whether guidelines in the private and the public media have been established in 
EU member states and to evaluate whether they are implemented to as a framework for 
8 
 
proper and autonomous self-regulation of journalists' activities on social media, or 
whether they are imposed on journalists by the governement. In 6 countries such 
guidelines exist for most media and do not seem to be problematic. 13 countries are at 
medium risk and 8 countries at high risk for political interference concerning this particular 
indicator. 
• Media self-regulation is a key measure to increase media quality because it indicates 
the degree of governmental and legal interference in the content. A media council 
typically serves as a complaints body, which treats citizens’ complaints through 
mediation with the concerned media outlet. An important component of media self-
regulation is a code of ethics. This can be developed by the national journalist union, 
by journalists and editors from different groups of media and individual media outlets.  
There is considerable variation among the independent complaint mechanisms in the 
member states as to their role and constitution. In some countries, major media outlets 
do not respect the councils' decisions. In other member states the councils lack 
sufficient budget, or the guidelines are too vague to prevent political interference. 
Other countries have no media councils nor a media ombudsman within the individual 
media outlets. 
In some member states membership of the media council is a condition to receive 
possible financial media support. 
After consultations with media representatives and in accordance with existing media 
self-regulatory stakeholders the Media Freedom Act should define tentative minimum 
standards for the establishment of media councils in every member state. The national 
journalist associations and the media should definethe organisation in detail as well as 
the function of the complaint mechanism. They should also develop codes of ethics at 
national, local or media outlet. Journalists, editors, and citizens should always be 
members ofthe boards of the media councils.   
 
Preparation of the Media Freedom Act 
The Media Freedom Act initiative aims to establish a common framework, which ensures 
consistent regulatory standards including on media freedom, pluralism, editorial 
independence, independent management, transparent and fair allocation of state resources 
and fair competition to ensure that EU citizens have access to a wide and varied media offer 
both offline and online.  
Given the lack of common standards for media regulation, only coordinated action at EU level 
can effectively remove the obstacles that European media market players are confronted 
with. At the EU level, a structured cooperation framework for independent media regulators in 
the European Regulators Group for Audiovisual Media Services (ERGA) could benefit from 
being equipped with further authority and resources. Europe’s People’s Forum has not yet 
developed proposals for the regulatory bodiesbut emphasizes that these bodies must remain 
independent of national authorities to ensure the pluralism and independent media required 
by the citizens. 
A Digital Services Act has been proposed by the European Commission to regulate the in-
ternal market for digital services, while ensuring the users’ fundamental rights. Europe’s 
9 
 
People’s Forum has chosen to treat digital services related to public or private media in this 
paper but has not treated them separately.   
EU media legislation would establish common principles for national scrutiny procedures 
concerning media market transactions as well as other measures to restrict market entry and 
regulate media operation. It would also include measures to enhance media market 
transparency.   
EU legislation would also define the principles to protect the editorial independence of media 
and the transparent allocation of state support in the media sector. It would also aim to lay the 
foundation for consistent regulatory and self-regulatory standards that enhance offline and 
online media pluralism in the internal market.  
The Recommendations of Europe’s People’s Forum 
On the basis of the citizens’ recommendations in CoFoE, Europe’s People’s Forum calls for 
the Media Freedom Act to be implemented as EU legislation along with an independent 
monitoring mechanism. 
As illustrated above, the degree of media freedom and the regulatory standards vary signifi-
cantly among the member states. Such differences are not desirable within the internal market 
for the media sector and within the democratic standards for the European Union. 
The foreseen Media Freedom Act should not be perceived as a detailed EU-regulation of the 
national media sector that substitutes existing national regulations that are insufficient to 
safeguard media freedoms in the internal marketEPF recommends to involve respective 
national stakeholders across Europe in a consolidated consultations process to define the 
future Codes and Standards Such codes and standards could apply to journalists, editors, 
public and print media, regulatory authorities etc. 
The EU Media Freedom Act initiative must define essential requirements for all levels of 
stakeholders related to media freedoms and the internal market.  
Cross-European dialogues would allow groups of journalists, groups of editors, public service 
media and private media representatives, or self-regulatory mechanisms and regulatory 
bodies to develop the required codes of ethics or minimum standards concerning specific 
issues related to the Act. Such a process would bring best practices to the fore as well as 
allow contributions from European organisations like EFJ, EBU, ERGA, COE, OSCE and 
others.   
Each agreed code or minimum standard (to be updated regularly) would form part of the 
overall Media Freedom Act to define the minimum standards for all member states in the 
media sector.  
Europe’s People’s Forum with its national technical partners has special expertise in the 
facilitation of national and European level participatory processes. Because of its ready 
access to a comprehensive informal network of media experts, Europe’s People’s Forum may 
assist with the independent design, implementation and facilitation of professionals’ dialogues 
to develop the best possible common standards of media freedoms for the media sector in the 
internal market.

Citizens and political decision makers 
Citizens demand independent, balanced and inclusive media in all member states but political 
decision makers, public authorities and even media or media owners may be skeptical to the 
idea of a European directive that defines higher standards for the regulation of the media 
sector.  
 It is suggested to organise a participatory process involving representative citizens within all 
member states and at European level in order to have their opinions, proposals and feedback 
to the process concerning the Media Freedom Act and indirectly to mobilize popular support 
for the Media Freedom Act initiative. 
Europe’s People’s Forum may also provide services concerning the design, implementation 
and facilitation of the participatory involvement of citizens in the preparation and 
implementation of a Media Freedom Act. 
 
 
Increased media coverage of the European Union’s policies and activities 
The citizens’ final recommendations from Panel 2, Session 3 on European Democra/Values 
and Rights, Rule of Law, Security in Florence stressed an additional issue concerning national 
and European media which should be considered in addition to the initiative of the Media 
Freedom Act. 
Recommendation 24 reads: “We recommend that the EU provides more information and news 
to European citizens. It should use any means that are necessary while respecting freedom and 
independence of the media. It should provide media outlets with ressources as well as a broad 
and reliable information about EU activities and policies. The EU should guarantee that the 
information is broadcasted evenly across all Member States by National and European media 
and should ensure that Member States encourage public broadcasters and public news 
agencies to cover European affairs”.  
  
Justification: “We recommend this because based on our personal experience and based on 
the data from Eurobarometer, the majority of European citizens are informed through the tra-
ditional media (press, radio and television) and the information currently offered in these 
channels about the EU is very scarce. The media, particularly the public, have a public service 
function, so reporting on EU issues that affect the European population is essential and 
indispensable to fulfill that function. We recommend that the information issued in the different 
Member States about the EU be the same in order to promote integration and avoid different 
information on different issues in each country. Using the already existing media channels is 
more feasible, and less expensive than creating a new channel and achieves the same 
outcome. The pre-existing channels also have the advantage that they are already known by 
citizens. No citizen should need to choose between different channels to be able to access 
different (national or european) content.”

A possible solution that does not infringe the media freedoms – including also how to cover 
issues and policies of the EU – could be inspired by the AVMS directive’s harmonised stand-
ard for a 30 % share of European audio-visual production. In this case within the internal mar-
ket for the media sector. For example, as below: 
• The national public service and private media do not always produce content con-
cerning policies and stories about the policies of EU and the other European countries 
as much as deserved from their journalistic relevance and their influence on the life 
conditions of national citizens.  
It could be considered to define a threshold of 30 % of the news programmes that 
should deal with European non-domestic content in linear and non-linear news pro-
gramming.

FAM’s positions on EU biodiversity strategy  
We w
holeheartedly agree that the protection of biodiversity is central for the achievement of a 
sustainable future. Furthermore, we support the Green Deal that the Commission launched upon 
taking office. Sustainability is a core value in everything we do, including in our forest 
management. We believe that sustainably managed forests can play a vital role in the green 
transition of our societies. We, as active within forestry and the forest industry, have a great 
responsibility to contribute to the ambitions and objectives set out in the biodiversity strategy for 
2030.  
To achieve increased biodiversity while also substituting fossil materials and fuels with fossil-free 
alternatives will require further development of the sustainable forest management practice. 
Also production forests have to live up to high standards and contribute towards the goals set 
out in the biodiversity strategy. This is already an integral part of the forestry practice in Sweden 
and Finland. Today a majority of production forests are certified according to FSC and/or PEFC. 
Among other things, the new standard of FSC requires that an additional five percent of 
productive forest land be set aside for protection or to be managed to promote natural 
biodiversity, in addition to the previous requirement of five percent.  
We believe that it is important to look at all of the services that forests provide, not only focusing 
on protection of specific areas. Production forests contribute to biodiversity and are habitat for 
many forest-living species. There is no contradiction in terms between production forests and 
biodiversity, as long as the forests are managed sustainably.  
The relationship between biodiversity and human activities is complicated. For instance, when 
inventorying a forest to ascertain the status for forest-living species, the usual practice is to look 
for specific species known to indicate the overall ecological status in an area. The presence of 
one of these species would signal that many other species associated with it are also thriving in 
the same area. One such species is the Upis ceramboides, also known as Roughened Darkling 
Beetle or Alaskan darkling beetle. It is a wood-living insect which benefits from forest fires. As 
the incidence of fires has been reduced in managed forests the beetle has become endangered.

Through sustainable management, Swedish and Finnish forestry (almost a third of Europe’s 
forests) combines a commercial forest sector with preserving biodiversity. Major changes to 
forest policies were adopted in Sweden in the early 1990s putting the goals of wood production 
and biodiversity on an equal level. In this way, we can preserve both our vital ecosystems and 
enable the forests to play an increasingly important role for climate mitigation and green 
substitution.

I
n closing, since reporting and the interpretation of definitions vary between member countries it 
is important to use a common baseline between member countries when determining restoration 
targets for the upcoming period. For instance, most if not all initiatives of forest owners to 
protect portions of their forests is excluded by Sweden in its reporting. This gives the false 
impression that Sweden underperforms in protecting its forests.  
There is also a lack of a coherent baseline for what constitutes a positive trend in ecological 
status. In some countries the point of comparison is the date of entry into the EU, while others – 
among them Sweden – compare ecological status today with that of the pre-industrial era. In 
this way the targets become more of ideals, rather than concrete and practical goals.

Basic considerations 
The German insurance industry supports the Solvency II review and wel-
comes many aspects of the European Commission’s proposals. 
We ar e convinced that the risk -based Solvency II regulation already  
ensures a very high level of policyholder protection and contributes signifi-
cantly to financial stability. Recently, it proved its worth in the challenges of 
the Covid-19 pandemic. Therefore, in our view, there is generally no need 
for fundamental changes to European insurance regulation. 
Nevertheless, meaningful amendments to Solvency II can bring further  
benefits to both stakeholders and the European economy. 
With investments of approx. 1,800 bn euros of German and more than 
10,000 bn euros of European insurers, the insurance industry stands ready 
to be a driving force of the green transition – we are fully committed to sub-
stantially contributing to a more sustainable and resilient European econ-
omy. 
To realize this potential, the risk-orientation of Solvency II should be further 
strengthened by correcting overshooting requirements and removing  
unnecessary burdens in all three pillars. We expressly welcome all aspects 
of the proposals that go in this direction.  At the same time, t he volatility of 
solvency positions and the already very high level of overall regulatory  
requirements should certainly not be further increased . This holds espe-
cially if a new extrapolation method is introduced in times of unprecedented 
low interest rates. 
We also understand that the crisis management of insurance undertakings 
justifies deliberate regulatory amendments. However, in light of the very  
limited systemic risk of the European insurance industry with only very rare 
failures of individual companies, we are convinced that the proposed Insur-
ance Recovery and Resolution Directive is too extensive. For instance, the 
chapters on recovery and resolution planning, resolvability assessments  
and resolution tools are overly granular and descriptive. In particular, the 
extensive requirements on bridge undertakings as a resolution tool may un-
dermine equivalent arrangements  already established in Member States 
which – as in Germany – ensure the continuation of insurance policies by 
portfolio transfers. Therefore, the IRRD should – in contrast to the BRRD 
for credit institutions – pursue a more principle -based approach to reflect 
the different significance of the issue for the insurance sector with a stronger 
long-term-orientation and to grant more leeway for NCAs and insurance un-
dertakings.

1. Quantitative Aspects and Long-Term Guarantee 
Measures 
The German insurance industry decisively supports the European Commis-
sion’s objective of mitigating the impact of short-term market volatility 
on the solvency position of insurers . This is a crucial factor for facilitating 
insurers’ ability to offering both long-term guarantees for the consumers and 
long-term financing for the European economy and the Green Deal. 
We also agree that the cumulative impact of all changes must not result in 
a disruptive deterioration of solvency positions.  Instead, a balance of  
tightening and easing should be maintained. 
Given these objectives, we support most of the European Commission’s 
proposals, particularly regarding the volatility adjustment, the risk margin, 
interest rate risk, long-term equity and the correlation of spread and interest 
rate risk. 
Regarding the extrapolation of the interest rate term  structure, however, 
further improvements are needed. The proposed new extrapolation method 
uses information from markets that are not deep, liquid and transparent.  
If, at the same time, the stabilizing effect of convergence is constrained, this 
leads to higher vol atility, against which companies that depend on suffi-
ciently deep and liquid (bond) markets for their large-scale capital invest-
ments cannot hedge. The proposed parameterization of the new method 
could in addition , at least in some Member States, deteriora te solvency  
positions to such an extent that this cannot be compensated for by relief 
elsewhere. Overall, the extrapolation proposal does not preserve the bal-
ance between a realistic market -based valuation basis and the avoidance 
of artificial volatility,  which endangers the supply of long -term guarantees 
and long -term investments contributing to  the Commission's objectives.   
To avoid a systematic deterioration compared to the current extrapolation, 
the convergence parameter would need to be set at 20  %. The proposed 
value of 10 % should therefore be significantly increased. Given the relief 
elsewhere proposed by the European Commission, a value of 15  % could 
possibly be justifiable. 
The envisaged linear transition mechanism starting at 20 % is positive but 
cannot be a substitute for a sensible permanent parameterization. The mod-
elling of long -term interest rates is not a temporary issue that would only 
affect the legacy insurance portfolio, which is also only slowly shrinking . 
New insurance products are affected by long-term interest rates as well, as 
consumers still need guaranteed lifetime benefits for old-age provision. 
 
page 4 / 42 
We support most of the envisaged amendments to the volatility adjust-
ment (VA). However, if in its calculation the risk correction would be based 
on a percentage of current spreads, the volatility -dampening effect of the 
volatility adjustment may not be sufficiently effective in critical market situa-
tions. Therefore, with a view on financial stability, this change is not sup-
ported. Instead, the current risk correction should be st be  maintained.  
Alternatively, the percentage factors applicable in times of a sharp widening 
of spreads should at least be significantly lower. 
As to the risk margin, we welcome the amendments considered by the 
European Commission as an important step in the right direction, although 
the risk margin still appears high even then . Regarding groups, however, 
the risk margin should be either diversified or calculated on group level to 
be consistent with the reality of how insurance groups are managed in  
practice and with the treatment of diversification within the solvency capital 
requirement (SCR). 
We support the envisaged amendments to interest rate risk. The combi-
nation of recalibrated risk factors that are also fit for negative rates and of 
the corrected order of stressing and extrapolating improves the risk-sensi-
tivity of standard formula interest rate risk significantly. 
We also welcome the European Commission’s considerations which aim to 
ensure that a broader range of equity investments can be recogni zed as 
long-term equity. The envisaged amendments would remove s ignificant 
barriers for the application in practice. 
Due to life insurers’ often very long-run obligations, even small changes to 
the calculation of long-term risk-free interest rates may have a big impact 
on their solvency position. All technical parameters that have a signifi-
cant effect on the solvency situation and, thus, also to the sector’s con-
tribution to European objectives such as the Green Deal should therefore 
be decided on by the Co-Legislators. 
To this end, the new extrapolation method and its convergence parameter 
must be specified in the Directive. This also holds for the residual volume 
criterion (percentage value of 6 %) for deep, liquid and transparent markets 
and the first smoothing point (Euro: FSP=20) for the first day of application 
of the amending Directive. 
The risk margin parameters and the correct order of extrapolating stressed 
interest rates should best be specified in the Directive, too. 
Finally, our analyses do not confirm a balanced outcome (or even a capital 
relief) for all  national markets  and for different market situations  (all 
changes fully applied). This is reinforced by the fact that the net effect of the 
proposal results from different positive and negative effects that are much 
 
page 5 / 42 
larger. Small changes/misestimations of these opposing effects may, thus, 
result in big changes of the net effect. 
For an informed decision on the European Commission’s proposals, more 
clarity on their impact should be provided regarding underlying assump-
tions, drivers, and their effects at Member State level. 
GDV position in short:  
Quantitative Aspects and Long-Term Guarantee Measures 
 
- Further improvements are needed for the extrapolation of the inter-
est rate term structure. The proposal does not maintain the balance 
between sufficient level of security and the much-needed investment 
capacity of life insurance companies. As part of the volatility adjust-
ment, the current risk correction should be maintained. As to the risk 
margin, diversification effects at group level should be reflected. 
- Apart from that, we essentially support the European Commission’s 
proposals on the volatility adjustment, the risk margin, the  
interest rate risk, and the correlation of spread and interest rate risk. 
- All technical parameters that have a significant effect on the sol-
vency situation and the sector’s contribution to European objectives 
such as the Green Deal should be decided on by the Co-Legislators. 
- More clarity on the proposals’ impact should be provided, especially 
on underlying assumptions and drivers including their effect at Mem-
ber State level.

2. Supervisory Measures 
We welcome strengthening policyholder and consumer  protection and fi-
nancial stability. We also take a positive view of the intended harmonization 
of resolution tools and the improvement of coordination and cooperation 
between the competent authorities in the various Member States. However, 
the measures and instruments envisaged must be proportionate; in partic-
ular, small and medium-sized enterprises should not be overburdened. Fur-
ther requirements should be reflected in a more principle -based and less 
granular approach of the IRRD. Especially a broad adoption of the banking 
recovery and resolution regime without considering insurance specifics is 
inappropriate and undermines the different resolution process of a failing 
insurer. Unlike banks, insurers regularly do not have to be resolved literally 
“overnight” as they can continue to rely on a steady premium income and 
their obligations towards policyholders become due over a longer period. 
Against this background, the extensive number of Level 2 and Level 3 pro-
visions should be critically questioned and it should be examined if compre-
hensive provisions are in line with the minimum harmonization character of 
the Directive and abiding by the principle of proportionality. 
The insurance industry has repeatedly proven its remarkable strength  
during the past crises, most recently during the Covid-19 pandemic. From 
our perspective, the experience from this crisis has shown that supervisors 
have all instruments they need even for such exceptional circumstances . 
Because of the specific characteristics of the insurance business  – 
long-term orientation, stable financing of liabilities, advance financing of in-
surance benefits through insurance premiums and the link of most insured 
events to external causes – systemic risks are very limited, and the insur-
ance industry usually works as a stabilizing factor in the financial system.  
The European insurance system under Solvency  II has proved inherently 
stable and failures of individual companies are a rare event. 
a. Supervisory ladder 
We agree that NCAs must be able to intervene effectively if an insurer is in 
crisis. However, we are skeptical regarding new early intervention pow-
ers for NCAs to be already available, although the insurer complies with all 
regulations and financial requirements. The current Solvency  II framework 
has proved to be fit for purpose. The defined thresholds, SCR and MCR, 
are cautiously calculated risk buffers and allow for either orderly return to 
financial solidity or – as measure of last resort – an orderly resolution. Su-
pervisory intervention powers follow a pre -defined ladder according to the 
severity of non-compliance with regulatory requirements. Following the pro-
posed amendment of Article 83 IRRD NCAs shall have the power to 
 
page 7 / 42 
intervene at still sound undertakings fulfilling all financial requirements and 
resolution tools should also be applicable in case of non -compliance with 
governance requirements, like proposed in Article 19 (3) lit b IRRD.  This 
could undermine the current ladder of supervision. 
 
German insurers oppose amendments that would undermine the current 
order of intervention powers which are necessarily linked to verifiable 
thresholds. In principle, hard intervention of NCAs should be possible  
only in case of a SCR breach. A seamless integration of the insurance-
specific recovery and resolution framework and of macroprudential tools 
into the existing supervisory ladder is essential.  
b. Macroprudential tools 
An extensive macroprudential framework  is already in place for  the  
European insurance industry – including EIOPA's risk dashboards and  fi-
nancial stability reports, stress tests and macroprudential elements of  
Solvency II (e. g. VA, transitionals). Therefore, we  see little need for addi-
tional macroprudential instrum ents or measures. In particular, macropru-
dential supervision should not go beyond the IAIS’s Holistic Framework to 
avoid impairing the international competitiveness of European insurers.  
Besides the abovementioned Article 83 IRRD supervisors shall  have the 
power to also intervene at still sound undertakings fulfilling all financial re-
quirements due to the macroprudential tools in Article 144b and c Sol-
vency II Directive. We are skeptical with regards of both, the possibility to 
intervene with healthy undertakings as well as the associated uncertainties. 
Intervention in healthy undertakings could have counterproductive effects 
from a macroprudential perspective (e.  g. increased uncertainty for inves-
tors regarding the availability of dividends, distortions in capital allocation).  

 
page 8 / 42 
With regards to sector-wide shocks, which are the main criterion for inter-
vention in Art. 144c, it remains unclear who will determine them. Compared 
with, e. g., Article 18 EIOPA-Regulation a clear decision mechanism involv-
ing the Council and the Commission is missing.  
c. EIOPA competences 
We accept that certain sound insurance groups or companies need to de-
velop preventive recovery plans to be better prepared in the event of a cri-
sis. The establishment of resolution authorities in addition to supervisory 
authorities is adequate as long as t he competencies are clearly defined, 
and the resources are efficiently allocated. We take a critical view of the 
proposed role of EIOPA as the risks for competence conflicts between EI-
OPA and NCAs are considerable. It is questionable why the pre-emptive 
group recovery and group resolution plans should be provided to EIOPA as 
requested by new Article 7 (5) and 11  (1) IRRD. In addition, Article 90 (3) 
IRRD states that EIOPA should create a permanent Resolution Committee 
that shall promote the development and coordination of resolution plans and 
develop methods for the resolution of entities that are failing. Further, EI-
OPA should maintain a central database of all penalties and other adminis-
trative measures according to new Article 90 IRRD. There is a risk that EI-
OPA will increasingly assume the role of an operational supervisory and 
resolution authority. We see possible overlapping competencies as an ob-
stacle in crisis management and prefer clear competencies at level of the 
NCAs since attention must be paid to national characteristics in the case of 
resolution. 
d. Insurance Guarantee Schemes 
We understand the motivation for providing national supervisors with a va-
riety of effective resolution tools in the event of a crisis. However, the reso-
lution tools establish ed by the IRRD should not interfere with Insurance 
Guarantee Schemes (IGS) established in Members States.  
For instance, Germany already provides for the continuation of life insur-
ance contracts by portfolio transfers to a statutory guarantee funds financed 
by German life insurance companies. The management of the guarantee 
funds is entrusted to a licensed and regulated insurance undertaking owned 
by German life insurers. This company operates on a status subject to ap-
proval by the German government with th e sole purpose of rehabilitating 
the insurance portfolios of life insurance companies in trouble. This mech-
anism bears strong similarities to a bridge undertaking as proposed in Arti-
cle 32 Para. 2 IRRD. Therefore, a distinctive line between bridge under-
takings and IGS must be drawn. Most notably, the different appropriation 
of bridge undertakings (undertaking-specific perspective) compared Io IGS 
 
page 9 / 42 
(portfolio-specific perspective) can hardly be aligned. It is essential that the 
implementation of bridge undertakings as a resolution tool must not under-
mine well-established IGS-structures which reflect the particular demands 
of the local markets. 
The aforementioned comments cast light on the wider question about the 
general interaction between the IRRD and IGS, in particular with regard to 
resolution funding, The EU -Commission states that insurance guarantee 
schemes could contribute to funding the resolution process. This statement 
is translated to the requirement for resolution authorities to consider IGS as 
a funding source and set out principles for sharing responsibilities with other 
sources (Art. 10 Para. 2 lit e.). It is not clear whether these principles may 
allow resolution authorities to deny a financial contribution or must set the 
stage for utilizing IGS for funding resolution. If the latter, we would like to 
point out that IGS do not have the same functions and are not based on an 
equivalent European framework as guarantee schemes in the banking sec-
tor. Moreover, it needs to be borne in mind that assets might not be availa-
ble for funding due to legal restrictions. In Germany, insurance ass ets and 
insurance liabilities are inextricably intertwined to a large extent (Guarantee 
Assets). Due to these differences IGS should be kept separate from the 
IRRD. At least it needs to be clarified that Member States have unre-
stricted discretion to determine whether, and if so, to what extent IGS 
are involved in financing resolution measures.  
e. Publication of additional calculations 
The proposed publication of additional calculations of solvency -related 
figures (sensitivities) would create “shadow supervisory regimes” that 
would not be in line with the sector’s experiences as a stabilizing ele-
ment. Shadow supervisory regimes are not needed, sensitivities should 
only be reported to the supervisor.

Supervisory Measures

Solvency II framework has proved to be fit for purpose. 
- The established supervisory ladder should be maintained. Hard in-

- Sensitivities should be reported to the supervisor. Any further publi-

3. Proportionality and Thresholds 
The German insurance industry strongly supports the proposed new pro-
portionality framework including the automatic application for Low-Risk 
Profile Undertakings (LRPU). The new framework can improve the applica-
tion of the proportionality principle , which means to regulate insurance  
companies according to the “nature, scale and complexity of the risks  
inherent”. 
While we highly welcome automatic application, the definition of LRPU  
does not fit for bigger markets  – like the German market – with an hetero-
genous structure including many SMEs. According to the Commission’s 
proposal the size threshold for LRPU is 100 mn euros for non -life and  
1 bn euros for life. In the German property/casualty market, only 46 of 190 
companies (approx. 25 %) have 15 –100 mn euros premium income and 
would comply with the size -criterion (they need to fulfil further criteria to 
qualify as LRPU) . These 46 companies only account for 3  % of market’s 
premium income. 
Against this backdrop , we propose increasing the size criterion to define 
LRPU to 500 m euros for non-life and to 5 bn euros for life. In consequence, 
about 20 % of the German market in terms of gross written premium would 
comply with the size criterion. However, these companies do not become 
automatically LRPU. They still need to fulfil five further criteria that assess 
the underlying risk profile of the company which we fully support. 
In addition, there are more, good reasons to increase the scope for the 
automatic application. First, the list of automatic proportionality measures 
is limited to governance and reporting (pillar 2 and 3). Hence, LRPU will still 
fully comply with the quantitative requirements of pillar 1. Second, supervi-
sory authorities always have the right and the duty to withdraw the LRPU  
status given sufficient and sound evidence. Thus, national supervisory will 
have all the necessary tools to ensure a high level of security for policy 
holders. Third, a broader scope for the automatic application  will also 
strengthen the risk-based approach to supervision and channel resources 
and capacities where needed most to protect policy holders. 
We support increasing the thresholds for the application of Solvency II. 
However, we think that the proposal of the European Commission (15 mn 
euros gross written premium)  could be impr oved by following EIOPA’s  
recommendation of a  threshold of 25 mn euros gross written premium.  
This would enable more very small insurance companies to apply a more 
proportionate, national regulation that still provides a high level of security 
for policyholders.

- To have an even greater effect, the size criterion for non-life should

4. Further issues 
Group Supervision 
For the German Insurers it is important that the scope of group supervision 
in Solvency II is consistent with IFRS and that intermediate insurance hold-
ing companies are taken out of scope. Moreover, it is unclear how the new 
Articles 144a–c Solvency II Directive shall or can be applied mutatis mutan-
dis at group level. So far it remains unclear whether supervisory measures 
due to Article 144a–c can or shall be taken at group level as well in cases 
where such measures are applied at solo entity level. As this would neither 
make sense nor would it be proportionate this should be clarified. 
Finally, the power to require a group to restructure would go beyond what 
is necessary to supervise groups. Organisational freedom is essential, and 
changes might have a huge impact on the group, including, for example, 
resources and taxes. In our view supervisory aut horities already have the 
necessary power to sufficiently supervise groups with complex organisa-
tional structures. Even if there are (few) cases in which this may be difficult 
we are convinced that this should be solved at level of the supervising  
authority and does not justify such an extensive intervention. 
There should also be no a dditional supervisory powers in the context of 
transitional measures within group own funds. The fact that supervisory ac-
tions could be taken would create de facto a level of intervention before the 
breach of group SCR which undermines the effectiveness of transitionals. 
Regarding group own funds, expected profits included in future premiums 
(EPIFP) should not be included in the group’s regular availability assess-
ment. EPIFP are the result of a valuation based on economic principles and 
part of the reconciliation reserve. They are fully recognised as unrestricted 
tier 1 items, and there is no justification apparent for burdensome continu-
ous assessment. 
70 % market coverage for resolution planning 
According to Art icle 9 (2) IRRD resolution authorities shall ensure that at 
least 70 % of the Member State’s life and non -life and reinsurance market 
shall be subject to resolution planning . Such a minimum market coverage 
is not appropriate as it does neither consider the specific situations of the 
national markets (e. g. existence of Insurance Guarantee Schemes (IGS) 
and other safeguards) nor the individual situation of the insurance compa-
nies affected. 
Further, a minimum market coverage of  70 % is to o extensive. The  
Solvency II framework is calibrated in such a way that insolvencies actually 
occur at only 0.5 % probability. So, many more insurance companies are 
 
page 14 / 42 
committed to resolution planning than expected to go into resolution. The 
target is also excessive in terms of global comparison. The IAIS states in its 
“Application Paper on Resolution Powers and Planning ” that in most juris-
dictions the development of a resolution plan is only required for a subset 
of insurers (e. g. size of the insurer with total assets above 50 billion euros) 
and not for the majority. 
We propose to replace the 70 % market coverage  by a risk -oriented  
approach. The NCAs shall require undertakings based on risk-oriented  
criteria like  risk profile, interconnectedness , and the likely impact of a  
failure on policy holders and on the financial market which also includes the 
consideration of IGS and further safeguards. 
VA: Specific modelling requirements for internal models 
Internal models are a key element of the Solvency II framework which have 
been developed in close cooperation with supervisors. The Solvency  II 
framework already contains high requirements on internal models which en-
sure that these are overall appropriatel y calibrated. The inclusion of new 
very detailed requirements as proposed in Article 122 (5) on a specific as-
pect of many internal models (the dynamic volatility adjustment) would not 
only increase the operational burden for internal model firms significan tly 
but also make a perfect fit to individual risk profiles more difficult. The spe-
cific design of internal models needs to be set in a dialogue between under-
taking and supervisor. 
Legal certainty with regard to the “eternal cancellation right” 
Article 186 Solvency II is not in line with more recent legislation such as the 
Consumer Rights Directive as it does not provide for a time limit of the cus-
tomers’ cancellation right. Consequently, under Article 186 any error in the 
customer information may trigger an “eternal cancellation right”. This allows 
customers to rescind the contract years or decades after its conclusion  in 
order to retrospectively avoid the contractually agreed costs. Article 186 
should be amended in accordance with the  Consumer Rights Directive, 
which stipulates a time limit of 12 months under any circumstances.  
Market access of third country reinsurers 
Absent regulation on the market access for third country reinsurers in  
Europe contributes to a fragmented regulatory environment in Member 
States and creates an unlevel playing for the (retro -)cession of insurance 
risks outside the EU. Therefore, the oppo rtunity of the review should be 
seized to tackle the problem and ensure a harmonized approach.

Obstacle to legally effective netting agreements 
Article 275 (1a) stipulates that the claims of the insured take priority over all 
other claims. The absence on an exception for netting agreements of  
derivatives makes it difficult for insurers to use this recognized risk mitiga-
tion technique which could help to reduce risk and to protect liquidity. Net-
ting agreements are rightly privileged in the European CRR and EMIR reg-
ulations and should also be possible under Solvency II. 
Mortgage loans 
Regarding mortgage loans, it is crucial that the risk mitigation by the (risk -
adjusted value of) mortgage continues to be adequately recognised.  
It should not be undermined by a floor for the loss given default of the mort-
gage loan (brought into discussion by the European Systemic Risk Board). 
Such a  blind takeover from the completely different banking regulation 
would neither be risk-sensitive nor in line with Solvency II principles (99.5 % 
Value at Risk). Further, a floor could make the low-risk real estate lending 
with low loan-to-value (LTV) ratios, in which insurers have been engaged 
so far, unprofitable. Insurers could be driven to grant loans with higher LTVs 
to achieve an adequate return compared to regulatory requirements. 
Mortgage loans are an important asset class with a plenty of small individual 
exposures with low risks and without short -run market value fluctuations.  
However, currently not all mortgage loans are subject to the counterparty 
default risk module but instead to the – inadequate – spread risk module.  
In particular, the limit of 1 m euros for mortgage loans subject to the coun-
terparty default risk module neither accounts for the developments of the 
local property market nor is it risk sensitive. Therefore, it should be deleted 
or at least raised. Moreover, not only mortgage loans to natural persons but 
also to communal and cooperative housing societies should be eligible. 
Property risk 
The standard formula property risk factor (calibrated solely with exception-
ally volatile UK data) is clearly too high given the evidence in the EU. In 
addition, a revised capital requirement could foster insurers’ contribution to 
the financing needs of economic recovery, the Capital Markets Union and, 
particularly, the decarbonisation targeted by the European Green Deal. 
Lapse risk 
In the life insurance lapse risk scenarios, the risk factors are currently only 
selectively applied to those contracts for which changed lapse rates  
increase the obligations. In practice, however, movements in lapse rates 
are largely homogeneous over all contracts. Therefore, the assumed 
 
page 16 / 42 
changes in option exercise rates should apply to all contracts in each sce-
nario. 
Moreover, the risk factors for the life and health mass lapse scenarios  
appear to be unreasonably high. Even in extreme situations of individual life 
insurers, lapse waves of 40 % or 70 % did not occur in Germany by far. In 
health insurance, the statistics of the German health insurance association 
PKV – covering a large part of the European market – speak for a risk factor 
of 25 %. The mass lapse risk factors for life and SLT (similar to life tech-
niques) health should therefore be significantly lowered (or at least be re-
placeable by an undertaking-specific parameter USP). 
Own Risk and Solvency Assessment and Prudent Person Principle 
The proposed integration of macroprudential aspects into ORSA (Article 45) 
and PPP (Article 132) goes too far. We agree that insurers need to reflect 
macroeconomic and financial developments in their investment decisions  
and their ORSA. However, especially the additional requirement that insur-
ers must assess the extent to which their behaviour “may affect macroeco-
nomic and financial markets’ developments and have the potential to turn 
into sources of systemic risk ” should be removed . Given the very limited 
systemic contribution of individual insurers’ behaviour and the diverse range 
of strategies employed across Europe, the potential benefits of this require-
ment seem small and do not justify the substantial costs involved. What is 
more, the feasibility of this exercise is questionable. For the individual com-
pany it will be very difficult, if not impossible, to determine its potential con-
tribution to sources of systemic risk. 
Solvency and Financial Condition Report 
Currently, the SFCR  reports are hardly noticed by the public  (only 33 
monthly downloads on average). An improvement is thus urgently needed. 
Otherwise, one SFCR report that is currently not read will become two  
reports that will miss the mark.  The amendments to Articles 51 and 256 
propose a division of the SFCR into two parts: one for policyholder and an  
additional report for other market participants. The industry generally sup-
ports this approach as long as it does not create an additional burden and 
the SFCR as a whole is streamlined and focused on relevant information  
only. This can only be achieve d if the SFCR for policyholders is limited to 
basic information directly relevant for the policyholder . As a guideline the 
scope should be limited to two pages. Also, the SFCR for other market par-
ticipants should contain only ready-to-use quantitative data which is based 
on already published QRTs  with neither narrative explanations nor addi-
tional calculations solely for SFCR purposes. In summary, we believe that

1 Systemic risks 
and crises  
preparedness  
We do not share the view that the experience with the Sol-
vency II framework so far “has underlined the absence of spe-
cific EU-level provisions to address the build-up of systemic 
risks, to ensure preparedness for crises or to resolve insurers, 
where necessary.” In the aftermath of the global financial crisis 
of 2008, reinforced by the impact of the extremely low / nega-
tive interest rate environment, an extensive macroprudential 
framework for the European insurance industry has already 
been put in place, including EIOPA's risk dashboards and fi-
nancial stability reports, stress tests and the integration of 
macroprudential elements into Solvency II (e. g. VA, transition-
als). From our perspective, the experience from the pandemic 
has shown that supervisors have all instruments they need 
even for such exceptional circumstances. 
5 Balance of the 
proposal 
The cumulative impact of all changes should not result in a dis-
ruptive deterioration of solvency positions. Instead, a balance 
of tightening and easing should be maintained. 
The presented estimations suggest a more or less stable or 
slightly increased capital in excess of regulatory requirements 
(at the end of the transitional period). However, our analyses 
do not confirm a balanced outcome (or even a capital relief) for 
all national markets and for different market situations (all 
changes fully applied). This is reinforced by the fact that the 
net effect of the proposal results from different positive and 
negative effects that are much larger. Small changes/misesti-
mations of these opposing effects may, thus, result in big 
changes of the net effect.

77a Extrapolation Regarding the extrapolation of the interest rate term structure, 
further improvements are needed. 
The proposed new extrapolation method uses information from 
markets that are not deep, liquid and transparent. If, at the 
same time, the stabilizing effect of convergence is constrained, 
this leads to higher volatility, against which companies that de-
pend on sufficiently deep and liquid (bond) markets for their 
large-scale capital investments cannot hedge. The proposed 
parameterization of the new method could in addition, at least 
in some Member States, deteriorate solvency positions to such 
 
page 24 / 42 
an extent that this cannot be compensated for by relief else-
where. Overall, the extrapolation proposal does not preserve 
the balance between a realistic market-based valuation basis 
and the avoidance of artificial volatility, which endangers the 
supply of long-term guarantees and long-term investments 
contributing to the Commission's objectives.  
To avoid a systematic deterioration compared to the current 
extrapolation, the convergence parameter would need to be 
set at 20%. The proposed value of 10% should therefore be 
significantly increased. Given the relief elsewhere proposed by 
the European Commission, a value of 15% could possibly be 
justifiable. 
Furthermore, due to life insurers’ often very long-run obliga-
tions, even small changes to the calculation of long-term risk-
free interest rates may have a big impact on their solvency po-
sition. All technical parameters that have a significant effect on 
the solvency situation and, thus, also to the sector’s contribu-
tion to European objectives such as the Green Deal should 
therefore be decided on by the Co-Legislators. 
To this end, the new extrapolation method and its convergence 
parameter must be specified in the Directive. This also holds 
for the residual volume criterion (percentage value of 6%) for 
deep, liquid and transparent markets and the first smoothing 
point (Euro: FSP=20) for the first day of application of the 
amending Directive. 
77a (2) Extrapolation We welcome the envisaged linear transitional mechanism 
starting at 20 %. 
There should be such a transitional mechanism for the euro in 
any case (“shall” instead of “may”). 
However, the transition mechanism cannot be a substitute for 
a sensible permanent parameterization. The modelling of long-
term interest rates is not a temporary issue that would only af-
fect the legacy insurance portfolio, which is also only slowly 
shrinking. New insurance products are affected by long-term 
interest rates as well, as consumers still need guaranteed life-
time benefits for old-age provision. 
77d (3) Volatility 
adjustment 
We welcome most of the envisaged amendments to the volatil-
ity adjustment (VA), in particular the increase of the application 
ratio to 0.85% and the non-recognition of the liquidity factor. 
However, if in the VA calculation the risk correction would be 
based on a percentage of current spreads, the volatility-damp-
ening effect of the VA may not be sufficiently effective in criti-
cal market situations. Therefore, with a view on financial stabil-
ity, this change is not supported. Instead, the current risk cor-
rection should best be maintained. Alternatively, the percent-
age factors applicable in times of a sharp widening of spreads 
should at least be significantly lower.

122 Volatility

We welcome that the use of a dynamic volatility adjustment in

Internal models are a key element of the Solvency II frame-

132 (6) Prudent  
Person  
Principle 
We agree that insurers have to take macroeconomic and fi-
nancial developments into account in their investment deci-
sions. However, in our understanding, this is already the case 
under the current provisions: PPP requires insurers to invest 
their entire capital in a way that security, quality, liquidity and 
profitability of the portfolio as a whole are ensured. This im-
plies that all observable systemic risks which could have a ma-
terial impact on their business have to be considered in an in-
surer’s investment strategies. In our view, the proposed addi-
tional requirements go too far and could impair the principle-
based approach of the PPP. 
In particular, the new additional requirement that insurers have 
to assess the extent to which their investment strategy may af-
fect macroeconomic and financial markets’ developments and 
have the potential to turn into sources of systemic risk, and 
that they also have to incorporate such considerations as part 
of their investment decisions should be removed.  
Given the very limited systemic contribution of individual insur-
ers’ investment decisions and the diverse range of investment 
strategies employed across Europe, the potential benefits of 
this requirement seem small and do not justify the substantial 
costs involved. What is more, the feasibility of this exercise is 
questionable. For the individual company it will be very difficult, 
if not impossible, to determine its potential contribution to 
sources of systemic risk. Also, the necessary balance between 
profitability, liquidity and security at the portfolio level of the in-
dividual insurer could be disrupted if insurers try to somehow 
internalise the system-wide impact of their decisions, even if 
this leads to a sub-optimal outcome from their individual point 
of view.

78 Balance of the 
proposal 
The presented figures suggest a balanced or even positive out-
come for capital requirements. However, our analyses do not 
confirm a balanced outcome (or even a capital relief) for all na-
tional markets and for different market situations (all changes 
fully applied). This is reinforced by the fact that the net effect of 
the proposal results from different positive and negative effects 
that are much larger. Small changes/misestimations of these op-
posing effects may, thus, result in big changes of the net effect.

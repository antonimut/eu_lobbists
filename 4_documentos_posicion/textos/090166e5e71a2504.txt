Position paper on the EU Commission's proposal for a revision 
of the Waste Shipment Regulation (WSR) 
 
 
Dear Ladies and Gentlemen,  
 
First of all, we would like to express our gratitude towards the Commission and thank them 
for the opportunity to voice our opinion on the draft revision of the Waste Shipment Regulation 
(WSR). 
  
I. General 
 
The three German recycling associations bvse, BDSV and VDM issue a joint statement         
below. 
 
The bvse - Bundesverband Sekundärrohstoffe und Entsorgung e.V. (German Association for 
Secondary Raw Materials and Waste Disposal) - is the leading industry umbrella organization 
representing the interests of around 1.000 mainly medium-sized waste management and re-
cycling companies from Germany and Europe. Our qualified environmental service providers 
employ about 50.000 emploryees with a total annual turnover of 10 billion Euros. Moreover, 
the bvse represents and is proficient in all areas of the recycling, secondary raw materials 
and waste management sectors. 
 
The Verband Deutscher Metallhändler e.V. (VDM) represents the entire non-ferrous metal 
trade as the federal association of non-ferrous metal wholesalers and the non-ferrous metal 
recycling industry. Its approximately 230 member companies represent about 80% of the 
metal market in Germany and Austria.  
 
BDSV- Bundesvereinigung Deutscher Stahlrecycling- und Entsorgungsunternehmen e.V. is 
the largest steel recycling association in Europe. The BDSV represents the interests of more 
than 700 member companies in the industry. The main task of the steel recycling companies 
includes the production of quality-assured recycling raw materials for the processing industry 
(steel mills and foundries) in Germany and worldwide. 
 
II. Preliminary remark 
 
With the revision of the WSR, the Commission is pursuing two declared objectives for the 
protection of the environment and human health: combating illegal shipments of waste and 
improving the internal market for secondary raw materials.

Both objectives are expressly supported and endorsed by the German recycling industry. 
 
This recycling industry is a crucial part of the circular economy. It is essential for environmen-
tal protection, human health and the achievement of the goals of the Green Deal.  
 
The severe restrictions on waste shipments of non-hazardous treated waste, such as metal 
scrap, paper, cardboard, etc., outside Europe as intended by the COM will lead to a massive 
disruption of the international circular economy, which is based on a global trade in raw ma-
terials from recycling. 
 
Even if this may have been presented differently to the Commission, e.g. by the lobbying 
associations of the steel manufacturers, the reason why the resources of non-hazardous 
waste in particular do not remain within Europe is not that the recycling industry would prefer 
shipment outside Europe. The decisive factor is rather that the domestic market alone cannot 
absorb the raw materials produced by recycling. In the case of recovered paper and metal 
scrap, for example, the annual surplus in the EU amounts to several million tons. 
 
The steel industry still prefers to manufacture products from primary raw materials. The mas-
sive restriction of the possibilities to ship non-hazardous waste to countries outside Europe 
does not lead to an increased use of secondary raw materials within Europe, but to an over-
supply with a corresponding drop in prices within Europe, as long as no corresponding re-
ceiving capacities are available.  
 
The revision of the WSR to make export more difficult is, in our opinion, the wrong way to 
promote the use of raw materials from recycling within the EU. Instead, there are many other 
possibilities in the form of market-based incentives (support for recycling companies, CO2 
pricing, etc.). 
 
The WSR should pursue the goal of globally protecting the environment and human health 
and promoting the circular economy.  
 
For this, however, it is necessary not to tighten the legal shipment of non-hazardous waste 
such as metal scrap, paper, cardboard, etc. without necessity.  
 
It is imperative to distinguish between hazardous and non-hazardous as well treated and 
untreated waste. 
 
The proposed revision therefore urgently requires some amendments and clarifications. 
 
III. On the draft in detail 
 
1. Art. 7 (Financial guarantee or equivalent insurance) 
 
We see the need for an obligation to provide a guarantee in order to ensure that waste that 
has not been properly treated can be properly disposed of.  
 
 
 
3 
 
 
 
 
 
We expressly welcome the fact that this is now to be done by means of a harmonized calcu-
lation procedure. However, we would like to see a prompt solution here.  
 
Nevertheless, the problem remains that large lump sums (up to 1 million euros) are tied up 
for a longer period and are not available to the companies.  
 
This withdrawal of liquidity can severely restrict future investments and the development of 
day-to-day business, especially for medium-sized companies.  
 
Unfortunately, it is currently not possible for recycling companies in most European countries 
to take out insurance-based financial guarantees that cover this risk. The only positive excep-
tion to this is Flanders, Belgium. 
 
Here, a harmonization should be urgently carried out. 
 
Therefore, we propose the following legislative amendment to Article 7: 
 
     Member States shall consider the possibility for operators to establish fair    
     insurance-based schemes for the financial guarantee obligations referred to  
     in this article. 
      
     In cases where no schemes are in place, the Member States are encouraged 
     to work with insurance companies to develop mature insurance systems. The  
     Member States coordinate this work with other Member States in order to faci- 
     litate harmonized facilitate. 
 
In addition, since such regulation should be prompt, the period of two years after the entry 
into force of the Regulation provided for in Article 7 (10) should be reduced to one year.  
 
2. Art. 11 (1) (prohibition of shipments of waste destined for disposal)  
 
The preventive ban on intra-European shipments of waste for disposal is critical. Strict regu-
lations on shipments for disposal have also been in place to date, which have prevented 
recoverable waste from being shipped for disposal. Even in a circular economy, waste is 
generated that must be disposed of in an environmentally sound manner. Not every member 
state has the appropriate and obvious infrastructure to do this. In Europe, it should generally 
be possible to treat waste where it makes the most economic and ecological sense. 
 
3. Art. 14 (Pre-consented recovery facilities) 
 
We expressly welcome the planned procedure of prior consent for recovery facilities by the 
competent authorities and the mutual recognition of all EU Member States. This can acceler-
ate shipments to waste recovery facilities that are operated according to quality standards.  
  
 
 
 
 
4 
 
 
 
 
 
However, we see both Article 14 (7) and Article 14 (10) as problematic.  
 
Both clauses are too general and unsubstantiated, so that there is a lack of legal certainty for 
the operators of the recovery facilities.  
 
We therefore propose: 
 
Art.14 (7): 
 
Art. 14 (7) should be deleted.  
 
It is not explained which criteria the authority may use as a basis for refusing prior authorisa-
tion. A purely subjective conviction cannot be considered, if only because of the rule of law. 
If it is a fact-based conviction, the facts on which the authority may base its conviction would  
have to be specified. 
 
All plants operating in the EU are authorised under relevant EU law and accordingly also meet 
the requirements for high-quality treatment. 
 
The paragraph is therefore superfluous. If the conditions for prior consent have changed, 
there is still the possibility of revocation under Article 14(10). 
 
Art. 14 (10): 
 
The prerequisites for revocation would have to be specified here. The decision of an operator 
to make investments on the basis of the prior consent requires legal certainty that the prior 
consent can only be revoked for well-founded factual reasons.  
 
We therefore propose the following legal wording:  
 
A pre-consent of a recovery facility may be revoked by the competent authority after prior 
consultation of the operator if 
 
a)  
the information that led to the pre-approval is subsequently found to be incorrect or has 
changed; 
 
b) 
there is evidence that the entity concerned has breached the conditions for pre-consent set 
out in Article 14(6); 
 
c) 
there is evidence that the entity has engaged in illegal activities; 
 
d) 
the facility poses a risk to the environment and human health for other reasons.

In case of imminent danger, the revocation of the prior consent may take place without hear-
ing the person concerned.  
 
The operator has 14 days to object to the revocation. 
 
4. Art. 18 (General information requirements) 
 
Depending on the design of the electronic reporting system, the provisions could lead to a 
considerable additional burden. 
 
The notification of green-listed waste one day before shipment within the EU internal market 
represents a considerable bureaucratic burden that is not objectively justified.  
 
This counteracts the desired goal of simplifying the EU internal market. Improvements must 
be made here. 
 
5. Art. 21 (Public access to notifications) 
 
There is an urgent need for a more detailed explanation of how the planned publication is to 
ensure that business secrets are not made accessible to an unlimited number of people with-
out being filtered.  
 
Business secrets must be protected. Publication can therefore only take place in anonymous 
form. The use of names/company names is not acceptable. 
 
6. Art. 26 (Electronic submission and exchange of information) 
 
In principle, the introduction of an electronic system is welcomed.  
 
However, experience has shown that it takes some time until such an electronic system is 
technically faultless and all Member States and the companies concerned are able to use it 
properly.  
  
It should therefore remain possible for a transitional period of 5 years to submit the data both 
in the electronic system and in the previous form. This would also prevent e.g. a system 
failure/overload of the electronic system from bringing waste shipments to a standstill. 
 
7. Art. 28 (Disagreement on classifications issues)  
 
a) Art. 28 (1) 
 
Art. 28(1) provides that in the event of differences between Member States (classification of 
waste/product, green/yellow waste, etc.), the classification subject to the stricter rules shall 
prevail. 
 
 
 
 
6 
 
 
 
 
 
However, in order to prevent complications for shipments, the current regulations must be 
retained for the EoW criterion (e.g. for FE scrap +Alu EU 333/2011, copper EU 715/2013 and 
glass EU 1179/2012).  
 
Provided that shipments of green-listed waste comply with the EoW criteria of the Member 
States, which are in line with Art. 6 of the Waste Framework Directive and have been notified 
by the European Commission, these criteria must be recognised throughout the EU.  
 
This mutual recognition is an important principle of the internal market that should be upheld. 
 
We therefore propose to amend Art. 28 as follows: 
 
...  
 
If the competent authorities of dispatch and destination cannot agree on the classification as 
regards the distinction between waste and non-waste, the object or substance shall be treated 
as if it were waste for the purpose of the shipment, except, when national end of waste 
criteria exist in accordance with Article 6(3) of Directive 2008/98/EC. 
 
b) Art.28 (4) 
 
We consider the regulation in Art. 28 para. 4 to be extremely problematic. Paragraph 4 could 
lead to restrictions on the EU's internal trade in green-listed waste. We reject the setting of 
"contamination thresholds" through delegated acts, as the introduction of further "contamina-
tion thresholds" is counterproductive. The process of the start-up guidelines No. 12 has al-
ready shown how difficult it is to set a practical limit value. In addition, a large proportion of 
intra-EU shipments are already subject to standardisation requirements that include threshold 
values. 
 
c) Addition to Annex IIIB 
 
Art. 28 para. 4 refers to Annex IIIB. We consider entry B3030 "Textile waste" to be problem-
atic. This needs to be supplemented or concretised. 
 
Clothing and accessories are among the typical items to be found in container collections and 
which are expressly requested to be handed in. However, these are not mentioned in entry 
B3030, so that the shipment of "clothing, accessories and footwear" as total waste for sorting 
and subsequent reuse would be stopped as an illegal shipment of waste.  
 
Annex IIIB of the Waste Shipment Regulations would therefore need to be amended to in-
clude used footwear and accessories and mixtures of used footwear, accessories, worn-out 
garments and other worn-out textile products within the scope and as potential future entries 
of the Basel Convention. 
 
Here we propose the following additions to Annex IIIB:

8. Art. 37-Art.40 (Export of waste to non-OECD countries) 
 
In their intended implementation, Art. 37- 40 lead in practice to a complete ban on exports of 
non-hazardous waste to non-OECD countries. 
 
The creation of a "list of countries to which exports are permitted" on the basis of complicated 
and bureaucratic verification procedures leads us to fear that this will bring the existing and 
well-functioning markets for secondary raw materials to a standstill and also make it more 
difficult to initiate new business. As an example, we can mention the review of the so-called  
State List and the amendment of EU Regulation 1418/2007, which came into force on 10 
November 2021. The update and review by the Commission took years and ultimately led to 
a provisional ban on exports of recovered paper to India due to a pure oversight. Similar 
concerns can be expressed with regard to the planned list for non-OECD countries, especially 
since the requirements are much higher. 
 
Furthermore, we assume that excessive requirements for non-OECD countries, such as the 
preparation or disclosure of a national waste management plan, will be perceived as exces-
sive and lead to these countries not reporting back. A complex questionnaire that must be 
filled out in English is also likely to create a further hurdle that burdens business relations. 
 
These requirements also represent an encroachment on the sovereignty of third countries. 
 
We expressly support measures that are suitable for ensuring that waste problems from the 
EU are not transferred to third countries and endanger the environment and human health 
there. We also support all measures that are suitable to prevent illegal waste shipments.  
 
However, there is no objective reason why non-hazardous, unproblematic, treated waste 
(metals, scrap metal, paper, cardboard, etc.) should be subject to such strict requirements. 
As explained above, the intended export restriction, which is equivalent to an export ban, 
would not lead to an increased use of raw materials from recycling within Europe. Demand 
from EU producers is insufficient or saturated, and recycling content targets for all waste 
streams (except for some polymers under the SEA Directive) do not exist. However, the de-
mand for these raw materials in other countries (e.g. Indian paper industry) is very high. In-
stead of leaving secondary raw materials in the EU as an unused surplus, it is in the interest  
 
8 
 
 
 
 
 
of an international circular economy to organise the shipment of non-hazardous processed 
waste to non-OECD countries in such a way that this is also practically possible. 
 
It is therefore imperative to distinguish between hazardous and non-hazardous treated waste. 
 
In Art. 39 (1) the sentence: 
 
"This application and any related documents or other communications shall be submitted in 
English." 
 
should be deleted. 
 
According to Art. 27, EU companies have the right to request documents in the language in 
which they operate. However, to then require non-OECD countries to submit all documents 
in English represents an unjustifiable unequal treatment of non-OECD countries. It is techni-
cally possible and also reasonable that the documents of non-OECD states that are submitted 
electronically in case of doubt are automatically translated into English. 
 
9. Art. 41 to 46 (Export of waste to OECD countries)   
 
The waste restrictions for shipments to OECD countries are also too sweeping and general. 
Here, too, the necessary distinction between hazardous and non-hazardous treated waste is 
lacking. 
 
a) Art. 42 (Monitoring of export und safeguard procedure) 
 
We have strong reservations as to whether Art. 42 is consistent with the OECD Council De-
cision and the regulations on free trade in raw materials contained therein.  
 
Particularly problematic is the provision in Art. 42 para. 2, according to which the EU Com-
mission reserves the right to intervene "within a short period of time" in the case of an increase 
in exports to an OECD country if there is "insufficient evidence" that the state in question is 
capable of recycling this waste in an environmentally sound manner.  
 
What is meant by "within a short time" is not explained, nor is the question of what evidence 
would be considered sufficient. This leads to unacceptable legal uncertainty and impairs the 
conclusion of contracts. 
 
In addition, Article 42(1) should limit monitoring exclusively to problematic, untreated waste. 
Only such waste poses a risk to the environment and human health.  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
9 
 
 
 
 
 
b) Art.43 (Obligations on exporters)  
 
aa) 
 
According to Art. 43 para. 2, the installations must be audited by an independent qualified 
third party. In principle, this is to be welcomed in order to ensure essentially equivalent envi-
ronmental standards. However, unrealistic and excessive requirements must not be placed 
on the third country. The self-sufficiency of existing quality assurance systems in the recipient 
country must not be undermined.  
 
Annex X No. 2 to Art. 43 Para. 2 is particularly problematic in this context. We regard the 
application of the BAT regulations, which are purely European regulations, as very critical. 
These provisions are not even harmonised within the EU. Extending them to OECD countries 
is tantamount to an export ban in practice.  
 
For this reason, Annex X No. 2 must be amended or deleted. 
 
bb) 
 
Article 43 (8) provides for an exemption from auditing if an international agreement is con-
cluded between the Union and an OECD country in which it is recognised that the facilities in 
this third country manage waste in accordance with Annex X in an environmentally sound 
manner.  
 
What is lacking here is a concrete explanation of what is required for such an agreement to 
come into being. Will the EU actively approach all OECD countries to conclude such agree-
ments? What concrete requirements will be placed on the respective OECD state for the 
conclusion of an agreement? 
 
Here, too, additional explanations are necessary. 
 
10. Art.60 (Penalties) 
 
When sanctioning violations of the WSR, a distinction must be made between illegal waste 
shipments that endanger the environment and human health, and mere typing/error in filling 
out the forms, which have no practical effect.  
 
The regulations are very bureaucratic and complex and therefore prone to error. Any typing 
or carelessness error, no matter how small, when filling in the forms constitutes an infringe-
ment that could result in heavy penalties. This, however, is not in the interests of the parties. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
10 
 
 
 
 
 
Art. 60 (s) should therefore be amended as follows: 
 
A penalty shall not be imposed, if the violation is an administrative 
error, that has no practical effect and cannot lead to a risk to the envi-
ronment or human health.

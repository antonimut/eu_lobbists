Submission by the Pew Charitable Trusts to the EC Call for Evidence “Microplastics pollution – 
measures to reduce its impact on the environment” 
 
The Pew Charitable Trusts is a global research and public policy organization, focused on 
improving public policy through scientifically backed solutions. We provide technical expertise, 
develop and manage research projects, and communicate scientific results to inform 
conservation policy and the sustainable management of natural resources. In the last few years, 
Pew has established itself as an expert on plastic emissions through the publication of several 
in-depth scientific reports. 
  
Pew has also been a keen participant in the EU microplastics stakeholder consultations. We 
commend the EU for undertaking this important work and for taking stakeholder input so 
seriously. It is our hope that the EU’s consultations on microplastics lead to effective policies 
that drastically reduce the flow of microplastics into the environment from tyres, textiles, and 
pellets.  
  
In collaboration with SYSTEMIQ, University of Oxford, University of Leeds, Ellen MacArthur 
Foundation, Common Seas, and an expert panel, Pew conducted a global analysis of the plastic 
pollution problem through robust scientific and data-driven process. As published in Breaking 
the Plastic Wave and the accompanying peer-reviewed article in the journal Science 
(“Evaluating scenarios towards zero plastic pollution”), upstream solutions are vital to reducing 
the plastic problem and at-source reductions of unintentional microplastics. In these areas 
policy can have the biggest impact. In our view, it will be critical to establish viable links 
between the policy options and the realised reductions of microplastic pollution using a 
scientific approach to measuring success. Below we outline our research and recommended 
approach to solving the plastic problem.  
  
Urgent action needed now  
 
The time for urgent global action to halt the rapid growth of plastic pollution is now. According 
to our research, the annual flow of plastic entering the ocean will nearly triple by 2040. Even if 
all current major industry and government commitments are met, these measures are likely to 
reduce annual plastic leakage to the ocean by only around 7 per cent in 2040 relative to our 
projections.   
  
There is no single solution to halt this growing flow of plastics, rather a systemic change 
throughout the whole life cycle of plastics is needed. Applying existing knowledge and 
technologies, about 80 per cent of the plastic waste going into the ocean can be reduced by 
2040 along with a 25 per cent reduction in plastic-related greenhouse gas emissions.  
  
In 2020, sixteen organizations representing businesses and civil society shared a Joint 
Statement on Preventing Ocean Plastic Pollution that presented a roadmap of actions based on 
Ref. Ares(2022)349360 - 17/01/2022
Breaking the Plastic Wave. This roadmap lays out a concurrent focus on 6 areas over the next 
20 years that can help achieve the goal of near-zero plastic pollution and reduced related 
green-house emissions:  
  
• Reducing plastic production and consumption  
• Designing all products and packaging—whether made of plastic or a substitute 
material—to be reused, recycled, or composted  
• Improving and creating new waste management services,   
• Restricting and regulating transboundary movements of plastic waste  
• Eliminating maritime sources and other sector-specific sources of plastic pollution   
• Tackling microplastic pollution  
  
This Joint Statement emphasised the importance of “Tackling microplastic pollution by using 
alternative materials and developing improved standards and capture technologies to reduce 
microplastic release from tyres and textiles, setting mandatory supply chain standards to 
prevent pellet loss, and eliminating microplastic ingredients in products that might be released 
into the environment.”   
  
Pew’s evidence-based approach  
 
Research conducted and supported by Pew adheres to the highest level of scientific rigour. The 
global plastic analysis detailed in Breaking the Plastic Wave was steered by a panel of 17 
experts representing diverse geographies and experiences across the full plastics value chain, in 
conjunction with input by more than 100 additional experts, as well as peer reviewed through 
the scientific publication process.   
  
In Breaking the Plastic Wave, we modelled four microplastic sources representing an estimated 
75 to 85 per cent of microplastic leakage: tyre abrasion, pellet loss, textile microfibers, and 
microplastic ingredients in personal care products. Global microplastic leakage into the 
environment is currently estimated at 4 million metric tons and is expected to increase to 10 
million metric tons by 2040.  This increase is driven by population growth, more vehicles per 
capita, increased consumption and production of plastic-based textiles, growing usage of 
personal care products containing microplastic ingredients, and rising plastic pellet production. 
We estimate that today 26 per cent of all microplastics end up as leakage to the ocean and an 
additional 63 per cent of releases end up leaking into other environments, including soil and air, 
with only 11% managed through collection and disposal or incineration. However, solutions 
exist today that could reduce microplastic leakage by about 60 per cent over the next 20 years 
– including a range of policy solutions such as investing in innovation and redesign of tires and 
textiles, mandating standards, or improving product labelling for consumers.  
  
Pew has also supported research in the plastic policy space. An analysis by Duke University 
synthesized the policy responses of governments in the report “20 years of Government 
Responses to the Global Plastic Pollution Problem”. Although the most frequently used policy 
instrument to date has been plastic bag bans, in recent years more policies are emerging that 
look at comprehensive approaches across multiple stages of plastic product lifecycles (with an 
increase from 25% of policies addressing multiple stages in 2000-2005 to 59% in 2018). The 
report also notes that regional and national level policies focused on microplastics are still 
relatively new and therefore rare.   
  
Engagement with the EU stakeholder consultation  
 
Pew has convened 7 microplastics experts to update the microplastics analysis initiated in its 
Breaking the Plastic Wave study. In addition to the four sources of microplastic losses from 
tires, textiles, pellets, and personal care products, the new analysis will include new data on 
paint, an important but overlooked source of microplastics. This work highlights microplastic 
loss pathways, estimates the magnitude of loss across microplastic sources, and assesses the 
impact of potential solutions on reducing microplastics in the environment. Pew will submit this 
work for publication in a peer-reviewed scientific journal at the end of Q1 2022.  
 
Pew is also contracting further research into tyres with Emissions Analytics, a leading 
independent global testing and data specialist for the scientific measurement of real-world 
emissions. Emissions Analytics will discern the chemical composition of up to 100 tyres. This 
research will give us more information on the potential environmental and health impacts of 
tyre wear particles. Emissions Analytics has also tested several brands to estimate their tyre 
wear rate in real world conditions. Understanding the chemical fingerprint of tyres and tyre 
wear rates would help to distinguish tyre wear from road wear particles.  
 
We hope the insights from our analysis and research can inform an evidence-based EC 
consultation process.  
  
Moving forward  
 
Plastic pollution presents an enormous and growing threat to the health of our communities 
and ecosystems. We recognise that addressing a problem of this size and consequence is a 
formidable task. Pew’s evidence-based approach, technical expertise, and experience in plastics 
policy research can contribute to a clear data-driven process for developing microplastics policy 
at the EU. With a focus on science-based measures, the EU can become a global leader in 
reducing microplastics at the source. We look forward to contributing our microplastics 
research from our upcoming scientific publication and tyre research from Emissions Analytics to 
the European Commission consultation process and partner with the EC in this and future 
consultations.

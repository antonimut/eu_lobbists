Eurits [the European Union for the Responsible Incineration and Treatment of Special waste] welcomes 
the revision of the Waste Shipment Regulation (WSR) (Regulation 1013/2006).  As we have previously 
commented during the evaluation of the WSR, Eurits believes that in general the WSR is a good piece of 
legislation which does not need fundamental revision but rather consistent implementation and 
enforcement. The primary aim of the legislation is to set out a series of nece ssary controls for the 
protection of the environment when shipping wastes across borders – this is largely achieved through 
ensuring that wastes are shipped safely to the right treatment facilities. 
 
Major concerns regarding the Commission’s proposed new Articles 4 and 11 
 
Eurits has  major concerns about the revised Articles 4 and 11 , which would completely change the 
framework for waste management across the EU and go against what is stated in the Waste Framework 
Directive and the principles of the European Green Deal. Circular Economy, Zero-Pollution and Climate 
Neutrality are the three competin g objectives of the European Green Deal  which need to be  treated 
with equal consideration . The Hazardous Waste Incineration sector plays a key role in realising and 
balancing these objectives: 
• The Circular Economy - by removing hazardous substances from the recycling cycles (process of 
decontamination)  
• Zero Pollution and Climate Neutrality  - by d estroying hazardous organic compounds and 
eliminating/irreversibly transforming hazardous inorganic compounds while respecting the best 
available techniques and avoiding greenhouse gas emissions.  
 
For the past 20+ years the EU has rightly encouraged more sophisticated waste management facilities 
(recovery and disposal) appropriately following the waste hierarchy. The result has been that a network 
of waste management facilities has been developed wi th an understanding that more specialised 
treatment facilities may not have the critical mass of particular waste streams to justify one facility in 
each EU Member State – the result has been an EU-wide network of waste management facilities. 
 
Some EU Member States may have recovery facilities and other EU Member States may have disposal 
facilities.  The impact of the changes introduced in the new Articles 4 and 11 is that EU Member States 
with insufficient disposal facilities (of which there will always be  some need whilst fully respecting the 
waste hierarchy) will be penalised and find it almost impossible to move their waste to an EU Member 
State with the appropriate disposal facility. 
 
It is our belief that the WSR is not the appropriate piece of legislation to re-interpret the waste hierarchy.  
Waste holders are already compelled to follow the waste hierarchy by the W aste Framework Directive 
with use of disposal facilities allowed where appropriate and necessary.

In the current WSR Articles 3 and  11, the framework is set out such that the procedures for shipping 
waste to disposal facilities  are laid out in Art icle 3 along with a series of reasons that an EU Member 
State can reject that notification as set out in Article 11. In the new Article 4 there is a default prohibition 
on exports of waste for disposal within the EU – this goes against what is allowed by the waste hierarchy 
and dramatically changes the ability to export for disposal.  Using the example of disposal of hazardous 
waste, e.g. POPs in a high temperature incinerator, not all EU Member States have a suitable treatment 
facility for POPs (which are not permitted to be recovered) so would find the new Art icle 4 conditions 
prohibitively difficult to comply with. 
 
The new Art icle 11 follows on with the reverse of the current rules by requiring that any shipments 
allowed by exception to Article 4 must comply with all the conditions of Article 11 (in the current Article 
11 the list is a set of reasons for objection).  The current Art icle 11 allows Member States discretion in 
how to apply the requirements appropriately, whereas the revised Article 11 allows no such discretion 
and contains some very onerous requirements. We strongly support the ability of the Member States to 
provide well -justified reasons for rejecting shipments, however the revised Art icle 11 provides no 
discretion and would almost certainly lead to no waste management company in the EU ever being able 
to ship waste for disposal within the EU. 
 
For example, in the proposed Article 11, point 1, paragraph (a), it should be for the Member States not 
the notifier to assess whether there is suitable capacity in the country of origin. A notifier is, in general, 
an economic operator and not an administration or a cou ntry representative – it is not always realistic 
for a notifier to be aware of all the possibilities within a Member State.  In the same point in paragraph 
(b) there is no qualification for seriousness of offence , e.g. a notification document incorrectly filled in 
by accident is technically an illegal shipment and placed on the same level as deliberate and intentional 
criminality.  Without the ability of the Member States to exercise discretion on this requirement it wi ll 
be almost impossible to ship waste for disposal (and not all wastes can be recovered).

Also, the consequence of a conviction is not limited in time, thus  a notifier or a consignee who has 
been convicted of any of the illegal acts defined in this article can be indefinitely banned from 
transferring or receiving waste. We believe that this is very strict and not justified and suggest adding 
a time limit, e.g. ‘the past two years’.

Annex IB - Movement document for transboundary movements/shipments of EU waste (bloc 17) 
 
 
Justification: 
‘Accepting waste’ or ‘receiving waste’ does not have the same meaning. When a facility ‘receives 
waste’ it implies that the waste is weighed and ready for the next phase, which is the acceptance 
procedure.  Including [Received] in Annex IB bloc 17 would make it consistent with the 
terminology used in Article 16, which means that the waste has arrived at the facility but has not 
yet been unloaded, sampled, analysed and accepted. The ‘receiving’ procedure needs to be 
differentiated from the ‘accepting’ procedure due to the complex analytical acceptance procedure 
following best available techniques as described in the Waste Incineration BREF.  
 
The time limit has been changed in the new Article 16 from 3 days to 1 day. However, often the 
acceptance procedure (as described above) does not take place on the day of receipt, thus it is 
important to ensure consistency with what is happening in practice and use the right terminology. 
Within 1 day, reception can be completed but not always the acceptance. 
 
This is of utmost importance as the compliance to Article 16 (and the fulfilment of the movement 
document) is a criterion in article 11 and 12.

 Who is EURITS? 
• The European Union for the Responsible Incineration & Treatment of Special 
waste  
• An association of hazardous waste management companies across the EU 
• Eurits’ members have a total capacity of high temperature incineration of around 
3Mt/y; this represents 90-95% of the total capacity in Europe 
 
 Main activities 
• Make sure hazardous waste is handled, managed and treated very specifically and 
in a correct manner: 
o To avoid any dispersion of hazards or contaminants into the environment 
o To ensure that the environment and public health are protected 
o To ensure that recovered materials are reliable  
 
 Key role 
• The EU’s leading voice on hazardous waste 
• Over 25 years of experience in hazardous waste treatment 
• Consistent promotion of best sustainable environmental practice 
• Representing the special waste treatment industry in the EU Parliament and 
Commission, in order to create adequate support and policy frameworks in 
Europe.

SUBJECT: EBF feedback to DG FISMA on the Banking Package proposal 
The European Banking Federation (EBF) welcomes the proposal of the European 
Commission for the review of the Capital Requirements Regulation and Directive in the 
context of the Banking Package.  
The EBF provides feedback in this document about the  Commission’s proposal for the 
Banking Package from the perspective of the EBF position on Basel III that has been largely 
discussed in the past. The objectives of the EBF position remain the same:  
− To implement the fundamentals of the Basel text in Europe 
− To do it without significantly increasing capital requirements 
− To take into account key European specificities 
− To pursue an integrated and efficient banking market in Europe, for the benefit of 
citizens, businesses and public sector 
The EBF feedback is organized in t hree parts: firstly, the EBF Basel III top-10 topics that 
have been largely discussed in the past years where the EBF has a longstanding position; 
secondly, the rest of Basel III items; thirdly, the additional topics included in the Banking 
Package, some of which are commented on within the limits of the have -your-say period 
and the availability of previous EBF positions on each topic. This feedback is not 
comprehensive, and the level of detail is limited. Further issues and greater details will be 
provided during the legislative process. Our comments are numbered for convenience in 
future discussions, however the numbers and the order of presentation do not necessarily 
imply priority. 
The Banking Package should strike the right balance between meeting the fundamentals 
of the Basel Committee reform, keeping the capital requirements for banks without any 
significant increase as mandated by the G20 and reiterated by the European Parliament 
and the European C ouncil and, also, observing key European specificities.  The 
Commission’s proposal intends to bala nce those objectives however many aspects will 
require careful examination by co-legislators and the wider scope of stakeholders affected, 
including banks, businesses and citizens. The impact of the Banking Package is said to be 
between 6.4% and 8.4% by 20 30. Nevertheless, such an estimate is uncertain due to 
various factors: the approach used to quantify the increase in capital requirements , the 
numerous technical regulatory standards (RTS) requested to the EBA  and the temporary 
nature of the transitional arrangements. Also, effects on specific low risk portfolios cannot 
be ruled out even in if a bank already meets the updated requirements.  
The Commission has used the shortfall approach 1 to estimate the impact on capital 
requirements, which results in e uro 27 billion. But the actual impact is the amount of 
capital that all European banks will need to raise in order to restore the current capital 
ratios after implementing Basel III , including the P2G and the safety headroom over the 
minimum requirement which represent s the creditworthiness of our banks . Such a

EBF TOP-10 BASEL III TOPICS  
1. For the sake of European banking market integration and efficiency, the output floor 
should be applied at consolidated level in each banking group.  
The effect of the output floor in Europe is exacerbated due to the size and scope of EU 
gold-plating requirements . Apart from the capital buffers required by the Basel 
Committee standards 2, the EU Capital Requirements Directive (CRD) and Regulation 
(CRR) include sizeable additional buffer requirements upon discretion of Member 
States and national authorities. Another European gold-plating requirement, the MREL, 
is based on risk-weighted assets, therefore it will be inflated due to the output floor. 
The EBF recommends applying the output floor only to the global BCBS requirements 
that apply in other jurisdictions, not to the gold-plating ones.  
The Commission indicates an issue of double counted risks due to the output floor. 
To solve this pr oblem, the proposal relies on supervisors and national authorities to 
reconsider the appropriate level of pillar 2 and systemic risk buffer, respectively. We 
think that double counting should be avoided by regulation, not left to national 
discretion. Moreover, the Commission’s position that only the model risk component 
should be considered when assessing double counting of risks is too narrow, as the 
identification of risk drivers in the buffers is not always clear. A more thorough analysis 
of double counted risks should be done for each Member State level and a common 
approach should be agreed to achieve a level playing field.

The proposal mitigates temporarily the effect of the output floor via transitional 
arrangements. While this measure can alleviate the short -term impact, it does not

provide relief for portfolios  under the standardized approach. Therefore, permanent

2. Unrated corporates and maintenance of the SME Supporting Factor.  
The solution proposed by the Commission to reduce the impact of the output floor on 
unrated corporates is welcomed, however, the temporary nature of the proposal should 
be reviewed. It could be replaced either with a permanent character or by a clause 
that preserves the transitional arrangement until a condition is met that the vast 
majority3 of European corporate exposures are covered by eligible external credit 
ratings. Moreover, in order to ease access to funding for unrated corporates,  we 
suggest implementing a suitable treatment for these exposures under the standardized 
approach 
The maintenance of the SME supporting factor and the infrastructure supporting factor 
is welcomed. Both instruments are supporting and stabilizing the European economy.  
3. The treatment of real -estate exposures under the standardised framework should 
accurately reflect lending practices with a higher level of granularity and risk 
sensitivity. Mortgage loans in Europe are mainly dual recourse loans for which the 
borrower is personally liable (no walk away option). This justifies that mortgage loans 
show a lower risk profile in the European Union than in other jurisdictions where real -
estate loans are only backed by the collateral provided by the borrower, and thus 
where borrowers are not personally liable.  
The EBF considers the proposal regarding residential real estate  as a feasible 
solution that gives due consideration to the European specificities, like the dual 
recourse to the property and the goods and income of the borrower, which explain the 
extremely low loss rate in the best quality tranches. The proposed treatment should 
be applied to primary as well as secondary residences so long as they mee t the 
conditions and should be extended to exposures under standardized approach too. The 
proposal indicates that the transitional arrangement for low-risk exposures secured by 
mortgages on residential property can be applied upon discretion of the Member State. 
Given the soundness of the arguments, we would argue that it should apply directly in 
all Member States and the transitional arrangement should be made permanent.  
As regards commercial real estate, the EBF recommends adopting one or two LTV 
buckets below 60% in order to achieve an adequate risk sensitivity of the framework  
as the CRSA is still not risk sensitive enough. As a consequence , the necessary 
investments in the sustainable refurbishment of commercial properties are neither 
properly incentivized nor correctly reflected since they actually enhance the value of 
the property and thus reduce risk. 
Lastly, banks should have the option to apply the whole loan or the loan splitting 
approach. 
Overall, we support the option to use the market value or the mortgage lending value 
approach for the valuation and revaluation of the property according to article 129(3). 
However, in our view the existing valuation principles for immovable property in article

5. As regards the CCF framework, we also suggest reviewing the UCC definition to better 
clarify its scope, as, among other things, it currently can be read as only including 
contracts with consumers. In our view, there should not be a limitation of the scope . 
This would also better align with the BCBS definition4. Also, the draft legislation seems 
to require application of CCFs to contracts proposed by the bank, but not yet accepted 
by the client, and arrangements excluded from the definition of “commitments” . We 
recommend deleting or clarifying proposed Article 111 (4), to ensure internal 
consistency of the framework and not to enlarge the scope of CCFs compared to the 
BCBS definition. On specialized lending , we acknowledge that some specific 
enhancements have been introduced into the framework , but concerns remain in this 
area. The risk weights under the Standardised Approach do not match the actual risk, 
and the same applies to the input parameter floors. In this regard, we see room for 
improvement to achieve greater consistency within the new article 495b with respect 
to the input floors, as well as the timing of the phase-in and the mandate given to EBA 
to develop weightings that reflect the robustness of these types of financing. Similarly, 
the slotting approach should be reviewed taking into account the adjustments 
introduced to this asset class, in order to ensure that there is consistency between the 
3 approaches: standardized, slotting and IRB.  
6. Regarding the new standardised approach fo r Operational Risk  we would like to 
remark that this represents a significant simplification of the current regulatory 
framework. Past losses should not be permitted to significantly adversely affect the 
measurement of risk. In our view the ILM should be introduced with a cap at 1.

OTHER TOPICS IN THE BANKING PACKAGE BEYOND BASEL III 
1.  Cross-border banking in the EU and third country branches  
CRD Article 21c proposes that non -EU undertakings must establish an EU branch 
to provide certain services in the EU, except where they are provided by “reverse 
solicitation”. The scope of Article 21c, which is defined by cross-reference to Article 
47(1) and (2), is ambiguous. One interpretation would apply Article 21c widely to 
all firms providing any of the activities listed in CRD Annex I. An alternative 
interpretation would apply it to credit institutions and “Class” 1 investment firms 
only. Either interpretation would have significant impacts for firms in the EU seeking 
to raise capital or invest, as the flow of credit and services into the EU from firms 
without an EU branch would be restricted. These firms currently rely on Member 
State specific cross-border licensing or overseas persons exemption regimes. Also, 
we note that art. 21c read together with art. 47(2) would conflict with the 
MIFIR/MIFID II provisions allowing third country firms to provide services on a 
cross border basis in a given Member State, subject to certain conditions (types of 
clients, equivalence assessment). 
In this context, we would request that the Commission’s policy objectives can also 
be met without restricting service provisions  from firms without an EU 
branch (including allowing Member States to opt for a branch requirement rather 
than i ntroducing a mandatory branch requirement; grandfathered rights for 
existing licensing/exemption regimes). 
We look forward to working with the Commission to clarify the scope of the services 
requiring the establishment of an EU branch and highlight that th is requirement 
may limit access of EU borrowers and investors to the global capital markets.   
2. Minority interests 
The current framework should be amended in order to correctly compute the real 
contribution of minority interests to the consolidated capital, and to address the 
unintended consequences of the current setup.

to pay dividends should be considered a capital requirement in order to calculate

3. Disclosures 
EBA c entral disclosure from 2025 represents a very ambitious goal. We would 
suggest adopting a more flexible date of application in order to avoid the risk of

The application date of central disclosure is not already stipulated in CRR III, but

We support small and non -complex institutions to benefit from less stringent

‘same activities, same risks, same rules’ is applied. 
4. Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) risks 
Banks’ shifting of resources towards a low -carbon economy and engagement with 
customers is key in financing the transition. While banks are fully aware of their 
responsibilities and are committed to play their role, it is not realistic  to expect 
finance to make this shift in the absence of a major change in the incentives of the 
underlying economy.  The Union policy objectives must be transposed in the 
industrial policies and relevant national legal frameworks. Banks should not be put 
in the position of being the primary enforcers of climate policy, neither should 
prudential framework be used as a substitute of direct mechanisms such as taxes 
or industrial measures.  
 
• We fully recognize that ESG factors could be a source of financial risk and therefore 
support the ongoing efforts of banks and regulators to ensure risks stemming from 
such factors are properly identified, understood, managed and supervised. Banks 
have an inherent interest in measuring and managing risks properl y as risk 
management and risk redistribution is core to the banking business. 
 
Management, transformation and absorption of financial risks will be a key element 
in the transformation of the EU economy to net zero as substantial investments 
e.g. in new tec hnologies need to be financed. While banks will be supportive and 
are expected to be aligning their strategies with the EU objectives, the business 
model and business strategy must remain the responsibility of banks’ governing 
bodies.  
 
The power for super visors to “reduce  risks arising from the institutions’ 
misalignment with relevant  policy objectives of the Union and broader transition 
trends relating to ESG factors” does not appear to be aligned with the  definitions 
of ESG and environmental risks to be introduced in the CRR, but instead refers to 
undefined policy objectives and trends. The misalignment with the EU objective or 
any broader transition trend itself should not be considered a risk, while there might 
be risks stemming from such misalignment. Where material risks exist, it must be 
up to the institution to cover or mitigate them in line with their own business 
strategy and risk appetite. The role of supervisors should be in ensuring that such 
risks are considered by the institutions, identified, understood, and managed.

Suitability (arts. 91a-91d) 
• The CRD needs to clarify the link between money laundering and terrorist financing 
and suitability. 
 
Although it is right for the CRD framework to expect ML/FT risk to be analysed by 
competent authorities in the context of institutions' governance arrangements, 
processes and mechanisms (e.g. AML policies), in the context of suitability of 
members of the management body the link should be with the persons themselves, 
and not the entities as a whole. Director’s individual suitability should not be 
assessed in light of material facts or risks of the institution. 
 
• Notification of renewals to competent authorities: no justification is provided  for 
the inclusion of this new obligation, which seems only to add a new layer of 
bureaucracy to the suitability regimes. Where no new suitability assessment is to 
be performed by competent authorities, public disclosure of renewals in entities’ 
websites at the time of submission to AGMs should suffice. 
 
• Key Function Holders: The main drawback of the authorities performing a fit and 
proper assessment of Key Function Holders (“KFH”) is the fact that KFH are 
employees of an institution. Indeed, according to some local labour laws, it is not 
possible to insert a condition precedent (the condition being the approval of the 
ECB/NCA) as a termination provision in the labour agreement for new appointee. 
As regards persons who are already in the institution, it is not possible to terminate 
employment on the ground of no longer being fit and proper. Additionally, the 
assessment of the KFH by supervisors would imply a considerable increase of the 
number of files with no real justification. This is not pro portionate to the objective 
assuming the fact that: 
o The appointment or removal of heads of internal control function is already 
subject to the supervisory function’s approval 
o Entities have to put in place suitability policy covering the assessment of 
this population. This policy is regularly provided to both the ECB and the 
NCA. This should suffice, as the appointment of this population should 
remain at institution level only.

• We are strongly against the assessment of KFH being carried out by the supervisor. 
It is likely to be a highly bureaucratic process and also be in contradiction with 
some national social laws. 
 
• Ex-ante approach: we strongly disagree with the removal of the option for Member 
States to choose between the ex-ante or the ex-post approach in consideration of 
the existing local regulatory framework. Indeed, the alternative allows to ensure 
consistency with the national company laws rules, without prejudice in any way to 
the final objective to have members of the bodies complying wit h fit and proper 
requirements.  
 
Furthermore, ex-post suitability assessments in case of urgency seem to be limited 
to the context of early intervention or resolution, which is not realistic. An entity 
may well have to face other circumstances (sudden illn ess, death or unexpected 
leave) that may make it crucial for it to replace the key person and allow her or 
him to take up the corresponding duties while competent authorities perform, in 
parallel, the corresponding suitability assessment. 
 
• Suitability assessment period: entities cannot be expected to await four months 
(set out in Art 91) before their directors are allowed to take up their positions. Such 
a situation would have profound implications on the day to day running of the 
entity. Members propose a more reasonable 1 -2 months in order to reduce the 
number of cases requiring the ‘urgent’ ex-post exceptions to be applied.   
  
Individual statements and mapping of duties: the allocation of different roles or duties to 
the members of the management body is  inadequate, given that no efficient or real 
separation of responsibilities can be implemented where company law conceives the 
management body as one inseparable (and collegiate) body through which all functions 
are performed. 
 
EBA Guidelines: we suggest a  suppression of the mandate to EBA as it would lead to too 
much red tape. Instead, flexibility is needed in order to take into consideration the 
individual circumstances, the scale and complexity of activities of the institution. We 
emphasise that it is not on the competence of the EBA to add obligations without a legal 
basis. In addition, EBA guidelines would not be necessary when one of the ECB already 
exists. 
 
Entry into force of the Directive: in order to allow a smooth transition, the provisions 
regarding internal governance and fit and proper should apply no earlier than 24 months 
from the date of application of this amending Directive. 
 
 
b) Remuneration:  
 
• Although we welcome the Commission’s efforts to accommodate the CRD’s wording 
to the more commonly used term “staff” instead of “officers”, the change in 
wording, if left as it stands, would have major implications in the persons whose 
remuneration would be overseen by the remunerations committee / management 
body in its supervisory function, and thus we kindly request it be corrected.

• Article 92(2)(f)  requires the Remuneration Committee to directly oversee “the 
remuneration of the senior staff in the internal control functions”. The term “senior 
staff” is unprecise and not appropriate for prudential requirements at all. Based on 
the assumption that point (f) intends to focus on the Heads of the control functions, 
we suggest that point (f) refers to the Heads of internal control functions as defined 
in Article 3, paragraph 1, point (9c) of the Draft CRD VI.  
 
• Separately, in EU member states with a two -tier board structure (e.g., i n 
Germany), the Supervisory Board (and its Remuneration Committee) has no 
decision rights on the remuneration amounts for staff who are not members of the 
management board. Instead, the Remuneration Committee, as a sub-committee of 
the Supervisory Board, oversees the design of the remuneration policy (i.e. not the 
actual amounts) for staff who are not members of the management board. Where 
national law prescribes two -tier board structures, the wording of point (f) should, 
therefore, require the Remuneration  Committee to directly oversee the 
remuneration policy of the heads of the internal control functions . Furthermore: 
Article 92(f) refers to "senior staff", which should be replaced by only referring to 
the heads of internal control function in Article (3) (paragraph 1) point (9C).  
 
• In addition, as the remuneration policy is already approved by the Board of 
Directors, the heads of internal control functions must comply with it when setting 
the remuneration of their staff.

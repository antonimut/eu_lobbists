About Naturvernforbundet: Our NGO was founded in 1914 and is Norway's oldest 
environmental and nature conservation organization. We are democratic and membership 
based and most of our work are done by volunteers, organized in over 100 groups all around the 
country. With 36,000 members Naturvernforbundet plays a significant role as civil society both 
locally and nationally in Norway, and we are internationally part of Friends of the Earth. 
 
General comments: 
- Naturvernforbundet welcomes this initiative. Microplastic pollution poses unacceptable risks 
to the environment and human health, and effective legislation is urgent. Without EU-action, 
risks for human health will increase and consequences for the environment will worsen due to 
microplastic accumulation.   
- There is a need to address toxicity of microplastics additives, not only physical releases.  
- Unintentional releases of microplastics are strongly connected to the production and 
consumption of plastics, the most effective way to tackle emissions would be to regulate 
the supply of plastics.  
 
Legal basis: 
As this initiative aims to first and foremost tackle an environmental problem, the legal basis for this 
action should be art 192 of the TFEU. Measures to tackle pollution should apply prevention and 
precaution as guiding principles.

Tires: 
A study commissioned by the Norwegian environmental Agency on land-based sources of 
microplastic pollution estimated that emissions from car-tires are the single largest source of land-
based microplastic emissions in Norway.1 The same holds true for the other Nordic countries.2 
 
 
Fig: Sundt.et.al 2021, p 6  
 
In general, strategies to reduce microplastic emissions from road traffic should be seen in relation to 
work in other policy areas such as local air quality, climate and environment, and public health as 
there are multiple synergies between and co-benefits from these measures. For instance, reducing road 
traffic and enforcing environmental speed limits will reduce microplastic emissions from tires as well 
as other tire wear particles (TWP) and reducing greenhouse gas emissions.34

The EU should apply a threshold limit for microplastic emissions in the type approval for tires sold on 
the European market, tires performing under the threshold should be restricted. Naturvernforbundet 
recommends increasing the threshold over time. Standardized tests for measuring tire abrasion should 
be developed at the latest by 2023.  
 
Toxicity  
Naturvernforbundet are concerned about the usage of end of life-tires (ELT) in open environment  
Recent studies have shown how rubber granules from ELT contain several hazardous chemicals.5 
Other studies has revealed how leaching of heavy metals (in particular Zinc) increases exponentially 
as the rubber particle ages.6 In cooperation with The Norwegian Consumer Council and the Norwegian 
institute for Air Research, FOE Norway conducted a screening for environmental pollutants in rubber 
crumbs from outdoor rubber flooring made from ELT at seven random playgrounds in South-East 
Norway. The screening showed high levels of chlorinated paraffins (SCCPs and MCCPs), several 
phthalates and a range of other chemicals.7 
 
Plastic pellets losses: 
Experience has shown that plastic pellet spills are near impossible to clean up after the fact.8 An 
incident aboard the ship Trans Carrier in the North Sea in February 2020 caused 13,2 tons 
(approximately 600 million plastic pellets) of plastic nurdles to end up in the sea, polluting a large area 
from the west coast of Sweden and down along the coast of southern Norway.9 A sixteen month long 
national response coordinated by the Norwegian Coastal Administration involving local governments, 
NGO`s and numerous volunteers managed to collect 4,4 tons of pellets. The fact that removal of 
pellets is extremely time consuming, extensive, and costly calls for a precautionary approach.  
There is a need for legally binding measures aiming at reducing plastic pellet losses, voluntary 
approaches are not sufficient. Naturvernforbundet recommends a mandatory regulatory supply chain

Textiles: 
 
The global fiber production has nearly doubled the last 20 years and is driven by the production of 
polyester and is the most prevalent anthropogenic particle found by microplastic pollution surveys 
around the world.11 About 60 percent of our clothing is made from synthetic materials, and about 70 of 
our household textiles, polyester being predominant.12  
Polyester is dominating due to its low price, and that the fabrics are strong and durable. Polyamide 
(nylon) is the second most common synthetic fiber. Other synthetic fibers are acrylic, polypropylene, 
polyurethan o.a.   
All fibers shed, both in use and laundry, synthetics too, but in variable degree due to the 
fabrics. Microplastics are shed from synthetic textiles throughout their lifecycle; from fiber and fabric 
manufacturing, through use and washing, to their final disposal whether it is by landfilling, 
incineration or recycling.  
 
Domestic washing during textile use is considered a relatively large source of microplastics leaking 
into the environment. Accordingly washing of synthetic textiles may account for up to 35 % of total 
annual microplastic releases. The fabric composition, laundry parameters including machine load 
(front vs. top), washing temperature, water consumption and the length of the wash cycle are all 
expected to influence the level of shedding of synthetic textiles. The most common type of 
microplastic found in wastewater treatment plants, are mainly polyester, followed by acrylic, nylon 
and polypropylene.13

Norwegian emissions of microplastics increased from 669 tons in 2014 to 1017 tons in 2020.14   
 
The use of nutrient-rich wastewater sludge in agriculture poses an additional environmental problem 
as this can lead to terrestrial microplastics contamination. In common with all plastics, microplastics 
do not degrade in the short term and accumulate in natural habitats, potentially degrading into even 
smaller fragments – so-called nanoplastics. Potentially toxic chemicals and additives from the textile 
industry form an additional risk.15  
 
Measures for reduction of microplastic pollution from textiles may be:  
 
Reduction og consumption of clothing: 
Stop the fast fashion industry. It results in enormous volume of clothing; overproduction and buy and 
toss mentality, with low level of caretaking and investing in better garments. This will reduce supply 
and demand, and thereby reduce landfills and pollution.  
 
Regulatory measures:  
- We need an EU-wide binding quantitative target for material use and consumption footprint 
reduction with specific objectives for textile products.  
- Mandate industrial prewashing of new textiles and garments, the use of filters to capture 
microplastics released throughout the manufacturing process (also cutting, printing, etc.)  
- Reduce the amount of microplastic emitted by both industrial wet treatments, industrial and 
consumer washing and drying (through design and filters).  
- Mandatory selection of the best performing fabrics and low impact manufacturing techniques 
during design / production to prevent release during production and use  
- Set mandatory minimum Ecodesign requirements at the EU level: set maximum thresholds on 
microplastic release and limit microplastic release throughout the life cycle of textiles 
(production, use, and end-of-life)  
- The Sustainable Product Policy Framework to incentivize the use of materials made from 
toxic-free and 100% biodegradable yarns.  
- Incentivize practices based on the reuse of fabrics and textile products.   
- PEF methodology does not cover microplastics. Complementary information on microplastic 
shedding must be added when communicating the results of a PEF profile.  
- User and consumer awareness: Microplastics warning label highlighting environmental and 
toxic impact of microplastic to inform buying decision. Clothes made of synthetic fibers or

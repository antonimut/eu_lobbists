 
 
EURATEX considerations on EC call for evidence “Measures aiming to 
reduce the presence in the environment of unintentionally released 
microplastics from tyres, textiles and plastic pellets” 
 
18 January 2022   
 
SUMMARY  
 
• EURATEX’s work has enabled real progress to tackle MP releases and built considerable 
knowledge, having engaged since 2017  
• EURATEX strategy to tackle microplastics is to find suitable solutions based on correct 
assumptions and science  
• Main c omment on the assumptions set in the consultation : the release of MP from 
textiles is largely overestimated  
• Main comment on the proposed measures set in the consultation : none of proposed 
measures appears sufficiently effective or realistic  
• Proposals for the “Way forward” are based on the available knowledge and include:   
o Harmonizing efforts at EU-level rather than pursuing national initiatives  
o Need for research to understand the real consequences of MP releases  
o Need for research to find suitable solutions  
o Need to coordinate EU policies rather than tackling MP releases in isolation  
o Creating solutions that are compatible with SMEs 
o Ensuring an international level playing field for affected companies 
o Developing practical definitions, standards and measurement methods 
o Enabling implementation and enforce ability of regulations by companies and 
authorities 
o Creating coherence with existing legislation and policy initiatives 
o Carrying out comprehensive impact assessments of the planned regulations 
o Analysing and addressing potential trade-offs 
o The short -term so lution is to promote sustainable consumption , h igh quality 
textiles and level playing field (respect of the rules) in the EU Market

1. EURATEX role in tackling Microplastics  
 
Since 2017, EURATEX, the European Ap parel and Textile Confederation, is engaged in 
tackling Microplastics (hereafter referred as MP), at this stage we wish to contribute to the call 
for evidence published by the European Commission with two inputs: 
o a joint statement1 with the CIA signatories building upon the knowledge gathered in 4 
years of work, and 
o an own response (i.e. the paper at hand) that stresses the impact of unintended 
consequences of the measures proposed in the textile value chain, and share s further 
considerations based on the business experiences across the Member States.

With reference to the first piece of work, we wish to recall that in December 2017 EURATEX 
together with its member CIRFS and the leading industry associations A.I.S.E., EOG and FESI

The CIA community has pooled together 43 experts from 13 countries in 3 continents and

While the reference to the CIA work and voluntary approaches mentioned in the call for

research landscape without coherent ways of measuring textiles for MP emissions. Thanks to

practice and it has delivered results useful for shaping effective policies for the benefit of both 
consumers and the industry. Transparency has also played a major role and information on all 
the CIA community meetings across the years has been publicly released. 
 
2. Suitable solutions based on science 
 
EURATEX stresses that tackling MP releases requires solid and science-based evidence. It is 
of paramount importance that a careful, scientific and fact-based examination of this topic 
is carried out. 
 
Although studies4 have already shown that textiles are not the main source of microplastic 
emissions entering the environment (most recently, a study in Flanders has shown that the main 
source of microplastics is road transport due to wearing  of tyres)5 textiles are much more in 
focus and criticized than other  sources. For instance, the call for evidence  contains twice as 
many proposed measures for synthetic textiles as for pellet loss and t yres, however the 
underlying assumptions of the proposed measures remain unclear. 
 
To find suitable and effective solutions more information is needed about: 
• a clear and reliable test method for measuring the microfiber loss of different types of 
synthetic clothing, including the exact differentiation between plastic and non -plastic 
fibre, fibre fragments, and particles;

3. Comments on the assumptions set in the consultation 
 
EURATEX observes several critical assumptions without supporting data. Unsubstantiated 
statements relate, among others, to risks to human health, reduction of consumer confidence in 
food products and the associated economic consequences, as well as the economic impact on 
activities such as fishing and tourism. 
 
Whilst for the areas above we refer to the points made in the CIA joint statement, we wish to 
elaborate on the assumption made on the (over)estimation of MP release from synthetic 
textiles.

share of textile fibres in relation to the total amount of microplastic pollution.  We argue that 
the order of magnitude relating to the release has to be reviewed considering that the numbers 
featured in public resources still lack unambiguous sampling and measuring met hods, thus 
leading to incomparable results and making the results and stated numbers questionable.

use of data and results from other studies, however, not all data for the used parameters  is

effect), advocating far-reaching measures such as eliminating the use of synthetic materials for 
clothing is not only premature, but given the market share of 40-60% (in clothing and textiles) 
not that simple or realistic. Moreover, not all synthetic clothing relates to loss-sensitive fleece.

a. None of the current measures is effective or realistic 
 
EURATEX wishes to emphasise the inappropriateness of the suggested policy options  since 
they – in some instances – challenge circularity practices 22. We though believe some 
adjustments can be made along these lines:  
 
➢ Eco-design requirements including using new materials  
 
While, in principle, eco-design requirements may be supported , these must be based on 
scientific information (i.e. on qualitative data  retrieved from the harmonized test method 
which is being developed), costs and implementation conditions in real-life manufacturing, 
and the wider sustainability debate with all potential impacts measured against a common 
framework (i.e. by aligning eco-design measures with SPI, CEAP, PEF, etc).  
 
Furthermore, a replacement of synthetic fibres with biodegradable yarns should in principle 
happen only in those textiles where function or durabili ty remains guaranteed. 
Biodegradable fibres have been developed  for years and are already in use. However, 
further improvements and optimizations can take place , although t his requires further 
financial support for research.  
 
➢ Improving manufacturing processes including pre-washing of clothes, before they 
are placed on the market  
 
Prewashing in Europe may be viable due to the existing efficient infrastructure of 
wastewater treatment plants . However, garment production and assembly in the EU is 
minimal. The global nature of value chains would make this hard to regulate as it removes 
the problem from the EU jurisdiction into regions over which the EU has little to no 
influence, creating thus only a shift in emissions. From an industry perspective, this would 
be fragmented and difficult to control due to the varied nature of suppliers, from vertical 
suppliers to those using contract laundries.   
 
➢ Establishing rules to make producers responsible for intervening before products 
can become waste, such as take-back schemes  
 
Take-back systems should only be installed on a voluntary basis and only where it makes 
sense. The EU already envisages in fact Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) schemes, 
which impose on manufacturers obligations in terms of taking responsibility for the entire 
lifecycle of their products  – including the waste management. In this context, prioritising 
one tool rather than another  is crucial not to create confusion amongst  operators and to 
avoid unintended duplications.  
 
Furthermore, i n the near future collection and recycling systems for textiles will be 
established within the EU for which discarded textiles are an important secondary raw 
material. Obligatory take-back schemes could then even represent a hindrance because the 
textiles first have to be put into the take-back system and only then may be transferred to a 
recycling system. 
 
➢ Facilitating recycled content or remanufacturing  
 
22 See CIA joint statement 
 
 
 
Funding systems in the research and development of textile recycling and the establishment 
of recycling hubs for textiles must be boosted. SMEs in the European textile and clothing 
industry must be supported by individual funding programs in the transformat ion process 
from a linear to a circular economy, among other things in the areas of digitalisation and 
improvement of manufacturing processes. 
 
Success cases may serve as an inspiration. For instance, unmixed plastics such as PET from 
bottles can be recycl ed very well and can be used, for example, in the manufacture of 
clothing and shoes. This should be further expanded by increasing the collection of single-
variety plastics, which would reduce the input of plastics into the environment and thus 
ultimately the formation of microplastics. 
 
➢ Applying filters or other technical solutions to washing machines, washer-dryers, 
and tumble-dryers23  
 
Whilst e nd of pipe solutions seem prima facie  easy, however, they come with several 
challenges. For example, tangible solutions rely predominantly on regular consumer 
interaction to achieve impact: engagement with consumers to commit and maintaining that 
commitment is difficult . Moreover, these solutions involve costs and are only viable in 
wealthy western countries, whereas MP is a global problem.  
 
More specifically on  filters, it would require an infrastructure to collect an d properly 
dispose the collected waste. Consumers would have to be informed on proper maintenance 
and handling. A proper Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) analysis could give insights on the 
net positive impact on microfibre release in relation to the impact of producing the filters.  
 
In Germany, investigations and tests foreseeing the equipment of sewage treatment plants 
and road drains with textile filters to collect microplastics have shown good results. Textiles 
can therefore be an important part of the solution to the microplastic problem. 
 
➢ Setting minimum sustainability and/or information requirements, and labelling 
products according to their level of microplastics emission 
 
Establishing such requirements – to be done in any case in coordination with other 
measures foreseen by eco-design, REACH and PEF – should only be possible when all 
knowledge gaps are closed and a harmonized test method is available.   
 
Concerning labelling, there has been no impact assessment of labelling on whether it will 
actually drive chang e at the point of sales, whether in stores or on -line. If a labeling 
obligation is introduced in connection with the washing of a textile product, then 
EURATEX will see the immediate need for  a harmonized European textile care labeling 
regulation. 
 
To date, there is currently insufficient information that could be printed on a label and the 
challenges of generating such information for every material are significant. Furthermore,

We wish to reiterate that the suggested measures should be looked at from a holistic perspective 
by duly considering the overall impact – also in terms of costs for the industry – of the circular 
economy action plan and the EU textile strategy . EURATEX believes that focusing only on 
microplastic release may cause negative impacts elsewhere and lead to greenwashing. 
 
Furthermore, we believe that measures can be expected to be most effective when focusing on 
specific textile product groups which are prone to microfibre shedding. Several studies 25 
already point out products that supposedly shed the most microfibres. Using a standardized test 
method will contribute to providing more insight and ultimately take effective and proportional 
measures, appropriate to specific risk product groups. 
 
5. The way forward  
 
We support the policy goal to reduce the presence and asso ciated risks of unintentionally 
released microplastics from textiles. However, we emphasise the existence of the knowledge 
gap which is also acknowledged by the Commission in the call for evidence.  
 
For policy measures to be effective we need mor e data and to build upon the outputs of the 
work that has occurred to date with the development and certification of a test method. We call 
on the Commission to value the time and effort spent on the development and standardisation 
of the test method within a reasonable timeframe. The existence of this critical first step now 
puts the industry in a good position, and with appropriate support from the Commission it will 
enable it to bridge the knowledge gap and rapidly scale the data required to create meaningful 
policy measures. 
 
a. Harmonizing EU efforts against national actions  
 
Initiatives adopted by single Member States alone like the ones on mandatory filters in washing 
machines in France26 and on mandatory labelling to inform consumers of the risk of  
microplastic shedding in Italy27 can be counterproductive if not coordinated with harmonised 
measures introduced at EU l evel. We call on the Commission to stand up for harmonisation 
instead of allowing fragmentation at a Member State level.

b. need research to understand the real consequence of MP releases 
 
The European textile industry is in principle willing to support future policy and legislative 
measures, however, further research – and corresponding financial resources – is necessary to 
better understand the MP problem and develop solutions based on concrete data. We invite the 
policymakers to help the industry to bridge the research and innovation gap through the 
facilitation of cross disciplinary, multi-regional, multi-sector support. 
 
c. need research to find solutions 
 
We need to b ridge the gap between research and innovation . EURATEX supports finding 
effective and economically feasible solutions by:  
- contributing to the development of international standardised test methods to 
identify and quantify MP present in water and the environment;  
- sharing information on progress of research, knowledge gaps, options, and 
priorities; 
- supporting and participating in industrial research for feasible and effective 
solutions. 
 
d. need to coordinate EU policies 
 
The MP topic has to be considered in the bigger picture which include at least 11 legislative 
actions28 which prioritize circular economy, consumers protection, chemicals, etc. Such new 
legislative pressure will create significant burdening to SMEs of the textile value chain  and 
create unbearable new costs. Therefore, any additional measure which pose management and 
enforcement challenges shall be excluded.  
 
In addition to legislation, we need a policy framework which incentivises: 
- multidisciplinary, multi-regional, multi-sector research; 
- a hub to address the MP issue at a global level; 
- prioritising risk/impact. 
 
Alignment between initiatives is needed to strengthen an essential ecosystem of the European 
economy like the textiles value chain. 
 
e. need to promote sustainable consumption, high quality textiles and level playing field 
(respect of the rules) in the EU Market 
 
The EU needs to become more active in countries that still permit uncontrolled waste dumping 
or that have no  landfill ban for domestic waste. We believe i t is time  to be proactive . 
Environmental and waste management technologies are already available and well tested in 
several countries, and this could be a key factor in tackling the MP issue.

            Introduction  The Cross Industry Agreement1 signatories have been working with the European Commission on this topic since 2018. Collectively, their associated memberships, and the community of research organisations and stakeholders that it has created are committed to tackling emissions of microplastics throughout the life cycle of products. As responsible sectors and organisations we welcome any effort that is science and risk-based, realistic, enforceable and proven to provide effective impact.   The signatories of the CIA welcome the opportunity to provide feedback on the impact assessment on the proposed measures to reduce the impact of microplastics pollution on the environment. The submission focuses on questions and assumptions that - if not resolved - could potentially render the proposed measures useless or in some cases counterproductive. Facilitating recycled content or remanufacturing There is no evidence to suggest that the use of recycled content or remanufacturing would impact the release of microplastics from synthetic textiles.   Applying filters or other technical solutions to washing machines, washer-dryers, and tumble-dryers End of pipe solutions, while seemingly easy, come with several challenges which highlight the need for addressing the root cause and not rely on end of pipe solutions: • Tangible solutions rely predominantly on regular consumer interaction to achieve impact. Engagement with consumers to commit and maintain that commitment is difficult. • These solutions are only viable in wealthy western countries, while this is a global problem. • Filters or other technical solutions require an infrastructure to collect and properly dispose of the collected waste.  Setting minimum sustainability and/or information requirements, and labelling products according to their level of microplastics emission  • There is currently insufficient information that could be printed on a label and the challenges of generating this information for every material are significant. Furthermore, accounting for how different materials are used in garments in a reliable and reproducible way is not understood.  • The relationship between the physical flows and concentrations of microplastics and the associated environmental impacts is not understood. While the industry is a considerable way towards a global standardised method to assess material loss from fabrics, this only provides a measure of quantity and significant challenges remain in translating from presence to impact.  • This needs to be well designed to drive impact and will only drive impact if supported by consumer campaigns. • There has been no impact assessment of labelling or if it will drive change at the point of purchase.  Developing voluntary approaches by industry We support this suggestion and highlight the Microfibre Consortium 2030 Commitment and Roadmap as an initiative that with appropriate support, could work with industry to scale data collection, and develop appropriate solutions.   Common position from signatories of the CIA  The signatories of the Cross Industry Agreement support both policy and legislative measures to reduce the environmental presence and associated risks of unintentionally released microplastics from textiles. However, we emphasise the existence of the knowledge gap that is acknowledged by the European Commission in the call for evidence.

For policy measures to be effective we need more data and to build upon the outputs of the work that has occurred to date with the development and certification of a test method. We call on the European Commission to respect the time and effort that has been spent on the development and standardisation of the method within a reasonable timeframe. The existence of this critical first step now puts the industry in a good position, and with appropriate support from the European Commission, able to bridge the knowledge gap and rapidly scale the data required to create meaningful policy measures.   As such, we call on the EU: • To support the industry to bridge the research and innovation gap as a priority through the allocation of targeted funding that is cross disciplinary, multi-regional, and multi-sector.  • For policy or legislative measures to be based on detailed risk and impact assessments. • For any eventual ecodesign measures to be well-developed and address the challenge in a scientific manner.   • For a harmonised approach that does not allow fragmentation at a member state level. • For alignment between policy and legislative initiatives (SPI, CEAP, PEF) that use a common assessment framework where possible.         About the CIA:  The Cross Industry Agreement (CIA) is a voluntary collaboration for the prevention of microplastic release into the aquatic environment during the washing of synthetic textiles. The signatories are five European industry associations representing the global value chain of garments and their associated maintenance, namely A.I.S.E, CIRFS, EOG, EURATEX and FESI.

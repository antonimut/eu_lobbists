1. Introduction, background and focus 
The Leibniz Institute of Freshwater Ecology and Inland Fisheries (IGB) is Germany’s largest research 
centre for freshwaters. Our research findings help to tackle global environmental changes and to 
develop strategies for sustainable water management – true to our guiding principle “Research for the 
future of our freshwaters”. Based on our research and expertise, we comment on the Call for Evidence 
on microplastics (MPs) pollution and measures to reduce its impact on the environment.  
The IGB scientists underline the high relevance of freshwater ecosystems for nature and society alike. 
Freshwater systems provide us with the vital resource drinking water and harbour a unique diversity 
of life. However, rivers, lakes, ponds, wetlands and groundwater are amon g the most threatened 
ecosystems in Europe and worldwide. Their decline is associated with multiple stressors , including 
pollution by manifold human activities. Framing the legislation for the protection of our inland waters 
to safeguard ecosystem servi ces such as clean drinking water, natural flood protection, fisheries 
resources, recreational spaces, and pollutant retention, will be vital for human livelihood in Europe in 
the coming decades. In this context, MPs and sub-micron-plastics (SMPs) pollution is an emerging and 
consistently increasing environmental stressor of high concern. European policy -making needs to 
address this topic with coherent and effective solutions. 
2. Critical aspects regarding MP pollution 
 
• Limitations in existing analytical methods to assess the occurrence, fate and effects of MPs 
The toxicity of MPs (1µm < particle size < 5 mm) and SMP (< 1 µm) is mostly associated with their small 
size which allows them to enter organisms’ bodies upon ingestion and inhalation. Most studies on MPs 
toxicity, however, are limited to determining the toxicity of polystyrene standard particles in high 
concentration. These model systems of MPs and SMPs are far from natural, where their form and 
chemical composition greatly vary in time and space. Furthermore, little information is available about 
the hazards of different MP/SMP types and sizes to (micro)organisms (Abdelsaleheen et al. 2021). This 
is mostly due to limitations in the existing analytical methods, which are not fully capable of tracking, 
characterizing and quantifying  MPs and SMPs in the natural environment and organisms’ bodies. 
Physicochemical properties of particles are not the only factor of MP/SMP toxicity, i.e., sorption of 
various chemicals onto MPs and SMPs a s well as their transportation in the environment can be of 
great concern.

At IGB, we work on analytical techniques and methods to enable the tracking, characterization and 
quantification of MPs and SMPs in complex matrices of environmental and biological samples. By using 
these set of techniques, we aim to better understand toxicity and biological fate of MPs and SMPs in 
organisms and cells as well as quantify their interactions with molecules and proteins.  
One of the major sources of MPs and SMPs in the environment are tyres. They differ from most other 
plastics as they consist of a mix of synthetic plastic and filling materials  as well as additives including 
heavy metals and organic chemicals (e.g., a ntioxidants), many of which can leach into water. The 
relative contribution of tyre wear to overall plastics pollution in aquatic systems is estimated to 
account for 47% of all MPs globally . Ca. 18%  of MP tyre wear  is directly washed into water bodies, 
where MPs, SMPs as well as leaching additives pose a significant risk to all aquatic life forms and thus, 
modify ecosystem functions. 
 
• Challenge: preparation of MPs and development of suitable measuring approaches in water 
To date, most of the studies on MPs have focused on the application of plastic beads from defined 
fabric-new plastics as models of MPs in toxicological studies, e.g., polystyrene. This may not represent 
the MPs in nature as they are made of particles of different composition, sizes and shapes. To account 
for the highly diverse nature of MPs in the environment, IGB has recently prepared MPs using the top-
down methodology in which larger, highly heterogeneous macroplastics break down into smaller MPs 
which cover a whole spectrum of size and chemical composition (Figure 1).

Measuring MPs in environmental samples is challenging because most of the available methods and 
techniques are only capable of analyzing larger plastic particles in clean water. When particles are 
surrounded by natural organic matter or sediments in aquatic systems, detection techniques face 
challenges to reliably identify MPs and SMPs. To tackle this challenge,  IGB together with Leiden 
University has developed a novel strategy to purify and extract MPs from simulated natural water 
samples for analysis  (Monikh et al. 2021). The preliminary results show that the method can 
successfully extract the particles from the natural water samples and characterize the particles in terms 
of their size and shape (Figure 2).

• MPs are hotspots for spreading antibiotic resistance genes  
MPs can serve as a surface for microbial colonization including fungi and bacteria. Some opportunistic 
bacteria are also able to use the MP’s surfaces as a source of food, which can also lead to release of 
additives from the surface of MPs. Over time, the microbiome structure on t he MP surface may be 
different from the surrounding environment. Since it is known that exposure to metals can facilitate 
the development of antibiotic resistance genes in bacteria, it is likely that the released metals from 
MPs allow these genes to be expressed in bacteria on the surface of MPs. This might be more likely to 
occur in wastewater treatment plants (WWTP) as 62% of all tyre wear MPs is estimated to end up in 
WWTPs. It is expected that MPs in WWTPs  are colonized by pathogenic bacteria from humans or 
animals, and the chances of spreading antibiotic resistance by horizontal gene transfer are higher. 
Although it is reported that most of MPs are removed from wastewater in WWTPs, substantial 
numbers of particles and associated microbes are still released into the environment. These might lead 
to the unintended spread of pathogenic species carrying antibiotic resistance genes and thereby 
spreading antimicrobial resistance to the broader natural non-pathogenic microbial population (Arias-
Andres et al. 2018a&b).  
 
• MPs as carriers of fungal pathogens  
Our field research in North-East Germany shows that the MPs incubated in different aquatic habitats 
along a river -estuary system including a WWTP were found t o harbour distinct fungal communities 
including several parasitic fungi.  In this way MP contaminants could collect, transport and disperse a 
substantial number of non-resident fungal pathogens from WWTPs along their transit from land-based 
over freshwaters to marine systems (Kettner et al. 2017).   
 
• MP can alter microbial communities and functions in aquatic environments 
IGB research results show that the microbial community attached to MPs and their functions were 
different from natural surface -attached and free -living microbial communities (Arias -Andres et al. 
2018c). Thus, the presence of MPs over a long time can alter the structure and composition of 
microbial communities as well as major biochemical processes in aquatic ecosystems.  
IGB is investig ating the ecological and health consequences of microbial interactions with different 
plastic particles, including biodegradable plastics and their associated additives in aquatic ecosystems.

In particular, IGB focuses on tyre wear MPs and associated ad ditives as they represent the major 
fraction of MPs in aquatic systems in the EU (5 4%). The implications of tyre wear microplastics on 
microbial communities and the functioning of biogeochemical cycles like nitrogen and carbon cycle in 
aquatic ecosystems are under investigation. Nevertheless, it has to be stated that the research field on 
MPs and SMPs is still evolving, and that more evidence -based knowledge has to be gathered. 
Therefore, the precautionary principle plays a central role in policymaking. 
 
• Precaution and the polluter-pays principle 
Sustainable policy making should include a serious and general reflection on suitable alternatives to 
the still increasing use of plastics. Key to avoiding new and mitigating existent MPs and SMPs pollution 
is minimizing or even eliminating plastic emission already at the source.  There is no ecological and/or 
societal justification to accept the potential destruction of aquatic ecosystems only to relocate efforts 
and costs of contaminant removal do wnstream. Manufacturers have to take responsibility for their 
emissions and products. Thus, the polluter-pays principle should be implemented consequently in all 
relevant fields, not only limited to single aspects such as single-use plastic and plastic bags. 
 
• EU-wide monitoring strategy with harmonised methodologies for MPs pollution  
A comprehensive EU-wide monitoring strategy to assess MPs and SMPs pollution in freshwater bodies 
is still missing. Most needed are harmonis ed methodologies to detect, analyse, quantify and report 
the pollution of water bodies and sediments with MPs and SMPs. In addition to the occurrence of 
plastics, monitoring programs should also aim to assess the environmental effects of MPs and SMPs. 
Once established, publicly funded monitoring programs measuring MP in freshwater bodies in the EU 
member states should ideally be retrievable from open and accessible repositories. These data sets 
should include the available spatial and t emporal resolution a s well as adequate meta information 
(such as a documentation of sampling and analytical methods). This data openness supports evidence-
based and efficient decision making. 
3. Conclusion 
From a research-based perspective, the IGB scientists underline that improved EU policies to efficiently 
prevent and mitigate MPs and SMPs emissions into the environment are urgently needed.  
A harmonised EU-wide monitoring would foster the production of crucial knowledge on the topic, and 
would directly support sustainable policymaking. Once released into the ecosystems, a retrieval of MPs 
and SMPs is basically impossible. Prevention directly at the source should be given priority, not costly 
end-of-pipe solutions. Given the high ecological and health risk of long-term MPs and SMPs emissions, 
current practices should be improved, and clear and consistent EU policy should be made, e specially 
with perspective to the own environmental goals of the EU. Considerable attention must be paid to 
the indirect adverse effects of plastics, such as facilitating the development and spreading of antibiotic 
resistance genes, which might harm human and environmental health alike.

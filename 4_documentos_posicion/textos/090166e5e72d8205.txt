 
   
 
 
SURFRIDER FOUNDATION EUROPE  
Answer to call for evidence on microplastics pollution 
 
18 January 2022 
 
GENERAL COMMENTS 
SURFRIDER FOUNDATION EUROPE (SFE) welcomes the European Commission’s 
initiative on microplastics and supports its current efforts to shape strong measures that 
will address microplastic pollution at source.  
Surfrider Europe calls for urgent action to  prevent and reduce all sources of 
unintentionally released microplastics, as all microplastic sources are equally and in 
unprecedented numbers impacting both the health of our blue planet, and our own health.  
 
Commission’s understanding of the problem 
Surfrider Europe welcomes the Commission’s understanding of the problem, as reflected in 
its ‘Problem the initiative aims to tackle ’ section and would like to echo its growing concerns 
on this pollution and its consequences. These concerns are widely shared by the blue 
community we represent and  are supported by strong science which has  evidenced the 
worrying features of microplastics pollution which is pervasive, chronic, persistent and 
transboundary. Science gives us  solid and ample  evidence of  the harmful impacts  of 
microplastic pollution on our environment, ecosystems and on public health and their huge 
contributions to the most worrying environmental crisis we face, from biodiversity loss to 
climate change. 
Occurrence in the environment and in our Ocean 
Microplastics pose a risk to the environment, which is currently uncontrolled.  
It is estimated that a total of 15 to 51 trillion microplastic particles have accumulated in the 
ocean.  
 
Microplastics have been reported across all marine waters including the deepest ocean 
trenches, in all environments observed (sediments, fresh water, sea ice and sediments in polar 
regions, terrestrial environment, air) including in the most remote areas.  
Microplastics have also been found in commercial fish  and shellfish species and in various 
foods and drinking water as rightly mentioned in the Commission’s document supporting the 
launch of its call for evidence on microplastic pollution.  
The exponential production and use of plastics and microplastics have generated increasing 
environmental release and concentrations of microplastics. As they are impossible to remove 
and persist almost  indefinitely, any release contributes to a progressively increasing 
environmental stock. 
Impacts of microplastic pollution

Despite knowledge gaps, t he more research is done, the more evidence there is of the 
detrimental impacts of microplastics. 
On habitats, marine life and the Ocean 
An increasing number of scientific publications highlight ever growing evidence of the negative 
effects of microplastics: microplastics are ingested by freshwater and marine species, hinder the 
growth of floating aquatic plants, absorb persi stent organic contaminants and release harmful 
chemicals, and bioaccumulate in animals across the aquatic food chain. As a consequence of 
their interactions with biota, in particular via their ingestion, microplastics have serious impacts 
such as inflammation, disruption to growth and reproduction.  Microplastics have also proved to 
cross biological barriers impacting cells or reaching intestines. Field observations have in addition 
proven that microplastics are able to spread through the food web by means of trophic transfer, 
from a specie to another.  
In addition to impact ing marine life and habitats, microplastic pollution is affecting the health of 
the Ocean and together with other sources of pollution, including from other types of plastics, is 
impacting the overall resilience of our ocean to human pressures and climate change. 
Microplastics pollution contributes to reducing the ability of our Ocean to provide ecosystem 
services as key as climate regulation, carbon sequestration, provision of energy, food, well-being 
and recreation to name a few. 
On our health 
Widespread in nature and our ocean, microplastics accumulate in the food chain, putting at 
risk our food security and reach our bodies, for example through the fish and seafood we eat. 
Concerns grow as microplastics contain harmful chemical additives that have been shown to 
have hazardous health effects. They also have the ability to attract, absorb and transport toxic 
particles and contaminants once in the environment. As humans, we are exposed to 
microplastics and the toxic components attached not only through ingestion but also through 
inhalation as proven by recent studies on microplastic s in the air and via direct skin contact. 
In other words, and as rightly outlined in the Commission’s document, we have evidence that 
we eat, drink and breathe microplastics that are able to enter our bodies. As a matter of fact, 
microplastic particles have been detected in human organs and even in the placenta of 
pregnant women. More and more studies are looking at the impacts of microplastics on human 
health. In the meantime, absence of their impacts on human health has not been shown and 
evidence of the impact s of plastics on animal species  which are suspected  to be similar on 
humans has grown. Finally, studies have revealed microplastics can act as vectors for disease 
and can increase our vulnerability to disease. 
 
All these pieces of evidence make the case for the EU to adopt strong measures to eliminate all 
emissions of microplastic s into the environment . Given the remaining knowledge gaps, the 
Commission should take action, complying with its guiding principles, including the prevention 
and precautionary principles.   
 
Commission’s proposed options and approach 
Surfrider Europe supports the general approach of the European Commission to address  
unintentionally released microplastics in a comprehensive and holistic manner under a same set 
of measures. Having said that, we are strong supporters of the Commission adopting a single 
definition of microplastics, no matter which uses these microplastics are made for. This definition 
should be in line with the definition given to intentionally added microplastics in the currently 
 
   
 
examined proposal for revision of the REACH restriction. The definition, in particular, should 
include biodegradable, water -soluble and liquid polymers and should not include a lower size  
limit. In line with the comprehensive approach the Commission wishes to adopt and that we 
strongly support, Surfrider Europe finally  strongly recommends the Commission not to limit its 
action and associated measures to only 3 sources of microplastics  - namely pellets, tyres and 
textiles as outlined in its paper, while we have strong evidence of equally worrying and impactful 
additional sources of unintentionally released microplastics. 
Scope  
EU measures need to reflect (and address) the diversity of sources of (unintentionally 
released) microplastic pollution and cannot be limited to pellets, tyres and textiles, no matter 
big contributors  these sectors are. It is important the Commission considers all sources of 
microplastic pollution if it wants to achieve impacts in reducing microplastic pollution o f our 
environment and its impacts on human health.  As such, we recommend the Commission to 
address other sources of primary microplastics, such as plastic biomedia, including biomedia 
which comply with the microplastic definition (mostly biobeads and b iostyrs) as well as 
secondary microplastics resulting from the degradation of larger items including bigger 
biomedia, and microplastics from agricultural sources, from the breakdown of plastic used in 
agriculture (e.g. mulch, polytunnels), from paints (including marine paints) and road markings, 
as well as microplastics from personal protection equipment and face masks, and microplastic-
contaminated waste water and geotextiles. 
As a consequence, we would like to strongly invite the Commission to consider the following 
sources and sector specific measures: 
Plastic biomedia 
Biomedia also referred to as filter media are small plastic cylinders ranging from some 
millimetres (mainly biobeads) to some centimetres in size. They are the media used as 
bacterial biofilm carriers in the wastewater treatment process. They are mainly made of 
polyethylene (PE) or high -density polyethylene (HDPE) plastic. In waste water treatment 
tanks, they can account for 70% of the volume of the basin. This can represent up to several 
millions of plastic pieces. They allow bacteria used to treat water, to settle on their surface and 
proliferate. 
Biomedia are spilled into the environment during their lifecycle as a result of malfunctions and 
accidents. As such, they had been identified in the Eunomia report as a source of non -
intentionally released microplastics (see Investigating options for reducing releases in the 
aquatic environment of microplastics emitted by (but not intentionally added in) products , 
Eunomia, ICF for the European Commission, 2018, p105). With the exception of the upper 
size limit often used to describe a microplastic which some biomedia (mostly biobeads) comply 
with and some others don’t, they fully comply with the definition and scop e of your study on 
unintentional releases of small plastic particles into the environment. 
Our proposals for policy measures: 
• Impose to W aste water Treatment Plants (WWTP s) designers and constructors to 
equip WWTPs with systems to retain biomedia when this technology is used 
• Oblige operators to report on the technologies used in the framework of the secondary 
treatment of waste water including on the types and formats of biomedia  
• Oblige operators and installers to provide instructions for use and storage, as well as 
details on procedures in place to prevent biomedia spills  
 
   
 
• Compel companies to follow and respect strict guidelines to prevent biomedia loss 
during handling, transport,  storage or use of plastic biomedia, and train their staff 
accordingly  
• Set legal obligations for frequent control of waste water treatment plants by public 
authorities 
• Monitor biomedia losses and report on incidents in the event of biomedia spills 
• Hold al l companies involved in designing, making, using or handling biomedia 
accountable by law in the event of spills 
•  Put systems and alert procedures in place to capture biomedia and alert authorities in 
the event of any accident or failure within the systems where they are used. 
For more information, see: Surfrider Foundation Europe, Bencivengo P., Barreau C., Bailly C., 
Verdet F.,(2018). Pollution of our beaches and wa tercourses by plastic biomedia, bacterial 
biofilm carriers used in the wastewater treatment process. 
Shipping and container loss at sea 
● Adopt measures to prevent the loss of microplastics at sea resulting from the loss of 
containers, so as to ensure and police "ship planning" vessel compliance , to cut the 
number of accidents  to prevent overloading of cargo ships , to mandate adequate 
storage of containers  and to ensure proper transparency on container losses by 
establishing a reporting system. 
For more information, see: Containers overboard! 10 proposals to prevent container losses , 
Surfrider Foundation Europe, 2019. 
We also support other measures for: 
Agriculture and geotextiles 
● Phase out the use of synthetic polymers and intentionally added microplastics in 
agriculture and horticulture 
● Develop measures to secure an adequate maintenance and the use of toxic free 
geotextiles 
● Promote sustainable alternatives to plastic mulch and other soil-polluting microplastics, 
such as synthetic geotextiles 
● Set short-term reduction targets for microplastic release from effluent and by-products 
(fat, sludge, etc.) of WWTP and regulations to achieve zero emissions of microplastics 
in the longer run 
Shipping & fisheries 
● Regulate the release of microplastics in greywater for all ships and vessels 
● Monitor microplastics in wild and farmed fish and seafood 
● Mark and track fishing gear to discourage discarding and mandate the reporting of lost 
gear to public authorities 
Current scope 
We support the following measures for the three sources which stand for now under the scope 
of the Commission’s foreseen measures on non-intentionally released microplastics. 
Plastic Pellets 
 
   
 
● Propose a regulatory system requiring the entire supply chain to adhere to best 
practice measures to stop pellet losses, supported by third-party, independent auditing 
and certification.  
● The regulatory supply chain approach should incorporate best practice standards and 
certification, and: 
○ require public disclosure of data and progress reports and mandatory third -
party independent auditing in place, 
○ set an annual independent audit of companies, on implementation of pellet loss 
prevention measures, 
○ require reporting requirements on types and tonnage of pellets being used, 
handled or transported and lost by the entire supply chain, 
○ set obligatory visual materials on site and labeling on all pellet packaging and 
containers should be introduce d, clearly denoting environmental and toxic 
impact of pellet spills and the importance of responsible handling, 
○ restrict the use of unsealed or flexible, non-robust containers, 
○ require the containment of all pellet handling sites, this should include plast ic 
production as well as recycling facilities 
○ require the mandatory use of filtering and capture systems to prevent leaks in 
facilities and all points of spillage risks at every stage of the plastic pellet life 
cycle; and 
○ ban plastic production facilities and plastic converters facilities to be built or 
operated near sites of high biodiversity value or high vulnerability. 
For more information, see: Surfrider Foundation Europe, Plastic Giants polluting through 
the back door (2020). 
Synthetic Textiles 
● Set an EU-wide binding quantitative target for material use and consumption footprin t 
reduction with specific objectives for textile products, 
● Include all types of textile products, including household textiles in the scope.  
● Set EU mandatory minimum codesign requirements, including  maximum thresholds 
on microplastics release throughout the life cycle of textiles from production to end-of-
life, 
● Require the selection of the best performing fabrics and low impact manufacturing 
techniques during design / production to prevent release during production and use, 
● Set ecodesign requirements, in the context of the Sustainable Product Policy 
Framework to ensure the use of materials made from toxic -free and 100% 
biodegradable yarns (in real life conditions). 
● Incentivise practices based on the reuse of fabrics and textile products.  
● Require industrial pre -washing of new textiles and garments and the use of filters to 
capture microplastics released throughout the manufacturing process, including 
cutting and printing stages.  
● Set design requirements and mandatory use of filters to reduc e the amount of 
microplastics emitted by both industrial wet treatments, industrial and consumer 
washing and drying 
● Include the environmental costs of plastic pollution in the product price, on the basis of 
the polluter-pays principle. 
● Mandate that complem entary information on microplastic shedding must be added 
when communicating the results of a Product Environmental Footprint (PEF) profile. 
 
   
 
● Set a label on microplastics on textile products for users and consumers, highlighting 
the environmental and toxic impact of MP to inform the purchase. Clothes made of 
synthetic fibres or recycled synthetic fibres must not be categorised as ‘sustainable’. 
Tyre Abrasion 
● Set mandatory minimum requirements on tyre abrasion included in the Type approval 
regulation, i.e. threshold limits for microplastic release from tyres, to be increased over 
time. 
● Add tyre abrasion in tyre labeling regulation, without delay  & establish a suitable test 
method, at the latest by 2023. 
● Mandate the removal of harmful chemicals from tyres and fund research for 
alternatives to problematic chemicals in tyres such as zinc, benzothiazole, bisphenols 
and 6PPD. 
● Address the issue of toxic additives and the ability to generate nanoparticles. 
● Retreated tyres can only be considered an option if it is ensured that they do not 
contain substances of very high concern, which is not currently the case. 
● Broaden the scope of measures to capture additional sources such as brake pad dust 
and road abrasion.  
● Include the environmental costs of plastic pollution in the product price, on the basis of 
the polluter-pays principle. 
● More broadly consider measures that reduce road transport and speed limits. 
 
Form of measures, targets and policy options 
On the proposed measures, Surfrider Europe recommends to focus on prevention measures 
and upstream measures which, contrary to remedial measures, have the potential to 
significantly reduce microplastic releases into the environment and the Ocean. Prevention 
measures should go together with source specific emission reduction targets. Surfrider Europe 
supports the statement made in the Commission’s document supporting the call for evidence 
that voluntary measures have not achieved reduction. This calls for the adopt ion of legally -
binding measures, against any voluntary initiative. In the case of pellet pollution, industry 
voluntary approaches are largely insufficient to address the issue: only 1,482 companies out 
of over 55,000 are signed up to Operation Clean Sweep in Europe, even though the scheme 
has existed for 25 years, while plastic pellet pollution continues to pollute our ocean and waters 
all over Europe. 
Surfrider Europe recommends that the only legal base for this legislation should be article 192 
(environment) of the Treaty of the European Union. 
Finally, our experience with microplastic pollution can attest to the variety of players involved, 
hence we would like to strongly support the Commission ’s multisectoral and multi stakeholder  
approach.  
 
Other comments and conclusions 
On monitoring aspects, we welcome the Commission’s proposal to develop monitoring efforts on 
microplastic pollution. Surfrider Europe has been for years consolidating its programme on 
 
   
 
monitoring marine litter  (including microplastics) , including through engaging  in and 
strengthening citizen science. We consider monitoring is a key tool to qualify and quantify waste 
and plastics in the ocean and rivers and to convert this observation into concrete policy measures 
and to contribute to control the impacts of measures on the ground.  To engage our community 
in citizen science, we have created several tools allowing volunteers in the field to quantify and 
report litter items (Ocean Initiatives reporting forms, Plastic Origins app, etc.). In 2019, Surfrider 
Europe's Ocean Initiatives pr ogramme brought together 91,160 participants over 2,279 
collections organized in 47 countries. Surfrider Europe has also deployed efforts to collect on the 
ground testimonies and observations on biomedia, plastic pellets and containers lost at sea. 
Surfrider Europe is also participating in an additional citizen science and observation project 
called Surfing for Science which aims at assessing the level of microplastic pollution in shoreline 
waters. Citizens participate in the project by collecting scientific samples whilst paddle surfing. 
The final objective is twofold: knowing how much and what type of microplastics are present in 
coastline waters to contribute to find solutions to reduce their impacts and fight them at the source 
and raise awareness of the problems that plastic pollution and in particular microplastics 
generate. It would be important to support microplastic monitoring activities  further. Finally, to 
make the most of this monitoring and achieve impacts, it should be used to set quantifiable targets 
and measure progress over time. 
Finally, Surfrider Europe would like to thank the Commission for its efforts and stay s at the 
disposal of its services to provide further information and evidence to support strong action to 
prevent and stop microplastic pollution. 
 
About Surfrider Foundation Europe 
SURFRIDER FOUNDATION EUROPE is a non-profit organisation whose purpose is to protect 
and showcase the importance of lakes, rivers, the ocean, waves, and coastlines. It currently has 
over 15,000 members and is active across 1 2 European countries through its volunteer -run 
branches. For 30 years, Surfrider Foundat ion Europe has been taking action in three areas of 
expertise: marine litter, water quality and public health, coastal management and climate 
change. For more information: surfrider.eu 
Surfrider Europe has been w orking on plastic issues since its creation in 1990, focusing on 
stopping marine litter pollution and releases of plastics, including microplastics to the aquatic and 
coastline environment through targeted environmental campaigns, advocacy efforts, awareness 
raising efforts, guidance to companies and citizen science initiatives in rivers and oceans to 
monitor and assess the presence of microplastics. Our latest campaign, the Break the Plastic 
Wave campaign, calls businesses, citizens and decision makers to action to fight against plastic 
pollution. For more information: surfrider.eu/en/our-missions/marine-litter/.

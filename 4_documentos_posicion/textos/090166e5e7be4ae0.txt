1. The Advertising Information Group (AIG), a network of European advertising and media 
associations that brings together various sectors of the advertising industry, welcomes the 
opportunity to provide feedback on the European Commission’s adoption of its proposed regulation 
on the transparency and targeting of political advertising.   
 
2. Advertising is a key driver of economic growth and competition.  Studies have shown that for 
every euro spent on advertising it generates €7 for the economy. It supports nearly 6 million jobs 
across the EU, and it helps with bringing new products and services to market and it helps SMEs 
to reach new customers.  
 
3. Advertising also funds sport, media, and culture. It also  plays an important role in the 
democratising access to informat ion for all citizens and funding of the free and open 
internet.  
 
4. Given the concerns of foreign influence and disinformation during elections and its potential impact 
to society and democracy, we understand the purpose behind the Commission’s desire to develop 
regulation on the transparency and targeting of political advertising.  
 
5. However, we feel strongly enough to respond to this consultation to say that such rules should not:  
 
a) Undermine existing regulation of commercial advertising which has been built up over 
decades. Commercial advertising is already regulated in accordance with EU laws 1 related to 
consumer protection, unfair commercial practices, and personal data protection among others. 
There is also a network of national self -regulatory bodies and codes across the EU. 2 Self-
regulation, in this context,  does not mean an individual companies’ own rules or community 
standards but instead are a network of self-regulatory bodies, funded by industry, and 
backstopped by statutory regulation. Additionally, the forthcoming Digital Services Act (DSA) is 
expected to provide rules on online advertising transparency appl icable to commercial and 
political advertising.  Therefore, it is important that the Commission’s proposal on political 
advertising does not conflict with the DSA and create further uncertainty about how commercial 
advertising is currently regulated. 
 
b) Be too broad in scope. Commercial advertising is constantly evolving, and organisations are 
tasked with developing campaign strategies that are innovative and appealing to consumers. 
Consumers are becoming increasingly conscious about the products and services they 
purchase and want confidence that retailers and service providers adopt fair, ethical and 
sustainable business practices. As such, it is important to preserve the freedom of expression, 
for example through issue-based ads, without unnecessary restrictions on businesses. 
 
c) Impose disproportionate obligations on publishers and advertisers. Article 7 and Article 
12 must be reviewed and critically questioned whether and to what extent the proposed 
regulations are suitable and necessary to achieve the purpose of the regulation. It would be 
disproportionate for publishers to have to publish deta iled business -sensitive information in 
their ads. It would mean that this information would be available to competitors without any real 
added value for consumers . Moreover, it  would be an additional financial and organisational 
burden for smaller companies to comply with.  
  
Our Recommendations 
 
6. We strongly believe that the Commission should amend the definitions set out in Article 2 to exclude 
commercial advertising or further qualify the exclusions that are already included.

Definitions of “political actor” and “purely commercial nature”  
 
7. The definition of political advertising in Article 2 (a) covers the “preparation, placement, promotion, 
publication or dissemination…of a message by, for or on behalf of a political actor unless it is of a 
purely private or a purely commercial nature”. We note the definitions of political actor in  Article 
2(4)(a) – (h).  
 
8. We believe Article 2(4)(g) , when read alongside the exclusion wording in Article 2(a)  is too broad 
and risks bringing commercial advertising into scope, despite the wording “unless it is of a… purely 
commercial nature ”. It would be helpful if the Commission  clarifies w hat is meant by “purely 
commercial nature” . The Commission could in fact consider alignment with the definition of 
“commercial communication” in Article 2(f) of  the Directive on electronic commerce (2000/31/ EC).  
 
9. Subsequently, we feel there is a degree of ambiguity in this text, and the recitals for that matter , 
what the intended meaning of “a political campaign organisation…established to achieve a specific 
outcome in an election or referendum” is. Brands may run ad campaigns that do not have exclusive 
objective of selling a specific product or service. Instead, it may wish to associate itself to a societal 
cause as part of its brand building, which is an important commercial objective. Yet others may see 
political motives behind such campaigns . For example, brands that make clear their stance on 
racism and other forms of discrimination  or brands that advertise their sustainability initiatives . 
Hence, commercial advertisers need more certainty, based on clear objective criteria,  on whether 
their advertising campaigns, specifically issue -based ads,  are considered in scope under this 
provision.  
 
10. In addition, Article 2(4)(h) is not entirely clear as to when will a natural or legal person represent or 
act on behalf of a person with a political objective . We believe this provision presents a risk to 
commercial advertisers who show support for societal issues, without the express intention to be 
political. Such issues may happen to align with similar issues that are discussed by political actors.  
Definition of “liable to influence”  
11. We have similar concerns with Article 2(b) which sets out second limb of the definition of political 
advertising. This is a broad definition, and we note t hat this does not provide exclusions like those 
stated in Article 2(a). We note the guidance text in Recital 17 but believe this does not go far enough 
to exclude legitimate commercial advertising that does not intend to influence the outcome of an 
election.  
 
12. Therefore, Article 2( b) presents an unnecessary risk to commercial advertising as it could be 
vulnerable to subjective interpretation. We would be concerned that, depending on perspective, an 
issue-based ad could be classified as political in nature and perhaps sufficiently political enough to 
influence the outcome of an election, even though this was not the original intention. 
Exclusion of advertising activities of certain charitable organisations  
13. We also advocate excluding advertising activities of certain charitable organisations from the scope 
of the regulation. This is because certain charitable organisations advocate on societal issues such 
as poverty or inequality which, as discussed above, can be construed as political issues but without 
an intention to influence official political outcomes . Such organisations do not present a risk to the 
issues the regulation seeks to address – namely threats to political integrity through disinformation 
and foreign interference. Additionally, their advertising activities  are unlikely to benefit from the 
regulation’s exclusion of “purely commercial” advertising given they are charitable in nature with a 
primary purpose of addressing a societal problem.

14. Therefore, this regulation would place  undue administrative and financial burdens on these 
organisations who may lack the necessary resources, such as staffing, legal counsel and funding,  
to implement compliance with legal requirements . It is important that these organisations can 
continue to advocate on societal issues without unnecessary restrictions or burdens.  
Our suggestions for amendments 
15. To conclude, we think that commercial advertisers should not be restricted in their issue -based ad 
campaigns. The proposed regulation in its current form lacks legal certainty on the exact scope of 
this regulation which presents practical difficulties for advertisers who need to plan and commit 
time, resource, and money to run certain campaigns. There is some concern that having to comply 
with the regulation’s transparency requirements would impede on the effective advertising area 
which would reduce effectiveness and increase costs. This in turn would have a detrimental impact 
on legitimate commercial activities.  
 
16. Therefore, in summary we call on the European Commission to make amendments to Article 2 and 
associated recitals:  
 
(a) In Article 2(2), the test for establishing whether an ad is political should be a cumulative 
assessment of (a) and (b), not alternative. Therefore, “or” should be replaced with “and”.  
 
(b) Regarding Article 2(a), more clarity and certainty is needed on who exactly is a political actor. 
Article 2(4)(g) should specifically exclude commercial organisations running issue-based ads 
or be supported by a recital that provides further explanation and clarity.  
 
(c) Article 2(4)(h) should clarify if it is just actors that are directly affiliated to a political party that 
are in scope of this provision an d a recital to clarify how this provision will operate in practice . 
 
(d) Regarding Article 2(b) and Recital 17, the regulation should make clear that remote or frivolous 
arguments, that could class an ad as liable to influence political activity , will not result in a 
commercial ad being within the scope of this regulation.  In addition , the wording “liable to 
influence” could be replaced with “intended to influence” as this provides more certainty and 
objectivity that Article 2(b) currently lacks.   
 
(e) Draft a new recital, like Recitals 18 and 19, to exclude advertising activities of certain charitable 
organisations from the scope of the regulation.

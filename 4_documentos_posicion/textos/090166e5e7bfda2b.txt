The ZAW appreciates the opportunity to comment on the proposal of the EU Commission for a 
Regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council on transparency and targeting of political 
advertising. As the overall representative of the advertising industry in Germany, the ZAW represents 
the interests of advertising companies, trade, media, advertising agencies as well as the advertising 
professions and market research. The present draft regulation affects the interests of many of our 
members and their affiliated companies in a special way – in particular against the background of the 
much too broad definition of political advertising, which also threatens to cover commercial 
advertising. 
Note: This paper reflects the current state of opinion within our membership. We therefore expressly 
reserve the right to make additions or changes. 
 
I. Definition of political advertising 
The draft regulation defines "political advertising" in its Article 2(2) as follows: 
“the preparation, placement, promotion, publication or dissemination, by any means, of a message: 
(a) by, for or on behalf of a political actor, unless it is of a purely private or a purely commercial 
nature; or 
(b) which is liable to influence the outcome of an election or referendum, a legislative or 
regulatory process or voting behaviour.”  
Since this is an alternative and not a cumulative enumeration, this definition in conjunction with 
Article 1, sentence 1, no. 1 ("service") could explicitly cover not only advertising by political actors, but 
also commercial advertising, as long as it is only suitable to influence the outcome of an election, etc. 
Lit. b) in the proposed version would result in an extension of the regulations aimed at political 
advertising to original commercial advertising, since a mere suitability to influence the outcome of an 
election or even a regulatory process can almost always be justified. For example, an advertisement in 
which a business enterprise addresses measures to combat global warming could be considered 
suitable to influence the voting decision of citizens in favour of those parties that particularly 
emphasise the issue of climate change in their election manifesto. Practically every business 
advertisement with evaluative, opinion-forming content has such a suitability. This is true not least of 
the increasingly popular so-called purpose marketing, in which companies communicate their stance 
on issues such as sustainability, ecology or social commitment. However, none of this changes the fact 
- and this is crucial - that commercial advertising ultimately always serves primarily to promote sales. 
The suitability to influence an election decision is therefore inherently unsuitable as a delimitation 
criterion. Against this background, it is also incomprehensible why the restriction of lit. a) second half-

sentence, according to which purely private or purely  commercial advertising - by or for a political 
actor - does not constitute political advertising within the meaning of the regulation, does not also 
apply to lit. b). 
In our opinion, the undifferentiated definition of political advertising cited at the be ginning and the 
accompanying extension of the scope of the regulation to commercial communication should be 
rejected for two main reasons: Firstly, such a broad definition of "political advertising" would extend 
the transparency obligations to commercial a dvertising, which does not actually aim to influence 
elections and political decision -making processes; secondly, the present definition could lead to a 
situation where, in the event of an existing Member State ban on political advertising (see Germany: 
for broadcasting and telemedia similar to broadcasting), certain forms of permissible commercial 
advertising would be covered by such a ban in the future. 
It should be recalled that, first and foremost, the Audiovisual Media Services Directive (AVMS 
Directive), the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (UCP Directive) and the Misleading and 
Comparative Advertising Directive already provide detailed requirements for the recognisability and 
attributability of (digital) commercial communication. The distinguisha bility and separation of non -
commercial, hence editorial content, e.g. journalistic contributions, is anchored in EU law with great 
clarity. Structural gaps in protection, also in the sense of a high level of consumer protection, are 
therefore not recognis able in this respect. These legal regulations are flanked by numerous 
specifications and concrete processes of the actors involved themselves, their associations and the 
institutions of self -regulation of the advertising industry. They aim to maintain tran sparency in 
commercial online advertising and to enforce it in practice as well as to weed out illegal or (further) 
self-regulatory inadmissible content of commercial communication in the online sector. 
According to recital 4, the objective of the Regulati on is: "to promote open and fair political debate 
and free and fair elections or referendums and to combat disinformation and unlawful interference, 
including from abroad". It goes on to say: "Political advertising can be a vector for disinformation, 
especially if the political nature and target is not clear from the advertising. Transparent political 
advertising helps voters better identify when they are presented with a political ad, who the provider 
of the ad is, and how they will be reached by a provide r of advertising services, so voters are better 
placed to make informed choices." Commercial communication, meanwhile, does not seek to disguise 
its objectives: apart from cases of surreptitious advertising - which is illegal under current law anyway 
- commercial communication is recognisable as being advertising. Of course, the advertiser will also 
make it clear whose advertising it is, namely his own - because this corresponds to his very own 
interest in marketing his product or service and attributing it to his company. For this reason, any form 
of commercial advertising must be excluded from the scope of the regulation; this must already be 
ensured at the level of definition.

We therefore propose in conclusion: Amend the definition of political advertising in Art. 2 para. 2 Draft 
Regulation in such a way that the criteria of lit. a) and b) must be fulfilled cumulatively - for example, 
by replacing the "or" with an "and".  Alternatively, the re -exception from Art. 2 para. 2 lit. a) 2nd half 
sentence Draft Regulation should be extended to lit. b) (i.e. pulled in front of the bracket, so to speak) 
and purely private or purely commercial (advertising) messages should be excluded from the scope of 
application of the Regulation. 
 
II. Media specific approach 
The regulation largely follows a "one -size-fits-all" strategy - i.e. it covers all political advertising, 
regardless of t he medium in which it is published - and, in the view of our members, does not 
sufficiently differentiate between the various media.  However, it is obvious that especially 
disinformation campaigns and manipulation of information in the online sphere in general and in social 
networks in particular can develop a different dynamic and must therefore be countered in a different 
way than is the case, for example, with advertising via print media or direct advertising. It is not without 
reason that the explanatory memorandum of the draft regulation repeatedly refers to technological 
change. Accordingly, a large part of the regulations seems to be geared exclusively to advertising in 
the online sphere, above all the requirements relating to targeting and amplification.  
Against this background, it is surprising that the legislator only very occasionally takes a differentiated, 
media-specific view, as for example in recital 30, which states: “The transparency requirements should 
also not apply to the sharing of information  through electronic communication services such as  
electronic message services or telephone calls, as long as no political advertising service is involved.” 
In the opinion of the ZAW, a "one -size-fits-all" approach is not suitable to take into account the 
differences between the individual (advertising) media . Therefore, with regard to direct advertising 
means such as telephone, letter or mailbox advertising or the personal encounter, explicit exemptions 
from the scope of application of the regulation should be incorporated, as it remains largely unclear 
how the implementation of the numerous regulations geared to the online sector should be 
implemented in the offline sector at all - a nuanced regulation, on the other hand, could avoid legal 
uncertainty. 
 
III. No disproportionate burden for publishers and advertisers etc. 
Finally, we would like to point out that the regulation should not impose disproportionate obligations 
on publishers and advertisers. Both with regard to the transparency requirements from Artic le 7 and 
the special requirements with regard to targeting and amplification from Article 12, it must be

reviewed and critically questioned whether and to what extent the proposed regulations are actually 
suitable and necessary to achieve the purpose of th e regulation and to achieve a level playing field 
between the different offline and online media. For example, it seems disproportionate to require the 
publisher to always include in the transparency notice "information on the aggregate amounts or other 
benefits received, in whole or in part, for the preparation, placement, promotion, pub lication and 
dissemination of the advertisement in question and, where applicable, the political advertising 
campaign, and the sources thereof". This obligation would result in business -relevant information 
having to be shared with direct competitors, without creating any real added value for consumers. The 
same applies to the obligatory disclosure of the parameters according to which an advertisement was 
placed, because this is regularly highly sensitive information from a competitive point of view. In 
addition, there would be the enormous additional financial and organisational effort to which the 
companies would be exposed by these obligations.

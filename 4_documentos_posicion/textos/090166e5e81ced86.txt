Preliminary remarks 
 
 
The Italian Banking Association (ABI) welcomes the opportunity to comment on the 
legislative proposal COM(2021) 664, intended to amend Regulation (EU) No 575/2013 
(Capital Requirements Regulation, CRR), as part of the proposal  known as “banking 
package”. This is expected to be the final step of the impressive wave of regulatory reforms 
launched following to the great financial crisis, and is an extremely important piece of 
legislation for European banks, encompassing a wide range of prudential measures thus 
in practice revamping the prudential framework.  
 
ABI acknowledges the effort of the European  Commission to draft a legislative proposal 
consistently transposing the Basel 3 finalisation package in the EU legislation while, at the 
same time, taking into account European specificities and the political mandate not to 
determine a significant increase in capital re quirements. The result is a quite balanced 
proposal, addressing large part of the key conc erns that the industry had raised in past 
months and that the ABI itself had noted
1.  
Anyway, in ABI’s view, the draft regulation could be further improved, with a view to the 
overarching goals of fostering equitable and sustainable economic growth, as well as 
employment and competitiveness of the EU economy. 
 
The pandemic crisis highlighted more than ever the crucial role of banks, and showed the 
importance of the banking regulatory framew ork and of appropriate measures to ensure 
that - without prejudice for financial stability - the financial sector can effectively support 
the real economy.  
Indeed, the new framework is expected to be applied at a time when the EU economy will 
be facing epochal challenges and the credit needs will be huge to finance the transition 
towards sustainability and digitalisation. This is particularly true in Europe, where banks 
are by far the main source of funding for corporates, SMEs and households.  
In addition, European banks are subject to increasing competitive pressure at international 
level, and also from less regulated entities. Maintaining a truly European banking industry 
to fund European businesses and trades is essential (especially for strategic sectors) to 
preserve the ability of the European economy and culture to compete on an equal footing 
with other geographical areas. 
 
In ABI’s view, the draft regulation should be assessed in this perspective. As a premise, a 
careful assessment of its impact would imply considering the overall effect, starting from 
the realistic assumption that the need for a dditional capital will not be limited to the 
shortfall to minimum capital requirements but will likely be commensurate with the market 
expectation that banks restore the current le vel of capitalisation. The long-term impact

In light of the above, ABI intends to provide comments on the legislative proposal and

to foster banks’ ability to serve the needs of the real economy, without jeopardising the 
soundness of the banking sector.  
ABI recommendations include both points related to measures present in the draft 
regulation and other points, not strictly pertaining to the Basel 3 finalisation package and 
not addressed in the proposal, which nonetheless can only be introduced by way of 
amendments to the CRR.  
In the present document, only the key issues are treated, illustrating ABI reasoning. This   
should be intended as initial feedback, while ABI remains fully committed to provide further 
advice in the course of the legislative process, with more detailed proposals for 
amendments on the identified topics (and possible additional points). ABI would be keen 
to discuss at any time its recommendations with the EU Co-legislators, the European 
Commission, banking and supervisory Authorities and all relevant stakeholders.  
Comments and recommendations involving leasing exposures have been elaborated 
together with Assilea - the Italian Leasing Association - and represent shared views of the 
two Associations.  
 
 
Specific comments and recommendations 
/g3
Implementation date 
The Commission sets 1 January 2025 as the implementation date for most articles of 
CRR3. ABI strongly recommends not bringing implementation forward in the final text. 
This also in consideration of the still weak macroeconomic context in light of the continuing 
difficulties due to uncertainties about the end of the pandemic and of the other factors 
that can affect households and businesses and the strength and speed of economic 
recovery (e.g. the price of raw materials, electronic components, energy).

Credit risk 
ABI commends the confirmation, in the legislative proposal, of measures already present 
in the EU legislation, which account for certain specificities of the European financial 
landscape: the so-called SMEs Supporting Factor  and Infrastructure Supporting 
Factor (Articles 501 and 501a CRR respectively ) and the specific risk weight for loans 
secured by salary or pension  within the retail exposure class under the standardised 
approach for credit risk (Article 123(4)). 
The confirmation of such measures in the final text is essential, even more in these 
challenging times, as they are effective in fostering the access to bank financing for certain 
categories of borrowers (such as the SMEs) or investments, otherwise penalised or not 
sufficiently incentivised under the international regulatory framework.  
 
Another European specificity is the limited availability of external ratings for corporates, 
which emphasises the importance of the prudential treatment of unrated corporates, 
especially if compared to jurisdictions where external ratings are not applicable for the 
purpose of capital requirements.  
In this regard, the legislative proposal introduces a key adjustment, i.e. the application of 
a 65% risk weight (RW) – instead of 100% - to exposures towards corporates qualified as 
‘investment grade’ (PD no higher than 0.5%), although only for the purpose of the 
calculation of the output floor by banks authorised to use internal models. 
ABI welcomes such adjustment and acknowledges its significance. Nonetheless, it has to 
be noted that this is set as a temporary arrangement, expiring in 2032. In ABI’s view, it 
should be permanent or maintained at least until sufficient coverage of external ratings is 
achieved for corporate exposures in the EU.  
 
Another framework of the utmost im portance is the definition of Credit Conversion 
Factors (CCFs) to be applied to off balance-sheet exposures. 
In this regard, while highlighting the key importance of the adjustment – although still 
temporary – provided for Unconditionally Cancellable Commitments (UCCs, for which 
the Basel text raises the CCF from 0% to 10 %) critical concerns remain as regards the 
treatment of exposures related to trade finance  and specifically the technical guarantees 
(Referred in CRR3 Article 111 and Annex1 bu cket 2 as performance bonds, bid bonds, 
warranties and standby letters of credit rela ted to particular transactions and similar 
transaction-related contingent items). Trade Finance is a crucial segment, as it serves the 
needs of European businesses engaged in both international and domestic activities. It is 
accordingly crucial that the prudential treatment of these exposures be kept in line with 
their actual riskiness, keeping the current 20% CCF instead of the 50% set in the proposal

(which would dramatically increase the cost of  these products and considered as overly 
punitive in regards of historically observed CCF). The conditions should also be ensured 
for the exclusion of the exposures related to trade finance from the scope of 
“commitments” where justified (under the premise that ABI welcomes, and recommends 
keeping in the final text, the exercise of the discretion, allowed by the Basel Committee, 
to exclude certain arrangements from the definition of “commitments”). 
As regards in general the CCF framework, the draft legislation – in the Article 111 (4) - 
seems to require the application of CCFs also  to contracts proposed by the bank but not 
yet accepted by the client, and to arrangements meeting the conditions for not being 
qualified as “commitments”. ABI recommends deleting or clarifying proposed Article 111 
(4), to ensure the consistency of the framework both internally and with the scope of CCFs 
defined by the Basel Committee. 
ABI also suggests reviewing the UCC definition, to better clarify its scope as, among other 
things, it currently can be read as only including contracts with consumers (introducing an 
unduly limitation of the scope).  
 
Undoubtedly mortgages are at the core of the banking activity in the EU, hence the 
profound changes that the legislative proposal introduces in their prudential treatment 
deserve maximum attention. ABI welcomes th at the draft legislat ion better defines, 
compared to the Basel text, the aspects pertaining to the valuation of the properties 
securing the loan, taking into account current market practices and the guidance already 
introduced at EU level (e.g. EBA Guidelines on loan origination and monitoring, 
EBA/GL/2020/06).  
It is essential that such elements are confirmed in the final text and namely: the possibility 
(to a certain extent) of upwards adjustment beyond the value at loan origination where 
justified, including in case of enhanced energy efficiency of the building; the possibility of 
carrying out the valuation and revaluation of properties by means of advanced statistical 
or other mathematical methods. 
On the other hand, ABI recommends reconsidering the proposed eligibility requirements 
(Article 124(2)) for the application of the so called “loan splitting” approach for risk 
weighting (Article 125(1)), und er the standardised approach . In ABI’s view, also loans 
collateralised by residential properties not used as primary residence of the borrower 
should be eligible for this treatment (insofar as the other criteria are met). 
Moreover, an important element introduced in the draft legislation in regard of the 
treatment of mortgages is the temporary preferential regime under Article 465(5), for the 
purpose of the calculation of the output floor by banks authorised to use internal models. 
ABI agrees that the features of the EU mortgage market, and especially the so called 
“double recourse”, justify under certain conditions the application of a lower risk weight.

ABI would argue that this also holds true for banks applying the standardised approach 
and wherever the eligibility criteria are met. ABI would therefore recommend applying the 
preferential regime to all banks and not subject to the exercise of a national discretion.  
Still on the real estate segment, in line with the Basel framework a new asset class has 
been specified, i.e. the Acquisition, Development and Construction (ADC) exposures. This 
is seen as a positive development compared to the discipline into force based on the 
definition of “speculative immovable properties financing”. Nonetheless, in ABI’s view an 
important element should be fixed, concerning the treatment of leasing real estate 
exposures. More precisely, the latter should be excluded from the ADC class whenever the 
underlying financing operation is aimed at selling or renting the immovable property in 
construction, as long as the borrower (lessee) provides the lender (lessor) with an already 
existing irrevocable obligation of a third part y to buy or rent the property. At least, the 
scope of the preferential RW (100% instead of 150%), currently limited to residential real 
estate ADC exposures, should be extended to include commercial real estate. 
 
An exposure class particularly hit by the new Basel standards is equity. On this topic, ABI 
welcomes the important adjustment that the Commission introduced in the proposal but 
would nonetheless draw the attention to the need for greater granularity of the framework 
(e.g a preferential RW for listed equities should be introduced) and for keeping into account 
diversification effect (as regards diversified private equity portfolios, the 190% RW 
currently applied is considered more appropriate).  
Furthermore, to preserve the variety of business models, to allow for diversification of 
banking groups’ activities and to strengthen  the level playing field, ABI recommends 
extending the 100% risk weighting for strategi c holdings, irrespective of the prudential 
approach used (standardised or internal model). The scope of the holdings falling under 
Article 495a should be clarified and widened to ensure that all relevant instances are 
encompassed. In any case, ABI recommends introducing a permanent fix for the treatment 
of equity holdings of insurance undertakings held by non-conglomerates (currently 
addressed by Article 471 CRR, expiring in  2024). Equity investments in insurance 
undertakings proved to be not riskier than other equity holdings, hence the deduction from 
CET1 currently envisaged (being insurance undertakings qualified as financial sector 
entities) would be unduly penalising.  
It would also be appropriate to provide clar ification on the conditions under which some 
exposures or instruments mentioned in art. 133 par 1, point c iv) d, e, and par. 2, 4, 5, 7 
are classified as equity exposures or instruments or fall in scope for preferential 
treatments. Examples of concrete cases should be identified and clarified.

In theme of Credit Risk Mitigation (CRM) , ABI recommends aligning the eligibility 
criteria applied for the recognition of the credit protection under the standardised and the 
IRB approaches. The mixed BCBS framework seems not explained by conservativism and 
ABI deems the proposed modification all the more be warranted in light of the new EU 
regulation on minimum loss coverage of NPLs. The latter stipulates different treatments of 
defaulted exposures, depending on whether they are secured or not, based on the 
approach applied for the calculation of capital requirements. Differences in the eligibility 
of the credit protection among the approaches therefore result in different loss coverage 
requirements among banks for the same exposure. In addition, the current non eligibility 
of the “other physical collateral” under the standardised approach heavily penalises low 
risk products as leasing exposures. Th e proposed solution would address the 
abovementioned level playing field concerns as well as (a) the unintended consequences 
of the changes introduced in the scope of application of the CRM rules, following to the 
changes in the scope of the IRB approaches, and (b) the impact of limitations in the 
recognition of collateral under the standardised approach on the exposures treated under 
IRB approaches via the output floor. 
 
Operational risk 
The operational risk framework is completely revamped under the Basel 3 finalisation 
package, among other things disallowing the use of internal models. 
In this regard, ABI remarks the importance of the exercise of the discretion, provided by 
the Basel text, to set the value of the Internal Loss Multiplier (ILM) at 1 for all banks. 
ABI would strongly recommend maintaining such arrangement which is coherent with the 
Basel framework in the final text, as the application of the ILM as defined in the 
international standards would unduly affect banks for past losses, irrespective of corrective 
actions taken, and would therefore not result in enhanced risk sensitiveness.  
On the contrary, an element genuinely enhancing the risk sensitiveness of the framework 
would be the consideration of the risk mitigation effects of insurance contracts, which ABI 
suggests introducing in the methodology. For this purpose, the deadline for the EBA study 
should be anticipated to be consistent with the timing of application of the new framework. 
For banks with a business model where commissions from services are the prevailing 
source of revenues, a change  in the methodology for the calculation of the Service 
Component should be introduced, in order to avoid a significant competitive disadvantage 
and a huge increase (in some cases 100%) in the capital requirement, not justified in 
terms of risk exposure.

Market risk, CVA, counterparty risk, Securities Financing Transactions (SFTs) 
The legislative proposal turns the new Basel market risk  framework, so-called FRTB 
(Fundamental Review of the Trading Book), currently applied – and not completely - for 
reporting purposes only, into the framework to be used for the calculation of the capital 
requirements for market risk. 
Given the degree of internationalisation of the trading activity, the level playing field with 
other jurisdictions in this field is key. Competing on an equal footing is essential in order 
to maintain significant European players, and this is all the more important in light of the 
objectives of the Capital Markets Union, i.e.  fostering the development of EU financial 
markets to ensure diversified sources of funding for EU enterprises. Being the EU the first 
one implementing this framework among major jurisdictions, ABI remarks the importance 
of empowering the Commission to delay the application of the new methodologies and/or 
introduce adjustments to ensure the level playing field, as set in the draft regulation.   
Under this premise, ABI would highlight concerns on specific elements of the framework. 
As to the banking book/trading book boundar y (reference is made to Articles 104 and 
106), while important refinements have been introduced and should be confirmed – e.g. 
regarding the own credit spread risk – the discipline of internal hedges needs to be 
improved (since for credit and equity risk exposures to be recognised as internal hedge, 
to “perfectly offset” the risk is required, which is a too strict condition that is hard to be 
met in practice). 
Another key issue is the treatment of funds. Despite certain positive developments, the 
look-through approach remains very demanding and not always applicable, hence a 
reasonable alternative is needed as the treatment currently envisaged is overly punitive 
for well-diversified products like funds (and would discourage banks from maintaining this 
business). 
Another important issue to ensure consistency and equal treatment among banks is the 
alignment of the treatment of sovereign issuers under the internal models with the 
standardised approach (i.e. removing the 0.03% PD floor currently envisaged under 
internal models). 
Several other aspects of the methodology need further consideration from the legislators, 
as refinements would be warranted, to ensure better alignment to the Basel text (including 
clarifications provided over time) and to a ddress implementation issues experienced by 
banks. Banks gained valuable experience working on this framework for years (for impact 
studies, dialogue with supervisors, and be ing now subject to reporting requirements) 
which can be profitably factored in the upcoming legislation.

Another framework which has been complete ly revamped is the methodology for the 
calculation of the capital requirements for CVA risk. ABI had always pointed out the crucial 
importance of keeping the exemptions currently applied at the EU level and therefore 
appreciates their confirmation in the legislative proposal and recommends maintaining in 
the final text. Anyway, it has to be noted that other provisions in the banking package 
proposal (including COM(2021)663), setting reporting obligations and mandating the EBA 
to identify “excessive” CVA risk, are likely to result in jeopardising the effect of such 
exemptions. ABI would take the occasion to remark that the prudential framework should 
ensure that supervisory policies cannot overwrite the choices of the co-legislator. 
 
As to the counterparty credit risk , ABI welcomes the adjust ment introduced in the 
legislative proposal, allowing to apply a lower multiplier (alpha=1 instead of alpha=1.4) in 
the formula used to calculate the exposure under the standardised approach (SA-CCR), 
introduced in CRR2 and applied as of June 2021. Anyway, according to the proposal, this 
adjustment is temporary and shall be applied only by banks using internal models for the 
purpose of the calculation of the output floor.  
As the industry has highlighted in past months, there is a strong case for setting the 
multiplier at 1, including level playing field considerations based on how the SA-CCR has 
been implemented in other jurisdictions (namely the US). ABI would therefore recommend 
making the change permanent and providing for the application of the lower multiplier by 
all banks to calculate the exposure under SA-CCR, not only in the context of the output 
floor.  
Finally, it has to be noted that the standardised approach for the Securities Financing 
Transactions (SFTs) is penalising because neither the high quality of the counterparty 
nor the short maturity of the transactions is taken into consideration. This creates a 
massive gap with the internal  model approach and would increase Sovereigns’ funding 
costs. In this regard, ABI recommends using the real maturity instead of a fixed maturity 
under F-IRB and especially to introduce a sh ort-term maturity adjustment under the 
standardised approach for credit risk to al low assign appropriate risk weights for these 
very short-term exposures. It would certainly be deserved as SFTs are critical to the well-
functioning of financial market and in particular the efficiency of European sovereign debt 
markets. 
 
Output floor 
The output floor represents a qualifying and probably the most discussed element of the 
new framework. ABI wants to highlight the relevance of the level playing field issue in this 
regard. It is of the utmost importance not to apply the output floor in the EU in a stricter

way than prescribed by the Basel standards. To this end, the application of the output 
floor at consolidated level only, i.e. not at the level of the single legal entity, is key./g3 
It should be borne in mind that applying the floor at the highest level of consolidation 
would foster a truly European banking market, while application at the individual level 
would increase fragmentation, reduce banking groups’ flexibility when allocating capital 
internally and make market consolidation even less attractive. 
ABI would therefore urge the EU legislators to confirm the approach taken in the legislative 
proposal. 
Furthermore, due to the conservative calibration of the securitisation standardised 
approach (SEC-SA), the output floor would ve ry significantly reduce or undermine the 
efficiency of securitisation tr ansactions. Considering the delays on the review of the EU 
securitisation framework which is an essential part of the CMU Action Plan, and without 
prejudice to its outcomes, an appropriate transitional arrangement for the purpose of the 
Output Floor calculation should be provided, in order to mitigate the unintended impact of 
the SEC-SA calibration and until its review in the context of the securitisation review is 
carried out. 
 
Leverage Ratio 
In this regard, we believe that the requirement should take into account the specific 
structure of cooperative banking groups and networks, where local banks are affiliated to 
a central institution.  
 
Scope of prudential consolidation 
ABI also recommends ensuring that the planned ch anges in the “ancillary activities” 
definition and scope of prudential consolidation , which were introduced in the first 
place to capture core services outsourcing and fintech, do not compromise traditional 
banks’ desire to diversify their activities by imposing on them unsustainable levels of 
capital over their non-banking activity.
 This would entail a level playing field issue, as such 
burden would only affect activi ties carried out by a legal entity which is part of an EU 
banking group. In this regard, ABI remarks that the regulation should always be based on 
the principle “same activities, same risk, same rules”.

Proportionality Aspects 
The aim to further embed proportionality in the framework is appreciated. However, ABI 
sees room for further improvements, for ex ample in regard to the new standardised 
approach for credit risk  and the proposed extension of ESG disclosures to Small & Non-
Complex Institutions.   In those matters, greater doses of proportionality should be 
included.  
 
Issues not addressed in the legislative proposal 
As said in the premise, the legislator should ensure that the banking regulatory framework 
be oriented towards the EU policy objectives. At present, one of the goals to which most 
efforts are devoted is the transition to a more sustainable economy. To this end, the 
prudential framework can play a fundamental role, as also acknow ledged by the draft 
regulation which mandates the EBA to assess the link between sustainability and riskiness 
with a view to factoring it in the regulation itself in the future. 
In this respect, ABI recommends accelerating the adoption of appropriate incentives, by 
means of introducing a capital relief ( SAF - Sustainable Adjustment Factor ) which 
could initially be applied to those asset clas ses for which lower risk in consequence of 
features of sustainability has already been demonstrated. This is the case for energy 
efficient buildings mortgage loans and equivalent leasing contracts, as per studies 
conducted by the European Mortgage Federation.  
The mandate should be maintained to EBA to study other asset classes in view of possible 
extension of the scope of the relief. 
*** 
Besides, ABI would strongly recommend extending the deadline set in Article 500 of CRR 
(as amended by CRR2), for the temporary measure allowing banks - in the context of the 
calculation of capital requirements using intern al models - to mitigate the impact on the 
Loss Given Default parameter (LGD) in case of massive disposals of NPLs.  
Indeed, the penalising impact on LGD (hence on capital requirements) discourages banks 
from massive NPLs disposals. At a time wh ere the COVID pandemic has heavily affected 
the European economy, and the volume of NPLs is expected to rise (albeit less than feared, 
thanks to the impressive public and private measures deployed), disposals should instead 
be incentivised, to avoid the piling up of huge stocks of new NPLs in banks’ balance sheets. 
Against this backdrop, ABI calls for the postpo nement of the deadline for disposals to be 
included in the scope of Article 500, until at least 31.12.2024. Such temporary measure, 
justified by the current extraordinary circumstances, would be fully consistent with EU 
policies and in particular the EU Commission NPL Action Plan of December 2020, which 
emphasises the importance of the secondary market to tackle the NPL issue.

*** 
ABI would also recommend reviewing the prudential framework with respect to the 
recognition of minority interests as consolidated CET1. A wider recognition of minority 
interests would entail positive effects, since, in general, the availability of capital backing 
assets, such as loans to households and enterprises, allows banks to enhance the support 
to the real economy - and consequently growth and employment. More specifically, a wider 
eligibility of minority interests would encourage parent companies to enlarge the capital 
base of subsidiaries and strengthening subsidiaries’ capital, therefore increasing financial 
stability. While remarking that the regulation should be, to the extent possible, neutral 
with respect to the organizational structure of groups, it might be worth noting that a 
refinement of this framework would also be beneficial to consolidation projects, removing 
current disincentives (the limited recognitio n of minority interests might represent an 
obstacle to consolidation initiatives, since, depending on the envisaged setup of the 
banking group post-consolidation, the resulting capitalisation can be lower than the initial 
one of the involved entities). 
*** 
A few refinements would also be appropriate as regards the treatment of Collective 
Investment Undertakings (CIUs) as collateral  under the credit risk mitigation 
framework, taking into account the applicat ion of the Look Through Approach as an 
alternative to the Mandate-Based Approach by banks, in line with the treatment of 
exposures in the form of units or shares in CIUs.  
Regarding the application of the Fallback approach to calculate the risk-weighted exposure 
amount for exposures in the form of units or shares in CIUs, banks should also be allowed 
to opt for deduction from CET1 instead of 1250% risk weight (for all their CIU exposures 
and not only those falling in scope of Article 132b).  
*** 
Last but not least, an intervention on the treatment of life insurance policies under 
the credit risk mitigation framework  is considered appropriate. More specifically, by 
applying a safety-net (e.g. volatility haircut calculated in line with current regulation 
applying for the constituents), banks should be  allowed to consider as eligible also life 
insurance policies whose surrender value is not declared by the company providing the life 
insurance and is non-reducible.

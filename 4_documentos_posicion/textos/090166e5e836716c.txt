Comment by the Association of Public Services and Enterprises Austria 
(VÖWG) 
 
on the European Commission’s Proposal for a Directive of the European 
Parliament and of the Council on improving working conditions in platform 
work 
 
Digitalisation, technical developments and the emergence of new business models have 
led to rapid, profound, and still ongoing changes in our labour markets. One of these changes 
is the importance of digital platforms both as economic players and in the day-to-day reality of 
many people's work. The digital transformation has not only altered the types of services that 
are offered online, but it has also changed how those services are provided. Platform work has 
blurred the boundaries between producers and consumers. Likewise, the question of  a 
demarcation between dependent and independent work has become more difficult than in 
“traditional” work. The question of employment status is one of the most pressing issues 
concerning platform work as the access to various rights and aspects of social security is linked 
to it. 
The number of people working through platforms is increasing – they are offering 
services (e.g. ride -hailing, food delivery, microtasks, etc.) online via hosting platforms in 
exchange for payment. Flexible employment opportunities like these often lead to a false sense 
of self -employment, called ‘dependent contracting’, and precarious working conditions. 
Dependent contracting describes the situation of a worker who, though not an employee of a 
company, is in a position of economic dependence on and under an obligation to perform 
duties for that company. This is one of the main reasons for precarious working conditions. 
Precariousness can arise from low wages, working hours and other conditions, e.g. in some 
cases equipment necessary  for work has to be provided by the platform worker, not by the 
platform.  
The fact that workers are defined as self -employed although being de facto employees 
of their contracting entity is a major problem. This false self -employment is particularly 
problematic regarding the access to social security. While for employees the contributions for 
social protection are usually shared between the employee and the employer, self -employed 
are responsible for the full contribution. This results in  digital labour platforms to externalise 
costs onto many people working through platforms and them not being able to access certain 
strands of social protection, e.g. unemployment insurance.  
It is evident that individuals misclassified as self-employed providing services online face 
more worrying issues than those providing services in the “traditional” economy. For these 
employees in the online segment, there is no sufficient legislative framework, providing them 
with legal certainty, while in the offline economy there is. This gap benefits online platforms 
since they are not forced to comply with minimum standards and leads to a di sadvantage for 
the traditional economy. It is necessary to establish a level-playing field – what is illegal offline 
must also be illegal online.

Whereas we appreciate the Commission’s intention of improving working conditions in 
platform work  and we  consider the Commission’s proposal is an overall  highly welcomed 
approach, we do see room for improvement in certain aspects. 
We welcome the proposed scope of this directive (set out in Article 1) as it aims to 
improve working conditions for platform work performed within the Union, regardless of the 
place of establishment of the digital labour platform. This provides for legal certainty for person 
performing platform work and prevents circumventing this directive solely by choosing a place 
of establishment elsewhere. It also helps maintaining the high level of social security standards 
in Austria, regardless of whether the digital labour platform was established in a third country 
or not. 
The definitions set out in Article 2 seem overall appropriate, however we consider that 
the definition of a digital labour platform in this directive and the definition of an online platform 
set out in Article 2 of the currently discu ssed Digital Services Act shall not be mutually 
exclusive. We emphasize that provisions and obligations proposed in the Digital Services Act 
regulation shall also be applicable to digital labour platforms if the criteria are met.  
The definition on what is considered platform work is broad, which is considered to be 
appropriate as it reflect the everchanging nature of how platform work is and will be provided. 
The distinction between persons performing platform work and platform workers is suitable as 
the concept of algorithmic management and decision-making needs to be taken into account 
for both employed and genuinely self-employed individuals. 
The information obligations in Article 12 and the provisions on algorithmic management  in 
Chapter III of this directive apply to all persons performing platform work, irrespective of the 
contractual relationship between the individual performing work and the digital labour platform.  
The legal presumption of an existing employment relationship as set out in Article 4 of 
the proposal is considered an important step in improving working conditions in platform work 
as it provides a solid legal foundation for all platform workers affected. In accordance with the 
possibility to rebut the legal presumption set out in Article 5, the directive is without prejudice 
to de facto self-employed individuals. However, we consider the criteria used for determining 
the control a digital labour platform exerts over an individual performing platform work does 
not adequately reflect the constant state of transformation the digital business world is going 
through. The introduction and implementation of new solutions on how control can be exerted 
on individuals performing platform work should be anticipated by either extending the list of 
criteria used or consider the fulfilment of one of the proposed criteria as sufficient. 
We support the platform’s proposed obligation to provide transparency on automated 
monitoring and decision -making systems (see Article 6)  and the use thereof , which is 
supportive in decreasing information asymmetries between platform workers and the platform. 
However, we emphasise that platforms must not misuse the provisions in Article 6 ( 3) to 
excessively update the information referred to in Article 6 (2), which could lead to an overflow 
of information and additional burden for platform workers. 
The provision of access to relevant information on platform work as set out in Article 12 
is necessary for labour, social protection and other relevant authorities to ensure compliance 
as well as for representatives of persons performing platform work to f ulfil their function. 
However, the six-month-period in which the information must be updated by digital labour 
platforms (twelve months for micro, small and medium -sized enterprises) does not take into 
account, that within this period potential claims and entitlements in accordance with national 
labour law could  no longer be made. This has serious implications on social, in particular, 
unemployment insurance as individuals need to be given legal certainty over their employment

status as quickly as possible to ensure entitlement to certain strands of social security and 
unemployment benefit. In this regard, the directive needs more clarification to ensure legal 
certainty for all parties involved. 
Especially large digital companies are exploiting legislative gaps in EU legislation and 
are hereby distorting competition and further strengthening their already dominant market 
power. As the range of digital services provided via online platforms is expected to grow further, 
an appropriate, legislative regulation is  a priority.  Individuals working under precarious 
conditions that might be left without access to certain strands of social security ultimately have 
to be supported by the public sector which puts national authorities and administration under 
additional pressure. 
To this end, we consider the proposal for a directive as an important first step.  It is a 
question of both protecting people working through platforms – e.g. against dismissal, ensuring 
minimum wages, occupational health and safety or working time regulations – and 
safeguarding core values of the European Union and fair competition.

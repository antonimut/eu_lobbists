ESBG response to the European Commission’s consultation on the 
Capital Requirements Directive (CRD) proposal 
 
 
The European Savings and Retail Banking Group (ESBG) welcomes the 
opportunity to comment on the proposal for a Directive of the European 
Parliament and of the Council amending Directive 2013/36/EU as regards 
supervisory powers, sanctions, third -country bra nches, and environmental, 
social and governance risks, and amending Directive 2014/59/EU. We would 
like to share  the following considerations which we hope will be taken into 
account by the European Commission.  
 
 
1. Overall assessment: 
 
ESBG positively assesses the legislative proposal published on 27 October 2021 
by the European Commission . It transposes the final elements of the Basel III 
reforms in the EU regulatory framework, and it pursues other prudential and 
supervisory objectives. We recognise that the Commission has sought to take 
into consideration most of the issues raised so far both by policy makers and 
the financial industry in order to find a well-balanced and realistic starting point 
for the co -legislators. Nevertheless, ESBG is of the opinion that the proposal 
requires further adjustments. 
 
The effort from the Commission to take into consideration specific 
characteristics of the European financial system and the Europe an banking 
market is noticeable, while respecting the objectives set forth by the Basel 
Committee. Similarly, the additional time given to properly implement the 
reform in the processes, IT-systems, and practices within the banks, signals 
sensitivity in the context of the recovery post Covid-19, during which banks will 
continue playing an important role.  
 
The capital impact according to the Commission’s own assessment is expected 
to lead to a weighted average increase in institutions’ Minimum Capital 
Requirements of 6.4% to 8.4% in the long term (by 2030), after the envisaged 
transitional period (n.b. According to the EBA’s estimates from its 2019 Report 
it estimated the impact to be a 13.1% increase based on an EU specific scenario 
which has been adopted in this proposal – ILM = 1, SME SF and CVA exemptions 
maintained). Although the Pillar 1 impact might be lower if compared with 
previous estimates, some Member States are still going to be particularly hit. 
Moreover, one should be careful with averages. Individual ESBG members with 
specific business models, e.g. with low risk mortgage and SME -portfolios, will 
be severely more affected than others.  One should also not forget that the 
impact study did not anticipate the Pillar 2 impact, e.g. the SSM already warned

that the neutralisation of ILM would require pillar 2 correction based on 
historical losses. 
 
 
2. Fit & Proper: 
 
We believe that t he implementation of the Basel standards within the EU 
regulatory framework should reflect the proportionality principle, also by taking 
into consideration the risk nature, scale and complexity of the activities of 
European credit institutions. This would allow financial institutions to carry out 
their activities under a non -detrimental regulatory framework which 
strengthens the European banking sector – the backbone of the EU’s ‘real 
economy’. A disproportionate regulation causing huge  regulatory burden 
would negatively impact banks, which could even push resources away from 
customer service. 
 
The banking package introduces detailed procedural rules for the suitability 
assessment of members of the management body. We propose to amend (or 
even delete) Articles 91a – 91d. The stated rationale for the Fit and Proper 
proposals is to give supervisors the necessary powers to enforce prudential 
rules but we believe that some adjustments should be made . ESBG members 
cannot find any evidence in either the EBA guidelines or the Commission impact 
assessment that justifies to only allow ex -ante assessments across the 
European Union. We strongly advocate to allow national discretion to use either 
the ex-ante or ex-post assessment criteria. We do not find evidence to support 
using just one regime, or  where the present ex-post regime has created a 
problem. Additionally, it contradicts the proportionality principle explicitly 
mentioned in the explanatory memorandum under number 2 if the proposal is 
to i ntroduce mandatory regulations that provide for an ex -ante assessment 
procedure for members of the management body in its supervisory function 
(supervisory board) to be carried out by all institutions and mandatorily include 
the key function holders in the supervisory regime. 
 
We therefore call on the European Commission to allow for both assessment 
processes to be allowed at the national level, and that the competent authority 
keeps to the assessment period mandated in EU law. For these Member States, 
some credit institutions have little influence on the nomination or selection 
process and so these institutions cannot carry out an ex-ante assessment. We 
also advocate that the responsibility for assessing the KFHs should remain with 
the institution.  
 
In detail:  
 
1. Ex-post vs. ex-ante 
 
Although ex -ante assessment is already applicable in some Member States, 
there are twelve Member States with an ex-post regime for the notification and 
assessment of members of the management body in its supervisory function 
(supervisory board). It is necessary to maintain this ex-post procedure in these 
 
 
Member States due to several legal requirements governing the election 
procedures for members of the supervisory board.  
 
Any ex-ante assessment procedure of members of the management body in its 
supervisory function (supervisory board) carried out by the institutions 
themselves (Article 91a (2)) or by the competent authorities (Ar ticle 91b (8)) 
would be unfeasible to comply with for some savings banks in the European 
Union, due to the recruitment process of members of the supervisory board.  
 
For some of our members it is true that national law stipulates that only the 
representation body of the municipality selects the members of the supervisory 
board. The candidates are not known in advance. Institutions get to know the 
new members only after their appointment / after they are elected. The same 
is true for ex-officio members, which are members by law e.g. because of their 
main occupation within the local public authority (mayor of the town), due to 
national rules that serve to ensure the fulfilment of the corporate purpose of 
the undertaking or of the public mandate. Every person who considers standing 
for election needs to have their suitability assessed before the election, as there 
would not be time to do the assessment between preparing the final voting lists 
and the vote itself.  
 
If, in these Member States, only an ex -ante assessment is allowed, then every 
single candidate on the municipal electoral list would be required to complete 
the assessment process, even though only a fraction will be me mbers. The 
efforts required, and expense needed for all of these assessments will not be 
proportionate to the expected benefits. However, the problem would arise that 
the democratic process of the selection of the supervisory board members by 
the municipality would be interfered with. 
 
In several Member States, some members of the supervisory board are elected 
by the employees of the institutions. Lists are prepared containing a range of 
persons, and – as is normally the case with elections – it is not poss ible in 
advance to predict which persons will actually then be elected to the 
supervisory board. Those institutions are not in a position to assess the 
candidates and submit notifications before the election / appointment.  
 
For all these reasons, the ex-ante suitability assessment should not apply at all 
for supervisory board members. The existing practice of the supervisory board 
members to perform the suitability assessment only following the appointment 
(ex post) has also proven its worth. If the NCA ma kes critical ex post remarks 
that there are concerns with regard to expertise, the candidate can also attend 
seminars to eliminate any deficits following their appointment. All NCAs get the 
necessary powers to remove inadequate members, Article 91a (7). An d if the 
NCA requests to dismiss a supervisory board member, a deputy is available in 
the first instance. Therefore, there is no need to change the assessment 
procedure. 
 
Internal assessment by the institution (Art. 91a (2) CRD): 
 
 
 
In some Member States (e.g. Germany, France, Italy), any internal assessment 
of supervisory board members by the institution (whether ex-ante or ex-post) 
would contravene national law. These laws stipulate that the municipal trustee 
is responsible for the selection of the members of the supervisory board and 
responsible for ensuring their suitability. The members are democratically 
legitimised, and the institutes must accept this decision. The institutions 
themselves ensure the suitability by special training offers for new members . 
However, if the institute decides whether the members elected by the municipal 
trustee are suitable or not, then a discrepancy arises with regard to the special 
governance of the public-law institutes. A change in this structure would mean 
a violation on  the autonomy of municipalities. For this reason, not the 
institutions, but the competent authorities alone may assess the suitability and 
if necessary order appropriate consequences in these cases. 
 
In these cases, the proposed exception that urgent cases can be examined ex-
post is not sufficient. Therefore, a general exception from this obligation in 
Article 91a (2) and in Article 91b (8) in accordance with Article 88 (2) sub-para. 
5 CRD is required for the named cases, regardless of the size of the institution 
(Article 88 (2) sub -para. 5 CRD states: “Where, under national law, the 
management body does not have any competence in the process of selection 
and appointment of any of its members, this paragraph shall not apply.”). 
 
2. Assessment Period 
 
The proposed 120-working days assessment period for supervisory suitability 
assessment (Art. 91b (4) CRD) is far too long and would lead to long vacancies. 
This proposal does not take into consideration the particularities of the 
recruitment process used in some Member States or the legal notice period 
between the proposal (usually by the Nomination Committee) and the 
appointment in case of Management Board members). It will be difficult to have 
such a time frame for both one-tier and two-tier systems.  
 
In addition, it has to be taken into account that in some cases the Shareholders’ 
meeting is in charge of appointing new members , and c onsidering that the 
convocation of the Shareholders’ Meeting must be published one month prior 
to the meet ing, we suspect that competent authorities will not be able to 
provide a final Fit & Proper ex-ante decision within such a legal timeframe.  
 
The current issue that banks are facing is that there is a delay in the ex -post 
assessment period, and for this reason the supervisors should apply the current 
EBA Guidelines which allow for an assessment period of 4 months1. 
 
For those Member States where the ex -ante process is already mandatory 
according to national law and considering that the ex -post assessment will

3. Key Function Holders 
 
Furthermore, we are against widening the suitability assessment by the 
competent authorities to so -called key function holders. This should remain 
within the responsibility of the board of the institution.  Applying suitability 
standards comparable to those for managing directors to key function holders 
would be disproportionate.  
 
Currently, the r egulatory framework (including assessment) for key function 
holders is laid down by the Joint ESMA -EBA Guidelines on the suitability of 
members of the management body and key function holders. These state that 
assessing the suitability of key function hold ers is the responsibility of the 
institutions. At the same time, the EBA Guidelines on Internal Governance 
stipulate that the management body is responsible for setting, approving and 
overseeing the implementation of the selection and suitability process f or key 
function holders. It is not necessary to change this approach, neither by 
expanding the role of supervisory authorities in the assessment of key function 
holders nor by including new provisions to this matter in the CRD. 
 
Whilst it is perfectly just ifiable from a regulatory point of view for the 
management body to have a CRD framework on the top of regulatory 
requirements since the management body is at the highest accountability level 
within an institution, key function holders should be treated dif ferently. Key 
function holders are strongly connected to the organisational, strategic and 
business-related particularities of an institution. According to the current 
approach of the EBA GL Fit&Proper, key function holders are the roles which 
have a signi ficant influence over the direction of the institution but are not 
members of the management body (for example, heads of internal control 
functions and other positions identified on a risk -based approach by the 
institutions). This definition is wide enough  to give institutions the necessary 
flexibility in taking into account the above -mentioned particularities when 
defining their key function holders. 
 
As outlined by the current definition, having internal control functions defined 
as key function holders w ithin an institution – regardless of its strategy and 
internal organisation – is appropriate from a supervisory point of view. 
However, going beyond this and laying down a legal requirement that more 
positions are considered as ‘key function holders’ could  conflict with the 
internal organisation and strategy of an institution. For example, depending on 
the business strategy and customer portfolio, in one institution the head of 
retail may be a key function holder, in other institutions not. This differs even in 
group structures where the administration of customer portfolios is allocated 
across the group in different group entities.  
 
 
 
Interfering with the organisational and strategic framework of an institution is 
a significant drawback in including the requ irement for performing fit and 
proper assessments for key function holders in the CRD. The requirements and 
profiles designated in the institutions for recruitment to such positions are 
sufficient. All institutions are likely to be aware of the particular importance of 
the key functions, with the result that they will already ensure in their own best 
interests that the relevant key function holders meet all of the requirements as 
fit and proper persons. It will not be possible to avoid function holders who 
prove to be unsuitable after the event by introducing a suitability assessment. 
For this reason, we do not consider it is necessary to change in any way the 
current approach regarding key function holders.  
 
In addition, the assessment by the competent authority would cause a 
considerable effort for all large institutions and their subsidiaries - this applies 
to both: ex post and ex ante. Moreover, an ex-ante assessment would also lead 
to long vacancies. At least we suggest a significant reduction of the assessment 
period by the competent authority for KFH. 
 
Another reason why ESBG would oppose a deviation to the currently applied 
system (joint ESMA-EBA Guidelines) is that there would be a large increase in 
the administrative burden and that ban ks need to be allowed to appoint Key 
Function Holders under national law provisions. Immediate replacement of Key 
Function Holders, as set out in Article 91d (1), does not provide any possibility 
for large institutions to immediately appoint a KFH if it is urgently needed. 
 
4. Others 
 
Procedural provisions:  The provisions in Article 91a and 91b of the draft, 
especially the proposed procedural regulations on the deadlines regime, have 
a relatively high degree of detail, which contradicts the character of a directive.  
Here, the Member State is deprived of the necessary scope for implementation. 
This is a violation of the principle of subsidiarity. Furthermore, we see no need 
for regulatory technical standards or implementing technical standards, cf. 
Article 91b (9), (10). 
 
No extension to subsidiaries:  Article 91b (8) and Article 91d (1) include also 
subsidiaries of large institutions into the ex-ante assessment procedure. We ask 
for exempt subsidiaries from these obligations if they are not themselves “large 
institutions”. Otherwise, a disproportionate burden would arise. 
 
Definition of Senior Management (Article 3 (1) Point 9 ): This new definition 
widens the perimeter of the persons concerned and no longer corresponds to 
the definition already used in some Member States, such as France, and which 
corresponds to the concept of “Direction Générale” (basically CEO and COO). 
This concept is used to determine the population liable in some circumstances 
such as the reporting of the independent control functions. ESBG t herefore 
calls to stick to the previous definition. 
 
 
 
 
3. ESG risks and sustainability: 
 
Institutions have a direct interest in supporting the transition to a sustainable 
economy. However, the tasks of banking supervisors may not be extended to 
urging institutions to adapt their business models to the respective Union’s 
policy objectives or to “broader transition trends” (CRD VI Art. 76(2), 87a(4,5), 
104(1m)). “Green” or “sustainable” investments are not per se less risky and do 
not necessarily generate stable earnings. Decisions on this must therefore be 
left to the institutions. 
 
According to the EBA’s report on management and supervision of ESG risks 
(EBA/REP/2021/18), the CRR III definitions (Art. 4 (1 52d) should be clarified 
to the effect that thes e risks are no separate risk categories but represent 
factors that materialise through other categories of financial risks, such as 
credit, operational and market risks. This is particularly important for the 
quantification of the exposure, which will be only meaningful if aligned with the 
existing risk types. Since there are no methodologies or industry standards for 
the quantification of the exposure at a more granular ESG risk level (i.e. social 
risk or governance risk), we would propose to disregard it. 
 
Strategies and processes to assess and maintain internal capital now refer to all 
risks to which institutions are or might be exposed in the short-, medium- and 
long-term time horizon (SREP, Art. 73 (1), 74 (1) and 76 (1)). While these time 
horizons are appropriate and necessary for strategies  especially on climate 
risks, introducing those diverse time horizons for the ICAAP (thus for all 
risks)would be unnecessary and counterproductive. We propose to skip the 
specification of the time horizons in Art. 73 (1) and 74 (1), as Art. 76 (1) already 
contains a requirement to consider also medium and long-term impacts of ESG 
factors within the strategies. 
 
 
4. Components of Internal Capital (ICAAP) (Art 73 CRD): 
 
In November 2018 the ECB published its Guide to the internal capital adequacy 
assessment process (ICAAP ) requiring a normative and an economic 
perspective. The declared purpose of this paper is to state the ECB’s 
understanding of the ICAAP requirements of Article 73 CRD and to assist 
institutions in strengthening their ICAAPs in order to ensure business continuity 
of banks  but also to duly take into account economic value considerations . 
Article 73 CRD stipulates that institutions shall have in place proper strategies 
and processes to assess and maintain on an ongoing basis the amounts, types 
and distribution of internal capital that banks consider adequate to cover risks. 
 
Footnote 41 of the ECB Guide is recommending (in general) a confidence level 
of risk parameters of 99.9%. In addition, a holding period of one year for all risk 
types was deemed as adequate by the ECB. In simple terms, the confidence 
level reflects the statistical likelihood that a specific event will not occur within 
a certain period in the future. In this context a required 99.9% conf idence level 
and a holding period for one year means that certain losses would occur once 
 
 
in 1.000 (!) years. Obviously, such a requirement is not consistent with the 
above-mentioned business continuity approach of the ECB (in other words: a 
going concern approach) as this confidence level of 99,9% is at least equal 
compared to the level of the requirements in the CRR for eligible own funds 
(Common Equity Tier 1, Additional Tier 1 and Tier 2 Capital). However, pursuant 
to the ECB ICAAP Guide (Example 5.1) Tier 2 Capital instruments shall not be 
part of the internal capital of an institution as it cannot be deemed available to 
cover losses. Moreover, in the ECB’s supervisory practice AT 1 instruments are 
often not accepted either. 
 
This expectation of the ECB is excessive as on the basis of the confidence level 
of 99,9 % such losses – as just mentioned – would occur only once in 1.000 years 
(which corresponds to a resolution scenario, thus a gone concern approach) . 
According to the ECB the IC AAP shall be imple mented in a proportionate 
manner. In this sense such a likelihood cannot justify the exclusion of Tier 2 and 
AT 1 instruments from internal capital at all. A non -allowance of those 
instruments in internal capital confronts the banks with another increase of cost 
of capital and is not justified. This approach would then have a negative impact 
for their global competitiveness.   
 
Against this background it has to be clarified in Article 73 CRD that the internal 
capital for ICAAP can consist of all eligible own funds according to the CRR as 
well as of economically comparable components or - alternatively - of the 
institution’s net present value assets. 
 
▪ Alignment of Pillar 2 capital composition: 
 
As the commission proposes minor amendments to the Pillar 2 requirement (Art 
104a) and states that “CRD V aligned the nature of regulatory capital that banks 
must hold to meet the Pillar 2 capital requirement with the minimal capital 
composition of the Pil lar 1 capital requirement”, we wanted to take the 
opportunity to advocate for exploring a further alignment of the composition 
of Pillar 2 capital guidance (Art 104b) with both Pillar 1 and Pillar 2 requirement.  
 
Although the current wording of Art 104b CRD V is already a good starting 
point as it leaves the composition of Pillar 2 guidance to the discretion of the 
authority. However, we believe an amendment to further align Pillar 2 guidance 
capital composition to the Pillar 1 capital requirement (as was done by CRD V 
regarding the Pillar 2 requirement) would be beneficial and ensure consistency 
in the Pillar 1 and Pillar 2 capital framework.  
 
 
5. Supervisory powers: 
 
The Commission’s proposal expands the list of supervisory powers available in 
the CRD to competent authorities to cover operations such as acquisitions by 
a credit institution of a material holding in a financial or non -financial entity 
(new Chapter 3 in t he current Title III), the material transfer of assets or 
 
 
liabilities (new Chapter 4) and merger or divisions (new Chapter 5). These 
supervisory powers should ensure that competent authorities are notified in 
advance, have at their disposal all the necessa ry information to perform a 
prudential assessment of these operations, and can ultimately oppose to the 
completion of operations detrimental to the prudential profile of the supervised 
entities undertaking them. 
 
While we understand that competent authorit ies need to be informed in 
advance in case of various important operations in the banking sector, we 
doubt that the proposed notification/approval requirements and additional 
supervisory powers will effectively improve the current supervisory framework 
and avoid undue administrative burden. The operations proposed to be subject 
to notification/approval requirements are partially already covered by the 
current legislative framework (both at EU level and in the member states) and 
will insofar overlap with cur rent applicable  requirements and procedures. 
Particularly, the proposed notification requirement for acquisitions of qualifying 
holdings exceeding 15% of the eligible capital of the acquirer in the proposed 
Art. 27a CRD overlaps to a certain degree with th e current notification 
requirement of acquisition of qualifying holdings in the current Art. 22 (1) CRD. 
In such case a double supervisory assessment is required for the same 
operation which is not consistent with the goal of avoiding administrative 
burdens, neither for the supervisory authorities nor for the supervised 
institutions.  
 
Before introducing new supervisory tools for the above-mentioned operations, 
we would prefer the Commission (and the supervisory authorities) to perform 
an assessment of simp lifying and reducing complexity in the current 
acquisitions framework in the financial sector.   
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
About ESBG (European Savings and Retail Banking Group) 
 
ESBG represents the locally focused European banking sector, helping savings 
and retail banks in 21 European countries strengthen their unique approach that 
focuses on providing service to local communities and boosting SMEs. An 
advocate for a proportionate approach to banking rules, ESBG unites at EU 
level some 900 banks, which together employ more than 650,000 people driven 
to innovate at roughly 50,000 outlets. ESBG members have total assets of €5.3 
trillion, provide €1 trillion in corporate loans (including to SMEs), and serve 150 
million Europeans seeking retail banking  services. ESBG members are 
committed to further unleash the promise of sustainable, responsible 21st 
century banking. Our transparency ID is 8765978796-80.

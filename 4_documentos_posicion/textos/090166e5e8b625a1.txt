ESWET contribution to the call for evidence on the 
Environmental impact of waste management 
 
ESWET – the European Suppliers of Waste to Energy Technology represents 
companies that have built and supplied over 95% of the Waste -to-Energy plants 
in operation in Europe. It seeks to promote the technology, which, within the frame 
of the waste hierarchy, recovers energy from waste that would otherwise end up 
in landfills. 
ESWET welcomes the Commission roadmap on the Environmental impact of waste 
management and is fully in support of waste reduction. However, while waste 
prevention is the most desirable goal in the long-term, waste generation is still on 
the rise in Europe and needs to be dealt with , including non -recyclable waste. 
Indeed, it is estimated that around 142 million tonnes of residual waste will need 
to be treated in 20351. 
Following the waste hierarchy (Article 4, Directive 2008/98), priority needs to be 
given to waste reduction; preparing for re-use and recycling. However, not every 
waste is recyclable – waste can be contaminated or polluted, and most materials 
cannot be recycled indefinitely – and have to be recovered, or at the last resort, 
disposed of in landfills. 
 
The detrimental impacts of landfills 
Landfills are one of the main emitters of methane emissions, a GHG gas 84-times-
more-potent than CO2 over a 20-year period,2 but may also have other detrimental 
impacts on public health and the environment, with long-term risks of leakages of 
other pollutants to ground water, air and soil. Moreover,  landfill sites occupy 
valuable lands, reducing the availability of space for both urban areas and natural 
habitats for wildlife.  The rehabilitation of lands previously occupied by landfills 
requires time and investments , especially considering that there is a total of at 
least 500 000 operational and closed landfills in the EU. Moreover, the impact on 
human health and the environment is long -lasting: even a closed landfill site is 
issuing leachate and GHG emissions for up to 25 years3. Incidents, such as fires, 
are also a substantial risk of widespread pollution.4

currently is and should still be considered as the last resort, following the waste 
hierarchy.

Member States (with some landfilling rates of municipal waste going beyond 70%,

The role of Waste-to-Energy in the circular economy  
Waste-to-Energy (WtE) is a recovery operation that safely treats non -recyclable 
waste that would otherwise be landfilled. Moreover, WtE plants turn residual waste 
into electricity and heat f or the community and industries . In 2018 in Europe, 
Waste-to-Energy plants exported around 40 billion kWh electricity and 90 billion 
kWh heat, which provided 18 mi llion citizens with electricity and 15.2 million 
citizens with heat. 6 As an example, Waste -to-Energy plants provide more than 
30% of the overall heat production in the district heating networks supplying 
Copenhagen and 16 neighbouring municipalities. This contribution to the circular 
economy was acknowledged in the Communication from the Commission on the 
role of Waste-to-Energy in the circular economy in 2017. 
WtE is also an essential part of the full waste management value chain. Indeed, 
the treatment of residual waste include s residues from recycling facilities,  as 
acknowledged by the recycling sector itself7. Furthermore, not all waste going into 
recycling is actually recycled, and need to be treated safely in WtE or disposed of 
in landfills8. On the other hand, m etals separated and recycled from incineration 
bottom ash are included in the amount of municipal waste recycled and contribute 
to the Member states’ waste management targets9.  
Furthermore, metals and minerals are recovered from incineration bottom ash and 
contribute to avoiding the extraction and processing of virgin materials10. Minerals 
constitute about 80-85% of incineration bottom ash, metals 10-12% (steel, iron) 
and non-ferrous metals 2-5% (mainly aluminum, but also copper and zinc)11. 
Using secondary raw materials is part of the transition to a circular economy, 
where waste is perceived and treated as a resource, and aligned with the Green 
Deal’s objectives and the 2050 climate neutrality target. Ferrous and non-ferrous 
metals, such as copper or aluminum, are necessary materials in the energy

The safe treatment of plastics 
Plastics, like any other waste streams, cannot be recycled eternally. Then, the 
most efficient treatment option  when recycling is not possible  anymore is 
incineration with energy recovery (and to some extent gasification), operated 
under the most stringent environmental standards. On the contrary, the landfilling 
of plastic waste present significant risks of leakages into the environment and the 
ocean. A 2016 study by McKinsey & Company and Ocean Conservancy found that 
one of the most effective tools for the reduction of plastic leakage is Waste -to-
Energy technologies, and that recycling alone is not a sufficient solution16. 
Indeed, WtE plants are a viable waste treatment solution to extract pollutants from 
the circular economy and prevent contamination. As seen during the COVID 19 
pandemic, household residual waste can represent sanitary risks, with for instance 
face masks and gloves. Here, WtE play a significant hygienisation role that cannot 
be overlooked in the current sanitary context. 
Another study highlights that “inappropriate or uncontrolled recycling is also one 
of the major release sources of substances of concern present in plastics, such as 
brominated flame retardants, polybrominated biphenyl ethers, toxic metals, etc., 
that contaminate lands”17, and that open burning or uncontroll ed incineration of 
plastics is another major source of contamination for both soil and air. 
On the contrary, we n eed to underline here that modern incinerators having the 
right controls on combustion conditions are capable of meeting very stringent

Improving waste management 
As explained above, WtE contribute to the circular economy, with for instance the 
recovery and recycling of minerals and metals from incineration bottom ash. WtE 
plants also provide flexibility and adaptability: non -recyclable waste is 
heterogeneous and i ts composition can vary depending on seasonality, design 
changes, climate, variations in materials, but can always be treated in a sound 
environmental manner in current installations. Alleged alternatives promoted at 
EU level such as Material Recovery and Biological Treatment (MRBT) facilities have 
shown their limitations18, will always need WtE to hygienise residual waste – or in 
the worst case, landfills.  
The esta blishment of a wel l-functioning secondary raw materials market , as 
announced in the New Circular Economy Action Plan  in 2020, will contribute to 
incentivise the use of recycled materials  and lessen the EU’s reliance on energy -
intensive virgin materials, th rough for instance  mandatory recycled content in 
products. 
Reducing GHG emissions also rel ies on the implementation of the current EU 
legislation. Indeed, the full application of the current EU waste legislation, including 
waste management targets, would deliver 150 Mt CO2eq of GHG emissions savings 
by 203519.  
The existing legislation also provides guidelines and tools that are not sufficiently 
employed by Member states and European municipalities, such as the polluter pays 
principle (PPP). According to th e European Court of Auditors (E CA), the current 
application of the PPP does not ensure that polluters cover the full cost of 
pollution20. Indeed, whil e “the costs of waste management, including for the 
necessary infrastructure and its operation, shall be borne by the original waste 
producer or by the current or previous waste holders”  (Article 14, Directive 
2008/98), the application of this principle is inconsistent. The reinforcement of the 
PPP, through Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) for instance, would help 
reduce the costs of waste management for loc al authorities and taxpayers , and

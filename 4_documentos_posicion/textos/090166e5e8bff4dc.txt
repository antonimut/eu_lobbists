Policy Hub’s Contribution to the Consultation on the Revision of the Waste Framework Directive  
Amsterdam | 22 February 2022 
 
The Policy Hub - Circularity for Apparel and Footwear welcomes and supports the European Commission’s proposal 
to revise the Waste Framework Directive (WFD).   
 
The ambition of the Policy Hub is to improve the circularity of the textile sector by maintaining as much as possible 
value of resources used in the production of textile products. To support th is aim, we advocate for full 
harmonisation between all EU Initiatives for the textile sector as well as full harmonisation between the Member 
States’ i mplementing national initiatives for the textile sector.  
For the WFD in particular, this means that the revision of the WFD must be completely aligned with the criteria set 
in other EU Initiatives for the textiles sector, such as the Sustainable Product Initiative including the Digital Product 
Passport,  Substantiating Green Claims Initiative, Empowering the Consumers Initiative, the EU Taxonomy, the PEF 
methodology and other upcoming policies that will impact our sector . In light of this, we would like to contribute 
as per below.  
 
We see the  revision of the WFD as an opportunity to incentivise and accelerate solutions for textiles end -of-life 
management to enable the circular economy .1 We expect that the WFD will leverage waste prevention and will 
boost the market for reused textiles and recycled fibers production , especially in light of the mandatory separate 
collection of textiles obligation applicable as of 2025. To handle these increased  volumes of collected post -
consumer textiles safely and efficiently, we will need a well-functioning and harmonised policy framework.  
 
In this regard, the Policy Hub’s key recommendations are as follows:  
 
1. Set relevant definitions and requirements for textiles;  
2. Harmonise sorting criteria for both reuse and recycling;  
3. Boost sorting and recycling infrastructure dramatically;  
4. Harmonise the separate collection of textiles;  
5. Harmonise EPR requirements for textiles at EU level; 
6. Set targets and other measures for waste prevention; 
7. Ensure consistency of other regulatory initiatives, such as the Sustainable Product Initiative, Waste 
Shipment Regulation, and the end-of-waste criteria.   
 
Please see below our recommendations in full detail.  
 
1. SET RELEVANT DEFINITIONS AND REQUIREMENTS FOR TEXTILES 
 
The differentiation between waste and non-waste materials as laid out in the current WFD is unclear for textiles, 
which results in different interpretations across EU Member States.2 A definition about when a textile garment 
becomes waste or ceases to be waste is still missing. Also, specific criteria do not exist today for an objective 
classification of discarded textiles either for reuse or recycling. This lack of definitions directly hampers a 
homogeneous classification of garments, the application of the waste hierarchy and consequently creates a 
barrier for its movement. Thus, we recommend to: 
• Define what ‘’textile waste’’ is and provide other sector -specific definitions, such as ‘’textile -to-textile 
recycling’’, ‘’reusable’’, ‘’recyclable’’, etc.

HARMONISE SORTING CRITERIA FOR BOTH REUSE AND RECYCLING  
Clear and harmonised requirements for sorting of post-consumer garments are currently lacking in the EU 
including, for example, common waste sorting categories. This further slows down the consistent feedstock 
streams for recycling. It will be key to harmonise sorting criteria for both reuse and recycling as part of the 
revised WFD:  
• Harmonisation of collection categories for post-consumer textiles could enable large scale circulation of 
garments across regions both for re-use and recycling. The EU should harmonise ‘collection categories’ for 
separate textile collection, e.g., clothes destined for re-wear or recycling. There is a need for harmonised 
rules of what makes items best destined for re-wear or collection for recycling. In addition, given that 
footwear has such different requirements, a separate collection category specifically for shoes as well as 
‘others’ might be valuable as well. 
• Harmonised sorting standards for 'prepared for recycling' should be provided to enable high-quality 
recycling.  
 
2. BOOST SORTING AND RECYCLING INFRASTRUCTURE DRAMATICALLY  
It is estimated that currently only about 1% of textile waste is recycled into new textiles, such as clothing.3 With 
the 2025 separate collection of textiles target entering into force, the volume of collected waste could increase to 
5.8 million tonnes in EU27.4 To effectively deal with this increase of volumes, we need to scale sorting and 
recycling technology in the EU which is currently still not able to deal with such an increase and address other 
roadblocks. To make recycling economically viable, companies need sufficient volumes of well-sorted textile 
waste of certain qualities on a regular basis. Therefore, the Policy Hub recommends the following:  
• Incentivise high quality automatic sorting and innovative recycling technology. The EU must support 
through finance or other initiatives the building of sorting, especially automated sorting, and recycling 
infrastructure, specifically the technology to enable the separation and recycling of mixed materials. The 
research, development, and infrastructure for sorting and recycling should be prioritised and scaled out 
as soon as possible - so that once textile collection starts under an EPR scheme, we have the capacity to 
deal with the increased volumes of textiles. Therefore, investing into high quality sorting and recycling 
innovations will be key, e.g., sustainable innovations that can decontaminate waste streams and recycle 
blended materials. 
• Building a strong and effective Infrastructure requires the involvement of the sorting and recycling 
Industry. The EU Comission should ask those industries, what their needs are to achieve strong and 
effective recycling. It should also make sure that those needs are reflected in the requirements set for the 
industry.  
• Build sorting and recycling hubs that provide capacity to deal with the collection levels of post-consumer 
products. Such hubs should consist of the textile relevant stakeholders like sorting and recycling industry, 
brands, manufacturers and  the designated Producer Responsibility Organisations (PRO's) under the 
upcoming EPR schemes in various countries among others.

The Policy Hub urgently calls for harmonised rules on separate collection of textiles across the EU, which will be 
essential for a circular closed loop system. Separate collection of post-consumer garments must become as easy 
as possible for consumers throughout the EU. We further recommend the following:   
• The revised WFD should establishing uniform national textile collection systems. To better understand 
what the best way to do this is, we recommend the European Commission to start the conversation with 
relevant stakeholders to gather their expertise.   
• Separate collection in store should remain possible. Also, separate collection by online retailers and 
online platforms should remain  possible in all EU countries (e.g., online take-back-systems). 
• The Commission should also address the issue of proliferation of unharmonised labelling requirements 
for waste disposal across the EU Member states, which is impeding sorting and recycling efforts. 
Proliferation of disparate national labelling schemes and individual Member States' requirements have 
already caused market fragmentation, burdened companies and confused citizens. In this regard, there 
should be harmonised and simplified rules for communicating information to the consumers (e.g. 
harmonising recycling symbols and material codes; separate collection, limiting the use of text on 
packaging). 
 
Role of consumers in separate collection 
Citizens and consumers have a role to play from preventing waste to contributing to the separate collection of 
waste. It is therefore important that the right information on textile waste management is available, accessible 
and up-to-date. More broadly, consultation and coordination on communication to consumers should be a 
central part of ongoing discussion and the revision of the WFD. The focus should be on streamlining and avoiding 
an overload of information, as well as harmonisation of separate collection practices in the EU. In light of this, we 
recommend the following:  
• It will also be important to incentivise consumers to participate in the separate collection process and to 
strengthen their awareness of the value of used garments. This could be done through clear instructions 
on how to sort post-consumer textiles to ensure that textiles are collected effectively. 
• The revision of the WFD provides a chance to design an EU-wide approach to waste sorting and empower 
consumers by enabling them to participate in separate collection efforts. The underlying problem is that 
whenever consumers don’t have a clear indication of how they should sort - recyclable products end up 
in the residual waste bin. To meet the EU’s separate waste collection target, there is a need for a 
harmonised rules across Member States that will educate consumers and boost the joint recycling 
efforts.  
 
In general, we see the WFD as the tool that can build the foundations to uniform and harmonised standards. If 
the EU does not align collection, sorting, and labelling requirements for waste disposal, business and citizens will 
struggle to achieve the ambitious goals set by the Circular Economy Action Plan. To fulfil recycling targets, we will 
need to empower consumers in their recycling efforts and ensure that divergence of national systems will not 
impede the free movement of goods and materials. 
 
4. HARMONISE EPR REQUIREMENTS FOR TEXTILES ACCROSS THE EU  
 
 
The Policy Hub highly welcomes the EU’s objective to implement the polluter pays principle and extend EPR to 
textiles. We believe that EPR will further enable circular economy in our sector and will help to deal with the 
increased volumes of collected textiles as per 2025 separate collection target. For a successful implementation of 
EPR schemes in our sector, we recommend the following:  
 
 
 
 
 4 
• To ensure harmonised EPR requirements for textiles across the EU. Several EU Member States are 
currently developing national EPR obligations (e.g., France, Sweden, the Netherlands, Spain, Italy, 
Belgium). We see such fragmentation of rules across the EU as a large risk slowing down the transition to 
more sustainability.  
• The EU-wide and harmonised framework should include:  
o A set of common principles, a clear scope (e.g., garments, home textiles, footwear) and 
allocation of responsibilities among all relevant stakeholders, a robust mechanism of 
monitoring, control, and surveillance; and a transparency and traceability system.  
o Rules on the appointment of PRO's in each country to create efficiency through scale, volume 
and experience.  
o Rules regarding the obligations of marketplaces to ensure compliance with EPR.  
o Eco-modulation of fees should be harmonised to encourage and reward circular design. The 
eco-modulation structure must be consistent with complementing EU Initiatives, such as the 
upcoming EU's Sustainable Product Policy Initiative, the EU Taxonomy and the development of 
the PEF methodology. This will involve harmonising criteria for what producers need to take 
into account when designing and putting their products on the market as well a securing a 
consistent fee structure for eco-modulation, e.g., if the fee should be based on the same criteria 
such as recycled content, durability, etc. If this is not harmonised, producers will have no 
financial incentive or practical resources to put more sustainable products on the EU market – 
they will pay the max flat fee under EPR schemes instead of getting a real impact in terms of 
developing and marketing more sustainable products. 
o Reporting: harmonise what data points producers must report on (to base the amount 
producers should pay). Also, harmonisation of the reporting format requirements is of 
paramount importance. 
• A shared approach should be used, whereby not all the accountabilities are placed on producers.  
• Financial support beyond EPR taxation should be employed. EPR schemes struggle to be profitable 
alone, thus additional financial mechanisms of support should be considered.  
 
For more recommendations on EPR for textiles, see our joint position paper.  
 
5. SET TARGETS AND OTHER MEASURES FOR WASTE PREVENTION 
 
We welcome the Commission’s efforts to focus on waste prevention and set relevant targets for this. As highlighted 
in the European Environmental Agency’s study, targets can set the stage and calibrate the ambition of the overall 
waste prevention strategy.5 In this regard, we recommend the following:  
• Any targets set for reuse or recycling should be done in consultation with the industry experts. 
• Such targets should be progressively increased over time  in line with the development of relevant 
infrastructure in the Member States and the market demand for secondary raw materials.  
• To realise any target for reuse or recycling, infrastructure for sorting, resell, and recycling must be in place.  
• Other prevention measures should be utilised: 
o Extend life cycle of products:  this should be done through setting the relevant durability 
requirements aligned with the Sustainable Product Initiative.  
o Focus on stimulating circular business models: business models such as repair, remanufacture, 
resell, re-commerce, product-as-a-service systems, and others will incentivise longer use of 
products and help to safeguard the value of products and make sure that they are used until the 
actual end of their service life.  
o Use waste prevention campaigns to educate consumers.

6. ENSURE CONSISTENCY WITH OTHER REGULATORY INITIATIVES 
 
To guarantee the efficiency and effectiveness of the provisions on waste prevention, it will be important to ensure 
the coordination with other initiatives impacting textiles: 
• Regarding design: The WFD lays the foundations for waste prevention policies, which is linke d to product 
"eco-design”. We believe that textile products should be designed and produced with their full lifecycle in 
mind, seeking to prevent waste and reducing impact along their lifespan to support the principles of circular 
design. Thus, the coordin ation between the upcoming Sustainable Products Initiative needs to be 
ensured. For more information regarding the SPI, see our position paper.  
• Regarding recyclability: The WFD lays the foundations for boosting the textile to textile recycling industry, 
which is dependent on post-consumer garments effectively being recyclable. The eco-design requirements 
and defin itions anticipated under the Sust ainable P roduct Initiative must be aligned. Also, setting 
requirements to design for recyclability in order to make sure an efficient recycling process is enabled, will  
be important for reaching the goals of the revised WFD.  
• Regarding end  of waste:  Harmonized end -of-waste criteria at EU -level, recognizing textile waste as a 
resource, are needed to develop a high quality and effective secondary raw materials market for textiles. 
It is also essential to guarantee mutual recognition between Members States when national regulations (or 
other measures) for end -of-waste conditions exist (recognition of the principle of mutual recognition of 
national legislation in the absence of harmonisation). 
• Regarding the movement of waste: we call the EU to make sure tha t any provisions regarding textiles 
waste are also aligned with the proposed Waste Shipment Regulation.  
• Regarding EU’s waste hierarchy: Further clarity on the  waste hierarchy principle in the WFD , should be 
considered.  Today, a significant amount of textile waste leaves the EU with the declared purpose of reuse, 
which, if this is not possible, ends up being disposed in third countries as there is no traceability about its 
destination (reuse or landfilling). Therefore, without undermining the waste treatment hierarchy, WFD 
should explore how to allow textile recycling when reuse is not assured. We recommend the Commission 
to define additional guidance for the interpretation of the waste hierarchy for textiles products to always 
ensure that textile waste is treated with the highest effect to eliminate waste, e.g., reuse might not always 
be most appropriate path from an environmental perspective if these end up in landfills in third countries 
and instead of being recycled in the EU.

ABOUT  
The Policy Hub – Circularity for Apparel & Footwear unites the apparel and footwear industry to speak in one 
voice and propose policies that accelerate circular practices. Launched in 2019, the Policy Hub has five partner 
organisations: Sustainable Apparel Coalition (SAC), Global Fashion Agenda (GFA), Federation of the European 
Sporting Goods Industry (FESI), Textiles Exchange, and ZDHC Foundation. In total, these organisations represent 
more than 700 apparel & footwear stakeholders including brands, retailers, manufacturers, and NGO.

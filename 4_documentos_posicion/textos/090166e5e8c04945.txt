 Basel 3 regulation and impact on Trade Finance transposition text 
(CRR) 
 
European Commission issued on the 27th of October 2021 a proposal of transposition of December 2017, Basel III text  
We would like to highlight two main issues that would impact heavily the Trade Finance market and seek for clarification 
of the texts on few others. 
1. The CCF treatment for tender, technical  guarantees (referred in CRR3 Annex bucket 2 as Performance bonds, bid 
bonds, warranties and standby letters of credit related to particular transactions and similar transaction -related 
contingent items)  which moves from 20% CCF to 50%  (and undrawn facilities for tho se guarantees which moves 
from 0% to 10% CCF)  while these instruments show a  historical track record of very limited claims and even less  
payments or defaults (see Appendix  ICC Trade register). It is worth precising that even if the Applicant is considered 
to be in default as per Basel definition, it doesn’t mean that the guarantee issued by order of this Applicant will 
automatically be claimed as the company generally continues to perform under their commercial contacts.   Even in 
case of claim, it doesn’t mean that it will be effectively paid  as the applicant can be anyway in a position to fulfill its 
contractual obligations.   
 These guarantees are widely used in real economy in order to secure national, intra -Europe and international 
commercial exchanges. In addition, trade finance deals are documented deals which are managed within a 
harmonized legal framework applied by the international trade community. This increase on CCF treatment is a major 
concern for Trade Finance, not only for banks but significantly for corporates in term of access to such mean to secure 
their commercial contracts (higher cost impacting their gross margin and competitiveness).  
 
(Réf. Article 111 and Annex I)

➢ Immediate consequences are:  
- Create a slow-down effect on the activities of companies because of increased costs and risks for corporates - 
regardless their size - and banks, leading all stakeholders to operate in a less secure manner with de-facto 
negative consequences on competitiveness, jobs and commercial contracts/projects. For example, the 
Engineering & Construction sector is witnessing low profitability and margins coupled with widening mismatch 
between cost and bid price. Under this backdrop, any upward revision in CCF for contract related guarantees 
will have far reaching implications on the project cost which may further widen the margin and slowdown the 
recovery from 2020 recession.         
- Limit the credit lines / facilities made available by European Banks to support the economy , leaving room for 
non-European banks to increase their footprint and market share with European clients 
- Diminish the level of guarantees agreed through contracts and consequently increase risks to do business and, 
from a risk based approach, overall risks involved in trade transactions  
- Create a competitiveness issue, giving major advantage to non-European financial institutions that would still 
apply a lower RWA and therefore the increased reliance of European corporates to non -EU-banks 
- This transfer of activity outside Europe will also translate in a loss of expertise and competences as European 
banks are traditionally large providers of ICC mitigants, guarantees particularly.  
 
➢ Proposal: Maintain CRRII existing CCF1 for these guarantees or enlarge the EBA mandate regarding the review 
of the application of Annex I, based on updated ICC data if the EU authorities estimate more historical data is 
required. 
 
2. Effective maturity vs 2,5 fixed maturity.  Even if the text foresees the possibility to apply the effective maturity for 
short-term self-liquidating trade finance transactions, as well as issued or confirmed letters of credit, it is not fully 
clear whether this is applicable when the F-IRB method is used . Indeed, there is the possibility for the competent 
authorities to allow for the use of effective maturity for some or all exposures. However, article 162(1) refers to the 
article 143 which rules for the permission to use the IRB Approach, and more precis ely to (i) the permission to use 
internal ratings, (ii) to the permission to use LGD own estimates or (iii) to the permission to operate material changes 
in existing models. The text doesn’t contemplate situations where, because of mandatory application of  the 
Foundation approach for some asset classes, where only the probability of default can be modeled, these asset 
classes can’t benefit from a competent authority’s decision in regards to the use of effective maturity.  The new 
element in the proposed text is that the F-IRB method will have to be applied to Large Corporates (>500m turnover) 
which are the largest user of trade instruments.

Appendix 2: Technical guarantee (referred in CRR3 Annex bucket 2 as Performance bonds, bid bonds, 
warranties and standby letters of credit related to particular transactions and similar transaction-related 
contingent items) 
 
  
There are 2 types of guarantees, financial guarantees and the most commonly used under Trade Finance 
business, the Technical guarantees (also called market guarantees). 
Definition :   
A Technical guarantee is an irrevocable undertaking, issued by a Bank upon request of its client (the Seller) in 
favor of a beneficiary (the Buyer) where such Bank, as Guarantor, agrees to pay the beneficiary in case of 
default of the Seller with respect to its technical obligations under the underlying commercial contract.  
By opposition with a technical guarantee, a financial guarantee issued by a Bank by order its client (most of 
the time the Buyer) is aiming to cover the latter’s payment obligations under the underlying contract. A 
financial Guarantee is covering financial obligations  while a technical guarantee is covering the Seller’s 
performance under an underlying contract. 
Purpose of technical guarantees : 
Technical guarantees are positioning along with the commercial relationship/contract (“the Underlying 
Contract”), from the bid offer up to the final receipt, covering then the whole duration of the underlying 
contract. They will enable, at different steps of such Underlying Contract: 
i) ensure the seriousness of the offer (Bid guarantee),  
ii) cover the reimbursement of the advance payment made by the buyer prior to delivery (Advance 
Payment guarantee), 
iii) cover the good execution by the seller of its contractual obligations and ensures its technical capacity 
to honor the contract (Performance guarantee),  
iv) cover the reimbursement of the last payment term in case the equipment delivered turns out to be 
defective during the warranty period (Retention money guarantee). 
 

 
  
7 
 
 
 
 
Example:   
The workflow for issuance of a performance guarantee (direct scheme*) is as follows: 
1. Signature of a commercial contract between a Supplier and a Buyer covering manufacture of an 
equipment. 
2. The Buyer requests from the Seller a performance guarantee covering the seller’s due fulfillment of 
his contractual obligations (i.e. manufacturing the equipment in accordance with the Underlying 
Contract), the Supplier (the Applicant) then instructs its bank to issue such performance guarantee  
3. The guarantee is issued in favor of the buyer (the Beneficiary) enabling the latter to call on the 
guarantee in case of seller’s non fulfilment of its contractual/technical obligations 
 
* A guarantee can be issued directly (direct issuance as scheme above) or through another bank 
(indirect issuance). In case of indirect issuance, the bank may either request another bank (often the 
beneficiary’s bank) to issue locally the guarantee under its counter-guarantee, or issue a guarantee 
at the request of another bank acting as counter-guarantor.

unconditionally cancellable commitment’ means any commitment the terms of which permit the institution

Comment Bucket 5:  
The Art. 5(10) definition of an Unconditionally Cancellable Commitment needs to be clarified since it may 
unduly restrict the scope to exposures to consumers by referring to consumer law. The definition of UCC 
should thus be aligned with the CRRII to avoid such a restriction. 
The “full extent allowable under consumer protection” provision is usually used to justify that even if there is 
a prior notice requirement in order to be able to cancel the line, the later can be considered as UCC since the 
prior notice is required by consumer protection law. It is noteworthy however that the Code Monétaire & 
Fin.  also imposes prior notice before cancellation of exposure to entity other than consumers and the 
existence of this prior notice may exclude the item from UCC, even though being by Law.   
Once this clarification is made, there is an overlap between the Art. 5(10) definition of an Unconditionally 
Cancellable Commitment and the other items of bucket 5 that are “of which items”. 
➔ Amendment: 
unconditionally cancellable commitment’ means any undrawn credit facilities comprising agreements 
to lend, purchase securities, provide guarantees or acceptance facilities which may be cancelled 
unconditionally at any time without notice, or that do effectively provide for automatic cancellation 
due to deterioration in a borrower's creditworthiness. Retail credit lines may be considered as 
unconditionally cancellable if the terms permit the institution to cancel them to the full extent 
allowable under consumer protection and related legislation

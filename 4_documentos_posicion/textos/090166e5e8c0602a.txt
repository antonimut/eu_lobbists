 
 
 
Stora Enso contributes to making the EU and world carbon neutral in following ways:  
• by capturing, or sequestrating, carbon dioxide in growing trees 
• by storage of carbon in harvested wood products 
• by substitution, when bio-based products and bioenergy replace other products that are fossil-based 
• by becoming the first forest products company to have its climate targets approved by the Science Based Targets initiative in 2017  
• In 2019, our total climate impact, including avoided emissions by product substitution and forest sequestration, was 12 million tonnes of CO2 
• In addition to that we also operate at the heart of the bioeconomy and contributes to a circular economy with our renewable and recyclable products 
 
Stora Enso supports the ambition to drastically 
reduce residual waste  
 
Stora Enso is a renewable materials company and 
one of the largest private forest owners in the world, 
employing 18 000 people in the EU. We deliver 
renewable and recyclable products that store carbon 
and provide low -carbon alternatives to fossil -based 
materials.  
 
Stora Enso believes that we should aim for more than 
a circular economy : we should aim for a circular 
bioeconomy, where waste and fossil -based products 
are reduced to a minimum, and renewable materials 
are used efficiently. 
 
We support the European Commission’s ambition to 
reinforce the waste hierarchy in the Waste 
Framework Direc tive (WFD) focusing on waste 
prevention, reuse, and recycling. We appreciate that 
the Commission wants to combine waste reduction 
measures with waste prevention measures, including 
improved separate collection requirements to further 
improve the quality of recycling. We also welcome the 
focus on residual waste, i.e ., waste that is not 
currently recycled. We would raise the following 
points for consideration in relation to the policy 
options and evidence for the WFD revision:  
 
1) Avoid a one -size-fits-all approach: different 
waste streams require different waste 
management practices 
 
Applying the waste hierarchy stringently to all waste 
streams without consider ing their specificities could 
result in sub-optimal recovery outcomes. The revised 
legislation should therefore incentivise and provide 
the flexibility to promote the most sustainable option 
for waste recovery.  
 
To achieve this, a whole life  cycle approach to 
products and materials should be taken to handle 
waste in the most environmentally f riendly manner. 
Prioritising reuse is not necessarily always the most 
sustainable choice.

A recent LCA (Life Cycle Analysis) study1 by Ramboll 
shows that fiber-based single-use packaging used in 
quick service restaurants in Europe demonstrates 
better environmental performance than reusable 
systems for the same use. The reuse system emitted 
2.8 times more CO 2 equivalent greenhouse gase s, 
consumed 3.4 times more fresh water, 3.3 times more 
metal, produced 2.2 times more fine particulates, 
depleted 3.4 times more fossil resources, and emitted 
1.7 times more terrestrial acidification compared to 
the fiber-based single-use system. The environmental 
impact of single-use and reusable packaging is case  
specific.  
 
The WFD already mentions that when applying the 
waste hierarchy, Member States should “ encourage 
the option that results in the best environmental 
outcome. This may require specific w aste streams 
departing from the hierarchy, where this is justified by 
life cycle thinking on the overall impacts of the 
generation and management of such waste.”  
 
The European Commission should retain this 
possibility and consider establishing waste reduc tion 
targets only where relevant LCA studies support 
doing so. 
 
In addition, setting ambitious reuse and recycling 
targets for construction and demolition (C&D) waste 
is an important step for slowing and closing th e 
construction sector’s resource loops. Such targets for 
material-specific fractions should be based on 
material type, as certain materials are more easily 
recycled than others (steel vs wood, for instance). 
Whilst wood fibers can be easily recycled, the 
recyclability of wood used for construction purposes 
is frequently hampered by the presence of paints and 
other chemicals, along with the lack of economic 
incentives to remove any  such chemicals and to 
transport waste wood long distances. Wood industry 
innovations will improve the reuse and recyclability of 
wood, but research and development will take some 
time.

2) Effective separate waste collection and 
labelling in the EU is key to improving waste 
management 
 
More effective s eparation of waste and better 
collection at source is important to improve the quality 
of recycled material. Today, paper and board 
packaging is the most recycled packaging material in 
Europe at 82.3% (EU27, 2019) , having a higher 
recycling rate than glass or metal  packaging.2 
However, this might not be enough for the future. We 
believe that m ore effective recycling will not only 
prevent waste, but also secure sourcing of recycled 
material for further use.  As a provider of renewable 
materials based on wood fibers, we strive to keep the 
material in the resource loop for as long as possible . 
Wood fibers can be recycled many times without 
experiencing any sign of “recycling collapse”3. Due to 
differences in fiber properties, processes used in 
recycling, and level of material losses in the recycling 
process, 5 to more than 25 cycles can be expected.

various way, for example, with Tetra Pak to increase 
the annual recycling capacity of beverage cartons  in 
Poland from 25 000 tonnes to 75 000 tonnes4. We are 
also working with a broad alliance representing the

Improving recyclability is key in winning eco -aware 
consumers’ hearts, but consumers also need to be 
informed about how and where to recycle. To ensure 
quality recycling at scale, and across Member States, 
it is important to foster effective waste separation 
across the EU.  However, some separate collection 
models do not enable effective waste separation , for 
example, models in which recyclable materials end up 
in residual waste, or commingled collection. These 
collection models should not be supported.  
 
Effective separate collection also requires 
harmonisation of what is collected for recycling across 
the EU. All recyclable materials should be separately 
collected for recycling. For packaging this means 
collection for paper and board (including paper and 
board composite packaging), plastic, metal, glass as 
well as compostable packaging.  With construction, 
this means collecting wood separately from other 
materials to facilitate recycling and reuse. In addition, 
it is vital that countries with well-functioning collection 
and sorting systems should be able to continue to use 
those systems, so that new rules do  not undermine 
high collection and recycling rates . Collection should 
be adapted to local circumstances.  
 
Furthermore, there should be more granular recycling 
targets. For example, introducing a mandatory 
collection target for beverage cartons at EU level 
would ensure that they are widely collected. It is also 
important to consider out-of-household collection, for

3) Support for prefabricated and other 
approaches that prevent construction waste 
 
Mass timber elements, such as cross -laminated 
timber (CLT), are precisely built under controlled 
factory conditions before being transported to a 
project site, where they are quickly assembled. Such 
prefabricated approaches can reduce construction 
site waste by up to 80 percent 5. This is because 
improvisation and errors that are inherent to 
conducting construction activities out doors, under 
variable weather conditions , are replaced by highly 
accurate industrial processes. There is also minimal 
factory waste, as, for example, the wood sector 
cascades any residues into additional beneficial uses 
such as bioenergy and particleboard. 
 
Thus, in keeping with the “prevention” aspect of the 
waste hierarchy, the WFD should encourage Member 
States to promote and support approaches that 
reduce waste flows, including prefabricated building 
solutions.

4) Streamline waste prevention across a wider 
variety of policy tools 
 
Based on the polluter pays principle, t he Extended 
Producer Responsibility (EPR) scheme has proven a 
successful solution to a more resource -efficient 
European society and to reduce the environmental 
impact of products 6. The Call for Evidence suggests 
several ways that EPR schemes could be better used 
to reduce waste . We support efforts to reduce 
residual waste. However, it is also important to note 
that EPR systems inherently reduce waste since they 
make producers responsible for what they put on the 
market. EPR fees should be aimed at incentivising 
recyclable products to encourage design for 
recycling. These criteria should be harmonised for 
different product segments and use material -specific 
design for recycling guidelines to determine which 
aspects enable recycling and what hinders recycling.  
 
In addition, it is important that the WFD mandate the 
national-level implementation of the EU Construction 
and Demolition Waste Protocol. This Protocol 
specifies many measures helpful to boosting 
confidence in the quality of C&D waste. Also, greater 
targets for C&D waste and increased confidence in 
their use must be matched by supportive demand-
side policies, including public procurement 
approaches, to ensure that these secondary 
materials are used to their fullest potential. 
 
Sustainable products should be the norm. The 
increasing demand for food, clothing, housing, 
energy, infrastructure, and consumer products 
means that we must act if we are to live within 
planetary boundaries. At Stora Enso, we believe the 
world needs a new approach to materials. Material 
substitution to phase out the use of non-renewable 
resources and design for waste minimisation should 
be the priority if we want to be carbon neutral by 
2050.  We must pay attention to the recycling of 
renewable material and setting up a system that is 
efficient and works well for our products to keep 
them in the loop for longer. That is why at Stora 
Enso we have set a target of 100% recyclable 
products by 2030.

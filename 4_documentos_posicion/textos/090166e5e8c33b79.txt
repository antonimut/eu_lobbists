I. Environment 
 
• The infrastructure projects are mainly financed by private financial institutions or publics funds, or both 
through a Public Private Partnership. Clients secure their investments  with contractual terms & conditions, 
including penalties to the contractor, applying in case of non-performance, delays on execution, termination 
of the commercial contract, contractor bankruptcy , and ensure the proper execution of the contract  by 
requesting technical guarantees from the contractor . Depending on how the project is financed, these 
guarantees can be delegated to financial institutions funding the project in order to ensure the proper execution 
of the contract.  
• These technical guarantees are usu ally issued by top -tier International banks on behalf of the contractor: 
Advance payment, performance, warranty guarantees.    
• Since 2008 , t he volume of guarantees imposed in large  infrastructure projects has steadily increased: in 
absolute terms as well as in relative terms compared to the contract sizes. In addition, some clients can request 
increasingly stringent restrictions on financial institutions such as minimum rating for the issuer or localisation 
in the client’s country. All of this reduces the number of financial institutions able to allocate capacity for these 
large projects, concentrates the capacity to issue withing a group of banks or insurance companies and reduces 
automatically companies’ capacity to negotiate the cost of these instruments.  
• Raising costs and limitation of capacity will put pressure on smaller rolling stock equipment suppliers. 
Ultimately reducing competitiveness.  it will disrupt the supply chain pushing out small players in favour of 
global powerhouses that have stronger influence over banks and better balance sheets. 
 
II. Common guarantees used for securitization are:  
 
• Performance Guarantees 
It is an irrevocable commitment by one or more financial institutions, banks, or insurance companies the 
“Guarantor(s)”, to pay the beneficiary (customer) if the contractor does not properly fulfill its contractual 
obligations. The Performance Guarantee is an indemnity given to the client . The Guarantors must be 
financially well rated, independent third parties, that will not interfere in a dispute between the contractor 
and the client, so the client can be confident the indemnity will be paid.

The Performance Guarantee normally represents 10% of the contract amou nt. As per international 
standards, it is remitted to the client in the days following the contract signature. In certain public markets 
however, the Performance Guarantee must be remitted prior to the signature of the contract.   
 
• Advance Payment Guarantees  
In recent years, particularly in Europe, contractual payment terms have become increasingly stringent and 
tough. Both public and private clients refuse payments during project execution and retain payments until 
full delivery or provisional acceptance under the contract straining industrials’ financial position.  
At the same time , industrials need cash to cover their working capital to perform under the contracts. 
Advance payment guarantees from Banks (or other fi nancial institutions) are required by clients upon 
which the industrials can receive the progress payments to finance the working capital. The Guarantors 
are committed to reimburse the cash if the contractor does not fulfil its obligations.  
For such terms of payment, the amount of the advance payment guarantees can cover 100% of the cash 
received by the contractor.  
  
• Warranty Guarantees 
These securities cover the terms of payment linked to the warranty obligation of a contract. It allows the 
contractor to receive the 5%-10% warranty amount in advance and secure the client to get his money back 
if the contractor does not perform its warranty obligations.  
 
III. Extremely limited risk for banks from issuance of bank guarantees  
 
• International tenders process 
International tender process es and the selection of contractors based on technical and financial 
assessments assure the good quality of the contractors and their ability to perform under the contract.  
 
• Relationship contractor/ client 
All parties want to properly execute the contracts. Guarantees are the strongest security for the client as 
no contractor will take the risk of reputation and cash  out in case of a call on a guarantee. A call on a 
guarantee is always a  dramatic event within the companies  and would fundamentally damage a 
relationship and future business opportunities between the parties. Eliminating a contractor from a market 
as a result of a call under a guarantee would reduce competitiveness and product choice in that market.  
 
• Bank guarantees are almost never called by the client 
Statistics demonstrate that risk of a bond being called is extremely low.  Based on ICC data on drawing 
rates, the claim rate on the number of performance guarantee is 2% and only 0,25 % of the guarantees 
issued are paid on drawing. By nature, these instruments are not drawn but are used as the threat of call 
(cash out for a company) in case of non-performing.  
  
IV. The proposed regulatory changes will impact European companies’s competitivity in two 
ways: 
 
• Increase the guarantee cost: According to the ICC’s estimate, the increase of the credit conversion factor 
(CCF) for technical guarantees will lead to a price hike of 150%. The technical guarantees are imposed to all 
competitors, small medium or large company, and are essential to facilitate the international transactions. 
 
 3 
Capacity to access to these technical guarantees at a reasonable pricing for industries has an important impact 
on their competitivity and their margin . Automatically it will reduce the competitivity of  medium &  small 
companies who don’t have a large access to the international banking market.    
 
Example: Cost impact on a project with €300m guarantees issued with a maturity of 5 years, comparison 
between 2 competitors: ratings, Baa2 & A1: 
 
 
 
CCF 20%: difference between the 2 ratings is €3 750 000  
CCF 50%: difference between the 2 ratings is €9 000 000 
 
In the normal course of business, cost of the guarantees is included in the project price, so in order to maintain 
their competitivity, companies will either increase their prices or reduce their margins. The lower the credit 
rating of the company will be, the greater the financial impact will be. Small companies, that are usually not 
rated by an external rating agency, will be even more impacted.   
 
In Europe, infrastructure projects impose large amounts of technical guarantees, the impact on the margin 
and the financial effort to maintain the industry competitivity on international tender will be huge if the CCF 
is modified. In other words, to maintain their competitivity, companies will have to compensate the financial 
impact by other savings which could impact further the European economy if it involves sourcing or 
manufacturing outside Europe.  
 
• And/Or Reduce the available guarantee capacity: Due to the increase of the CCF for guarantees from 20% to 
50% financial institutions will be reluctant or even unable to provide the bonding capacity necessary to enable 
proper works within the European industrial complex. The increase of the CCF will significantly increase the 
risk weighted as sets for the product and thus leads to a reduction  of guarantee capacity available. In large 
infrastructure tenders, at prequalification stage the bidders must often remit a support letter from financial 
institution already imposed by the client to demonstrate banking capacity is secured to issue guarantees under 
the project.  
 
V. Additional considerations:  
 
Further to the above-mentioned changes, large groups will be impacted through their multi-layer organizations: the 
parent company’s rating will not apply to its subsidiaries. This will automatically have an impact on guarantee pricing 
which will be recharged on the contract price and will reduce the competitivity of our industries  and companies, 
furthermore on public markets when the award of the contract is on the “lower price”.    
 
The standard approach to finance working capital by clients in large infrastructure projects is through issuance of 
guarantees. With the planned Basel III changes, banks will be forced to allocate high capital to these products. This is 
likely to force banks to reduce their support to the industry at a time when it is looking to expand in Europe  and 
worldwide with all public investment programs supporting Green mobility.

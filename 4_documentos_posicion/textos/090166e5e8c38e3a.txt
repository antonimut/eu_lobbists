 February 22, 2022 Enerkem Comments on the Call for Evidence  – Environmental impact of waste management – revision of EU waste framework – Lead DG: DG ENV B3 ---------------------------------------------------------------------- Introduction For the EU to transition to a resource-efficient circular economy, preventing waste is a key element. With large volumes of waste still being generated, novel technologies for waste disposal could play a crucial role to reach this transition. The Waste Framework Directive (Directive 98/2008/EC article 4) introduced a five-step waste hierarchy where prevention is highlighted as the best option, followed by re-use, recycling and other forms of recovery, with disposal such as landfill as the last resort: • reduce the amount of waste generated; • maximize recycling and re-use; • limit incineration to non-recyclable materials; • phase out landfilling to non-recyclable and non-recoverable waste; • ensure full implementation of the waste policy targets in all EU Member States. 
 Figure 1: EU Waste Hierarchy Although the Directive has been successful in driving increased recycling rates, as the Call for Evidence notes, progress more recently has slowed / stalled.  In particular achieving recycling rates above 60% is proving challenging in most markets, largely due to the limitations of mechanical recycling from standard waste streams.  Improved collection and source segregation would likely deliver some incremental gains over the coming years – in particular for certain new and emerging processes aimed at specific waste types – however, the mechanical recycling efficiency is likely to limit these gains.

Key challenges The key challenges when it comes to the waste ‘reject’ fractions - those fractions going into landfill or to an incineration plant - are the following.   - Waste is mixed / ‘contaminated’.  This can either be a collection / source-segregation issue or a more fundamental challenge that results from product design.  ‘Laminate’ materials that combine cardboard, plastics and/or foil for example are challenging to separate out.  Food packaging is also likely to contain food residues.    - Waste comprises of ‘low-grade’ / ‘low value’ materials.  Certain types of waste are hard to mechanically recycle and have little to no value as a secondary recyclate material.  This can include recyclates that do have value, but whose quality has degraded when recycled previously.   Options Factoring in human behaviour, and the degradation of recycled materials over time, Enerkem sees the following main options for recycling the current ‘reject’ fractions from recycling plants: - Product (re-)design.  Financial incentive that promote the use of materials that are easier to recycle will facilitate higher recycling rates and reduce reject volumes.  A key challenge here however is higher product costs – existing product design typically focuses on lowest cost options – and the issues surrounding the gradual degradation of materials often remain.  Switching from plastics bottles to glass bottles (+90% recycled) is an example of where the latter would not be the case, but such a change comes with other ‘costs’ given the higher shipment costs – both price and carbon – given the increase in weight.  There is still however significant potential for ‘win-wins’ through improved design.  - Introduce (further) financial incentives to recycle.  There are a number of examples where deposit return schemes have delivered marked improvements in recycling, most notably in the drink bottle sectors.    - Broaden the definition of recycling to include other techniques.  Current recycling techniques centre predominantly mechanical recycling techniques, which simply washes and melts plastics which turn into less valuable products. Certain new emerging processes enable the ‘chemical recycling’ of waste, recovering the ‘molecular’ resources in unrecycled waste and re-introducing them into the production cycle in a different form.  These processes bring two key advantages, namely that existing product design / material use can be retained and allow recycling of hard to recycle due the limitation of mechanical recycling and limitation in human behaviour is more or less removed as a factor.    Enerkem’s solution Enerkem’s patented technology is an ‘’Advanced carbon recycling process’’ (novel gasification) that chemically recycles carbon molecules contained in waste materials into added-value circular products. consists of transforming the solid non-recoverable and non-recyclable waste materials into an ultra-clean and usable intermediate called a synthesis gas: a building block from which the industry can bolt on proven industrial processes to synthesize specific molecules such as methanol, ethanol, ethylene, olefins and higher value add chemicals. The process is conceived on carbon recycling and provides a clear scalable solution that is largely feedstock agnostic, and capable of processing a wide range of different – and mixed - waste types. Enerkem’s technology is part of a family of new technology processes that complement conventional recycling options by focusing on reject fractions from conventional, mechanical recycling processes. It sits alongside these processes and addresses some of the current limitations in other closed-loop pathways related to mixing, degradation and contamination (which in the current plastics system is produced in high volumes) or of unfeasibly low volume (e.g. novel materials).  In simple terms: Enerkem’s process can handle waste fractions that the other processes cannot, including degraded recyclates that are no longer suitable for closed-loop pathways. The ‘molecular’ recycling process results in circular plastics products are identical to fossil-based virgin plastics without extracting new fossil-based primary resins.

Enerkem’s recommendation therefore is that “Advanced Recycling” – processes that allow the conversion of wastes materials into new products such as circular chemicals, are recognized as part of the recycling category within the waste hierarchy.  We urge the Commission to include this option in the impact assessment carried out.

 *** Enerkem Headquartered in Montréal, Québec, Canada, Enerkem operates a full-scale commercial demonstration facility in Edmonton, Alberta, Canada as well as an innovation centre in Québec. A full-scale commercial facility in Varennes, Quebec, Canada is currently under construction and commissioning is scheduled for 2024. Two additional facilities are under development in the EU: • In El Morell (Tarragona, Spain), the Ecoplanta project is one of seven projects preselected for support by the European Commission Innovation Fund. A joint venture between Repsol, Enerkem (Canada), and SUEZ Recycling & Recovery Spain, this project will use Enerkem’s technology to convert non-recyclable fractions of waste into circular chemicals and advanced biofuels, producing 237 kt/y of methanol in a facility on a petrochemical complex near the port of Tarragona, Spain, and thereby recycling over 70% of the carbon present in the residual waste materials. The Ecoplanta project is a ground-breaking illustration of the circular economy.  With its emphasis on maximizing carbon recycling from residual waste materials, Ecoplanta will contribute towards the Waste Framework Directive target for which recycling and re-use of municipal waste must reach 65% in Europe by 2035. These targets require a large shift in EU waste management practices, where currently only around 40% of municipal waste is recycled, and 30% is still landfilled. Ecoplanta provides a clear pathway towards the achievement of these targets. This project will increase the recycling rate of Spain.  • In Rotterdam (Netherlands), the Waste-to-Jet project will use a combination of Enerkem’s technology and Shell’s Fischer-Tropsch technology to produce Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) and Recycled Carbon Fuel. Enerkem is also working on several upcoming projects in other member states that will have a significant impact in recycling rate increases as well as reaching GHG emission reduction targets.

 Feedback on the Proposal for a Directive  on improving working conditions in platform work   About Bolt Bolt, founded in Estonia in 2013, is the leading European urban mobility platform, offering ride-hailing, electric scooter, bike, car sharing as well as food delivery services. Bolt operates in over 400 cities in close to 45 countries around the world, including 22 EU Member States. Over 100 million customers and more than 2.5 million drivers and couriers globally use Bolt’s platform to offer transport and food delivery services.   1- The consequences of the current drafting of the Directive proposal  - The initial goal of the Commission was to improve social dialogue between platforms and platform workers. This ambition is legitimate and supported by Bolt. But the Directive proposal mainly focuses on the classification of the relationship between platforms and workers, with a list of criteria (art. 4.2) of which two need only to be satisfied to trigger the presumption of employment.    - However, given the way these criteria are drafted in the Directive proposal, the overwhelming majority of ride-hailing drivers in Europe would be reclassified as employees since many of these criteria are inherent to the legitimate  functioning of a  safe ride-hailing platform but also to its regulations (liability of ride-hailing platforms in particular)   - The presumption of employment as defined in the legislative proposal is neither collective nor individual. This means that while the vast majority of ride-hailing drivers do not want to be employed (all studies without exception evidence it1), classification as an employee will be imposed on them automatically, including for those who, for the most part, would prefer to remain independent. Indeed, ride hailing drivers value the flexibility of working on multiple platforms, deciding on their schedules or the ability to accept or decline rides.   - This presumption of employment imposed on the platforms will lead to more than one out of two ride-hailing drivers in Europe to lose their source of income, i.e. at least 150,000 ride-hailing drivers across the Community (see Compass Lexecon study2).   - In a model where drivers are employed, drivers would work shifts determined by the platform, without the right to refuse trips and with an optimized and maximized "usage". They would not be able to ‘multi-home’ a key source of enhanced income. In this scenario, the number of drivers needed to meet demand is less than the current number of drivers. In particular, drivers who do platform work on top of another job  (about 50% of the 300,000 drivers across Europe) would be removed from the market. In addition, for the remaining drivers, their revenues would be reduced to the minimum wage. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY Bolt welcomes the idea of improving social rights for platform workers, but finds that the reclassification of service providers to become employees will constitute a disproportional solution. It is contrary to the evidenced wishes of platform workers, who appreciate their independent status as service providers, and it will entirely disrupt the business model of platform operators.  Against that background, Bolt respectfully suggests that the Commission should recall the proposed directive, and instead it should present a proposal on social rights for dependant contractors. That concept would cover service providers that have a close cooperation with a limited number of clients, and who therefore depend on those clients for their continuing business.  Such relationships are reclassified in some Member State legislation on employment, but Bolt submits that this mainly serves the fiscal interests of those Member States that have set taxes on service provision lower than employment. It is also relied upon by disgruntled service providers seeking additional compensation at the end of a contract.  However, in the opinion of Bolt such considerations cannot justify the stifling of information society services, of which platform working has become a central element. In the field of person transportation, the use of platform services as opposed to traditional taxis has entailed higher customer satisfaction, increased price transparency, and higher incomes for drivers.  Also at a technical level, Bolt finds that the proposed directive does not have appropriate mechanisms. The presumption for employment is based not on a majority of criteria, but on two out of five criteria, of which two represent just two sides of the same criterion, and of which three are essential to any business operation. This concerns the two aspects of quality management, in setting and supervising levels, and also the setting of prices.  In the opinion of Bolt, the Court of Justice of the European Union has exhaustively defined the difference between service provision and employment in the Yodel case, 4 as set out below. Thus, the distinction between service provision and employment should be based on an overall assessment, and it should be excluded where the Yodel criteria are fulfilled. Alternatively, if the proposed model of presumption and rebuttal is to be maintained, Bolt would find that the Yodel criteria, and not as currently a reference to Member State law, would be the correct set of rebuttal criteria.  Additionally, the proposed directive sets out to regulate several practical aspects of platform operation, which should better be regulated by amending existing and general Union law, or by leaving such issues to be regulated by Member States under the principle of subsidiarity.   On the other hand, for the distinction between service provision and employment, which is essential for setting up the business model for platform operation, the directive must provide an exhaustive regulation. While Member States may in other regards provide additional rights for workers, the central distinction must be the same in all jurisdictions, as also underlined in the case law of the Court.

RECLASSIFICATION Proportionality The explanatory memorandum, published as part of the proposal, states on page 8: • The principle of proportionality is respected considering the size and nature of the identified problems.  • For instance, the rebuttable presumption proposed to address the problem of misclassification of the employment status will only apply to digital labour platforms that exert a certain level of control over the performance of work.  • Other digital labour platforms will thus not be concerned by the presumption.  Bolt submits that this statement does not fulfil the requirements of Article 5(4), which provides that 'under the principle of proportionality, the content and form of Union action shall not exceed what is necessary to achieve the objectives of the Treaties'.   The mechanism of proportionality is further explained in amongst other Case C-331/88 Fedesa, 5 where the Court held that ‘the lawfulness of the prohibition of an economic activity is subject to the condition that the prohibitory measures are appropriate and necessary in order to achieve the objectives legitimately pursued by the legislation in question; when there is a choice between several appropriate measures recourse must be had to the least onerous, and the disadvantages caused must not be disproportionate to the aims pursued’.  Objective Concerning the first requirement, of a legitimate objective, Bolt notes that the explanatory memorandum has exclusive focus on the social protection of workers, which in itself is naturally a legitimate objective. However, several studies conclude that a majority of platform workers prefer their independent status as service providers, which offers them a higher level of income than can be expected from employment in similar functions. 6  This raises the question of whether it is appropriate, for the purpose of social protection, to impose to all ride hailing drivers and all food couriers (as well as other trades such as plumbers, handypersons and other workers who may provide their services via platforms) the status of employee. Bolt has found that the overwhelming majority of plaintiffs, who do claim employment status (indeed almost all), are drivers that are no longer accepted as service providers for the platform, and who hope to gain some additional financial advantage once they have been discontinued, typically in the form of holiday pay which it is alleged is wrongly withheld.  Therefore Article 4 of the proposal will do nothing to provide rights to drivers while they are providing services via a platform, since the drivers will take all measures possible to be recognised as independent service providers. Instead of negatively forcing drivers into a state of employment, that they do not wish to have, the proposed directive should focus on providing social rights for independent service providers.  Likewise, Bolt finds that a major concern for Member States in relation to reclassification appears to be preservation of the fiscal income for the state, since service provision is often taxed at a lower level than employment, and also often excluded from payment of social taxes. Onerous effects Concerning the second requirement, Bolt notes that reclassification from services to employment has very onerous effects for platform operators. For traditional service provision, it may be argued that the purchaser pays a price for the service, which may instead be considered to constitute a salary, allowing for tax and other obligations to be imposed.  However, in the platform economy, a three party relationship exists between the platform operator, the driver and the passenger, where the passenger pays the driver for the transportation, and the driver pays the platform operator a small commission for the provision of intermediation services within the definition Information Society Services as provided in Article 1(1)(b) of the Directive 2015/1535, 9 which refers to 'any service normally provided for remuneration, at a distance, by electronic means and at the individual request of a recipient of services'. Thus, there is no existing payment stream that can be regarded as employment, and instead it is the entire business model that must be discarded.  A less onerous effect would be to regulate the service agreement between the platform operator and the driver, where the status of 'dependant service provider' might be introduced, so as to ensure certain rights for the driver, without disrupting the existing relations between the operator, driver and passenger. 10 This would be in line with the Union policy of promoting information society services.  Against that background, Bolt argues that the proposed directive is based on a legislative model that does not constitute the least onerous option for regulating the platform economy, since a less onerous option would be the introduction of the concept of dependant service providers. Disproportionate disadvantages Finally, concerning the third requirement, the disadvantages caused must not be disproportionate to the aims pursued. This requires a balancing of interests, which especially must take place where no less onerous options are available.  In that regard, Bolt notes that taxi companies and other private hire companies that undertake a traditional dispatch service without providing a platform service (with which operators such as Bolt in practice compete) will not be subject to the proposed directive. Thus, discrimination will occur, since drivers will face the options of maintaining their independent status by working for traditional companies, but will be forced to undergo employment in order to work for platform operators.   That can hardly be in line with the policy of the Union to promote the use of information society services. Indeed it provides an absurd disincentive to transport service providers not to offer their services via digital channels.   Furthermore, a less onerous option does exist, as set out above, and against that background it seems obvious that disruption of the entire business model for platform operation cannot in any manner be regarded as a proportional disadvantage. The Union legislator must be obliged to take instead the less onerous route and legislate to provide social rights for dependant service providers, rather than forcing such service providers to assume the status of employees. Secondly, if the Union should not be empowered to regulate the definition of independent work, then it would seem that Commission also would be prevented from proposing a directive to reclassify independent work to constitute employment.  More fundamentally, it is well established in the case law of the Court that judgments given in reply to preliminary references have legal effects not only for the Member State court that submitted the reference, but that they have legal effect in general. 13 Thus, the Commission was not in any manner authorised to set aside the Yodel case.  In that judgment, the Court has held in case that the concept of worker 'has an autonomous meaning specific to Union law' and that a national court must 'make an overall assessment of all the circumstances of the case brought before it, having regard both to the nature of the activities concerned and the relationship of the parties involved'.  This does not resonate with a presumption model, based on fulfilling 2 out of 5 criteria, as set out in the proposed Article 4(2), which is significantly different from the overall assessment stipulated in Yodel. Naturally, it may be argued that supplementing the presumption with the right to rebuttal in the proposed Article 5 does allow for an overall assessment, but it becomes an unbalanced model in that respect.  Suspensive effect Secondly, Article 5(2) provides that rebuttal 'proceedings shall not have suspensive effect on the application of the legal presumption'. As argued above, for the platform economy a reclassification from services to employment does not only provide basis for a different level of taxation and social rights, but instead it entirely disrupts the three party relations between platform operators, drivers and passengers.  Thus, it becomes impractical to imagine a platform operator, first relying on service contracts, secondly being forced to introduce employment contracts, and thirdly after a successful rebuttal reverting to service contacts. That procedure would further increase the disruptive effect of the proposed directive.  Primacy of Union law Thirdly, Article 5(2) refers to rebuttal based on the law, collective agreements or practice in force in the Member State in question, with consideration to the case-law of the Court of Justice. This appears in direct contravention of the case law of the Court, as referred to in the explanatory memorandum.   Thus in case C-256/01 Allonby 14 the court held that 'the formal classification of a self-employed person under national law does not change the fact that a person must be classified as a worker within the meaning of that article if his independence is merely notional'. Against that background, it makes little sense that rebuttal should be based on classification in national law.  Furthermore, the explanatory memorandum states on page 8 that 'the proposed Directive provides for minimum standards' and that ' Member States may also decide to go beyond the minimum standards set out in the proposed Directive', as provided for by Article 20(2), as further addressed below. Yodel criteria Apart from a general reservation for fictitious arrangements, the Yodel criteria defined by the Court comprise that the person is afforded discretion: • To use subcontractors or substitutes to perform the service which he has undertaken to provide; • To accept or not accept the various tasks offered by his putative employer, or unilaterally set the maximum number of those tasks; • To provide his services to any third party, including direct competitors of the putative employer, and • To fix his own hours of ‘work’ within certain parameters and to tailor his time to suit his personal convenience rather than solely the interests of the putative employer.  It should be noted that in setting these criteria, the Court did not include any provisions concerning price or quality control, which are central to the proposed directive, and which are dealt with below. Bolt argues that control of price and quality is essential to any party that does business with service providers to avoid passenger exploitation or price gouging, and that the setting of price and quality norms does not have any impact on the independence of the service provider.  An example of passenger exploitation and price gouging could be a driver demanding a premium to transport a female passenger from a place in which she feels unsafe. However, determining customer prices forms an integrated part of the outsourcing of passenger transportation by bus. This is done to protect the passenger against high prices, and it is combined with specific quality criteria to be met in relation to the appearance, conduct and frequency of services.   Despite this format, nobody would argue that the company authorised to operate a bus line should be considered an 'employee' of the public authority that has decided to outsource such bus lines, whether as a concession or as a service contract.   Likewise, taxi companies are often formed with subcontractors providing the driving services, but with prices fixed or capped by public authorities. Also here, the fact that prices are fixed or capped has no impact on the status of subcontractors as independent service providers.  More generally, in line with the logic of case C-629/19 Yodel, it may be argued that the independence of the driver lies in the deciding when, for whom and how much to work, including work for several platforms. Therefore price and quality requirements do not express control, but simply set the parameters within which the drivers may exercise their independence.  In that regard, a comparison may be had with the residence market, where the Court underlined in case C-390/18 AirBnb 17 that 'it is not apparent, either from the order for reference or from the information in the file before the Court, that Airbnb Ireland sets or caps the amount of the rents charged by the hosts using that platform. At most, it provides them with an optional tool for estimating their rental price having regard to the market averages taken from that platform, leaving responsibility for setting the rent to the host alone'.  However, the transport and the residence markets have important differences. Thus, the booking of a residence is normally done well in advance and based on a comparative study of the facilities, location and price of residences offered. Different from this, the passenger transport market typically requires here and now services, where the passengers mainly want to receive information about price-quality categories and waiting time.  ALGORITHMIC MANAGEMENT The proposed directive notes in Article 6(1) of Chapter III that it is subsidiary to Directive 2019/1152 on transparent and predictable working conditions, 21 and against that background it is clear that the purpose of the proposed directive is to provide supplementary rights.  In that regard, it should be noted that the proposal for a Regulation on Artificial Intelligence likewise sets out to provide such supplementary rights, especially in Chapter 3 of Title I. 22 Bolt would suggest that initiatives for supplementary rights should best be addressed in that proposed regulation, as further developed below in relation to Article 8 of the proposed directive.  Provision of information The core information obligations are also set in out Article 6(1) and concern the right of platform workers to be informed about the use of automated systems that concern respectively monitoring and decision-making. The term algorithm is used only in the title of the chapter, which is entitled Algorithmic Management, but the use is explained in Recital 8, which refers to 'automated monitoring and decision-making systems powered by algorithms'.  The information obligation covers the use of such systems, as the categories of actions monitored and decisions taken by the systems. For decision-making systems, the information obligation additionally covers the main parameters of the system, and the grounds on which decision negatively affecting the worker may be taken, especially in relation to accounts, contracts and remuneration. CONCLUSION Bolt argues that it is doubtful whether the proposed directive serves the claimed objective in an appropriate manner, and that in any case the legislative model chosen, with presumption and rebuttal, does not constitute the least onerous form of regulation, and that for that reason the proposed directive violates the Union principle of proportionality.  Within that legislative model, with presumption and rebuttal, Bolt argues that the proposed directive fails to respect that the distinction between workers and service providers must be based on autonomous Union concepts. This calls for an overall assessment, or at the very least fulfilment of a majority of presumption criteria.  As the reclassification from service provision to employment will entirely disrupt the business model of platform operators, the rebuttal procedure must have suspensive effect, and the rebuttal cannot be based on national law, but must be based on the criteria developed by the Court of Justice in the Yodel case.   Within the presumption criteria, Bolt argues that price and quality must be regulated by the platform operator, and that this does not impact on the independence of the drivers. Indeed price and quality are not among the Yodel criteria, as the true independence of the driver lies in the deciding when, for whom and how much to work, including work for several platforms.  Without price control by the platform operator, working and transport conditions will worsen for respectively drivers and passengers, as the latter will not have any protection against neither high prices nor prices so low as to cover substandard performance.   For quality control, Bolt notes that the proposed directive has two overlapping criteria and cover different aspects of quality control, respectively standards to be met and verification. The criteria should be conflated into one criterion, and essentially it could only be the appearance of the driver, since the conduct and performance of service must be controlled by the platform operator.  Thus, Bolt essentially argues that the presumption and rebuttal model of the proposed directive should be replaced by a codification of the Yodel criteria. However, if that model is maintained, the price and quality control criteria should be removed, and the rebuttal criteria should be replaced by the Yodel criteria.  Concerning the issues of algorithmic management, Bolt argues that provision of information should be subject to the protection of business secrets, while relations with the GDPR Regulation should be explicitly regulated in the text of the directive. The concept of undue pressure and unacceptable risk should be defined in the directive, while the issues of statement of reasons and time limits for review should be regulated in a general manner for sectors of the economy.   In the same manner, the issue of transparency should be regulated by the existing Union legislation on coordination of social security systems. For remedies and enforcement, the issues of legal standing for worker representatives, communication between platform workers and their representatives, disclosure of evidence, protection of complainants and burden of proof should be regulated by existing Union legislation and otherwise left to be regulated under Member State sovereignty.  Finally, the distinction between employment and service provision should be regulated exhaustively in the proposed directive, so as to provide legal certainty for both platform operators and service providers concerning the extent to which they may rely on service provision as an alternative to employment in all Member States.

However, Bolt would more generally recommend that the proposed directive be recalled and that a more suitable instrument be developed in close cooperation with the platform workers and operators.  In that regard, it should be recalled that in the published letter from the president of the European Commission to the Commissioner for jobs and social rights, 37 the instructions are to provide 'dignified, transparent and predictable working conditions are essential to our economic model.

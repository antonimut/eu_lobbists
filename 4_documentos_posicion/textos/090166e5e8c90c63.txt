THE WASTE FRAMEWORK DIRECTIVE 
 
 
 
 
Position accompanying Flexible Packaging Europe’s feedback to the European’ Commission’s call for 
evidence for an impact assessment about the revision of the Waste Framework Directive  
 
22 February 2022 
 
 
 
About Flexible Packaging Europe  
 
Flexible Packaging Europe (FPE) represents the interests of the European producers of 
flexible packaging, covering all kind of flexible packaging, made mostly of plastics but also 
aluminium and paper, and mainly used as primary packaging of Fast-Moving Consumer Goods 
(FMCGs). FPE comprises more than 80 small, medium -sized companies, and multinational 
manufacturers. Those operate with workforce of 57,000+ people at more than 350 sites all 
over Europe and account for above 85% of European flexible packaging sales.  
Flexible packaging represents half of food primary packaging placed on the EU market (in 
product units) while accounting for only one sixth of packaging material used (in weight). This 
fact demonstrates the key function of flexible packaging, which is to use a very small amount 
of material to protect products. The requisite protection is achieved by selecting appropriate 
materials, sometimes in combination, to benefit from the cumulated materials’ properties.  
 
 
General comment  
 
The revision of the Waste Framework Directive (WFD) is a necessary complement to the 
ongoing revision of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive. While the 2018 revision 
has brough t many advances such as higher recycling targets and capturing the waste 
hierarchy in legislation, this time round the WFD can address circularity blockers and turn them 
into enablers in line with the ambitions of the 2020 Circular Economy Action Plan.   
 
 
Waste prevention as the top of the waste hierarchy  
 
Waste prevention is on top of waste hierar chy for a good reason, because it is proven to be 
the most efficient progress action to reduce the environmental impact not only at the waste 
stage, but of the entire product throughout its lifecycle. This is in particularly relevant for 
packaging, when pa ckaging is warranted for necessary product protection and remains 
essential, making weight reduction the most straightforward option, as supported by many LCA 
studies.  Recycling lightweight packaging is then additional step to further reduce an already 
low environmental impact.   
However, many provisions of the WFD are not binding and are left at the discretion of Member 
States.  
This means that Article 9 WFD should be complemented with a more targeted definition of 
waste prevention coupled with a common methodology to track (household) waste 
prevention, which does not only inclu de re-use.  This could include resource productivity 
ratios by weight, changes in waste streams per weight or per capita weight targets.  In our 
view, the best approach would be the one which encourages reduction of resources use at

The review of the Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive is expected to introduce waste 
prevention measures, which should be consistent  with the general approach of the 
WFD. However, as more specific legislation, PPWD should incorporate lifecycle concerns. 
Packaging sustainability must be understood holistically, both from a resource minimisation 
perspective but also the protective function. It is well proven that the resources embodied in 
the packaged products are higher than those in the packaging itself, therefore prevent ing 
product spoilage and extending shelf life must be a priority. Appropriately designed packaging 
which prevents food and product waste is a powerful lever for reducing the overall impact of 
products throughout their lifecycle. With a holistic approach to  packaging sustainability 
supported in legislation, this would allow operators to make informed choices which fits to the 
products and supply chain organization whilst contributing to the EU climate agenda.  
 
 
Collection  
 
We particularly welcome that the Ro admap identifies sub-optimal waste collection as one 
such problem.  We understand that this depends on the choices of local authorities, but there 
are many practices which are proven to deliver  good results across geographical and 
economic contexts.  
While the WFD allows derogations from separate collection of waste if this entail 
disproportionate economic costs, we believe that in the case of packaging this is not 
compatible with the 2024 duty to introduce eco -modulated EPR fees.  This argument 
becomes even more relevant in the context of the future PPWD which wi ll result in more 
material available for recycling and more advanced recycling technologies, making for a 
convincing business case. 
The wording of the current Article 10 is also not suitable for packaging because it refers to 
separate collection as a way to “facilitate preparing for other recovery operations”, an 
option which is not compatible with the goal of making all packaging recyclable or 
reusable by 2030. Therefore, the stakes are higher and the economic analysis underpinning 
any derogations must account for this.   
This same approach should apply in case of sorting from residual waste, and we would 
suggest assessing the benefit of mandating sorting of recyclables from residual and 
organic waste before disposal in landfill, incineration or organic recycling. This step 
should however be accompanied by strong consumer communication to ensure separate 
collection of recyclables is prioritized, and misthrows minimized when separate collection 
exists.  
Therefore, WFD Articles 10 and 11 should be clarified in line with above. More concretely, 
we would welcome a provision giving effect to mandatory separate collection of all 
packaging covered by EPR schemes. This will have the beneficial effect of higher net-cost 
fees flowing towards packaging whose recycling streams are still scaling up.  
In the case the legislation results in proposing a harmonized collection model or a Guid ance 
on separate collection models, we would support a separate collection at source of flexible 
packaging in a stream with other lightweight packaging, including rigid plastics, metals 
and beverage cartons.  As a minimum, the legislation should clarify WFD Article 10 (2) and 
(3) when it comes to “material properties” and “comparable quality”.   
In any case, derogations should be coupled with a “comply or explain” approach,  with 
information available to economic operators. This information could be used for better 
investment planning, channelling of EPR funds and sharing of best practices.

EPR schemes  
 
We invite the Commission to go forward with the Guidance on EPR schemes and the 
modulation of financial contributions mentioned in Article 8 (5)  WFD. To increase visibility of 
actual recycling performances for flexible packaging, it is paramount that PROs are transparent 
on the end-of-life destination of flexible packaging, with governments exercising oversight o n 
PROs’ reporting to ensure they operate appropriately and comparably.  Full implementation of 
minimum requirements EPR schemes is needed, along with an assessment of whether they 
need to be strengthened to guarantee transparency and oversight.  Traceabili ty and reliable 
data would also help to understand actual recycling. 
 
 
Definition of recycling  
 
With recycling becoming the main end of life treatment for a diverse range of products  
(including flexible packaging) there is a race to the top with regards to technologies. This often 
goes beyond currently established technologies to include emerging ones with various 
technical readiness levels . Therefore, the definition of recycling should be clarified and 
adapted also to processes which yield multiple substances and include several steps.  
With clear advances in recycling technologies in certain markets, it is paramount to have a 
more updated harmonized definition. This is important for a successful implementation of 
the Waste Shipment Regulation and intra-EU movement of waste for recycling.   
 
 
Coherence 
 
Finally, we hope the revision and the assessment of new waste policies will be done in 
coherence with upcoming legislative changes, such as the Packaging and Packaging Waste 
Directive.

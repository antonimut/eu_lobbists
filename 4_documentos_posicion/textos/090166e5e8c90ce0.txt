 Confederation of European Waste-to-Energy Plants 
 
CEWEP’s feedback to the Commission Call for evidence on the revision of the Waste Framework 
Directive 
Waste management done in a responsible and efficient manner is the obvious link offering solutions 
to two major matters of our time : climate change and the transition to a circular economy . CEWEP 
supports the efforts of the European Commission to align the WFD to these objectives and offers the 
following feedback in this context. 
- Proper implementation and extension of targets to further waste streams  
Improving the implementation of the current waste legislation  should be the main focus of this 
revision, particularly with regards to waste prevention, better collection and better source separation 
in order to achieve high quality recycling. Proper implementation of the current legislation will lead to 
significant CO2eq emission savings, as demonstrated in a recent peer -reviewed study by Prognos and 
CE Delft (https://www.cewep.eu/prognos-study-release-january-2022/). 
But more can be done. Municipal waste represents only a small part of the waste generated in Europe. 
If, apart from successfully implementing current municipal waste targets,  the same recycling and 
landfill targets would apply to Industrial and Commercial waste by 2035, the European waste 
management sector would significantly improve the CO 2 avoidance potential, delivering a saving of 
150 million tonnes CO2eq., as calculated by the Prognos and CE Delft study for the EU27+UK (see 
above).  
With a more ambitious performance it would be possible  to almost double these savings, to nearly 
300 million tonnes CO2eq savings.  This would be achieved with even higher recycling targets and if 
waste that can be recycled or treated for energy recovery would not be allocated to landfills. The study 
shows how the largest gains are made by reducing landfilling, in particular of organic waste materials, 
such as paper, cardboard and biowaste, achieving a reduction of up to 120 million tonnes CO2eq. This 
clearly highlights the large impact of methane emissions from landfills, in accordance with the recent 
IPCC report’s emphasis on the urgency to reduce GHG-emissions.  
Therefore, when discussing the review of the WFD, the forthcoming review of the EU Landfill Directive 
has to be already anticipated. More ambitious landfill diversion targets for all waste streams that 
cannot be recycled or treated for energy recovery have to be set in full respect of the waste hierarchy, 
to close the circular economy loop and to save significant amounts of Greenhouse gases. 
- Recycling is not only about quantity, and that applies to residual waste too 
CEWEP supports measures and guidelines to decrease waste generation, improve separate waste 
collection to yield optimal recycling results, including by avoiding contamination of recyclable waste, 
as envisaged by the European Commission. 
An overview of waste prevention and treatment should be carried out across Europe before setting 
higher targets, in order to see if the present targets are achieved and if not, why, in order to 
anticipate adequate legislative tools to accompany the sectors towards the fulfilment of their 
objective. Once this is done, higher objectives can be set. 
Recycling is about quality and not only about quantity. As highlighted in the call for evidence, some 
Member States might not achieve the 2025 targets for the recycling of municipal waste. More 
ambitious quantitative targets are already set for the following decade for this waste stream and will

be equally challenging to achieve for many Member States. It is therefore worrying that the 
European Commission has been hinting at the idea of setting further quantitative reduction targets 
for the residual – non recyclable – municipal waste stream.  
Such a target  could come with a range of negative implications  and questionable benefits . 
Quantitative reduction of r esidual waste alone would not solve the waste problem but would 
potentially increase fly-tipping and residual waste ending up in recycling bins even if this waste is not 
suitable for quality recycling. This would make sorting and recycling operations less efficient and more 
costly. It would also potentially contribute to even more illegal waste treatment, an already deep crack 
in the European environmental policy (see, inter alia, Interpol report from August 2020 on the rise of 
plastic waste crime - https://www.interpol.int/en/News-and-Events/News/2020/INTERPOL-report-
alerts-to-sharp-rise-in-plastic-waste-crime). Instead, the policymakers should strengthen the Waste 
Hierarchy, looking into ways to boost eco-design, so that less waste is generated in the first place and 
support source separation to enable quality recycling.  
Limiting the capacity to treat residual waste would also risk security of waste treatment and hamper 
the resilience of the waste management chain in the face of climate adaptation. There should indeed 
be room for emergency cases like floods that cause tremendous amounts of w aste that need to be 
treated quickly for hygienic reasons. During the catastrophic floods of July 2021 in Belgium and 
Germany for instance, there was no way to recycle all these suddenly occurring enormous waste 
streams and even thermal treatment capacities to treat these waste streams was not sufficiently 
available everywhere where needed. 
We therefore ask the Commission to not propose a quantitative target on residual waste, as it would 
shift the focus on quantity and not quality of recycling . This could lead to devastating effects on th e 
recycling market, and harm the resilience of the waste management chain in case of natural disasters. 
We are convinced it would be better to focus firmly on the implementation of the existing recycling 
targets for all Member States and to anchor our suggestions mentioned above for more circularity in 
the future in European legislation, implementation and enforcement. As a result, the amount of 
residual waste will automatically decrease without affecting the quality of recycling.

operators of Waste -to-Energy plants across Europe. CEWEP’s members are committed to ensuring

production from remaining waste that cannot be recycled in a sustainable way.

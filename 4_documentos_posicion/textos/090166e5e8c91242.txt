the European Commission’s  Proposal for a REGULATION OF THE EUROPEAN

regards requirements for credit risk, credit valuation adjustment risk, operational

22 February 2022 
 
Abstract 
The EUF welcomes the opportunity to provide its comments on the proposed amendments to the CRR aimed 
to finalize the Basel III framework. In this perspective, the EUF believes that the European Commission should 
consider the following comments and proposals: 
• On the definition of “ancillary services undertaking”:  to clarify better the Commission’s concerns 
about “factoring undertakings” out of the scope of prudential consolidation and we consider the 
inclusion of “factoring” among the “ancillary services” as provided in point (18)(1)(b) as 
inappropriate. 
• On credit insurance: the EUF supports the introduction of the new Article 506 mandating EBA to 
report to the Commission on the eligibility and the use of credit insurance as a credit risk mitigation 
technique and on the appropriate risk parameters they should be associated with under the SA -CR 
and foundation IRB approach, which is fully consistent with the desires of the industry. The EUF also 
strongly advi ses to delete the proposal related to fraud by the obligor under amended Article 
183(b)(iii) and Article 213(1)(d). 
• On purchased receivables: to include in the mandate to the EBA to propose regulatory technical 
standards not only for the IRB approaches but also under the SA-CR, recognizing the low risk profile 
of purchased receivables by way of: 
o the possibility to consider receivables as eligible CRM tools under the Standardized 
Approach, thus allowing the possibility to opt for the risk weight of the account debtor, even 
in the case where the agreement provides partial or full recourse to the client; 
o the amendment of art. 178 to allow institutions to apply the definition of default, in the case 
of purchased receivables, at the level of an individual credit facility (“retail approach”) rather 
than in relation to the total obligations of a borrower; 
o the application of a lower risk weight for exposures to purchased receivables to unrated 
corporate (or other entities), within the range of 50 to 75%.

On the EUF 
The EUF is the industry body and voice for the European factoring industry. The EUF’s members consist of 15 
national factoring and commercial finance associations (representing 1 3 EU-member states, namely [in 
alphabetic order] Austria, Belgium, Croatia, the Czech Republic, Denmark, France, Germany, Greece, Italy, 
the Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, Spain ), plus Norway and UK  as partner and the international factoring 
association FCI. The banks and the financial companies that are part of the EUF network are more than 200. 
In the year 2020, they serviced approximately 1,7 trillion euros of trade receivables, mostly paid in advance 
to businesses (in particular SMEs). The European factoring market is number one in the world and represents 
2/3 of the world market. Factoring is a European specificity and excellence that supports the treasury of all 
businesses. 
 
Detailed comments 
The EUF welcomes the opportunity to provide its v iews and insights within the public consultation on  the 
Proposal for a  REGULATION OF THE EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL  amending Regulation 
(EU) No 575/2013 as regards requirements for credit risk, credit valuation adjustment risk, operational risk, 
market risk and the output floor issued by the European Commission. 
In general, the EUF appreciates that the new draft makes some steps forward toward a strengthened and 
more resilient prudential risk framework and also considers some of the comments that the EUF provided on 
the basis of the consultation paper in 2020. 
 
On the definition of “ancillary services undertaking” 
The EUF notes the following amendments to the definition of “ancillary services undertaking” (page 44 of the 
consultation paper): 
(b) point (18) is replaced by the following: 
‘(18) ‘ancillary services undertaking’ means an undertaking the principal activity of which, whether provided 
to undertakings inside the group or to clients outside the group, the competent authority considers to be any 
of the following: 
(a) a direct extension of banking; 
(b) operational leasing, factoring, the management of unit trusts, the ownership or management of property, 
the provision of data processing services or any other activity that is ancillary to banking; (c) any other activity 
considered similar by EBA to those mentioned in points (a) and (b);’. 
The EUF wishes to express its perplexity regarding the inclusion of factoring among ancillary service 
companies. 
Factoring and Commercial Finance companies (the factors) work with client businesses to provide finance to 
enable them to trade. These factors offer businesses (the sellers) the funding they need to meet day-to-day 
business expenses, including payments to suppliers, salaries, rent and rates. This  funding is known as 
“working capital”. The amount of funding that a factor can provide is contingent on the volume of sales the

Factoring and Commercial Finance is based on the idea of selling / assigning a business’s unpaid receivables 
(sales invoices) to the factor for a payment equivalent to the value of the invoices less a fee for offering the 
service and a charge for the period the invoice is financed. This means that instead of having to wait 30 or 60 
days, or even longer for payment from their customers (the buyers), the seller can have access to their funds 
usually within 24 hours and often sooner. The factor may then collect the invoice payment from the Buyer 
and recover their advance  or the Seller will collect and pass over the proceeds on behalf of the factor to 
achieve recovery. 
The factor will typically pay a certain amount (about 80%) initially with the balance when the buyer pays. This 
allows for the charges referred to above but  also allows a buffer for the dilution events that are typical in 
open account sales (e.g., issue of credit notes for damage in transit or price differences). 
The factor can also offer additional services to the Seller. They will offer assessments of the creditworthiness 
of the buyers that the seller is selling to. This service will be available to the seller both on existing and 
potential buyers and is a constant review service, helping the seller to make  informed real time decisions 
about offering credit. The factor can use this assessment to further support the Seller by taking responsibility 
for any buyer credit default, for example protracted late payment or insolvency. 
The factor can include sales ledger management and collection services. These may be  fully outsourced to 
the factor or may be kept in house by the Seller depending on the product offered.  Note that when the 
factor’s assignment of the receivables is disclosed to buyers the factor is actually collecting the receivables 
they own so this is not technically “outsourcing ”, but it gives an equivalent experience. When the Seller 
collects the receivables, they do so as the factor’s agent. The factor’s ownership of the receivables underpins 
the high degree of security that is achieved and as detailed below. 
For an SME, the opportunity to outsource its collection activity and manage the ledgers can be a very valuable 
benefit. For larger companies with developed and dedicated accounts departments, the collection and ledger 
monitoring and management activity can more easily be kept in house. The factor’s involvement in collection 
can help confirm the receivables proving their value to the factor and thus enabling factors to support even 
very young, small or fast-growing Sellers who might otherwise struggle to access sufficient working capital. 
The factor can offer credit cover, a type of buyer default protection which can give the seller greater  
confidence in selling in volume to its buyers, also in a cross -border context. Because of economies of scale, 
the cost of such credit cover is usually much less via the factor than it would be for the Seller to obtain on a 
stand-alone basis. 
Which version of the factoring offer is used depends on the detailed circumstances of the Seller, its  Buyers 
and the local market. The factor’s control suite is adapted to whether the assignment is disclosed to the buyer 
or kept undisclosed. 
The EUF understands that the intent of the Commission is to clarify the provisions on prudential consolidation 
to ensure that financial groups that are headed by fintech companies or include, in addition to institutions, 
other entities that engage directly or indirectly in financial activities are subject to consolidated supervision. 
However, in that perspective, we believe that the wording should be changed to achieve that goal.

Factoring is a form of financing in its own right  and should not be considered as ancillary to other (banking) 
services. Moreover, it is not comparable to any of the other services listed in letter (b). Finally, factoring is 
expressly included among lending activities  admitted to mutual recognition (see Annex 1 of Directive 
2013/36/EU). The vast majority of factoring companies in Europe is part of a banking group (and their 
exposures are already consolidated) and, in many EU Countries  but not all, financial companies specialized 
in factoring are individually supervised similarly to banks by local regulators.  
Factoring companies purchase receivables and normally hold the receivables to collect them at due date 
(although securitization is an option, it is not their business model). Therefore, factoring, in its most common 
forms, is offered to business and cannot be counted among those “transactions aimed at moving assets out 
of the scope of prudential consolidation, even though risks remain in the banking group ” as highlighted by 
the Commission in the explanatory memorandum. 
However, some business models might indeed rely on subsequent securitization or transfer of the purchased 
receivables to fund their operations. This is common among the most innovative undertakings. 
The EUF suggests that the  Commission further clarifies its concerns about “factoring  undertakings” and 
reckons that the inclusion of “factoring” among the “ancillary services” as provided in point (18)(1)(b) seems 
inappropriate. It might be the same for leasing. A more general reference to the “purchase of receivables by 
undertakings out of the scope of prudential consolidation” might better fit the purpose. 
 
On the eligibility of credit insurance as a CRM technique 
The EUF welcomes the recognition that “A high level of expertise and risk management has been developed 
by institutions in the EU […] in the use of credit insurance, in particular for trade finance purposes”.  
Credit insurance is a well-established product which, in its most basic form, provides protection against the 
risk of non-payment of receivables from the trade of goods or services. It is offered worldwide by specialist 
insurers (or via specialised units), but it is primarily found in European markets. As a result, the majority of 
(re)insurers active in the credit insurance sector are EU-based and regulated under the Solvency II regime. 
Due to the protection it offers, credit insurance has also become an important risk mitigation technique, used 
by many banks and often adapted to fulfil the current CRR requirements, facilitating hundreds of billions of 
Euro’s worth of trade-related financing such as factoring to the real economy. 
In this regard, the factoring industry strongly welcomes and supports the introduction of the new Article 506 
mandating EBA to report to the Commission on the eligibility and the use of credit insurance as a credit risk 
mitigation technique and on the appropriate risk parameters they should be associated with under the SA -
CR and foundation IRB approach , which is fully consistent with the desires and needs expressed by  the 
industry.  
Any recognition given to credit insurance as a CRM technique should also not seek to change features of 
credit insurance policies which are standard to insurance products more generally or which may be subject 
to negotiation between a bank and insurer as a commercial decision. This includes elements of conditionality 
which may be found in credit insurance policies. Such elements may be included as a result of the insurer’s 
wider management of its risks (including in meeting requirements under Solvency II), but they do not affect 
the quality of the protection provided to the bank.

On the proposal related to fraud by the obligor under amended Article 183(b)(iii) and Article 213(1)(d) 
The intended aim of the proposed amendment to Article 183(b)(iii)  and 213(1)(d) is understood as seeking 
to ensure that any credit protection contract which can, in the event fraud of the obligor, be cancelled or of 
which the extent of credit protection can be diminished, shall not be considered as unconditional. However, 
we believe that the proposal as currently drafted requires further clarification, as well as investigation into  
its potential impact particularly on credit insured invoice financing.   
As noted above, credit insurance provides protection against non -payment of receivables related to the 
specific trade(s) of goods or services. While a lending institution is expected  to have performed reasonable 
and appropriate due diligence (including as noted in line 7 of the proposed subparagraph iii), it is still possible 
that despite all best efforts, financing may be provided for an invoice which turns out to have been fraudulent 
(fraud committed by the counterparty of the lending institution). As the invoice is fraudulent, no specific or 
identifiable trade has occurred, which is the essential requirement of a credit insurance policy. Without an 
object of insurance, indemnification of the loss would stand outside the terms of the policy.  
Moreover, we are afraid that interpreting an EU Regulation as requiring the enforcement of guarantees even 
in the event of fraud, would raise high legal concerns regarding the compliance with the general principles 
of prohibition of fraud and abuse of rights  enclosed in the European legal framework. Such a provision was 
not included in the previous CRR . In several member states it may not be possible to comply with this 
requirement due to national legislation which would not make it possible to cover losses resulting from a 
lending agreement (between obligor and lending institution) that becomes void due to fraud. Finally, such a 
requirement would need a twist in the best practic es of the industry and potentially give room to moral 
hazard and opportunistic behaviours. The EUF therefore suggests deleting the words “Any credit protection 
contract which can, in the event of fraud of the obligor, be cancelled or of which the extent of  credit 
protection can be diminished, shall be considered to not meet those requirements” in both Article 183(b)(iii) 
and Article 213(1)(d). 
In any case, we believe that in factoring agreements covered by credit insurance policies, the “obligor” should 
be identified as the buyer (which is the subject that will pay the invoice and whose non-payment is covered 
by the insurance), not the client. 
If left unamended, the propose d text could have significant negative impact on the provision of credit 
insurance in such circumstances, which as outlined above, is an important tool for banks  and factoring 
companies, underpinning large volumes of financing arrangements.  
However, if credit insurance is given proper recognition as a result of the above-mentioned report prepared 
by EBA (and preferably together with EIOPA) and any subsequent proposal put forward by the Commission,

approaches but also under the SA-CR by way of: 
i. the possibility to consider purchased receivables as eligible CRM tools under the Standardized

In particular, with reference to point ii) in the above -mentioned list, the EUF would like to stress the 
opportunity to address the treatment of purchased receivables in the default of an obligor, in order to avoid 
misclassification as “default” of exposures to  debtors due to one or more trade payables that are past due 
for reasons other than financial distress , thus reducing the risk sensitiveness of the definition of default . A 
certain flexibility  in payments  is in nate in trade relationships, and it  is not affected by the fact that an 
institution purchases the receivables: on one hand, t he buyer does  not have any financial agreement with 
the institution (and may even be completely unaware of the assignment in the ca se of not notification); on 
the other hand, the buyer has signed a purchase agreement with the assignor (the client of the institution), 
under which the former agrees to pay a certain amount in consideration of a purchase of goods or services  
that fulfil specific requirements. Such conditions normally refer to the quantity, quality and price of the goods 
(services) to be sold, to the delivery times, to the ability and terms to issue the invoice, to the payment terms, 
to particular price adjustment such as premia, discounts, and so on. The occurrence of events related to the 
trade relationship, although perfectly normal in conducting business, might generate delays in the payment 
of invoices of which the counterparties are generally aware  but, in case of an institution purchasing the 
receivables, can influence the classification of the buyer as “defaulted” even when it does not show any sign 
of a deteriorating financial situation or delay on other exposures. 
In fact, the issue of misclassification to default  affects in particular the large enterprises and the very best 
debtors in terms of credit standing and financial robustness (such as Public Sector Entities), that have a more 
complex and formalized approval process to authorize the payment of the invoices of any supply. 
It is important to highlight again the fact that many businesses, in particular SMEs that are not able to access 
traditional bank lending, gain access to finance through the assignment of receivables thanks to the capacity 
of purchased receivables to consider the creditworthiness of the debtors as a mitigation of the credit risk of 
the client. Without the support provided by banks and supervised financial companies by way of the purchase 
of receivables, these businesses , mostly SMEs,  would suffer severe liquidity constraints. It is therefore 
indispensable to avoid unnecessary classification to default and assuring, at the same time, a timely 
recognition of the default of the borrower.  
The possibility to apply the facility level appro ach (already available for retail exposures) also to exposures 
to purchased receivables (i.e. at the level of the single invoice) would definitely reduce the risk of such 
misclassifications (“false positive”) and provide a balanced approach to the identification of default : the 
individual invoices past due by more than 90 days would be classified as “non performing” and would still 
attract the whole position  of the debtor  if the amount of past due invoices exceeds 20% of the total  
exposures, thanks to the pulling effect. In this regard, we believe this amendment would further increase the 
risk sensitiveness of the prudential regulation without negatively affecting the timely recognition of a default.

On Maturity of corporate purchased receivables 
The short-term and self -liquidating nature of Trade Finance, including Purchased Receivables, has already 
been recognized in the previous CRR: the effective residual maturity can be applied when calculating capital 
requirements. In addition , in the proposed wording of article 162.3 (second subparagraph, point b ), an 
exception is  provided for self -liquidating short -term trade finance transactions , including corporate 
purchased receivables. This is consistent with the short-term nature of the product. However, the currently 
proposed wording leaves room for possible misunderstandings as the exception to deviate from a 2 ,5 year 
Maturity appears at a first reading to be related to features uncommon in the factoring business, namely the 
requirement of re-margining and daily revaluation (first subparagraph of art .162.3).  
The outcome of this unclarity in the proposed wording might be a negative impact on the access to capital of 
SMEs. By nature, Trade Finance deals have very short maturities (normally up to 90 days), are self-liquidating 
and linked to underlying trade transactions. Applying an average M of 2 ,5 years to this kind of transaction 
will create a significant and unnecessary price increase for exposures on European corporates and SMEs.  
The EUF proposes to allow the use of effective maturity under F -IRB as well for purchased receivables, in 
article 162.1, as it is allowed for Securities Financing. And we propose to maintain the permission to break 
the 1-year floor set in article 162.6 for purchased receivables.

EuRIC Feedback on the Call for Evidence on the revision of the 
Waste Framework Directive 
22 February 2022  
 
The European Recycling Industries’ Confederation (EuRIC) represents the recycling industry at a 
European level. Gathering the vast majority of national recycling federations from EU/EEA Member 
States, the Confederation represents about 5.500+ recycling companies – from market leaders to SMEs 
– generating an aggregated annual turnover of about 95 billion € by treating various waste streams 
such as household or industrial & commercial waste including ferrous and non-ferrous metals, end-of-
life vehicles (ELVs), electronic waste (WEEE), packaging (paper and plastics), end-of-life tyres or textiles.  
 
EuRIC thanks the European Commission for publishing the Call for Evidence for an Impact Assessment 
with regards to the revision of the Waste Framework Directive  (WFD) and is pleased to provide some 
preliminary feedback essential for the European recycling industry.  
 
EuRIC acknowledge s the fact that the WFD has played a key role in the development of waste 
management in the EU. Yet, it is absolutely essential to ensure that any revision of the WFD will factor 
in the paradigm shift according to which waste is a resource to be recycled and not a risk to be treated. 
Among EuRIC key recommendations are to speed up harmonisation at EU level of end-of-waste criteria 
in order to ease market access and reward quality of raw materials from recycling (RMR), two elements 
which are absolutely essential to accelerate substitution of extracted raw materials and thus reduce 
GHG emissions and energy consumption.  
 
In addition, EuRIC calls the European Commission to set binding recycled content targets for base 
metals, paper, plastics (including technical plastics from WEEE and ELVs), rubber in tyres and glass in 
order to pull the demand for RMR in semi-finished products and products and thus boost circular value 
chains.   
 
On EPR Schemes, a one-size-fits-all approach is not suited to specificities of the various waste streams. 
For the existing EPR Schemes, it is absolutely essential to ensure an effective eco-modulation of fees to 
connect the dots between product design and recycling as well as to ensure that EPR schemes are not 
responsible for unfair competition in recycling markets, as currently witnessed and which is a major 
threat for circular value chains.   
 
1. Expanding the scope of European end-of-waste criteria (Art. 6) 
 
As of today, only 12% of raw materials used by the EU’s industry come from recycling ( EUROSTAT). 
Levelling the playing field with extracted raw materials is absolutely essential to boost circular value 
chains. In order to boost the substitution of extracted raw materials with raw materials from recycling 
(RMR), it is absolutely essential to speed up the harmonisation of EU -wide end-of-waste criteria at a 
much faster pace than what is currently envisaged: 
 
- Reinforce Article 6 of the WFD by laying down that the European Commission shall lay down 
EU-wide end -of-waste criteria for paper, plastics, end -of-life tyres and shall, based on the 
monitoring it carries out, automatically propose EU-wide end-of-waste criteria each time three 
Member States have adopted end-of-waste criteria for one given waste stream;  
- Provide that when a Member State has adopted end -of-waste criteria for one given  green-
listed waste stream, in line with Art. 6 par. 1 of the WFD, these criteria shall be recognised in 
other EU Member States in the framework of cross -border shipments in order to ensure that

2. Updating the waste hierarchy by taking into account new technologies (Art. 4) & adapting 
definitions to enhance circularity (Art. 3) 
 
EuRIC is not fundamentally in favour of changing definitions or overarching principles of the WFD. Yet, 
should the European Commission decide to update some key concepts enshrined in  it, it would be  
essential to ensure that these changes factor in some technological developments and hierarchise them 
based on their impacts on the environment.  
 
EuRIC advocates to keep the recycling definition (Art. 3 (17)) consistent, as the recycling industry has 
invested heavily to meet the expectations of this definition.  Yet, should the European Commission 
decide to revise the actual definition which is technology-neutral and target-oriented, it could be made 
more robust by highlighting the substitution element of recycling, since recycled materials substitute 
primary raw materials or substances: 
 
“Any recovery operation by which waste materials are reprocessed into products, materials or 
substances whether for the original or other purposes which substitute primary raw materials in  a 
production processes or in manufacturing. It includes the reprocessing of organic material but does not 
include energy recovery and the reprocessing into materials that are to be used as fuels or for backfilling 
operations”. 
 
According to this, chemical recycling it is included within its definition, whilst excluding substances used 
in energy recovery or as fuels (i.e., not including the transformation of waste into syngas/oils to be used 
as fuels). Thus, a distinction has to be drawn between chemical recycling to obtain polymers (products) 
and chemical recovery to obtain syngas and oils to be used as fuels.  
 
Another example of enhancing clarity with regards to terminology would be the overhaul of  the 
definition of waste (Art. 3 No. 1) to reflect the fact that wastes are resources (when properly recycled) 
and an enabler of the circular economy. 
 
EuRIC also firmly supports a more granular definition of the waste hierarchy where the recycling and 
recovery methods are structured according to their environmental footprint, especially for plastic 
waste. As shown below (Figure 1), chemical recycling sho uld be below mechanical and dissolution 
recycling and it should be separated from the polymer to oils, fuels and energy conversion.

3. Expanding rules on recycled content  
 
As stressed earlier on, the vast majority of raw materials used by the EU’s industry  do not come from 
recycling but from extracted raw materials . Worse, despite the fact that the circular economy has 
become a political motto globally, the world is not becom ing more circular but actually more linear. 
According to the Circularity Gap Report 2022, the world is only 8,6% circular, less than it was back in 
2018 (9,1%): 
 
The harsh reality is that between Paris and Glasgow, more than half a trillion tonnes of virgin 
materials were consumed. Also, the Circularity Gap got worse, not better. In the six years between 
headline-grabbing climate conferences, the global economy consumed 70% more than what the 
Earth can safely replenish.* This cannot continue—we only have one planet. 
  
Not only mineral extraction contributes to biodiversity losses and resource depletion but it also 
contributes directly to emissions of greenhouse gas es (GHG) since “ 70% of all GHG are related to 
material handling and us”. 
 
Recycled content for PET in the single-used plastics directive (SUP) have proved to be the most efficient 
tool to drive circularity from design to end-of-life stage. For the first time, the price of recycled materials 
is not correlated to primary (oil -based) material prices, which fail to internalise negative externalities. 
In practical, it is resulting in a virtuous circle where product design, waste collection, quality recycling 
and use of raw materials from recycling are dramatically improving as meeting these targets result in a 
collective responsibility to achieve circularity.  
 
It is thus essential in all waste legislations, starting with the WFD to adopt a life -cycle thinking which 
sets not only recycling  targets but also recycled content targets for all material streams. Recycled 
content will directly support the reduction in the use of extracted raw materials, an objective in line 
with the European Parliament request to the European Commission to “propose science-based binding 
EU mid -term and long -term targets for the reduction in the use of primary raw materials and 
environmental impacts;”1. 
 
Thus EuRIC calls for setting science-based recycled content targets either at product or semi -finished 
product level for at least: 
 
- Base metals 
- Paper 
- Plastics (packaging and technical plastics in WEEE and ELVs) 
- Glass  
- Rubber in tyres 
 
Moreover, a strong methodology for any recycled content targets  which distinguishes pre-consumer 
from post-consumer waste and robust reporting obligations are crucial to avoid possible green-washing 
opportunities.

4. Establishing more effective rules on fee eco-modulation for EPR schemes (Art. 8)  
 
Extended producer responsibility (EPR) can be a useful tool to strengthen re-use, prevention, recycling 
and other recovery of  some waste streams. Nonetheless, before establishing new EPR schemes or 
expanding their application, a differentiated approach should be taken for the specific waste streams 
concerned and it should be assessed whether other regulatory measures may be more suited to achieve 
its goals. 
 
Firstly, minimum requirements to boost the harmonization of effective eco-modulation are essential to 
ensure that fee modulation effectively contributes to the placing on the market of circular products.  
In practice, the modulation shall be sufficiently dissuasive to ensure that: 
- Poorly recyclable products are subject to dissuasive fee modulation to make them less if not 
uncompetitive;  
- Highly recyclable and using recycled materials products are subject to a modulation of fees 
which reward their circular design (bonus). 
 
Secondly, when it comes to recycling, regulatory measures creating a stable or increasing demand for 
raw materials from recycling are the only way to provide investment and planning security for the 
development of recycling infrastructure. Introducing new or expanding existing EPR schemes should 
therefore not push to the background that mandatory recycled content targets is the only way to 
strengthen recycling of waste on a systematic scale and effectively contribute to a more circular 
economy. 
 
Before setting extended producer responsibility for new waste streams, the E uropean Commission 
should undertake thorough investigations for determining whether the EPR -schemes already in place 
are actually contributing to eco-design, high waste collection rates and high-quality recycling or if there 
are any other measures that are more a ppropriate and/or cost-efficient to obtain goals for e.g. waste 
reduction and recycling. 
 
The introduction of new EPR schemes lead to important modifications in the waste management chain. 
In-depth studies and analyzes of each waste stream that may be affected and of the management chain 
of that flow should therefore indispensably be carried out beforehand. Well -functioning and highly 
consolidated waste streams and markets shall be maintained. Where there is an effective relationship 
between the waste producer and the manager, new requirements on EPR must be avoided. 
 
Finally, when setting new requirements on EPR or expanding the scope of existing EPR, consideration 
should be given to the establishment of harmonized mandatory eco-modulation requirements on EU-
level. 
 
5. Preventing any unfair competition from EPR Schemes in recycling markets  
 
EPR Schemes are increasingly interested in taking an operational role in the same markets where their 
role is to collect and finance proper waste treatment. Not only, this is the diversion from their essential 
role and the areas where EPR should improve – connecting the dots with producers to decisively steer 
eco-design for recycling – but it is also in blatant breach of basic principles of EU competition law. An 
entity which has among its responsibilities the role of organizing a market  by financing the co st of 
collection and granting contracts for recycling through tender procedures cannot also play an active 
role in the same market. Such a conflict of interest should be explicitly banned as it not only breaches 
EU competition law but it also poses major risks to circular value chains. In practice, it limits or entirely 
precludes access to RMR for product manufacturers, as PROs may be tempted to retain materials for 
their members or to decide unilaterally on the right type of use. A circular economy requires open and

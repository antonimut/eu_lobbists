Abstract 
FoodDrinkEurope welcomes the initiative to review the Waste Framework Directive to further 
decrease waste generation and improve waste collection. To achieve these objectives, a combination 
of policy options should be considered to ensure an effective implementation of specific waste stream 
policies under revision, specially the packaging waste directive and specific targets on food  waste. 
Such policy options include: 
Reviewing the definition of waste and recycling according to available infrastructure 
• Setting high recycling targets for plastic and ensuring effective separation of plastic. 
• Ensure harmonisation in waste management practices and finalise the harmonisation of Extended 
Producer Responsibility 
• Schemes and support investments in recycling and recognise enhanced recycling as a valid 
technology for recycling of materials. 
• Assess available Food waste data and build on food and drink industry’s efforts to set future food 
waste targets.

Introduction 
FoodDrinkEurope welcomes the initiative to review the Waste Framework Directive to further 
decrease waste generation and improve waste collection that will lead to better recycling outputs.  
Such review needs to help build a circular economy for packaging and avoid food waste in the EU, 
especially for the effective recycling of packaging and litter  avoidance. We believe that to achieve 
these objectives, a combination of policy options should be considered  to ensure an effective 
implementation of the packaging waste directive and specific targets on food waste. This list of options 
is outlined in this paper and in the FoodDrinkEurope Framework conditions for an effective roll-out of 
the reviewed EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive1. 
 
Ensuring predictability for operators 
The legislative review should guarantee legal and financial predictability for market operators and 
investors on the issue of packaging circularity including: 
 
Coherence and alignment  between all elements of the EU legislative framework for packaging, 
including the Waste Framew ork Directive, the Single -Use Plastics Directive, the PPWD and the EU 
taxonomy regulation;  
 
An appropriate transition phase that can accommodate business changes and ensure that all 
relevant players in the value chain are involved, including retailers; and a policy on packaging materials 
should  
 
Set generic objectives while avoiding to be too descriptive to allow for the necessary innovation to 
happen. Industry in this case is best placed to develop adequate solutions to reach the intended 
climate neutrality and environmental protection objectives. 
 
Definition of waste 
To achieve a more circular, resource efficient economy, it is important to clearly distinguish measures 
aimed at reduction of waste from measures aimed to reduce the quantities of materials lost to the 
environment and not recycled, and to clearly define th e ultimate common goal of those measures. 
The main goal of both types of measures should be to achieve minimum environmental footprint for 
the provision of products to the consumer up until the end of life and allow innovation at scale. The 
definition of w aste should be modified to reflect such considerations. Materials that are widely 
collected and recycled in closed loop  should be recognized as resource and not be considered as 
waste.

Definition of Recycling 
The definition of recycling should consider available recycling infrastructure and future technologies, 
including a classification and definition of different types of recycling technologies . This definition 
should also be consistent with the definitions provided by the Commission Food Contact Materials 
Regulation. Also the definition should provide clarity on the status of chemical recycling and on mass 
balance, which is necessary to account for chemically recycled plastic used in packaging and for making 
claims. The reviewed directive should also consider the definition of high-quality recycling and closed 
loop recycling to help redirect recycled packaging materials back to its original application. 
 
Setting high recycling targets for plastic, including at polymer level 
Increase recycling targets: Ambitious recycling targets are the single most important factor that will 
drive collection, sorting and recycling of plastics, including formats that today are difficult to recycle.  
They constitute the starting point for all further development on improvements along the packaging 
value chain (better infrastructure, less waste, more recycled content supply, etc). By setting ambitious 
enough targets for plastics in general, but also at  polymer and/or packaging type level, the whole 
waste system will have to ramp up to achieve them, instead of simply relying on recycling of ‘easy to 
recycle’ plastics such as PET, HDPE and clear PE/PP. General target for plastics recycling should be at 
a minimum 65%. Ideally, there should also be a specific target per polymer or per packaging type, e.g. 
50% for flexible packaging. The targets should be established in national legislation and need to flow 
down to the EPR schemes in each country, thus also avoiding a proliferation of local regulation that 
could hinder innovation and impact the internal market.  
Alongside high recycling targets for plastics, three further measures should be implemented to ensure 
that waste is sent for recycling: 
Landfill bans: Landfill is at the bottom of the waste hierarchy and must be avoided. Governments in 
the EU should ban landfilling of separately collected waste and of waste that is suitable for recycling. 
At a minimum, the current landfill restrictions tha t are to come into effect only in 2035 should be 
anticipated. In countries where an outright ban of landfill is not yet implemented, landfill taxes will 
help dissuade waste managers from pursuing this end of life.  
Gate fees for incineration : Incineration should be reduced. An effective way to do so would be to 
establish gate fees that make the economics of sending plastic waste to recycling relatively more 
attractive.  
Enforce 2018 PPWD reviewed recycling calculation method: The new calculation method should 
be applied uniformly in all member states in order to boost quantities of high quality recycled 
materials. 
Waste hierarchy:  Identifying a clear and adequate end of life for each material along the waste 
hierarchy is important and further where does each technology fit within the hierarchy eg waste to 
energy, chemical recycling, mechanical recycling, landfilling etc. The Commission should give guidance 
on the priority of such end of life options.

Reuse measures : Reuse measures, if related to a clear environmental benefit,  can assist towards 
achieving the EU green deal and reducing waste. The revision should promote and include me asures 
to incentivise and encourage the uptake of reuse measures be implemented. 
 
Ensuring effective separate collection and sorting 
Mandatory separate collection: If not yet implemented, separate collection should be considered 
country-wide and ensure all plastic packaging is separately collected and is separated from paper and 
glass. This means having separate collection of at least three streams : 1) all plastic packaging (with 
metal and cartons in most cases); 2) paper and cardboard; and 3) glass. In some countries, this will 
mean broad infrastructure roll-out to boost widespread separate collection, with increased costs for 
collection to be covered by EPR schemes. High gate fees for incineration will help to avoid that 
separately collected waste is diverted towards incineration. To facilitate recycling in closed loop  and 
to improve the availability of food grade recycled material, separate collection for specific materials 
and applications should be considered, i.e. for PET from beverage containers.  
Consumer education: Citizens are a key part of a successful waste management system and they 
need to be supported to properly sort waste at home. For example, with clear easy to understand on-
pack symbols that are harmonized at EU level – to avoid barriers to trade – including campaigns that 
explain the importance of waste sorting at home2. Even though the EPR schemes are often responsible 
for these campaigns, national and local governments can support them with specific budgets and 
channels to reach a maximum number of citizens.  
Improve sorting plants:  Investments need to be made in selected sorting facilities to  be able to 
process all difficult to recycle packaging (including flexible packaging).  Such investments should be 
directed to incorporate existing innovative technologies (such as robotics and A.I.) as well supporting 
R&I to find solutions to sort diverse types of packaging formats/polymers.  In countries with a high 
number of sorting plants (e.g. > 50), merging would facilitate the upgrade of the facilities. 
Alternatively, a number of plants need to be selected for improvements, which in practice means 
adding sorting lines for specific packaging.  
 
Harmonisation of waste management practices 
Promote full implementation : The provisions on waste prevention, preparation for re -use and 
recycling should undergo full implementation (e.g. enhanced cooperation with Member States (MS) 
and enforcement). 
Enforce provision on minimum quality of waste management infrastructures: The development 
of a common minimum quality requirement and an explicit role of the European Commission to define 
minimum requirements, as defined in Article 8.5 of the Waste Framework Directive (WFD), would 
ensure greater harmonization, performance and efficiency of waste management practices an d 
infrastructure across the EU.

Harmonise sorting requirements for biowaste  collection: It is necessary to ensure, with the 
support of the industry, that member states have properly implemented their obligations to drive 
circularity and collect compostable packaging for composting, recycling and recovery to meet the 
levels of recycling/recovery targets prescribed by EU legislation. Within this context, we would 
recommend, in support of the single market, to unify the approach of the sorting and collection o f 
compostable biowaste, so that compostable packaging is accepted in the biowaste bin  across all MS 
in the same way  if they meet defined requirements.  Requirements are to be review ed reflecting 
current industrial composting practises. Coherence with future policy considerations on compostable 
packaging in the upcoming PPWD review is essential for this as well. 
Improvements in Extended Producer Responsibility 
Publication of the guidance and minimum requirements for EPR: The Commission should finalise 
the work on the development of guidance(s) for the good functioning and Eco-modulation of Extended 
Producer Responsibility (EPR) to ensure harmonised measures across EU.  
Development of minimum requirements for DRS:  The Commission should include in their work 
program the development of guidelines and minimum requirements for an effective functioning of 
DRS schemes. 
End-to-end accountability:  Producers will continue to take joint responsibility over collection, 
sorting, recycling.. It is therefore also logical that EPRs have the necessary influence across the value 
chain to improve the overall system. Currently their influence is limited, with various municipalities as 
well as public and private sorters and recyclers, leading to wide variety in quality and efficiency. While 
majority operate on a not -for-profit basis, EPRs schemes should, where practical, form a panel of 
suppliers for each servi ce to stimulate effective processes and quality improvements. Governments 
should ensure oversight of PROs, which need to be accountable for what is recycled, based on 
thorough and detailed reporting and audit. This information is largely missing in many European EPR 
schemes today.  To facilitate  recycling in closed loop  where feasible, EPR s and DRS should  grant 
producers priority access to material that is collected through the system.  
Fee modulation: To stimulate circular design and improved recycling, EPR fees need to be  specific 
enough to reflect the real and net cost of processing (collecting, sorting and recycling) each material 
type, without generating excessive administrative burden for compani es and the EPR organisation. 
The so-called ‘fee modulation’ or ‘eco -modulation’ principle will bring necessary detail on fees by 
material and packaging type on the basis of clear definitions. European Commission guidelines on EPR 
eco-modulation should reflect on these concepts. We call on the commission to swiftly approve their 
due guidelines on EPR Eco modulation 
Waste management transparency and oversight: Companies forming part of an EPR organisation 
should have the possibility to supervise the way waste  is managed and EPR organisations should 
provide information on the end -of-life destination in a transparent manner. Governments will of 
course exercise oversight on EPR reporting to ensure they operate appropriately and comparably. 
Supporting investments in recycling  
Clarify the role of enhanced recycling for food contact materials: To recycle a diverse range of 
packaging materials it will be needed to both upgrade current mechanical recycling technology and to 
use enhanced recycling. The European Commiss ion should set clear legal indications to regulate in a

harmonised way across the Single Market the safe use of enhanced recycling techniques  to produce

We call on the Commission to include enhanced recycling in the definition of recycling and consider it 
as technique to be accounted in the achievement of the EU recycling targets.  We also call EC to clarify 
traceability matters and shed more light on the mass balance and allocation rules across the EU. 
Free movements of  recyclable waste and recycled material  within the EU as a priority : To 
support investments into nascent recycling infrastructure (mechanical and enhanced) we will need 
sufficient feedstock which cannot be stopped within national borders. We are concerned about 
measures that would discriminate recycled plastics that come from outside one country, and would 
impede waste feedstock to travel within the EU to be recycled in the country if the country of origin 
origin does not have yet the appropriate recycling infrastructure. Also t he European Commission 
should put in place clear measures to prioritise high -quality recycling of packaging materials within 
the EU. Such measures should limit the expo rt of recyclable materials to third -countries. This will 
ensure that recyclable materials are recycled in the EU and reused, in priority, into new products in 
the Union.  Finally we call on the Commission to limit the export of recyclable materials to third-

Food Waste 
Develop EU guidelines and training tools: New guidelines and training tools should provide support 
to help implement the Delegated Act on food waste measurement adopted in 2019. These guidelines 
could encourage and assist the dif ferent actors in the supply chain with interpreting the data and 
reporting to Member States (MS). 
Assess Member States data: The commission will have soon available the data collected from the 
two first reporting years (2020 -2022) mandated by the WFD in 2018. since it is the first time MS, 
alongside organizations in the supply chain, are engaged in such a comprehensive exercise.  This 
collection should give provide an appropriate ground for assessing the level of the targets to be set. 
Carry out thorough and scientifically based Impact Assessments : The impact assessment should 
asses carefully the policy measures/options proposed, taking into consideration the various actors in 
the food supply chain alongside the three pillars of sustainability. The impact assessments should also 
focus on trade-offs (i.e., packaging v food waste). 
Further encourage and build upon  efforts:  At EU and MS level,  efforts have already 
been undertaken by the various actors of the food supply chain to reduce and prevent food waste, 
including by the food and drink industry. It is important to note that some actors might have taken 
effective waste reducing actions in the past which means further implementing measures need to be 
even more efficient to effectively tackle the problem.

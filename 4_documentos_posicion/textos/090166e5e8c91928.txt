Improve the overall environmental outcome of waste management 
DUH welcomes the objective of the initiative to improve the overall environmental outcome of waste 
management in line with the waste hierarchy and the implementation of the polluter pays principle.  
We welcome the revision of the Waste Fra mework Directive (WFD) as an opportunity to promote waste 
prevention and re-use over waste recovery and disposal. Since total waste generation has been increasing 
over the last years, it is important to prioritise the reduction of waste through re-use of products or com-
ponents. Reducing mixed waste and increasing preparation for re-use or recycling of waste by improving 
separate collection is a valid and important objective. 
In order to reach these goals, neither the full implementation of the existing prov isions on waste pre-
vention, preparation for re-use and recycling, nor additional guidance on these provisions and their ap-
plication will be sufficient. While the latter two are important, the  introduction of further regulatory 
measures is inevitable.  
In this position paper, we would like to comment on the proposed policy options and make further 
suggestions regarding changes in the WFD. A small number of propositions also concern other legisla-
tive acts, such as the PPWD, but were added for reasons of comprehensiveness.

Feedback on the proposed policy options 
Targets on waste reduction/targets on residual waste reduction 
 
We highly appreciate the introduction of legally binding and mandatory waste reduction targets in the 
Waste Framework Directive (WFD). To minimize the ecological impact of products and waste overall, the 
generation of waste has to be reduced . Therefore, the definition of verifiable, ambitious quantitative 
waste reduction targets and concrete deadlines for their transposition is inevitable. Waste reduction tar-
gets are important to measure success and set an overarching objective for all. 
 
Waste reduction targets for residual waste should be as follows:  
 150 kg per capita and year by 2027  
 120 kg per capita and year by 2030  
 
Waste reduction targets for packaging waste should be as follows: 
 120 kg per capita and year by 2027  
 90 kg per capita and year by 2030  
 
Waste reduction targets for bulky waste should be as follows:  
 140 kg per capita and year by 2027  
 90 kg per capita and year by 2030  
 
In line with the farm to fork strategy, food waste should be reduced by 50% until 2030.  
Capping the bio-waste content in residual waste  
 Max. 25% bio-waste content in residual waste by 2030  
 Max. 15% bio-waste content in residual waste by 2035  
 
Waste reduction in the construction and demolition sector 
Mineral waste and construction and demolition waste is the largest waste stream by mass and is respon-
sible for a high level of resource consumption and greenhouse gas emissions. It is therefore necessary to 
ensure that waste is avoided as far as possible, for example to reduce unnecessary transport emissions. 
One possibility is to prioritize renovation over demolition and building of new buildings, or to use exca-
vated soil directly on site before it reaches waste status.  Furthermore, a pre-demolition audit to identify 
reusable components prevents those from becoming waste. The Commission should call for and promote 
such and similar measures, if possible also within the framework of the WFD.

Expansion of the role of EPR schemes in attaining waste-prevention objec-
tives 
To further strengthen waste prevention, it is necessary to implement regulation into existing or new EPR 
schemes, which provide incentives to prevent waste generation, be it by influencing product design for 
better reusability, repairability or recyclability, or by setting economic incentives to reduce use of re-
sources.  
» Ecomodulation of fees in EPR schemes  
EPR schemes should be set up in a way that they favour the usage of recycled material and recyclabil-
ity, but also the prevention of waste in general. This ecomodulation of fees in EPR schemes can be 
implemented by setting fees high enough to not only allow  for adequate funding of separate collec-
tion and high-quality recycling processes, but to also raise costs on the use of virgin materials, which 
gives recycled materials a relative advantage. Furthermore, the acquired funds can be used to fund 
the development of measures of waste prevention or support (the development of) reuse systems.  
» Duty of care 
Duty of care is a form of product responsibility and has recently been introduced to German law (‘Ob-
hutspflicht’). It applies to all products, covers their distribution - including their transport and storage 
- and requires the maintenance of the usability of the product. It prioritizes waste avoidance over 
disposal as waste. This includes the obligation for the responsible party, such as a producer of ele c-
tronic goods or textiles, to not turn a product into waste by a volitional decision without necessity. 
One exemplary application of this principle is a ban on the destruction of returned goods in online 
retailing.  
» Waste prevention in EPR schemes for construction material  
Additional measures on waste prevention for construction material could be to oblige manufacturers 
of building materials to provide evidence of recyclability and their pollutant-free status, specifying the 
percentage of products that can  be recycled (a) as clean offcuts or construction site waste and (b) 
from dismantling or refurbishment. 
 
Introducing minimum requirements for source segregation and separate col-
lection of waste 
The separate collection of waste is a precondition  for high-quality recycling, preparation for reuse  and 
reuse. It also prevents hazardous substances from contaminating other waste streams as well as commu-
nities and the environment. 
» Collection points/ ‘recycling centres’  
To improve the prevention and collection of municipal waste, DUH proposes to extend regulations for 
collection points (e.g. recycling centres) to ensure a certain level of quality. Collection points must be 
obliged to separate certain amounts of delivered goods (e.g. bulky waste, WEEE) for reuse. For furni-
ture, textiles, shoes, household goods, WEEE, toys, books, bikes etc., collection points must form co -
operations with reuse initiatives or offer on-site sharing spaces for free takeaway. They must also care 
for appropriate collection and storage of reusable goods. When arriving at collection points, the con-
sumer must be given the chance to separate intact goods for reuse. In addition, collection point em-
ployees must collect reusables from the delivered goods on their own initiative.

In this context, collection points must also provide information for consumers on the environmental 
benefits of reuse and on where different types of reusables can be collected for reuse (online plat-
forms and stationary collection points). Municipalities must be  supported to operate second hand 
stores where collected goods, e.g. from collection points, can be examined, repaired and sold.  
» Close-to-home sharing systems  
Close-to-home sharing systems (free drop -off and takeaway) for small reusables should be estab-
lished legally promoting the reuse of textiles, household goods, books, shoes, toys etc. These may be 
reuse boxes, shelves in town halls, public buildings, shops etc. These systems must be supervised by 
professionals, so that they remain clean and are protected from theft.  
» Promotion of reuse business models  
Municipalities must be supported and encouraged to operate second hand stores where collected 
goods, e.g. from collection points, can be examined, repaired and sold. Overall, the EU should set 
incentives to business models to promote reuse, sharing or lending. This can be applied to many ma-
terial streams.  
» Bulky waste  
Despite a universal agreement on the waste hierarchy, the principle of reuse is still not implemented 
sufficiently, especially in the case of bulky waste. To ensure that more products are used and reused 
for a longer time period, non-destructive collection of bulky waste must be mandatory. Collected end-
of-life products must be refurbished or repaired. Mandatory tests to determine whether i t is techni-
cally possible and economically reasonable (including if there is a market) must be put in place. Ex-
ceptions to the obligation to collect and reuse non -destructively may only be permitted if reuse can 
be ruled out for technical reasons and this can be documented and recorded by authorities. In Ger-
many but also in Austria or Flanders in Belgium, several waste management companies are already 
demonstrating how preparing for reuse and subsequent sale of still-functioning goods in second-hand 
stores can succeed and, in addition to climate savings, also contribute to the creation of green jobs.  
» Separate collection of demolition waste  
To enable recycling in the first place, materials need to be separated in the demolition process. Thus, 
the controls of obligations for separation of demolition waste must be increased and enforced. Fur-
thermore, it is important to expand the separation obligations for relevant product groups according 
to material classes, such as insulation materials, in order to allow re cycling of particular insulation 
materials, e.g., stone wool.  
 
Expanding EPR schemes to other product categories 
Many manufacturers have not been held accountable for the environmental impact of the products they 
place on the market. Consequently, the ecological follow -up costs of many products are not reflected in 
their price. Therefore, EPR schemes should be expanded and made mandatory at least in reg ards to the 
following products: 
 Building materials ( Further conditions regarding an EPR scheme for building materials are pre-
sented on page 8)  
 Textiles  
 Furniture

Improving enforcement of EPR requirements, in particular for products sold 
online to customers in the EU  
To improve the enforcement of EPR requirements, it is especially important to consider the role  online 
marketplaces play in distributing products. It is important to require online marketplaces to assume re-
sponsibility in meeting the obligations of EPR schemes.  
 Online marketplaces and fulfilment service providers  shall be defined as an actor in the supply 
chain by legal definition – e.g., as importer.  If no other actor located in the EU is liable to consum-
ers, online marketplaces and fulfilment service providers must take full responsibility of the prod-
ucts they enable to sell. 
 Online marketplaces and fulfilment service providers must be subject to comprehensive due dil-
igence obligations for the products for wh ich they enable the sale. This includes, for example, 
checking that manufacturer, distributor and collector obligations are met.  
 Online marketplaces must show the full contact information of the EU actor , which is liable to 
consumers, and in the case of products subject to extended producer responsibility further info-
mation, such as the manufacturer's registration number for the products of which they enable the 
sale.  
Further need for amendments of the WFD 
Article 3: Definitions  
» Improve the definition of recycling  
A revised definition of “recycling” must ensure that only usable and safe material with sufficient 
quality that originates demonstrably from a post-consumer waste fraction is defined as “recycled”. 
Calculation rules must account for all additives, solvents, losses and chemical reactions within recy-
cling processes and preclude dissolution with virgin material. Additionally, process products that are 
usually incinerated (such as fuels, aluminium  for cement kilns) must be clearly expelled from the re-
cycling targets. For the case of plastic recycling, only plastic -to-plastic recycling should contribute to 
recycling targets to avoid downcycling and truly closing the loop.  
» Classification of chemical recycling as ‘chemical recovery’   
Only mechanical recycling and chemical recycling in which plastic is not broken down to molecular 
level is acceptable as “recycling”. In this sense, thermal decomposition such as pyrolysis or gasification 
must not contribute to recycling targets or recycled content.  
Conversion of plastic waste into molecules level feedstock via thermal decomposition should instead 
be classified as chemical recovery in the legislation and added as a new level in the waste hierarchy 
below chemical recycling (polymer to monomer level depolymerisation via techniques such as solvol-
ysis). Both techniques should be placed below mechanical recycling. See position paper 
https://ecostandard.org/publications/joint-position-paper-chemical-recycling-and-recovery/   
This classification is based upon the following rationale: Thermal decompositi on techniques result in 
lowest level feedstocks which are used in the refining process. The produced molecules are utilised 
at the onset of the plastic production value chain. Efficiencies and recovery rates are low while energy 
demand and carbon emissions  are high. Material leakages and energy requirements do not enable 
true circularity.

From current knowledge, it is quite evident that mechanical recycling is more environmentally -
friendly than chemical recycling and recovery. For environmental impacts of c hemical recycling see 
https://zerowasteeurope.eu/library/understanding-the-environmental-impacts-of-chemical-recy-
cling-ten-concerns-with-existing-life-cycle-assessments/ (LCA review).  
» Recovery  
Processes of “thermal decomposition” such as pyrolysis and gasification should be classified as “chem-
ical recovery” (see above). Chemical recovery should be the very last resort for waste that cannot be 
mechanically or chemically recycled. Ecodesign principles should focus on prevention, reduction, and 
reuse as main priorities. Specific design criteria for recycling should only be targeted to m echanical 
recycling and should not be established for chemical recycling and chemical recovery. With effective 
redesign of products using toxic-free materials, chemical recovery should have a diminishing role and 
scope in plastics waste treatment.  
» Exclusion of the mass balance approach  
DUH opposes allowing the calculation of “recycled waste” via mass balance approach. The current 
calculation method proposed for techniques such as chemical recycling/recovery is not reliable and 
may promote false developmen ts and greenwashing. It allows marketing of products as “recycled” 
although they do not contain any recyclates. Traceability of recycled materials is important for the 
credibility of recycling systems, especially when virtual trade between plastic types is  allowed. Fur-
thermore, incentives for eco -design would be lowered if “creative accounting” beyond plastic types 
were to be acknowledged. DUH proposes batch level mass balance approach as described in 
https://zerowasteeurope.eu/library/joint-letter-to-the-european-commission-avoid-the-mass-bal-
ance-approach-to-block-ambitions-for-increased-recycled-content-in-plastics/ and 
https://zerowasteeurope.eu/library/determining-recycled-content-with-the-mass-balance-ap-
proach-10-recommendations-for-development-of-methods-and-standards.  
» Composting and fermentation of ‘bioplastics’  
For compostable packaging, the definition of recycling must be clarified in order to exclude compost-
ing or fermentation processes from the recycling definition. During composting and fermentation, 
mainly carbon dioxide, methane, water and only very little amounts of plant nutrients or other bene-
ficial soil constituents are generated. For the case of composting, not even energy is recovered from 
the material. To fulfil the waste hierarchy, compostable plastics, as other plastics, should preferably 
be avoided and designed for long durability and (mechanical) recycling. I f bioplastics are recycled, 
recycled products must be usable polymers for common plastic applications. From an environmental 
perspective, composting/ fermentation of packaging made from bioplastics should be considered as 
disposal. 
 
Article 11: Preparing for re-use and recycling  
» Promote reuse through separate mandatory reuse targets  
While the introduction of mandatory recycling targets did prove effective, a mandatory reuse target 
is lacking up to this point. To better implement the waste hierarchy and i ts higher stages, in addition 
to mandatory waste reduction targets, mandatory reuse targets are necessary. Some of these could 
be regulated in other directives, such as the PPWD.

We propose the following:  
Reuse targets for textiles should be as follows:  
 at least 10 %  by 2027  
 at least 15% by 2030  
Reuse targets for bulky waste should be as follows:  
 at least 10 %  by 2027  
 at least 15% by 2030  
Reuse targets for sales packaging, shipment packaging, transport packaging and beverage packag-
ing 
Reuse targets for sales /shipment/transport and beverage should increase by at least 15 % by 2025 
and 30 % by 2030 in relation to 2018. In Germany, there is a reuse target of 70 percent for beverage 
packaging. That should be the norm in the EU. Beyond beverage p ackaging, a reuse target of 25 per-
cent by 2025 and 40 percent by 2030 should be set for sales packaging. For transport packaging, a 
reuse rate of 70 percent should apply from 2025.  
Furthermore, an assessment of a potential reuse targets for construction material, construction and 
demolition waste should take place (further elaboration on this topic in the next section). 
» Specify and increase recycling targets and improve calculation method  
To calculate the achievement of recycling targets, the calculation met hodology should be based on the 
final output of the recycling process and not the input into the final recycling operation (Article 11a 1. 
(c)).  
 Increase recycling targets – Article 11 2.  
By 2030, the preparing for re-use and the recycling of municipal waste shall be increased to a 
minimum of 70 % by weight and the preparing for re-use and the recycling of packaging waste 
shall be increased to a minimum of 80 %. Furthermore, the Commission shall propose a separate 
and ambitious recycling target for plastic. Also, an assessment of material-specific recycling tar-
gets a for construction material, construction and demolition waste should take place (further 
elaboration on this topic in the next section).  
 Backfilling should be excluded from the recycling rate calculation of construction and demoli-
tion waste Article 11 2. (b)  
Backfilling of building materials and demolition waste should not be considered recycling, since 
low-quality applications such as filling up mines or applications in road construction are not pre-
venting the use of primary construction materials for buildings. In Germany, the recycling rate 
due to backfilling is 90% - but the substitution of primary construction material is just about 13%. 
Thus, backfilling should be removed from Article 11 Paragraph 2(b).   
 Backfilling of construction and demolition waste should not be implemented in applications 
without going through recycling treatment first  
In order to retrieve valuable materials for high-quality application from construction and demoli-
tion waste before direct backfilling without any recycling treatment process should be pre-
vented. A specific recycling category should be introduced that is allowed for backfilling while 
higher quality recycling outcomes should be prevented to go into backfilling.

Construction material and construction and demolition waste  
The construction industry is booming, which is creating great challenges in the area of environmental and 
resource protection. This is increasingly catching the attention of policy-makers, who have so far concen-
trated primarily on energy-efficiency in buildings. Measures in the construction sector, however, need to 
be addressed along the whole life cycle to lift the full potential. Thus, we would like to suggest the follow-
ing:  
 Introduction of an EPR scheme for construction materials 
The introduction of a mandatory extended producer responsibility (EPR) scheme for construction 
materials is more than overdue, due to the environmental impact of such materials on the climate 
and environment. We suggest an introduction of EPR for construct ion material similar to the EU-
wide approaches for batteries, packaging and electrical appliances. An effective approach is al-
ready taken by France introducing an EPR scheme for construction materials in 2023. Extended 
producer responsibilities for construction materials would increase collection targets and ensure 
a disposal free of charge for building owners. Thus, building owners are not drawn to low -cost 
landfilling or incineration. In addition, effective measures such as a mandatory take -back system 
for properly collected construction cut -offs can be introduced, as such material can often be in-
cluded directly into the production process. An EPR scheme could therefore ensure that essential 
measures are taken to create a sufficient circularity for construction materials.  
In the following, we would like to draw your attention to particular suggestions to improve the waste 
management of construction material and demolition waste:   
 Mandatory pre-demolition audit to every deconstruction and major renovation 
A compulsory inspection of the building and components prior to every deconstruction and major 
renovation project should be introduced. Before the deconstruction can begin, hazardous material 
should be removed beforehand. Thus, an effective deconstruction can take place to avoid prevent-
able waste and enable reuse and high quality recycling in the first place. Common EU wide guide-
lines such as the “Guidelines for the waste audits before demolition and renovation works of build-
ings” should be set as minimal standard for such pre-demolition audits.  
 Expand the waste collection obligations for construction materials 
Higher quality recycling can be enabled when particular construction material groups are collected 
separately by material class. It would be b eneficial to expand the obligations in the collection of 
construction materials and demolition waste for relevant product groups such as insulation mate-
rials to be collected by specific material classes, such as stone wool, EPS, XPS, PU etc.   
 Assessment of material-specific recycling targets and potential reuse targets for construction 
materials  
Recycling targets for construction materials should be specified for each of the major construction 
material categories such as, glass, plastic, metals, wood, ins ulation material, bitumen, gypsum, 
clay bricks, concrete and tiles. In order to do so, an assessment of recycling targets should be 
started to set ambitious but practical targets. Additionally, a study of potential reuse targets for 
specific material categories will enable to also set reuse targets in the near future.   
 Specify End-of-Life criteria 
 A detailed specification of the End-of-Life criteria for construction materials is important to clarify 
managing reusable and recycled materials for every actor in the supply chain. It will also increase 
the acceptance of secondary or reusable products, due to applicable definitions as “products” and 
not as “waste”.

Article 22: Bio-Waste  
With a share of 34 percent - 86 million tons in absolute terms in the EU -28 in 2017 - biowaste makes up 
the largest single fraction in municipal waste in the EU (European Environment Agency (2020): Bio-waste 
in Europe). High-quality recycling of biowaste is therefore key to the transition to a circular economy and 
to achieving the recycling target for municipal waste of 65 percent by 2035. If the amount of residual 
municipal waste is to be halved by 2030, according to CEAP, this ca n only be achieved with consistent 
separate collection of biowaste. In line with the farm to fork strategy, food waste should be reduced by 
50% until 2030.  
» Implementing separate collection, strict interpretation of exemptions   
Separate collection obligations from 2024 must be implemented consistently in order to leverage cli-
mate protection potentials of high-quality fermentation and composting of biowaste. Exemptions ac-
cording to Art. 10 (3 c), d)) regarding technical feasibility and economic costs of sep arate collection 
should only be rarely applied by member states. Technical feasibility has been tested and established 
with existing composting and fermentation processes for years. Likewise, economic feasibility is given, 
as confirmed by numerous studies (Bourka et al., 2015; Rodrigues et al., 2015; Niskanen and Kemppi, 
2019). Derogations should therefore not only regularly reviewed by member states but require an 
independent third-party scientific assessment.  
» Mandatory kerbside collection system for higher collection rates  
Consumer-friendly design of bio-waste collection is crucial for high collection rates. Experience from 
Germany clearly shows that only the provision of bio -bins achieves significant collection volumes 
(over 20kg per capita). If cons umers have to take their biowaste to collection points e.g. recycling 
centres or public collection points far off, only a small fraction of the biowaste is collected. In addition, 
it has been proven that consumers predominantly use their car for those disp osal ways, which con-
tradicts climate protection measures. This is why, a kerbside collection system at least for kitchen and 
food waste shall be made mandatory within the WFD , certain derogations as outlined in Art. 10 par-
agraph 3 can be applied in exceptional cases. Additional collection points can be a useful addition 
for  biodegradable garden and park waste, but not as a replacement for a biowaste garbage bin.  
» Exclusion of biodegradable plastics from bio-waste  
The second sentence of Article 22 (1) should be deleted to ensure that no bioplastics are composted 
or fermented during organic waste processing. Composting bioplastics a) may rise problems in treat-
ment facilities b) promote release of particulate plastics and  pollutants into the environment and c) 
is not environmentally preferable. Both arguments will be further illustrated in the subsequent para-
graphs.  
Firstly, composting of biodegradable plastics often results in problems for composting or digestion 
plants. In Germany, such products are often regarded as impurities, not composted according to EN 
13432 and removed by pre -sorting (see survey of DUH among composting plants in 2015/2016: 
https://www.duh.de/fileadmin/user_upload/download/Projektinformation/Kreis-
laufwirtschaft/Verpackungen/180920_DUH_Ergebnisbericht_Kompostierungsumfrage.pdf). In this 
context, compostable packaging (with the exception of carrier bags for collection of biowaste) are not 
allowed for separate organic collection in Germany. This regulation was further clarified during recent 
amendment of German Biowaste Ordinance (beginning of 2022). Additionally, municipalities have the 
possibility to further restrict allowed products for separate organic waste collection and the survey 
(see above) revealed that only 13 % of German municipalities allow such specific bags fo r biowaste 
collection. Other municipalities, therefore, do not allow all types of compostable packaging for sepa-
rate collection of organic waste.

In addition, plant operators in Germany are speaking against treatment of compostable packaging in 
organic was te treatment plants (see https://www.bvse.de/images/pdf/Presse/00-Posi-
tion_Verb%C3%A4nde_BAW_2019-final_3.pdf and https://www.wirfuerbio.de/).  
Secondly, if compostable plastics do not degrade completely within treatment facilities, significant 
amounts of plastic particles/microplastics as well as pollutants may be released into the environment 
via produced composts or digestates. Please note that current standards such as EN 13432 still allows 
that 100% of the material can still be present in the form of particles (10% > 2mm and 90% < 2 mm = 
microplastics) at the end of composting. In addition, conditions for the composting process are quite 
unrealistic (e.g. concerning composting times which are often much shorter in Germany (6-8 weeks)). 
Furthermore, there are no restrictions for toxic organic pollutants in the standards. As a consequence, 
plastic particles/ microplastics and pollutants may end up in agricultural soils, accumulate and lead to 
negative long-term effects on soil fertility.  
Thirdly, composting of packaging should not be considered environmentally preferable. T o our 
knowledge, c ompostable packaging usually does not contain significant amounts of nutrients and 
mainly degrades to CO2 and water without generation of valuable material for soil structure. Addi-
tionally, energy contained in the material is usually lost during composting  and a circulation of the 
material would be technically possible (through application of conventional plastics or build-up of the 
respective infrastructure). According to the waste hierarchy, waste should be avoided or reduced by 
reuse in the first place. This is also of importance for compostable packaging manufactured from bio-
based material, because agricultural production of renewable raw materials is also connected to con-
siderable environmental impacts.   
» Requirements for home composting  
The encouragement of home composting (Art. 22 para. 2 b) must be subject to certain preconditions, 
e.g. proof of a minimum size of the garden, which needs to be controlled by municipal waste disposal 
companies to avoid overfertilization. The Federal Enviro nment Agency in Germany recommends a 
necessary minimum size of seventy square meters. A relevant part of kitchen waste is not suitable for 
home composting (e.g. meat, citrus fruits, bones), making biowaste bins also necessary for home 
composting households. Moreover, improper home composting can contribute to the formation of 
climate-damaging methane.  
» Capping the bio-waste content in residual waste  
In addition to mandatory separate collection and recycling targets for bio -waste, further incentives 
are needed to increase bio-waste collection volumes and to prevent the incineration and landfilling of 
biowaste. We propose the introduction of targets to reduce the bio -waste content in residual waste 
as follows:  
 Max. 25% bio-waste content in residual waste by 2030  
 Max. 15% bio-waste content in residual waste by 2035  
Member states need to report these figures together with the figures on recycling rates to the EU 
Commission. Depending on the design of the EU ETS reform, measurements of the fossil carbon and 
organic carbon content of residual waste in incineration plants could be combined here in order to 
reduce efforts.

Further measures to pave the way for more waste prevention and recycling  
Further measures to emphasis waste prevention, reuse and recycling would be the following:  
1. Safeguard achieving the targets  
Assurance of target achievement via interim targets, early warning systems (Article 11b) and mon-
itoring of statistics. 
2. Restrict and ban landfilling 
We demand a landfill ban on untreated municipal and industrial waste by 2025. Landfilled munic-
ipal waste has to be reduced to 5% by 2030.  
3. Introduce levies on certain disposable items 
For certain disposable items, such as single-use beverage packaging, an additional levy should be 
charged. The effectiveness of levies on environmentally harmful single -use products could be 
shown in Ire-land, where a levy on plastic bags led to a 96 percent reduction in consum ption. To 
build on this success, the Irish environment ministry is now planning levies on several other single-
use products like single-use coffee cups.  
4. Expand Green Public Procurement 
There should be an obligatory preference of reused products and reusable/refillable packaging or 
products with a reliable ecolabel and an obligation to state the reasons in case of deviations.

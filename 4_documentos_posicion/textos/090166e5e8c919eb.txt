 
  
Revision of the Waste Framework Directive (WFD) 
TOMRA feedback on the Call for Evidence 
 
TOMRA, a leading provider of collection and recycling solutions, supports the upcoming revision 
of the Waste Framework Directi ve 2008/98/EC (WFD) and its objective of improving waste 
management in the EU by, among other things, reducing mixed waste and increasing preparation 
for re-use or recycling of waste to achieve the set targets. 
We believe that the EU waste hierarchy should be s trictly followed with prevention as a priority. 
For the purpose of this paper we focus on three elements we believe should be carefully 
considered in the revision process, as they have a key role to play in the achievement of the 
aforementioned objectives.  
Firstly, certain definitions and the EU waste hierarchy must be updated to reflect the current 
circular economy ambitions and ensure the promotion of high -quality recycling over downcycling 
and other forms of recovery. The waste hierarchy should include additional categorisation to 
distinguish between closed -loop and open -loop recycling (with priority given to closed -loop 
recycling), as well as between mechanical and chemical recycling to ensure that proven 
mechanical recycling processes are prioritised where technically possible.  
Secondly, in addition to further strengthening separate collections , mandatory mixed waste 
sorting prior to landfill and incineration should be introduced in order to increase the capture rate 
of valuable recyclable materials . Introducing mixed waste sorting combined with advance d 
mechanical recycling processes can ensure that valuable resources are effectively circulated back 
into the economy , reduces GHG emissions from waste management, and brings EU Member 
States closer to reaching their municipal waste recycling targets.  
Thirdly, in line with the upcoming Textiles Strategy, and the overall objectives of the Green Deal 
and the Circular Economy Action Plan of enhancing the overall circularity of the textile sector, the 
revision of the WFD should also address certain downstream considerations linked to textile 
waste, including recycling targets and the introduction of mandatory Extended Producer 
Responsibility (EPR) to enable the scaling up of collection, sorting and recycling infrastructure.  
Introduce clear definitions and refine the waste hierarchy  
Define the term “high-quality recycling” to close the loop on materials 
The term “high-quality recycling” is mentioned on several occasions in the current legal text of the 
WFD, however, a clear explanation of what the term stands for is lacking. We believe the revision 
of the WFD should introduce a clear definition of the term in the spirit of circular economy intentions 
to promote recycling of materials whereby they retain their highest and best use. 
Refine the waste hierarchy to favour closed-loop recycling over open-loop recycling 
The EU waste hierarchy establishes an order of waste management options with priority given to 
waste prevention, followed by reuse and then recycling, and has been instrumental in guiding 
Member States’ policies and funding decisions. In the face of climate urgency and rising ci rcular

economy ambitions, we believe it is now time to refine the waste hierarchy by introducing additional 
categories that will help provide more clarity and drive circularity. 
In particular with regards to recycling, additional distinction should be introduced between closed-
loop and open-loop recycling. With closed-loop recycling, materials are recycled back to the same 
or similar application, and this could be done for multiple cycles. Under open-loop recycling, a 
product at the end of its life is recycled into less demanding applications whereby the materials are 
downgraded and not anymore fit for recycling for the same  purpose. Thus, only closed-loop 
recycling enables the continuous recycling and return into the circular economy  to ensure that 
primary raw materials are preserved in the long run.  
For polymers, the most successful example of a closed -loop recycling process is the system for 
polyethylene terephthalate (PET) beverage contain ers. In markets with Deposit Return Systems 
(DRS) in place for beverage containers, PET bottles are collected in a dedicated closed loop to 
ensure they can be recycled back into new bottles in the most effective way possible. The collection 
of empty beverage containers can be automated through reverse vending machines, which helps 
avoid contamination from other types of waste. So far 10 EU Member States have in place DRS 
and others are following suit.  Currently, while high-quality rPET has become a widely availa ble 
feedstock, high-quality recyclates from other polymers (PP, HDPE, etc.) are still the exception. 
More nuanced classification and definitions, combined with other legislative policies (such as 
mandatory recycled content targets or plastic taxes) would incentivise high-end applications and 
circular economy approaches towards a wider range of polymers. 
A closed-loop system not only ensures high quality of the recyclates, 
it also provides a higher quantity of recyclates. For example, when 
placing 100 bottles made from virgin plastic on the market, depending 
on the collection and recycling process, the amount of material that is 
captured and subsequently recycled varies significantly. Say a 
separate collection system is implemented, with a capture rate of 49%. 
This would mean that out of the original 100 bottles, 49 are captured. 
Assuming a 75% recycling yield, these 49 captured bottles would be 
recycled into 38 new ones. These 38 bottles would then re -enter the 
market, and go through the same process. After the second cycle, 19 
bottles would have been captured (38 * 0.49), and recycled into 14 
new ones. Etc, etc… After several more iterations, using an original 
input of 100 virgin plastic bottles, you would have produced 60 
recycled ones before the material is depleted. If the collection rate was 
higher however, the amount of recycled bottles would greatly 
increase. If it can achieve a 90% separate collect ion rate, as is the 
case with Deposit Return Systems, out of 100 virgin plastic bottles, 
208 recycled bottles could be produced, more than triple the amount 
of bottles produced with an open-loop system (see Figure 1).  
Recognise the role of mechanical processes for closing t he loop and i ntroduce definitions for 
chemical processes 
Given today’s technologies, data suggests that mechanical recycling remai ns the most effective 
method to recycle plastics, when considering  ecological footprint , energy use, and economic 
feasibility. Thus, mechanical recycling should always be the preferred method  when technically 
possible to treat plastic waste. 
Chemical recycling has yet to reach commercial and global scale and may be relevant for very 
challenging waste streams . It should not be used for waste streams with proven (advanced) 
mechanical recycling pathways.

The term chemical recycling  can encompass different processes and technologies. For a 
meaningful discussion, it is absolutely necessary to introduce clear and harmonised definitions of 
the main categories of different chemical recycling processes/ technologies and to align relevant 
legislation and standards. There should be a differentiation between those processes whose 
outputs are in line with the recycling definition (e.g. materials) and those which are not (e.g. fuels). 
This differentiation should also be reflected in the EU waste management hierarchy, by giving an 
order of priority to mechanical recycling and making the lower levels of the hierarchy (recycling 
and below) more granular and explicit in terms of where different chemical recycling processes 
stand. 
Separate collections for certain materials should be strengthened 
Separate collection is key for improving the quantity and quality of recycling and enabling a circular 
economy. For waste streams such as paper, glass or plastic, it has played an i mportant role in 
ensuring valuable recyclable materials are effectively prepared for recycling.  
The EU introduced in the Single-use Plastics Directive adopted in 2019 a separate collection target 
for plastic beverage bottles of 90% by 2029. We believe th at similar separate collection targets 
should be introduced also for other waste streams, such as beverage packaging made of glass, 
metals and carton, in order to improve the capture rates and facilitate high quality recycling. 
Improve waste management by introducing mixed waste sorting requirements 
While separate collection s are  a key element of the circular economy, the capture rates are 
unfortunately remaining insufficient, in particular for plastics. As a result, currently, mixed residual 
waste contains many valuable materials that could be recovered but instead end up in lan dfill or 
incinerated. This is causing significant negative environmental impacts (waste of resources, GHG 
emissions), and also seriously compromises the ability of Member States to achieve recycling 
targets set under EU waste legislation. 
Therefore, it is necessary to reconsider how residual/ mixed waste is being managed. With current 
state of technology, it is possible to recover materials from mixed waste and make them available 
for recycling. In combination with advanced mechanical recycling techniques, in particular plastic 
materials from mixed waste could be transformed into recyclates with properties similar to virgin. 
This approach should be seen as complementary to separate collections to maxi mize recycling 
and associated environmental benefits. 
Needed to mitigate environmental impacts of wasted resources and GHG emissions 
As a leading technology provider of sorting equipment for packaging sorting plants and materials 
recovery facilities (MRFs) across Europe, TOMRA has wide access to waste stream data including 
on sorting and recycling of different waste fractions. Over the years TOMRA has gathered a 
significant amount of empirical evidence, for instance, waste composition/ sorting analyses or 
waste flows from different EU countries.  
Overall, we see that despite progress with separate collection over the past decades,  effective 
capture rates still remain low in the EU.  For instance, our data analyses for Germany show that 
only about 55% of all plastic packaging is currently captured through separate collection (with 35% 
collected in a yellow bin/bag and 20% in the Deposit Return System (DRS) for beverage 
containers). As much as 40% of other plastic packaging is found in the residual/mixed waste bi n, 
alongside other non -packaging plastics. Data for Greece shows a split between 20% of plastic 
packaging found in separate collection and 75% in mixed residual waste.  
 
In addition to losing valuable resources, leaving these materials in mixed residual waste has a 
significant impact on GHG emissions. Research mandated by TOMRA and conducted by 
sustainability consultancy Eunomia has shown that extracting recyclable mate rials from mixed 
waste can lead to significant reductions in GHG emissions , reducing the emissions from 
incinerating a tonne of mixed waste by 0.349 tonnes of CO2e and from landfilling – by 0.309 tonnes 
of CO2e. Given that in Europe, around 90 -100 million tonnes of waste is incinerated each year, 
and 65 million is landfilled, introducing mixed waste sorting prior to incineration and landfilling could 
lead to a reduction of 55 million tonnes of CO2e/ year (90 million * 0.349 + 65 million * 0.309). This 
would bring significant CO2 reductions and can help contribute to the EU decarbonisation and 
climate neutrality goals.

Necessary for Member States to reach EU law targets  
Given today’s realities with capture rates that are too low, it will not be possible for most Member 
States to reach the recycling targets set in the EU waste legislation only with materials captured 
through separate collection.  
The effect of the revised recycling calculation method set out in Implementing Decision 2019/1004 
is yet to be seen, but it is certain that it will bring down the reported recycling rates by  Member 
States. From a technical point of view, plastics coming out of sorting plants undergo losses of 
approximately 30% depending on the material (rigid vs flexibles) with the now required additional 
treatment steps before recycling. Indeed, in its Review No 4/2020 the European Court of Auditors 
provided an estimation of the impact of the new calculation method on the recycling rates for plastic 
packaging. It found that recycling rates for plastic packaging would fall by at least 10 percentage 
points from the currently reported 42% (potential decrease to 29 -32%). Given the current 
performances and the ambitious EU recycling target for municipal waste to be reached by 2035, it 
will require Member States to realise a significant increase that cannot be achieved by using 
separate collection as it currently stands.  
Where EPR schemes are implemented, they should cover the  costs of deploying mixed waste 
sorting to recover relevant fractions from mixed waste given the role such solutions play in helping 
Member States achieve EU waste management targets . Indeed, mixed waste sorting can be 
construed as being part of the treatment necessary to meet the EU waste management targets, 
since Member States cannot achieve these targets with separate collection alone. 
Proven solutions exist 
Mixed Waste Sorting can provide an efficient and effective solution helping to increase Member 
States recycling rates. Sorting mixed residual waste to divert it from landfill and incineration can 
ensure that valuable materials that have been incorrectly separated in households are effectively 
extracted for recycling. 
A 2021 study mandated to sustainability consultancy Eunomia assessed the performance of three 
European case studies under current systems and under recommended Holistic Resource System 
approach, where a combination of DRS, separate collections for specific fractions, and mixed 
waste sorting is used to achieve t he best performance . The study finds that with this improved 
approach to waste management, the recycling rates for the case studies of Friesland 
 
(Netherlands), Milan (Italy), and Stavanger (Norway) could be increased between 10 -15% thus 
meeting or being very close to meeting t he EU’s 65% municipal waste recycling target by 2035. 
The contribution of mixed waste sorting is 5.2, 5.8, and 5.6 percentage points respectively for the 
three regions.

As a global frontrunner in improving the circularity of resources, TOMRA is working in partnership 
with key players across the waste management ecosystem to develop effective mixed waste 
sorting solutions prior to incineration and landfill. We have i nstalled over 300 successful mixed 
waste sorting plants worldwide, which, associated with advanced mechanical recycling, have 
delivered significant results.   
Traditional mechanical recycling is centred around cold washing and not many additional 
processing steps, which limits its capacity to produce high quality recyclates. On the other hand, 
advanced mechanical recycling processes include a number of purification and decontamination 
steps, such as pre-sorting, hot washing, enhanced flake sorting into colours and materials, and 
deodorization to extract any fragrances. As result, advanced sorting and mechanical recycling 
techniques have proven it is possible to recycle even materials recovered from mixed waste to the 
same quality as virgin and fit for a range of high-quality applications depending on intrinsic material 
properties. 
Currently, the only well -established advanced mechanical processes today are for PET for food 
grade. Recently however, TOMRA adapted these processes to help create the world’s first closed-
loop system for polystyrene (PS), together with partners from Styrenic Circular Solutions, the joint 
initiative which aims to advance the circularity of styrenic polymers. Here, TOMRA technology is 
used to identify, sort, and remove impurities from PS found in mixed plastic waste, which is then 
processed and extruded into new high purity recycled polystyrene (rPS). The result is a high-quality 
final product, with no impurities, regardless of the waste collection stream, that can be reused into 
new, highly-demanding applications, such as food contact packaging.

Create a comprehensive legal framework for textiles 
In light of the upcoming Textiles Strategy and the objectives of the Circular Economy Action Plan 
and the European Green Deal to improve the sustainability of the textile sector, the revision of the 
WFD should take i nto account downstream considerations for textile waste. Currently, Member 
States are required to separately collect textile waste from 2025. The current revision of the WFD 
in addition to should consider holistic measures to help scale up collection, sorting and recycling 
infrastructure, including mandatory Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) schemes, as well as 
mandatory recycling and recycled content targets.  
Textile policies should follow the waste hierarchy 
Prevention should be prioritised: reducing waste at the source and promoting long -lasting textile 
products that can be reused for a long period.  Proper separate collection schemes and manual 
sorting are needed to capture and make available textiles for reuse.  
Finally, those textiles which are not fit for reuse and become waste, should be collected, sorted for 
recycling, and integrated in new (textile) products. No textiles should be incinerated or landfilled, 
and an end should be put to any existing practices of destroying unsold/ returned goods. 
The importance of automated sorting in textiles for effective recycling of non-reusable textiles 
Today, manual sorting does not meet the demands of recycling companies and the fashion industry 
because it is not able to provide the consistent quality and large volumes that are required for 
textile recycling at large scale. As a result, only a small quantity of discarded textiles is recycled 
back into fibre applications (new textiles).  Automated sorting is the link that is currently lacking 
between textile collection and high-quality textile recycling because it has proven extremely high 
efficiency and precision. 
The potential for increasing textile recycling is tremendous. For instance, it is estimated that just in 
Sweden, only around 5% percent of the almost 140,000 tons of new textiles that are placed on the 
Swedish market each year are recyc led. Using automation ( near-infrared optical sorting 
equipment) to further sort this waste fraction is opening up the possibility for non-reusable textiles 
waste so be sorted into well-separated, quality-assured flows in large volumes ready for recycling. 
Building upon our long experience of successfully using technology for the recycling of plastics, 
paper and beverage containers, TOMRA has developed an automated sorting technology which 
has demonstrated a high level of efficiency and precision, and in February 2021, we helped deliver 
the world’s first fully automated textile sorting plant at an industrial scale in Malmö, Sweden.  
TOMRA and Stadler deliver the world’s first fully automated textile sorting plant in Malmö, Sweden 
 
EPR schemes for textiles are needed 
EPR schemes, including Deposit Return Systems (DRS) where relevant, can play a significant role 
in supporting the whole value  chain and improve textile collection, sorting , and recycling. The 
revision of the WFD should therefore introduce mandatory EPR schemes, which have proven to 
be an effective way to increase collection and recycling and are gaining increased interest from 
Member States. This would also help finance the much-needed scaling up of collection, sorting 
and recycling infrastructure, in line with the “polluter pays'' principle. Furthermore, there is currently 
a lack of data and transparency on the flow of textiles in the supply chain on the EU market. 
Implementing EPR schemes can help centralise information and keep track of the amount of the 
material that is placed on the EU market, and subsequently treated.  
Furthermore, mandatory targets on recycling and recycled content should be considered to boost 
both the demand and supply side for recycling. The WFD currently sets a s eparate collection 
obligation without specific recycling targets for textile waste. In order to ensure that the collected 
textile waste is indeed recycled, the EU should set mandatory recycling targets for textile waste, 
following the approach set for othe r waste streams. Recycling targets will boost the supply of 
secondary raw materials, and should take into account the share of reusable and non -reusable 
textiles. Moreover, recycled content targets in new textile products would promote the uptake of 
recycled materials in textiles, and would drive the demand side for recycled materials, as well as 
create the predictability the recycling industry needs to engage in long term infrast ructure 
investments.

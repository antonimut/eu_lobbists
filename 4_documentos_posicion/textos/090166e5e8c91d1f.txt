 
 
 
   
 
ESBG response to the European Commission consultation  
on the Banking Package proposal 
 
 
The European Savings and Retail Banking Group (ESBG) appreciates the 
opportunity to provide feedback  on the Capital Requirements Regulation 
(CRR) proposal as regards requirements for credit risk, credit valuation 
adjustment risk, operational risk, market risk and the output floor . We would 
like to share  the following considerations which we hope will be taken into 
account by the European Commission.  
 
(For our comments on the Capital Requirements Directive (CRD) proposal, 
please refer to the paper we submitted in the consultation page under the 
respective section or find those points at the end of the current paper).  
 
 
1. Overall assessment: 
 
ESBG positively assesses the legislative proposal published on 27 October 
2021 by the European Commission. It transposes the final elements of the Basel 
III reforms in the EU regulatory framework, and it pursues other prudential and 
supervisory objectives.  
 
We recognise that the Commission has sought to take into consideration most 
of the issues ra ised so far both by policy makers and the financial sector in 
order to find a well-balanced and realistic starting point for the co-legislators. 
Nevertheless, ESBG is of the opinion that the proposal requires further 
adjustments. 
 
The effort from the Commi ssion to take into consideration specific 
characteristics of the European financial system and the European banking 
market is noticeable, while respecting the objectives set forth by the Basel 
Committee. Similarly, the additional time given to properly imp lement the 
reform in the processes, IT -systems, and practices within the banks, signals 
sensitivity in the context of the recovery post Covid -19, during which banks 
will continue playing an important role.  
 
The capital impact, according to the Commission’ s own assessment, is 
expected to lead to a weighted average increase in institutions’ Minimum 
Capital Requirements (MCR) of 6.4% to 8.4% in the long term (by 2030), after 
the envisaged transitional period (n.b. According to the EBA’s estimates from 
its 2019 Report, it estimated the impact to be a 13.1% increase based on an EU 
specific scenario which has been adopted in this proposal – ILM = 1, SME SF 
and CVA exemptions maintained). Although the Pillar 1 impact might be lower 
when compared with previous esti mates, some Member States are still going 
to be particularly hit.

Moreover, one should be careful with averages. Individual ESBG members with 
specific business models, e.g. with low-risk mortgage and SME-portfolios, will 
be more affected than others. One should also not forget that the impact study 
did not anticipate the Pillar 2 impact, e.g. the SSM already warned that the 
neutralisation of ILM would require pillar 2 correction based on historical losses. 
 
Finally, we believe that the implementation of t he Basel standards within the 
EU regulatory framework should reflect the proportionality principle, taking 
into consideration the risk nature, scale and complexity of the activities of 
European credit institutions. This would allow financial institutions to carry out 
their activities under a non-detrimental regulatory framework that strengthens 
the European banking sector – the backbone of the EU’s “real economy”. A 
disproportionate regulation causing huge regulatory burden would in fact 
negatively impact b anks, which could even push resources away from 
customer service. 
 
 
2. Implementation timeline: 
 
In general, ESBG positively notes the implementation timeline of the new rules 
beginning in 2025 and the introduction of several transitional arrangements, in 
particular, those related to the output floor, which lead up to 2030 and extend 
to 2032 in some cases. This in our view will give supervisors and banks more 
time to prepare for the implementation and progressively adjust to the impacts 
while focusing on supporting the post -pandemic recovery. In this context, we 
also note that out of the 28 Member States in the Basel Committee, only very 
few other jurisdictions have already started the transposition process of the 
finalised Basel III reforms into national law. 
 
At the same time, it becomes apparent that for institutions using exclusively 
the standardised approach for credit risk, without using internal models, the 
implementation will not be cushioned. For them, the granularity of the 
framework will increase drast ically as of 2025, as there are hardly any 
transitional arrangements foreseen. This will create significant challenges in the 
implementation and finally will result in a disproportionate burden. The 
regulators should address this by at least introducing fu rther transitional 
provisions and reduce further the complexity of the rules for institutions 
applying exclusively the standardised approach for credit risk. 
 
 
3. Output floor:  
 
The Commission chose to adopt a “single -stack” approach for the 
implementation of the output floor with safeguards for double-counting of the 
EU-specific Pillar 2 and Systemic Risk Buffer (SyRB) requirement (both of which 
will remain frozen until the supervisor reviews those requirements for double 
counting), a 5 -years phase -in period  until 2030, as well as transitional 
arrangements.  
 
 
 
The proposed single-stack approach would mean applying the output floor to 
EU-specific components of the capital requirements, on top of internationally 
agreed ones, instead of using the output floor as a backstop by applying it to 
internationally agreed capital requirements only. Such application requires that 
supervisory powers are more clearly framed and that the arrangements 
mitigating the impact of the output floor are of a longer -term nature or 
permanent.  
 
The application of the output floor as envisaged in the Basel text, i.e. at the 
highest level of consolidation (Article 92 Para 5 CRR3) is welcomed. In this 
respect, we support the Commission’s solution for the home -host issue by 
prescribing a fai r distribution of any eventual capital increases due to the 
output floor among lower levels of consolidation. However, further 
clarifications on the excess capital requirement allocation mechanism would be 
needed, particularly for entities that do not have  to calculate capital 
requirements on a solo level. 
 
Regarding the transitional arrangements under the output floor referring to 
low-risk mortgages and unrated corporates, we note that these do not address 
underlying structural factors that prevail in the European banking sector. Such 
structural factors are the prevalence of “dual recourse” for mortgage lenders in 
most EU Member States, and that the corporate sector uses bank financing over 
capital market financing, for various historical, legal and structu ral reasons. 
Therefore, a large part of the corporate clients of European banks does not 
have an external credit rating. In this respect, ESBG proposes that the 
arrangements are made permanent as an alternative, or at least phased out 
based on the actual o bservation of structural changes, such as the share of 
mortgages securitised and out of the bank’s prudential scope, as well as the 
share of corporates rated by eligible rating agencies in the case of unrated 
corporates. 
 
Furthermore, since the same underl ying structural factors prevail for 
institutions applying the standardised approach, the logical next step would be 
to extend such structural flexibilizations also to institutions using the 
standardised approach for credit risk. This is particularly important to ensure a 
level playing field, thus creating a higher degree of consistency  between risk 
and capital weights both for institutions applying internal models and covered 
by the output floor and for institutions applying the standardised approach. 
Likewise, the structural flexibilisation applied to derivative exposures 
calculated under the SA-CCR should also be extended to the standardised 
approach.  
 
 
4. Operational risk:  
 
ESBG recognises the proposal to disregard historical operational losses for all 
institutions within the calculation of capital requirements for operational risk, 
 
 
meaning that the Internal Loss Multiplier (ILM) is effectively being set equal to 
one. This is a discretion provided in the framework. 
 
The proposal also encompasses new rules to ensure that sound operational risk 
management frameworks are in place in banks. The new rules touch on 
governance aspects, monitoring and assessment of operational risks, among 
others. 
 
Regarding the disclosure of operational risk losses, the general approach in the 
Basel text is to require mandatory disclosures for those elements involved in 
the determination of the Pillar 1 capital requirements.  The requirement to 
disclose information that is not directly used for the calculation of the capital 
requirements can  be seen as an inconsistency against the backdrop that 
historical losses will not be used for the calculation of capital requirements to 
cover operational risk in the EU.  
 
Furthermore, some additional clarifications or technical guidance regarding the 
criteria to exclude or include historical events in the losses database may be 
necessary. 
 
 
5. Real estate:  
 
We do not see the need for the newly introduced non-income producing real 
estate requirements to go beyond the Basel standards by adding a requirement 
that a residential real estate should be the primary residence of the obligor. 
 
According to Article 124(2)(a), where the exposure is secured by a residential 
property, the exposure shall not qualify as an IPRE exposure and shall be 
treated in accordance with Article 125(1) where the exposure meets any of the 
listed conditions. The wording in CRR means that only those exposures that 
meet all conditions could be seen as an exposure that is secured by a non-IPRE 
residential property that can use the SA -LS approach. According to the final 
Basel III Standards, residential properties that are IPRE can use the SA -LS 
approach regardless, if the property meets any of the listed conditions. A non-
IPRE residential real estate does not have to meet the listed conditions at all in 
order to qualify for the SA-LS approach. The text should be adjusted to mirror 
the Basel Standard, otherwise the EU implementation is more re strictive than 
the Basel Standard. 
 
▪ Mortgages:  
 
See our comments on the transitional arrangement for low -risk mortgages in 
section 2 on the output floor (page 3). 
 
▪ ADC lending: 
 
Given the debate that has taken place between supervisors and credit 
institutions on the classification of all Acquisition, Development and 
 
 
Construction (ADC) lending exposures as speculative immovable property 
financing, we welcome that the revised Basel provision that introduces more 
risk sensitivity to the treatment of these exposures has been incorporated into 
EU regulation. This was needed in order to avoid creating prejudice from one 
economic sector towards others and to avoid that banks limit financing to this 
sector in order to reduce the impact on their solvency ratios. 
 
▪ Commercial real estate: 
 
In the new ADC asset group (Article 126a CRR), ADC exposures to residential 
property may be risk weighted at 100 %, provided that certain criteria are 
fulfilled. We believe this exception should also be applicable to commercial real 
estate in order to obtain the goal of a more risk-sensitive standardized method.  
 
According to the Basel standards, the value of the property will be maintained 
at the value measured at origination unless national supervisors elect to require 
banks to revise the property value downward. However, in the legislative 
proposal amending CRR 2, the Commission integrates the Basel standard to 
better reflect the actual value of the proper ty (Article 208, Para 3 point b). 
While we welcome the addition of the Commission, we are of the opinion that 
the proposal introduces several challenges: Firstly, the cyclicality of real estate 
prices should be covered by the Countercyclical Capital Buffer  (CCyB). 
Secondly, the proposed regulation creates cyclical competition effects as it 
incentivizes refinancing during an upturn. It can also be questioned whether the 
risk of the exposure will be correctly reflected as a mortgage on the same 
property that is moved from one bank to another would get a lower risk weight 
in a market with increasing prices than if the loan had not been moved, although 
the second bank had less knowledge of the customers’ willingness to pay. 
Finally, the proposal will cause the need for changes in the banks’ IT-systems.  
 
Considering this background, we support the use of the current method of 
using the market value when valuing real estate. 
 
▪ Calculation of the ETV ratio:  
 
According to Article 124 Para 5 CRR 3 the Exposure-To-Value (ETV) ratio shall 
be calculated by dividing the gross exposure amount by the property value. 
Article 208 Para 3 point b CRR 3 foresees that the value of the property shall 
not exceed the average value measured for that property or for a comparable 
property over the last three years for commercial immovable property, and 
over the last six years for residential property. At the same time, modifications 
made to the property that improve the energy efficiency of the building or 
housing unit shall be considered as unequivocally increasing its value.  
 
In the BCBS standards it is generally foreseen that modifications made to the 
property that unequivocally increase its value could also be considered in the 
ETV (Para 62). Hence, improvements to the energy efficiency of the building or 
the housing unit sho uld just be seen as one example. Other improvements to 
the building or housing unit should be considered as unequivocally increasing 
 
 
its value as well and leading to an upward adjustment of the property value. 
Therefore, Article 208 Para 3 point b CRR3 shall be aligned with the Basel text, 
thus envisaging that modifications made to the property that unequivocally 
increase its value shall lead to an upwards adjustment of the property value.   
 
Moreover, there is no reference for the definition of energy efficiency. 
 
 
6. Unrated corporates: 
 
ESBG basically welcomes the introduction of a transitional treatment for 
corporates that do not have an external rating - the so-called “hybrid approach” 
– which sets a 65% RW for unrated corporates with a probability of default (PD) 
of 0.5. However, while this appears to address the fact that in Europe many 
corporate clients do not have an external credit rating and under the 
standardised approach for credit risk they would receive a flat risk weight of 
100%, the solution is only temporary until 2032 (with possibility of extension) 
and only applicable to IRB banks.  
 
In this context, since in the transitional phase, the difference in risk weights 
between the internal ratings -based approach (IRBA) and the standardized 
approach is very large, we suggest extending this temporary solution also to 
banks using the standardised approach for credit risk. 
 
Finally, while the Commission tries to facilitate improvements on the availability 
of ratings across the EU, we see the need for a clearer initiative for EU 
authorities and banks to set up structures for the coverage of external ratings. 
This initiative should take into account the additional costs and effort for IT 
implementation within the banks by establishing many granular checks in the 
systems and processes to ensure that the risk weights are correctly assigned. 
 
 
7. Equity: 
 
The introduction of a more favourable treatment for long-term strategic equity 
investments is important. Consequently, ESBG proposes to implement a new 
category with a lower 100% risk weight for long term strategic equity 
investments that are held in the banking book.  
 
Rightly, the Commission’s proposal recognises the need to differentiate 
amongst different equity investments given their disparity when regarding  
investment horizons and purpose. Within this context, there remains a need for 
higher risk sensitivity by considering the timeframe in and intent with which an 
equity position is held. For this purpose, we would also welcome a clarification 
on the criteria of what should be considered exercising a significant influence 
for a strategic investment eligibility purpose, so as not to have a too restrictive 
scope of application. 
 
 
 
An example of long-term strategic equity investment can be identified in the 
context of the current digital environment, where technological partnership 
could be key for banks to remain competitive towards Fintechs and Bigtechs. 
These partnerships that a bank invests in are of a long-term nature and are not 
oriented to earn profits from increases in the value of such entities or 
institutions (i .e., partnerships with technological companies involved in the 
bank’s systems and infrastructure). In order not to condition banks’ business 
structure in an unintended way in cases like this one, we  support the 
implementation of a new category of long -term strategic equity maintaining 
the lower 100% risk weight. 
 
Another example of long -term strategic equity investment is a construction 
where a number of local and regional financial institutions have joined forces in 
the operation and ownership of a number of strategic companies (so -called 
"sector owned entities") in order to achieve economies of scale in a number of 
areas such as IT, mortgages, asset management etc. While local and regional 
financial institutions jointly hold strategic shares in the sector owned 
companies, the large SIFI's have the critical mass needed to elevate similar 
functions and services within their own group. However, the risk weights for 
long-term strategic equity investments  are different in the two situations 
according to the Commission’s proposal, namely 250% in the case of a sector 
owned entity and 100%, when the entity is included in the same scope of 
consolidated supervision as the institution. 
 
Furthermore, we welcome the proposal about intragroup exposures and equity 
within Institutional Protection Schemes (IPS) to receive a 100% RW (Article 49 
Para 4 CRR3).   
 
 
8. Specialised lending:  
 
The proposal to increase the risk sensitivity for unrated object finance 
exposures is positive. The lack of risk sensitivity that was embedded in the Basel 
text for these exposures would have implied unintended consequences. We 
welcome the introduction of a “high -quality” principle in the standardized 
approach for credit risk,  that will recognise prudent and conservative 
management of financial risks. We also welcome the phase -in application of 
input floor under the IRB approach, and in fact wish that the timeline is slightly 
postponed in order to match the EBA mandate completio n and the legislative 
revision. 
 
 
9. EU specificities:  
 
We welcome that the SME and Infrastructure supporting factors (Article 501 
and 501a CRR3) have been maintained, together with the Credit Valuation 
Adjustments (CVA) exemptions.  
 
 
 
These features, which were already embedded in the current EU regulatory 
framework on capital adequacy, have been carefully calibrated to support 
important segments of the European market. They play a crucial role in 
supporting the economy and are even more appropriate in the context of the 
recovery post Covid-19. For these reasons, it was important to retain them. 
 
Even though we appreciate that the infrastructure supporting factor remains, 
there are however still some concerns regarding its implementation, which 
were unfortunately not yet solved in the current proposal. We would appreciate 
clarification and simplification of the criteria on the infrastructure supporting 
factor, as only then can it lead to the intended increase in infrastructure 
financing operations.  
 
We then question why current conversion factors not affected by the revision 
of Basel III in particular are nevertheless revised by the Commission: this applies 
to technical guarantees required for construction and for export. In this respect, 
we urge the legislator to ensure that current EU specificities are maintaine d. 
Also, we find that EBA mandate (CRD Article 104 Para 3) to evaluate the 
excessive CVA risk is redundant to the EBA mandate on CVA exemption 
reporting, and totally undermines the Pillar 1 decision to exempt transactions 
with end-users. This should be removed.  
 
 
10. Credit Valuation Adjustment (CVA) risk:  
 
The application of the simplified CVA method according to Article 385 CRR is 
referring to the EU specific “small portfolio” (Article 273a CRR 2) and restricting 
the application to the derivatives portfolio  smaller than EUR 100 million 
measured in absolute market value, whereas the Basel Committee had originally 
envisaged a threshold of EUR 100 billion measured in total notional amount of 
non-centrally cleared derivatives. We do not see any ground to specifi cally 
align the use of the simplified CVA method to the simplified method for 
calculating the exposure value for derivatives, and suggest sticking to the Basel 
provision, which - unlike the thresholds stated in Article 273a - also takes into 
account the fa ct that derivatives  cleared with QCCPs are not considered 
‘covered transactions’ in the CVA framework.  
 
In fact, according to the Basel text, an institution whose aggregate notional 
amount of non-centrally cleared derivatives is less than or equal to 100 billion 
euros may calculate their own funds requirements for CVA risk as the risk -
weighted exposure amounts for counterparty risk for non -trading book and 
trading book positions. 
 
 
11. Trade Finance transactions:  
 
Some of the proposed changes would have a significant negative impact on the 
trade finance instruments that are crucial for commerce. This effect mainly 
comes from two sources: the increase of the Credit Conversion Factor (CCF) 
 
 
for guarantees from 20% to 50% under the Standardised Approach for credit risk 
and setting the effective maturity risk component (M) equal to 2.5 under the F-
IRB approach. Both changes would likely cause significant increases in the cost 
of trade finance products that pay a vital role in commerce. 
 
Since those CCF were not reviewed by Basel 3 and in the absence of a specific 
quantitative study that justifies challenging previous legislative decisions, we 
advocate to maintain the CCF of 20% for trade instruments, i.e., to move the 
trade finance instruments from the bucket 2 to the bucket 4 or 5 of the Annex 
Classification of off -balance sheet items. According to the International 
Chamber of Commerce and the Global Credit Data, the empirical CCF is closer 
to 8% based on 1.25 million transactions, not even close to the 50% that is 
proposed. 
 
The actual use of an effective maturity risk component (M) should be extended 
to the F -IRB approach, not just limited to the A -IRB approach, which going 
forward will not be allowed for large corpor ate exposures. Alternatively, the 
fixed maturity should be lowered to 6 months for trade finance instruments 
such as advance payment guarantees: indeed, those instruments have a very 
short duration of around 90 days, not 2.5 years. This difference has a ma terial 
impact on capital requirement and leads to very inconsistent treatment of 
similar instruments between small and large corporates even though the same 
terms apply in terms of effective maturity. 
 
 
12. Credit Conversion Factor (CCF): 
 
With regard to the application of CCF to exposures for which an institution has 
received permission to estimate a loss given default (LGD) under the IRB 
approach in Article 166 Para 8 CRR3, it should be clarified that the CCF for 
defaulted exposures shall be set to zero (rega rdless of whether Para 8a or 8b 
applies). The reason for this is to avoid double counting, as the new Article 181 
Para 1 letter k CRR3 already requires that additional drawings after default are 
to be accounted for in the LGD. 
 
The change in the scope of application of IRB CCF models will not only require 
changing own funds requirements calculation for non -revolving facilities but 
also its reflection in the relevant data sets used for development and calibration 
of the CCF models applied to revolving facilities. The latter adaption is a formal 
step, as the models currently in place already ensure the proper application of 
a CCF to revolving facilities, and should therefore not be considered a material 
change in the meaning of Article 143 Para 3 letter a CRR3 (i.e. if not combined 
with other triggers for material changes). 
 
We generally agree content wise with the new methodological details specified 
for the CCF estimation in Article 182 Para 1 letter g to h CRR3 from the 
standpoint of harmonisation of practices. At the same time, Article 182 para 5 
CRR3 commissions the EBA to issue regulatory technical standards (RTS) that 
specify the methodology that institutions shall apply to estimate the CCF by 31 
 
 
December 2026. To avoid inefficiencies in changing and appr oving new CCF 
models multiple times (also considering the previous bullet point), at least once 
to timely comply with the Article 182 Para 1 letter g to h CRR3 and once with 
the RTS, we kindly ask to include the methodological details of Article 182 Para 
1 letter g to h CRR3 into the EBA’s RTS rather than specifying them directly in 
the CRR3, to align the issuing date of the RTS with entering into force of the 
CRR3 or to set a respective transition period. 
 
According to Article 111 Para. 4, both contractual arrangements that have been 
offered by institutions, but not yet accepted by the client and that would 
become a commitment if accepted, as well as contractual arrangements that 
are not to be treated as a commitment according to Article 5 point 9 in the first 
place, have nevertheless to be backed by own funds. We believe, Article 111 
Para 4 is illogical, contradicts Article 5 point 9 and should therefore be deleted. 
 
Furthermore, we believe that unconditionally cancellable commitments (UCC) 
in the meaning o f Article 5 point 10 should retain a 0 % CCF. The planned 
regulations would lead to cancellations and reductions of committed credit 
lines. This, in turn, would lead to considerable disadvantages for customers due 
to poor predictability of drawdowns.  
 
▪ Article 166 Para 8 CRR3: 
 
This Article stipulates the following: “An institution that does not use IRB-CCF, 
shall calculate the exposure value as the committed but undrawn amount 
multiplied by the SA-CCF concerned. An institution that does not use IRB-CCF, 
shall calculate the exposure value for undrawn commitments as the undrawn 
amount multiplied by an IRB -CCF.” Concerning the second sentence, it is 
unclear how an institution that does not use IRB -CCF shall calculate the off -
balance sheet item by multiplying it by an IRB-CCF. 
 
▪ IRB-partial use (Article 150 CRR3): 
 
This Article has been significantly changed in comparison to the current Article 
150 CRR 3. Based on the new wording in Article 150 Para 1 point c, we 
understand that an institution can request for per mission to apply the 
standardised approach for certain exposures within the exposure class, taking 
into consideration the size and risk profile of those exposures. In the following 
sentence within Article 150 Para 1 point c, it seems that the IRB approach can 
be applied only for some types of exposures. It is unclear how this 
subparagraph interacts with the previous sentence (related to the PPU 
permission and immateriality of the portfolio) and with Article 148 CRR3, 
according to which “once an institution implements the IRB Approach for one 
of those exposure classes, it shall do so for all the exposures within that 
exposure class, unless it has received the permission of the competent 
authorities to use the Standardised Approach permanently in accordance with 
Article 150.”  
 
 
 
To our understanding, once the institution is permitted to implement the IRB 
for at least one exposure class, it can apply for permanent partial use (PPU) 
permission for certain exposures within this exposure class. This corresponds 
with respective sub-paragraph in Article 150 about the PPU permission and the 
immateriality of the portfolio. But Article 148 doesn´t say anything about the 
situation when the institution is permitted to apply IRB only for certain 
exposures and the remaining e xposures within the respective exposure class 
shall be treated in the standardised approach without any permission.  
 
 
13. Reporting and disclosure: 
 
While we welcome the proposed centralization of the disclosures for small and 
non-complex institutions, it should also be ensured that in the case of central 
disclosure by EBA done on the basis of reporting data, there is no negative 
repercussion on the preparation of banks’ annual financial statements and 
related processes. 
 
In consideration of the proportionality principle and with the aim of simplifying 
and harmonising public disclosures, we believe that the process for validation 
and confirmation of the correctness of the data to be published by the EBA 
should be clarified. The procedural re quirements should be kept simple and 
should not result in any additional data  requests/requirements on the 
institution’s side. At the same time, the correctness of the data should be 
ensured. 
 
On the other hand, with respect to the centralization of disclo sure for 
institutions other than those that are small and non-complex, a clarification on 
the process of disclosing qualitative requirements would be highly appreciated, 
especially in terms of timelines and expectations from the institutions.  
 
Furthermore, to avoid contradicting the goal of easing administrative burdens 
for small, non-complex institutions, we call for their exemption from reporting 
and disclosure requirements in the area of ESG exposures, as long as those 
institutions do not communicate in this area. The requirements should also be 
consistent with the non-financial reporting requirements under the Taxonomy 
Regulation and Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), as they 
have similar objectives. The CSRD Draft foresees exemptions for small 
institutions, CRR 3 should not go beyond that. Supervisory authorities are 
already sufficiently informed about all material risks via the information 
collected in the SREP, so that no further reporting or disclosure requirements 
are needed. 
 
In order to further enhance proportionality, the disclosure requirements should 
be further limited in general. For non -listed institutions we plead for reduced 
disclosure requirements, e. g. omitting qualitative disclosure information. There 
is no public inter est, as these institutions are “non -listed”. Reducing it to only 
“quantitative” information would make it easier to use existing reporting data 
for disclosure purposes only (similar to the SNCIs.). 
 
 
 
 
14. Definitions and capital deductions: 
 
The change in the definition of the term financial institution in Article 4 Para 1 
point 26 CRR 3 means that in future, so-called "ancillary services undertakings 
(ASU)" would also be classified as a "financial institution" and would thus be 
subject to the deduction rules, e .g. in Article 36 CRR, as companies in the 
financial sector. This would lead to a very significant additional burden for the 
institutions. The inclusion of providers of ancillary services in the deduction 
rules is not justified in our view, as they do not engage in lending business or 
similar businesses. Multiple use of equity capital - which is to be prevented by 
the deduction rules - cannot occur in this respect. The economic risk is 
sufficiently covered by the balance sheet valuation. From our point of view, this 
consequential effect on equity is disproportionate to the risks involved. We 
therefore advocate for either adapting the definition of the term accordingly 
or, alternatively, inserting exceptions in this regard in the deduction rules. 
 
Additionally, the definitions of ASU in Article 4 Para 1 point 18 CRR should be 
clarified in order to make clear that only those companies fall under the 
definition, whose main activity is ancillary to the principle activity of one or 
more institutions. So far, the wording of the new definition is ambiguous as it is 
not clear whether the phrase “that is ancillary to banking” in lit. b) is referring 
to all possible activities or only to “other activity” 
 
▪ Definition of SME (Article 5 point 8 CRR3): 
 
According to the Com mission Recommendation concerning the definition of 
micro, small and medium-sized enterprises (definition currently used for CRR3 
purposes as per Article 501 Para 2 letter b) a definition of SME also covers 
microenterprises. However, the definition accordi ng to Article 5 point 8 draft 
CRR3 is referring only to a small and medium-sized company, thus it is not clear 
if this definition also covers microenterprises. 
 
 
15. Additional comments: 
  
o Retail exposures: In Article 123 on criteria for an exposure being considered 
as a retail exposure, it is stated that the EBA shall issue guidelines, in 
accordance with Article 16 of Regulation (EU) No 1093/2010, to specify 
proportionate diversification methods under which an exposure is to be 
considered as one of a signi ficant number of similar exposures.  We would 
like to point out that we do not see any need for this mandate. It should 
rather be deleted. However, if it is retained: it is important that conditions in 
smaller jurisdictions are considered when specifying these criteria. 
o Exchange rate risk: The market risk section seems to have an incomplete 
wording since it leaves the application of Article 351 CRR uncertain for 
entities applying the alternative standard approach (ASA). Article 351 
indicates that those entities whose positions in foreign currency exceed 2% 
 
 
of their own resources must calculate capital requirements for exchange 
rate risk, thus being exempt below the threshold. Under the current CRR2, 
institutions applying standard methods can make use of the application of 
that exemption of article 351. However, under the new wording of the CRR3 
proposal, it seems that only institutions that apply the simplified 
standardised approach could make use of the application of the article 351. 
We believe that there may be an error in the wording since it seems logical 
that all standard methodologies (either the simplified or the alternative 
standard approach) should be able to maintain the exemption provided by 
article 351. Otherwise, we would be in a situation for which an entity with a 
negligible exchange rate risk (exposure of less than 2% of its own resources) 
using the simplified standard approach would be less punitive than using the 
alternative standard approach, which from our point of view would be an 
inconsistency. We therefore suggest introducing a reference in Article 325 
to Article 351 to show a clear path to the Article 351 for institutions applying 
the alternative standard approach and, thus, erase any doubts on its 
applicability. 
o Corporates: we believe exposures to corporates with rating from the credit 
quality steps equivalent to the range from BBB- to BBB+ should be assigned 
a risk weight of 65% as these too are, in our opinion, to be considered 
investment grade. 
o Business Indicator (operational risk): w e ask for clarification on what is 
considered by “last eight reporting reference dates” in article 316. Is it 
referring to COREP C16 (thus, the reporting reference date would be 
quarterly) and so it would be the last two 2 years. 
 
 
Other topics in the Banking Package beyond Basel III: 
 
 
1. Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) risks and sustainability: 
 
Institutions have a direct interest in supporting the transition to a sustainable 
economy. However, the tasks of banking supervisor s may not be extended to 
urging institutions to adapt their business models to the respective Union’s 
policy objectives or to “broader transition trends” (CRD VI Art. 76(2), 87a(4,5), 
104(1m)). “Green” or “sustainable” investments are not per se less risky and do 
not necessarily generate stable earnings. Decisions on this must therefore be 
left to the institutions. 
 
According to the EBA’s report on management and supervision of ESG risks 
(EBA/REP/2021/18), the CRR III definitions (Art. 4 (1 52d) should be c larified 
to the effect that these risks are no separate risk categories but represent 
factors that materialise through other categories of financial risks, such as 
credit, operational and market risks. This is particularly important for the 
quantification of the exposure, which will be only meaningful if aligned with the 
existing risk types. Since there are no methodologies or industry standards for 
the quantification of the exposure at a more granular ESG risk level (i.e. social 
risk or governance risk), we would propose to disregard it. 
 
 
 
Strategies and processes to assess and maintain internal capital now refer to all 
risks to which institutions are or might be exposed in the short -, medium- and 
long-term time horizon (SREP, Art. 73 (1), 74 (1) and 76 (1)). While these time 
horizons are appropriate and necessary for strategies  especially on climate 
risks, introducing those diverse time horizons for the ICAAP (thus for all 
risks)would be unnecessary and counterproductive. We propose to skip the 
specification of the time horizons in Art. 73 (1) and 74 (1), as Art. 76 (1) already 
contains a requirement to consider also medium and long-term impacts of ESG 
factors within the strategies. 
 
 
2. Fit & Proper: 
 
We believe that t he implementation of the Basel standards within the EU 
regulatory framework should reflect the proportionality principle, also by taking 
into consideration the risk nature, scale and complexity of the activities of 
European credit institutions. This would allow financial institutions to carry out 
their activities under a non -detrimental regulatory framework which 
strengthens the European banking sector – the backbone of the EU’s ‘real 
economy’. A disproportionate regulation causing huge  regulatory burden 
would negatively impact banks, which could even push resources away from 
customer service. 
 
The banking package introduces detailed procedural rules for the suitability 
assessment of members of the management body. We propose to amend (or 
even delete) Articles 91a – 91d. The stated rationale for the Fit and Proper 
proposals is to give supervisors the necessary powers to enforce prudential 
rules but we believe that some adjustments should be made . ESBG members 
cannot find any evidence in either the EBA guidelines or the Commission impact 
assessment that justifies to only allow ex -ante assessments across the 
European Union. We strongly advocate to allow national discretion to use either 
the ex-ante or ex-post assessment criteria. We do not find evidence to support 
using just one regime, or  where the present ex-post regime has created a 
problem. Additionally, it contradicts the proportionality principle explicitly 
mentioned in the explanatory memorandum under number 2 if the proposal is 
to introduce mandatory regu lations that provide for an ex -ante assessment 
procedure for members of the management body in its supervisory function 
(supervisory board) to be carried out by all institutions and mandatorily include 
the key function holders in the supervisory regime. 
 
We therefore call on the European Commission to allow for both assessment 
processes to be allowed at the national level, and that the competent authority 
keeps to the assessment period mandated in EU law. For these Member States, 
some credit institutions h ave little influence on the nomination or selection 
process and so these institutions cannot carry out an ex-ante assessment. We 
also advocate that the responsibility for assessing the KFHs should remain with 
the institution.  
 
 
 
In detail:  
 
1. Ex-post vs. ex-ante: 
 
Although ex -ante assessment is already applicable in some Member States, 
there are twelve Member States with an ex-post regime for the notification and 
assessment of members of the management body in its supervisory function 
(supervisory board). It is necessary to maintain this ex-post procedure in these 
Member States due to several legal requirements governing the election 
procedures for members of the supervisory board.  
 
Any ex-ante assessment procedure of members of the management body in its 
supervisory function (supervisory board) carried out by the institutions 
themselves (Article 91a (2)) or by the competent authorities (Ar ticle 91b (8)) 
would be unfeasible to comply with for some savings banks in the European 
Union, due to the recruitment process of members of the supervisory board.  
 
For some of our members it is true that national law stipulates that only the 
representation body of the municipality selects the members of the supervisory 
board. The candidates are not known in advance. Institutions get to know the 
new members only after their appointment / after they are elected. The same 
is true for ex-officio members, which are members by law e.g. because of their 
main occupation within the local public authority (mayor of the town), due to 
national rules that serve to ensure the fulfilment of the corporate purpose of 
the undertaking or of the public mandate. Every person who considers standing 
for election needs to have their suitability assessed before the election, as there 
would not be time to do the assessment between preparing the final voting lists 
and the vote itself.  
 
If, in these Member States, only an ex -ante assessment is allowed, then every 
single candidate on the municipal electoral list would be required to complete 
the assessment process, even though only a fraction will be members. The 
efforts required, and expense needed for all of these assessments will no t be 
proportionate to the expected benefits. However, the problem would arise that 
the democratic process of the selection of the supervisory board members by 
the municipality would be interfered with. 
 
In several Member States, some members of the supervisory board are elected 
by the employees of the institutions. Lists are prepared containing a range of 
persons, and – as is normally the case with elections – it is not possible in 
advance to predict which persons will actually then be elected to the  
supervisory board. Those institutions are not in a position to assess the 
candidates and submit notifications before the election / appointment.  
 
For all these reasons, the ex-ante suitability assessment should not apply at all 
for supervisory board members. The existing practice of the supervisory board 
members to perform the suitability assessment only following the appointment 
(ex post) has also proven its worth. If the NCA makes critical ex post remarks 
that there are concerns with regard to expertise, the candidate can also attend 
 
 
seminars to eliminate any deficits following their appointment. All NCAs get the 
necessary powers to remove inadequate members, Article 91a (7). And if the 
NCA requests to dismiss a supervisory board member, a deputy is avai lable in 
the first instance. Therefore, there is no need to change the assessment 
procedure. 
 
Internal assessment by the institution (Art. 91a (2) CRD): 
 
In some Member States (e.g. Germany, France, Italy), any internal assessment 
of supervisory board members by the institution (whether ex-ante or ex-post) 
would contravene national law. These laws stipulate that the municipal trustee 
is responsible for the selection of the members of the supervisory board a nd 
responsible for ensuring their suitability. The members are democratically 
legitimised, and the institutes must accept this decision. The institutions 
themselves ensure the suitability by special training offers for new members . 
However, if the institute decides whether the members elected by the municipal 
trustee are suitable or not, then a discrepancy arises with regard to the special 
governance of the public-law institutes. A change in this structure would mean 
a violation on the autonomy of municipal ities. For this reason, not the 
institutions, but the competent authorities alone may assess the suitability and 
if necessary order appropriate consequences in these cases. 
 
In these cases, the proposed exception that urgent cases can be examined ex-
post is not sufficient. Therefore, a general exception from this obligation in 
Article 91a (2) and in Article 91b (8) in accordance with Article 88 (2) sub-para. 
5 CRD is required for the named cases, regardless of the size of the institution 
(Article 88 (2) su b-para. 5 CRD states: “Where, under national law, the 
management body does not have any competence in the process of selection 
and appointment of any of its members, this paragraph shall not apply.”). 
 
2. Assessment period: 
 
The proposed 120-working days assessment period for supervisory suitability 
assessment (Art. 91b (4) CRD) is far too long and would lead to long vacancies. 
This proposal does not take into consideration the particularities of the 
recruitment process used in some Member States or the legal notice period 
between the proposal (usually by the Nomination Committee) and the 
appointment in case of Management Board members). It will be difficult to have 
such a time frame for both one-tier and two-tier systems.  
 
In addition, it has to be taken into account that in some cases the Shareholders’ 
meeting is in charge of appointing new members , and c onsidering that the 
convocation of the Shareholders’ Meeting must be published one month prior 
to the meeting, we suspect that competent authorities will not be able to 
provide a final Fit & Proper ex-ante decision within such a legal timeframe.  
 
 
 
The current issue that banks are facing is that there is a delay in the ex -post 
assessment period, and for this reason the supervisors should apply the current 
EBA Guidelines which allow for an assessment period of 4 months1. 
 
For those Member States where the ex -ante process is already mandatory 
according to national law and considering that the ex -post assessment will 
continue to be allowed, we propose to reduce the deadline of not more than 60 
working days (3 months) in total (including any extension) for the ex-ante 
suitability assessment by competent authorities, applicable to members of the 
management bodies. 
 
3. Key Function Holders: 
 
Furthermore, we are against widening the suitability assessment by the 
competent authorities to so -called key function holders. This should remain 
within the responsibility of the board of the institution.  Applying suitability 
standards comparable to those for managing directors to key function holders 
would be disproportionate.  
 
Currently, the regulatory framework (including assessment) for key function 
holders is laid down by the Joint ESMA -EBA Guidelines on the suitability of 
members of the management body and key function holders. These state that 
assessing the suitability of key function holders is the responsibility of the 
institutions. At the same time, the EBA Guidelines on Internal Governance 
stipulate that the management body is r esponsible for setting, approving and 
overseeing the implementation of the selection and suitability process for key 
function holders. It is not necessary to change this approach, neither by 
expanding the role of supervisory authorities in the assessment of key function 
holders nor by including new provisions to this matter in the CRD. 
 
Whilst it is perfectly justifiable from a regulatory point of view for the 
management body to have a CRD framework on the top of regulatory 
requirements since the management body is at the highest accountability level 
within an institution, key function holders should be treated differently. Key 
function holders are strongly connected to the organisational, strategic and 
business-related particularities of an institution. Acc ording to the current 
approach of the EBA GL Fit&Proper, key function holders are the roles which 
have a significant influence over the direction of the institution but are not 
members of the management body (for example, heads of internal control 
functions and other positions identified on a risk -based approach by the 
institutions). This definition is wide enough to give institutions the necessary 
flexibility in taking into account the above -mentioned particularities when 
defining their key function holders.

As outlined by the current definition, having internal control functions defined 
as key function holders within an institution – regardless of its strategy and 
internal organisation – is appropriate from a supervisory point of view. 
However, going beyo nd this and laying down a legal requirement that more 
positions are considered as ‘key function holders’ could conflict with the 
internal organisation and strategy of an institution. For example, depending on 
the business strategy and customer portfolio, i n one institution the head of 
retail may be a key function holder, in other institutions not. This differs even in 
group structures where the administration of customer portfolios is allocated 
across the group in different group entities.  
 
Interfering with the organisational and strategic framework of an institution is 
a significant drawback in including the requirement for performing fit and 
proper assessments for key function holders in the CRD. The requirements and 
profiles designated in the institutions for recruitment to such positions are 
sufficient. All institutions are likely to be aware of the particular importance of 
the key functions, with the result that they will already ensure in their own best 
interests that the relevant key function holders meet all of the requirements as 
fit and proper persons. It will not be possible to avoid function holders who 
prove to be unsuitable after the event by introducing a suitability assessment. 
For this reason, we do not consider it is necessary to change in any way the 
current approach regarding key function holders.  
 
In addition, the assessment by the competent authority would cause a 
considerable effort for all large institutions and their subsidiar ies - this applies 
to both: ex post and ex ante. Moreover, an ex-ante assessment would also lead 
to long vacancies. At least we suggest a significant reduction of the assessment 
period by the competent authority for KFH. 
 
Another reason why ESBG would oppo se a deviation to the currently applied 
system (joint ESMA-EBA Guidelines) is that there would be a large increase in 
the administrative burden and that banks need to be allowed to appoint Key 
Function Holders under national law provisions. Immediate replacement of Key 
Function Holders, as set out in Article 91d (1), does not provide any possibility 
for large institutions to immediately appoint a KFH if it is urgently needed. 
 
4. Others: 
 
Procedural provisions:  The provisions in Article 91a and 91b of the draf t, 
especially the proposed procedural regulations on the deadlines regime, have 
a relatively high degree of detail, which contradicts the character of a directive.  
Here, the Member State is deprived of the necessary scope for implementation. 
This is a violation of the principle of subsidiarity. Furthermore, we see no need 
for regulatory technical standards or implementing technical standards, cf. 
Article 91b (9), (10). 
 
No extension to subsidiaries:  Article 91b (8) and Article 91d (1) include also 
subsidiaries of large institutions into the ex-ante assessment procedure. We ask 
 
 
for exempt subsidiaries from these obligations if they are not themselves “large 
institutions”. Otherwise, a disproportionate burden would arise. 
 
Definition of Senior Management (Art icle 3 (1) Point 9 ): This new definition 
widens the perimeter of the persons concerned and no longer corresponds to 
the definition already used in some Member States, such as France, and which 
corresponds to the concept of “Direction Générale” (basically CEO and COO). 
This concept is used to determine the population liable in some circumstances 
such as the reporting of the independent control functions. ESBG therefore 
calls to stick to the previous definition. 
 
 
3. Components of Internal Capital (ICAAP) (Art 73 CRD): 
 
In November 2018 the ECB published its Guide to the internal capital adequacy 
assessment process (ICAAP ) requiring a normative and an economic 
perspective. The declared purpose of this paper is to state the ECB’s 
understanding of the ICAAP requirements of Article 73 CRD and to assist 
institutions in strengthening their ICAAPs in order to ensure business continuity 
of banks  but also to duly take into account economic value considerations . 
Article 73 CRD stipulates that institutions shall have in place proper strategies 
and processes to assess and maintain on an ongoing basis the amounts, types 
and distribution of internal capital that banks consider adequate to cover risks. 
 
Footnote 41 of the ECB Guide is recommending (in general) a confidence level 
of risk parameters of 99.9%. In addition, a holding period of one year for all risk 
types was deemed as adequate by the ECB. In simple terms, the confidence 
level reflects the statistical likelihood that a specific event will not occur within 
a certain period in the future. In this context a required 99.9% confidence level 
and a holding period for one year means that certain losses would occur once 
in 1.000 (!) years. Obviously, such a requirement is not consistent with the 
above-mentioned business continuity approach of the ECB (in other words: a 
going concern approach) as this confidence level of 99,9% is at least equal 
compared to the level of the requirements in the CRR for eligible own funds 
(Common Equity Tier 1, Additional Tier 1 and Tier 2 Capital). However, pursuant 
to the ECB ICAAP Guide (Example 5.1) Tier 2 Capital instruments shall not be 
part of the internal capital of an institution as it cannot be deemed available to 
cover losses. Moreover, in the ECB’s supervisory practice AT 1 instruments are 
often not accepted either. 
 
This expectation of the ECB is excessive as on the basis of the confidence level 
of 99,9 % such losses – as just mentioned – would occur only once in 1.000 years 
(which corresponds to a resolu tion scenario, thus a gone concern approach) . 
According to the ECB the IC AAP shall be implemented in a proportionate 
manner. In this sense such a likelihood cannot justify the exclusion of Tier 2 and 
AT 1 instruments from internal capital at all. A non -allowance of those 
instruments in internal capital confronts the banks with another increase of cost 
of capital and is not justified. This approach would then have a negative impact 
for their global competitiveness.   
 
 
 
Against this background it has to be clarified in Article 73 CRD that the internal 
capital for ICAAP can consist of all eligible own funds according to the CRR as 
well as of economically comparable components or - alternatively - of the 
institution’s net present value assets. 
 
▪ Alignment of Pillar 2 capital composition: 
 
As the commission proposes minor amendments to the Pillar 2 requirement (Art 
104a) and states that “CRD V aligned the nature of regulatory capital that banks 
must hold to meet the Pillar 2 capital requirement with the minimal capital 
composition of the Pil lar 1 capital requirement”, we wanted to take the 
opportunity to advocate for exploring a further alignment of the composition 
of Pillar 2 capital guidance (Art 104b) with both Pillar 1 and Pillar 2 requirement.  
 
Although the current wording of Art 104b C RD V is already a good starting 
point as it leaves the composition of Pillar 2 guidance to the discretion of the 
authority. However, we believe an amendment to further align Pillar 2 guidance 
capital composition to the Pillar 1 capital requirement (as was done by CRD V 
regarding the Pillar 2 requirement) would be beneficial and ensure consistency 
in the Pillar 1 and Pillar 2 capital framework.  
 
 
4. Supervisory powers: 
 
The Commission’s proposal expands the list of supervisory powers available in 
the CRD to competent authorities to cover operations such as acquisitions by 
a credit institution of a material holding in a financial or non -financial entity 
(new Chapter 3 in the current Title III), the material transfer of assets or 
liabilities (new Chapter 4) and  merger or divisions (new Chapter 5). These 
supervisory powers should ensure that competent authorities are notified in 
advance, have at their disposal all the necessary information to perform a 
prudential assessment of these operations, and can ultimately  oppose to the 
completion of operations detrimental to the prudential profile of the supervised 
entities undertaking them. 
 
While we understand that competent authorities need to be informed in 
advance in case of various important operations in the banking  sector, we 
doubt that the proposed notification/approval requirements and additional 
supervisory powers will effectively improve the current supervisory framework 
and avoid undue administrative burden. The operations proposed to be subject 
to notification /approval requirements are partially already covered by the 
current legislative framework (both at EU level and in the member states) and 
will insofar overlap with current applicable  requirements and procedures. 
Particularly, the proposed notification requirement for acquisitions of qualifying 
holdings exceeding 15% of the eligible capital of the acquirer in the proposed 
Art. 27a CRD overlaps to a certain degree with the current notification 
requirement of acquisition of qualifying holdings in the current Art. 22 (1) CRD. 
In such case a double supervisory assessment is required for the same 
 
 
operation which is not consistent with the goal of avoiding administrative 
burdens, neither for the supervisory authorities nor for the supervised 
institutions.  
 
Before introducing new supervisory tools for the above-mentioned operations, 
we would prefer the Commission (and the supervisory authorities) to perform 
an assessment of simplifying and reducing complexity in the current 
acquisitions framework in the financial sector.

General comments. 
The Spanish Banking Association (AEB) welcomes the European Commission proposal for 
the EU transposition of the “Basel III: Finalizing post-crisis reform” standard.  
AEB acknowledge the efforts made by the EU regulators to achieve several goals such as: 
- Reducing the impact in terms of capital requirement  which will foster the 
efficiency of the EU banking sector. It is important to highlight that capital 
increases have a direct impact on lending volumes or lending prices. Reducing the 
impact in capital requirements will grant stability in volumes and prices.  
- Considering EU specificities  which will ease the access to financial resources 
for EU corporates. Having a good understanding on these EU specificities will 
create a proper environment for corporates to finance their businesses.  
- Balancing between risk sensitiveness and simplicity  which will reduce the 
regulatory burden for the European banks and will enhance comparability of 
capital ratios while setting a risk-sensitive prudential framework. 
We are of the view that the proposal, is balanced and addresses some of the key concerns 
raised by Member States and banks with different business models. In particular, we 
positively welcome the following measures that have been included in the legal package: 
- Operational risk: We fully support the EC pr oposal as regards of disregarding 
past operational risk losses setting the capital calculation in terms of the Business 
Indicator Component, using a national discretion granted by the Basel Committee. 
These are good news since past losses are not good pred ictors of future losses 
and do not reflect the entities’ risk profile. 
- We welcome the proposed treatment for equity intragroup  holdings, which 
helps recognizing the banking model based on subsidiaries. We also welcome 
other measures that smooth the impact of the new equity treatment, stimulating 
the financing of the economy through equity 
- The maintenance of the EU specificities : The SME and infrastructure 
supporting factors, and the CVA exemption, are important measures for banks to 
support the financing and hedging of risks of EU businesses in an economy where 
banks provide 75% of their external funding 
- The transitional provisions that postpone the application of a 10% CCFs on the 
Unconditionally Cancellable Commitments (UCC ) to 2033 , however we 
consider other measures could have a very negative impact on trade finance in 
the EU and therefore need to be reviewed. 
However, we think there is room for further improvement to recognize the reality of the 
EU banking sector and for this reason we would l ike to comment on some specific 
elements.

Proposal for further improvements to the CRR3/ CRD6 package  
In this section we would like to raise some specific elements which are more deeply 
developed in the Annex.  
1. Extend the transitional arrangements regarding unrated corporates, SA-
CCR and low risk mortgages to the Standardized Approach  
Article 465 of the CRR3 legislative proposal lays down certain transitional arrangements 
for the standardized approach that serves as reference for the calculation of the output 
floor. These arrangements allow for the application of a more favorable capital treatment 
for unrated corporates of high credit quality, low risk mortgages and the standardized 
approach for counterparty credit risk (SA -CCR) for th e calculation of the output floor, 
provided certain conditions are met, until the end of the transitional period in 2030-2032. 
In addition, by the end of the transitional period, Art. 465 empowers the Commission, 
based on the assessment of EBA reports, to propose the extension of this beneficial 
treatment for the output floor calculation beyond the transitional period. 
Having analyzed Article 465 CRR3 , we believe that there are sound arguments 
that would justify extending these temporary improvements for the output floor 
calculation to the general standardized approach:  
‒ The same arguments that underlie the temporary improvements in the standardized 
approach for the output floor calculation are also applicable to the standardized 
approach in general: lack of rating coverage of EU corporates and the need to ensure 
EU corporates are able to hedge their business risks at reasonable costs. These 
measures would be most effective if extended to all banks that apply the standardized 
approach. 
‒ Having to apply more than one standardized approach adds complexity, operational 
burden, and costs to all EU banks, whether or not affected by the output floor.  
‒ The Basel Standards only refers to one standardized approach.  
The same arguments would apply should the transitional arrangements were to become 
permanent. Permanent or not, this situation could raise some level playing field issues 
among players.  
Unrated corporates 
In the case of unrated corporates (Art. 465.3), a procedure should be 
implemented in the standardized approach to identify investment grade unrated 
corporates and apply them a risk weight of 65%, irrespective of their listing.  In 
this regard, there are several soundly implemented metrics (PDs) that follow existing EU 
regulations, that are even decoupled from the capital metrics, and are used by all 
institutions regardless of the approach they use. These metrics allow institutions to 
identify investment grade unrated corporates with the same level of guarantee as 
regulatory PDs. 
In this regard, institutions may make use of the internally estimated PDs for those 
exposures for the purposes of both the economic capital calculation and the accounting 
expected credit loss calculation, consistent with the ‘EU Guidelines for the estimation of 
risk parameters for the IRB approach’ or the ‘EU Guidelines for the credit institutions' 
credit risk management practices and accounting for expected credit losses’. Therefore, 
such PDs should have followed the necessary internal validation and governance 
processes to ensure maximum rigor and compliance with the principles set out in the 
above-mentioned guidelines, including any necessary adjustments if needed  in their 
operations. These PDs are calculated by counterparty and are widely used by banks.

SA-CCR 
In the case of SA-CCR, we consider that the current scope of Art 465.4 is too restrictive, 
and it is not justified that the alpha should be set to 1 only for the purposes of the output 
floor calculation. One of the reasons for this significant impact is the  alpha factor, which 
has the impact of increasing exposures by 40% and, was originally calibrated to 1.4 in 
2005 at international level, and no longer reflects the market environment among other 
concerns. The US has acknowledged this and has neutralized th e impact of the alpha 
factor by setting it to 1 for derivatives closed with Commercial End Users. In addition, the 
recital in the Securitisation Quick Fix mandated the Commission to review SA-CCR before 
June 2021 to ensure EU corporates were able to hedge their financial risks in the context 
of the recovery from the Covid -19 pandemic. Therefore, our proposal is to extend 
the application of an alpha factor of 1 to all the standardized approach 
calculations, whether or not linked to the output floor.  
Low risk mortgages 
Finally, in the case of low risk mortgages (Art. 465.5) we believe that any temporary 
improvement in the calculation of the standardized approach for output floor purposes 
should be extended to the standardized approach in general. 
 
2. Operational risk  
 
Business Indicator: Interest, Leases and Dividend Component (ILDC).  
The CRR3 proposal lays down the formula of the Interest, Lease and Dividend component 
(ILDC), part of the Business Indicator (BI) for the calculation of the operational risk 
requirements: 
 
 
 
 
The first part of the formula, the interest component, comprises the institution’s net 
interest margin (NIM) but incorporates a 2.25% cap of certain interest-generating assets. 
We understand that the formula includes this cap with the aim of not generating distorted 
capital requirements in high interest rate geographies or high NIM institutions (due to 
high PDs), given that in this cases, high levels of this input is not directly related to 
operational risk measurement.  
With the new BIC calculation require ment at a consolidated level, in our opinion it is 
necessary to clarify that for the calculation of the ILDC, when determining the minimum 
between the NIM and 2.25% of determined interest earning assets, it should be assessed 
at jurisdiction level. Otherwise, if this determination of the ILDC was performed at group 
level the possibility of applying the cap would be in some cases practically invalidated, for 
example in the case of banking groups present in jurisdictions with high and low NIM. In 
these cases,  if assessed at jurisdiction level there would be different ILDC formula 
applications (either NIM or 2.25% cap) which if applied at consolidated level only one 
option would be possible. This implies that the reality of the jurisdictions in which 
the banking group is operating would not be recognized.  
Since the Commission proposal does not specify anything as to whether to determine the 
banking group’s cap before or after its financial margin aggregation, we believe it is 
appropriate to clarify in the CRR 3  proposal that the cap should be applied at a

Use of insurance for operational risk  
In addition, the CRR3 proposal mandates EBA to report to the Commission on the use of

Taxonomy on operational risk. 
Regarding the EBA mandate under to develop a risk taxonomy on operational 
risk and a methodology to classify the loss events included in the loss data set (Article 
317), we are concerned on the fact that EU is developing a new taxonomy without being 
previously discussed at international level. This will add complexity to banks’ operatio ns

jurisdictions. This point is of particular concern for banking groups that pres ent in non-
EU countries. 
Therefore, we think any exercise to review the operational risk taxonomy should 
previously take place at international level by the Basel Committee. 
 
EBA Level 2 mandates

4. Trade Finance: Technical Guarantees 
Technical Guarantees (including performance bonds, bid bonds, etc.) are a traditional 
trade finance product widely used in the real economy, as these kind of guarantees are 
required by corporate clients whenever they need to secure a national or internatio nal 
commercial contract. Through Technical Guarantees a Financial Institution guarantees to 
the buyer of the contract that the seller will perform their obligations as agreed. Therefore, 
Technical Guarantees are essential to facilitate commercial transacti ons and reduce the 
risk.   
The AEB considers that the new proposed treatment of the CRR3 proposal (50% CCF) is 
overly punitive and will have a significant impact on the corporate business, and 
advocates for its inclusion in the bucket 4 of Annex I therefor e maintaining the current 
20% CCF treatment, based on the following arguments: 
• Cost Increase: the new treatment will make the price of Technical Guarantees 
to increase by 150% heavily impacting the corporate business. In addition, the 
new measure will reduce the capacity available in the market for this product due 
to the increase in RWAs for the banks providing the guarantees.  
• This will lead  to reduced competitiveness and econ omic impact for EU 
corporates. European corporates will lose capacity to compete and bid for 
commercial contracts, especially in large infrastructure and energy projects where 
this instrument is typically required.  AEB considers this to be extremely sensitive 
in a scenario where the EU is facing important challenges, including the Covid19 
recovery and the energy transition, two topics which could lead to an increase 
need of Technical Guarantees to support project development.  
• Banks’ significant RWA increa se. A 150% increase in the CCF (from 20% to 
50%) that means also an increase of 150% of the RWA associated. 
• This RWAs increase is overly Punitive based on historical low observed 
CCF: ICC1 and GCD historical data shows that Technical Guarantees 20% CCF is 
adequately conservative. Through the analysis of +1.25 Million transaction from 
+ 12 banks it is observed the empirical CCF (which means the % of Technical 
Guarantees that are claim when an applicant  is already on default) is only 8%, 
providing sufficient buffer until the current 20%. The ICC report also shows that 
it is a low risk product with very minimal default rates (0.25%).  
• Uneven playing field: The increased burden for banks when issuing Technical 
Guarantees will create an uneven playing field for European Banks, as other 
institutions (such as insurance companies) are offering the product without having 
the same capital requirements.

6. Specialised lending: Slotting approach 
Banks play a key role in infrastructure financing in the EU and it is important the prudential 
framework is appropriately calibrated.  
With regards to the slotting approach, further granularity is required especially 
with respect to projects with higher cr edit and lower risks in order to ensure a 
level playing field among banks. In the same way that the CRR III introduces a variety 
of ratings going from risk weights of 20% to 150% for those specialised lending exposures 
that go under the standardized approa ch, it would also be necessary to introduce more 
granularity in the slotting approach to ensure the consistency of the methods, given that 
both are based on a fixed risk weights with the difference being that one is based on the 
external rating and the other is based on the internal rating.  
It is therefore proposed that the slotting approach is reviewed, taking into account 
the adjustments introduced to this asset class under the other approaches in the 
CRR3, in order to ensure that there is consistency an d a level playing field between the 
three approaches: standardized, slotting, and IRB. 
 
7. Minority interests  
The EC has introduced some changes on the minority interest’s framework, which 
according to the introduction text of the proposal “do not alter the current calculation of 
minority interests but aim at clarifying the legal text as a follow -up to recent answers 
provided by the Commission via the EBA Single Rulebook Q&A tool.” We are of the view 
that the proposed changes are very relevant in terms of  how the minority interests are 
calculated. 
As a general principle, we think that what should be considered to calculate the 
minority interests that can be recognized at a consolidated level is the capital 
that is available to absorb losses locally in a st able way. We consider that the 
framework should consider the following elements:  
- Banks must meet with a complex regulatory requirement framework and 
supervisory expectations not limited to the minimum Pillar 1 and Pillar 2 capital 
requirements as the minority interest framework sets. This is more relevant in the 
case of subsidiaries ba sed in third countries, where capital requirements may 
differ from the European ones (e.g. macroprudential measures going beyond CRR 
II or CRD V). The minority interest’s framework should acknowledge that such 
requirements are binding for subsidiaries , and  the contribution of the 
subsidiary to the consolidated requirements should be calculated accordingly.

8. Market risk framework 
The EC proposal on market risk sets a different treatment for DRC (IMA) (Default Risk 
Charge) for sovereign, public sector entities, multilateral development Banks and 
international organizations exposures which have a similar treatment as sovereign under 
the current standardized approach in the CRR. The zero default RW allowed under 
standardized approach should be extended to IMA for sovereigns, public sector entities 
and multilateral development as the one allowed for the Standardized approach. We are 
of the view that this asymmetric treatment creates potential problems of level playing 
field and regulatory arbitrage in favor of those less risk sensitive baking entities that apply 
the Standardized Approach.

General comments. 
 
Governance arrangements (art. 88) 
● It is crucial that the Commission reconsider the wording of the newly -added para. 3 
of article 88 and eliminate the obligation of establishing individual statements 
setting out the roles and duties of each member of the management body , 
in particular given that:  
○ it is not aligned with corporate laws which do not envisage an individual 
accountability regime and where the allocation of different roles or duties 
to different board members is inadequate for one-tier systems, given that 
no efficient or real separation of responsibilities can be implemented where 
company law conceives the board as one unique and inseparable body 
through which all functions are performed;  
○ the duties of the members of the management body are already envisaged 
in the Regulations of the Board of Directors and, where applicable, the 
Committee Charters/Regulations;  
○ the Commission provides no rationale for the changes, which would 
deviate from commonly accepted governance practices as per existing 
regulations and governance codes; 
○ the wording is not fit for members of the management body, who have no 
reporting lines or lines of responsibility other than membership of the 
management body as such. The management body bears ultimate 
responsibility and is thus not, strict ly speaking, part of the organizational 
structure but independent to it and above it; and 
○ in most of the jurisdictions the Board is a collegiate body, where the 
allocation of specific responsibilities is not possible. The Board members 
have and are subject to the same roles and duties, and in practice the only 
differentiation in the execution of their roles is due to be member of the 
different committees, both in its management or in its supervisory 
function. In this case, their roles are carried out taking  into account the 
responsibilities assigned to the Committee and its rules of functioning.

1. Article 12a: Consolidated calculation for G -SIIs with multiple resolution 
entities 
In regards to the adjustment in article 12a, some amendments should be performed in 
the CRR (recital 2 and 3; and article 12a) and in the BRRD (articles 45d and 45h of the 
BRRD). Those will avoid problems and uncertainties that may arise when performing the 
adjustment refer to in article 12a. 
First, we believe the comparison between the hypothetical SPE and the sum of RWA of 
each “resolution entity” keeps third country subsidiaries out due to the fact that the 
comparison in the 12a of the CRR refers to articles 45d an d 45h of the BRRD, and the 
BRRD does not include subsidiaries in third countries nor differences between RWA that 
arise from different calculation criteria between third countries and member states. It 
only includes differences between Member States. There fore third countries should be 
included. 
In addition, we would propose to clarify recital 3 of the European Commission’s proposal. 
The amendment described below will ensure an alignment in the comparative between 
the hypothetical SPE consolidated requiremen t and the sum of the resolution entities 
requirements in an MPE model. 
• When calculating the hypothetical SPE consolidated requirement all resolution entities 
are considered: in Member States and in third countries with or without a resolution 
regime.  
• The calculation of the sum of resolution entities as an MPE should follow a symmetric 
approach and therefore should also consider all resolution entities (with or without 
resolution regime) to be consistent and be able to be applied the adjustment if 
necessary.

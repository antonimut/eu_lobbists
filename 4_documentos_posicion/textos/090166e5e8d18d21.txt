CECA COMMENTS ON THE COMMISSION PROPOSAL FOR A REGULATION OF THE EUROPEAN 
PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL AMENDING REGULATION (EU) No 575/2013 AS REGARDS 
REQUIREMENTS FOR CREDIT RISK, CREDIT VALUATION ADJUSTMENT RISK, OPERATIONAL 
RISK, MARKET RISK AND THE OUTPUT FLOOR 
 
The Spanish Association of Savings and Retail Banks (CECA) welcomes the opportunity to 
comment on the Commission proposal for a Regulation of the European Parliament and of the 
Council amending Regulation (EU) No 575/2013 as regards requirements for credit risk, credit 
valuation adjustment risk, operational risk, market risk and the output floor (hereinafter called, 
proposal for a Regulation). 
 
On general terms, we assess positively the proposal for a Regulation  issued by the European 
Commission on 27 October since it transposes the final elements of the Basel III reforms in the 
EU regulatory framework making use of some of the national discretions allowed by the Basel 
Committee (e.g. those applicable to the new treatment for operation risk)  and maintaining 
European specificities already included in CRR (e.g. SME and infrastructure supporting factors).  
 
However, we believe that some adjustments should be made in order to improve the regulatory 
framework, promote the level playing field and avoid unintended capital increases. 
 
 
I. SPECIFIC REMARKS TO THE PROPOSAL FOR A REGULATION 
 
Own funds requirement for operational risk 
 
The Basel Committee’s finalisation of the Basel III framework sets a new standardised approach 
for measuring minimum operational risk capital requirements that replaces all existing 
approaches in the Basel III framework. T he methodology of this new approach is based on the 
Business Indicator Component (BIC) -a measure of gross income- and the Internal Loss Multiplier 
(ILM), which is based on a bank’s own internal losses history over 10 years. 
 
In our view, the new operational risk framework does not address properly the link between 
capital incentives and management of operational risk, because it does not take into account 
the most relevant factor for risk management which is the strength of a bank’s control 
environment. Due to fundamental issues in the design of this new framework, disproportionate 
capital requirements for many jurisdictions are generated, such as Spain.  
 
The introduction of the ILM in the formula does not present such link because instead of 
providing risk sensitivity it makes the requirements backward looking, which, in fact, was one of 
the issues at the heart of the shortcomings that ended with the suppr ession of the Advanced 
Model Approach (AMA) 1. That implies that once a significant loss enters into an institution’s 
data set, capital numbers rise significantly until the loss event is out of the time window. In 
practice, without adjustments, past losses linger on the capital numbers long after the causes of

those losses have been remediated. Consequently, the ILM will not directly create any incentive 
to improve a bank’s risk management capabilities, as operational risk control improvements will 
only start to have a positive impact on capital several years after having been put in place. 
 
In our opinion, having a backward -looking component in the capital requirements is not going 
to help banks to better align their incentives to properly manage operational risk. Instead, it 
could result in a disproportionate burden which will penalise banks twice, both in their capital 
requirements and when committing the investments required to remediate the failure in the 
origin of the operational loss. Indeed,  what is going to guarantee past losses are not going to 
happen again, and consequently, improve the operational risk profile of a bank, is to take the 
measures to remediate such failures. In that regard we do not favour the ILM irrespective of the 
years included in it.  
 
Additionally, there exist some fundamental issues on the relationship between the Loss 
Component (LC) and the Business Indicator Component (BIC) in the ILM function . The LC is 
incorporated in the ILM function as a ratio with the BIC (the LC-to-BIC ratio). The ILM was initially 
calibrated on the basis that the LC -to-BIC ratio should be capturing an industry representative 
‘average risk profile’. Banks above (below) this reference ‘average risk profile’ would be subject 
to higher (lower) capital requirements. However, after the first Consultative Documents of the 
framework, the coefficients for the Business Indicator ( BI) and the loss multipliers for the LC 
have been arbitrary recalibrated to try to mitigate the disproportionate impacts of the SMA. 
Since the first Consultative Document up to the final Standardised Measurement Approach, the 
applicable coefficients for calculating the BIC and the LC have  moved from wider and  more 
granular structures to narrower and less granular ones. 2 These changes resulted in a higher 
weight of the LC in the ILM, thus amplifying impacts and volatility in capital requirements for the 
most affected banks.  
 
However, the Basel Committee raises the possibility of applying certain national discretions in 
the transposition of the new standardised approach for operational risk . Specifically, the 
following possibilities are considered:  
 
• setting the value of Internal Loss Multiplier (ILM) equal to one for all banks; 
• increasing the threshold to include a loss event in the data collection and calculation of 
average annual losses from EUR 20,000 to EUR 100,000 for banks in buckets 2 and 3; 
and  
• using less than 10 years of historical data to calculate the Loss Component. 
 
According to the outcome of the  impact assessment carried out by CECA on the effects of the 
three national discretions allowed by the BIS standard on the capital requirements of its 
members, w e consider that the ILM should be set at 1 for the following reasons: (i) the 
alternative of using the bank’s own ILM does not capture idiosyncratic aspects of each

The proposal for a Regulation on treatment for operational risk mitigates significant part of the 
problems mentioned above , since it applies the national discretions allowed by the Basel 
Committee, among them, the removal of the historic loss component from the calculation of 
the capital requirements for operational risk (i.e. setting the ILM equal to 1 for all institutions in 
a jurisdiction), thus neutralising the role of historical losses in the requirement.  According to 
this, t he minimum own funds requirements for operational risk will be solely based on the 
business indicator component ( BIC) as it is mentioned in Article 312: “ The own funds 
requirement for operational risk shall be the business indicator component calculated in  
accordance with Article 313 ”. We welcome the application of this national discretion  for the 
reasons explained above.  
 
Besides, it should be noted that  setting the ILM equal to 1 will also help banks to continue 
supporting the economy recovery after a major and unprecedented shock as the Covid-19. This 
will be especially useful to avoid adverse consequences from markets front-running behaviour, 
correct asymmetries with other jurisdictions, and to prevent negative feedbacks from the real 
economy. 
 
More specifically, we also welcome the possibility of excluding certain exceptional operational 
risk events that are not relevant for the credit institution’s risk profile anymore  (Article 320). 
However, it would be useful if the new CRR wording could specify the criteria that an historical 
event must meet for it to consider that there are no “similar exposures” (not similar or residual 
legal exposures). According to the current wording , some events that might have only 
coincidental linkages with past events might be considered as representative. 
  
Therefore, we advocate for maintaining these discretions in the final text of CRR.  
 
In addition to the transposition of the new standardised approach for measuring minimum 
operational risk  capital requirements , the proposal for a Regulation  introduces additional 
requirements (not covered by Basel III) in terms of risk management and disclosure of historical 
loss data. We considered that the introduction of additional requirements should be avoided, in 
order to maintain the level playing field with other jurisdictions in the context of operational 
risk. For example, we believe that disclosure requirements should be aligned with the elements 
involved in the determination of the Pillar 1 capital requirements. As long as historical losses will 
not be used in the EU, banks should not be required to disclose them.

Credit risk 
 
➢ Equity exposures 
 
The proposal for a Regulation introduces a new treatment for equity exposures under the 
standardised approach for credit risk, in line with the Basel text. It is a relevant issue for credit 
institutions since risk weights have been increased significatively, penalising long-term strategic 
equity investments that credit institutions maintain in non-financial companies. These holdings 
do not serve to earn profits from increases in the value of such exposures but are maintained 
for strategic reaso ns (e.g. digi tal transformation), therefore a risk weight of 100% should be 
implemented. 
 
Also, intragroup participations are penalised by the new treatment. Under CRR intragroup 
participations might be subject to a risk weight of 100%, provided the credit obligations to such 
counterparty can be assigned a risk weight of 0%. We consider necessary to maintain CRR rules 
for these participations.  
 
Although we welcome the introduction of a phase -in for the application of the new treatment 
and the grandfathering clause for existing investments, it would be beneficial to have a more 
permanent solution in place for long-term investments, which will also preserve the level playing 
field. 
 
We would also appreciate clarification on the application of the  transitional period for equity 
exposures (article 495) since it is not clear how to proceed with previous exposures that have 
assigned a risk weight higher than 250%, when new Articles 133 and 495 entry into force. Will 
credit institutions be able to appl y a risk weight of  100% and progressively increase it up to 
250%? We provide the following example for illustration purposes:  
 
− Current risk weight on an insurance position: 370% 
− According to Articles 133 and 495 (2) of the  proposal for a Regulation  this exposure 
could be risk weighted at 250%. 
− By the application of the transitional period, the institution can assign a risk weight of 
100% to that exposure and progressively increasing it to 250%. 
 
In addition, and also regarding the transitional arrangements for equity exposures, it has to be 
noted that Article 495a(3) establishes the following: “ By way of derogation from Article 133, 
institutions may continue to assign the same risk weight that was applicable as of [OP please 
insert the date = one day before the date of entry into force of this amending Regulation] to 
equity exposures to entities of which they have been a shareholder at [adoption date] for six 
consecutive years and over which they exercise significant influence in the meaning of Directive 
2013/34/EU, or the accounting standards to which an institution is subject under Regulation (EC) 
No 1606/2002, or a similar relationship between any natural or legal person and an 
undertaking.” The concern here is the introduct ion of the term “ adoption date” which has not 
been defined in the Regulation and due to this we fear that the risk of misunderstanding may

arise. We believe that it would be clearer if “adoption date” is substitute by “date of 
application”. 
 
➢ Loans financing land acquisition, development or construction (ADC)  
 
We consider very positive the replacement of the existing treatment of ‘speculative immovable 
property financing’ with the treatment of ADC exposures provided by the Basel III standards 
since it introduces a more granular approach that differentiates high risk exposures from those 
that do not pose a higher risk by allowing the assignation of a risk weight of 100% if certain 
conditions are met (e.g. significant amount of pre-sale contracts). 
 
We believe that further specifications for applying the risk weight of 100% are needed by 
competent authorities at EU level in order to achieve minimum harmonised criteria but taken 
into account national real estate market specificities. 
 
Market risk (Exchange rate risk) 
 
Article 351 of CRR indicates the following: “ If the sum of an institution's overall net foreign -
exchange position and its net gold position, calculated in accordance with the procedure set out 
in Article 352, including for any foreign exchange and gold positions for which own funds 
requirements are calculated using an internal model, exceeds 2 % of its total own funds, the 
institution shall calculate an own funds requirement for foreign exchange risk  (…)”. Thus, 
institutions whose positions in foreign currency are below the threshold of 2% are exempted to 
calculate own funds requirements for foreign exchange risk. 
 
Under CRR II, institutions applying standardised approaches can make use of the application of 
that exemption mentioned in Article 351. However, under the new wording of the proposal for 
a Regulation, it seems that only institutions that apply the simplified standardised approach 
could make use of the application of Article 351.  
 
We consider that all standard ised methodologies (either the simplified or the alternative 
standardised approaches) should be able to maintain the exemption provided by Article 351. 
Otherwise, the following inconsistency can appear: an institution with a negligible exchange rate 
risk (exposure of less than 2% of its own funds) using the simplified standardised approach would 
be less punitive than using the alternative standard ised approach. Therefore, w e suggest 
introducing in Article 325 a  reference to Article 351 to show a clear path to Article 351 for 
institutions applying the alternative standardised approach and, thus, erasing any doubts on its 
applicability. 
 
Output floor 
 
We consider that the introduction of the output floor should enhance the comparability of 
capital ratios among different jurisdictions and institutions, as the current situation is biased by 
different RWA density levels. Therefore, and given that the proposal for a Regulation introduces 
transitional arrangements under the output floor referring to low -risk mortgages , unrated 
 
7 
 
corporates and SA-CRR, we consider that these should be extended to the rest of the framework 
(i.e. institutions applying the standardised approach) , and not only for the out put floor 
calculation purposes. Structural problems need structural solutions for the whole framework.  
That would help to maintain the level playing field , avoiding the coexistence of different 
treatments for the same exposures. 
 
Environmental, social and governance risks (ESG risks) 
 
The proposal for a Regulation addresses the ESG risks mainly by introducing new definitions for 
ESG risks and reporting requirements to competent authorities. On this regard it is important to 
avoid considering ESG risks as se parate risk categories since they represent factors that 
materialise through traditional categories of traditional financial risks (i.e. credit risk, market risk 
and operational risk). 
 
 
II. ADDITIONAL REMARKS TO OTHER PARTS OF CRR 
 
Regulatory treatment of non-performing loans (NPLs) 
 
As a consequence of the build-up of NPLs in the banking sector due to the global financial crisis, 
the European Union introduced a set of minimum coverage requirements. It is composed by 
three complementary frameworks: (i) Pillar 2 supervisory expectations in the ECB SREP letter, 
(ii) Pillar 2 supervisory expectations in the addendum to the ECB Guide on NPE coverage, and 
(iii) a Pillar 1 regime in CRR consisting in  a minimum provisions requirement according to the 
years the operation is classified as NPL (known as, prudential backstop). While the SREP letter 
applies to operations already classified as NPLs by 31 March 2018, the Addendum to the ECB 
Guide applies to operations classified as NPLs from 1 April 2018 to operations formalized before 
26 April 2019, and the prudential backstop applies to those operations formalized as of 26 April 
2019. 
 
Whereas supervisory measures (Pillar 2) tackle the increase of NPLs in a more flexible way and 
promote the supervisory dialogue, the prudential backstop is a rigid measure that does not allow 
any operation exclusion. Besides, it only consents capital deduction as the single alternative to 
accounting provisioning. As a rigid measure, the treatment offered by the prudential backstop 
cannot be adjusted to the realities of some European countries, as for example, Spain. 
 
In particular, the prudential backstop does not take into account the different lead times of the 
mortgage foreclosure. In Spain, these lead times are extended , approximately, until six years 
due to the application of the Law 5/2019 regulating mortgage loan agreements (LCCI), which 
requires to wait one year until starting the foreclosure proce ss and where the process can be 
extended between three and five years once it is initiated. 
 
The prudential backstop also affects corporate loans given its very punitive treatment when 
dealing with debt restructurings that are up to date . It clearly disincentives offering long term 
 
8 
 
solutions to corporates dealing with  short term difficulties but that are viable  (e.g. maturity 
extension). Applying the prudential backstop may imply the classification of exposures of those 
corporates as impaired with the correspondent 100% coverage requirement in a very short  
period of time. Consequently, this treatment will have a negative impact for the economy since 
it “closes the door” to debt restructurings. 
 
Impacts may be relevant, with their consequent negative effects on funding capability and ability 
to supporting real economy. The prudential backstop implies value adjustments and may force 
credit institutions to sale these funds (value transfer). Incentivising a massive value transfer from 
banks to private funds that invest in distressed assets generates negative economic effects. 
Banks may be forced to undersell their portfolios to funds that are not subject to restrictions 
and benefits from the capture of value, but without contributing to financ e and service the 
economy. 
 
For the reasons explained above, we consider that the prudential backstop (Pillar 1) should be 
adapted to a  Pillar 2 measure.  In practice, Pillar 2 measures have demonstrated their well -
functioning: they ensure a minimum  coverage of provisions but at the same time allow 
considering the different loan characteristics and national specificities. Moreover, all the 
regulatory and institutional reforms that have been introduce since the financial crisis, including 
Basel framework reforms, have sought solving the root of the problems observed in the past. 
 
In case the above proposal is not considered, we believe that , at least, some adjustments in 
current treatment of the prudential backstop are required: 
 
• First, there should be allowed some exclusions to make the prudential backstop less 
rigid. It will adjust the prudential backstop towards to Pillar 2 measures. For instance, it 
would allow a more appropriate treatment for certain operations (e.g. operations with 
collateral and subjective non-performing loans). 
 
• Second, there should be introduced adjustments in the calendar and minimum levels of 
coverage, extending temporary periods and softening minimum percentages of 
provisions in order to make the prudential backstop less punitive.  If some type of 
operations cannot be eligible to be excluded fr om the backstop application  (option 
above), then it may be appropriate to concede different provisioning calendars for 
different types of operation categories.  
 
The options proposed could b e envisaged as permanent  solution for the purpose of reflecting 
structural changes that have occurred since the 2008 financial crisis, or as transitory measure in 
line with the proposal of the European Commission for gradual implementation of some Basel 
III components (e.g. output floor). 
 
Finally, it is worth mentioning that the  prudential backstop was created to entry into force 
during a period of normality, but the impact of the pandemic has generated an atypical period 
without precedents, which effects are expected to be last several y ears. We think that it is

another relevant reason that j ustifies the need of introducing flexibility in the prudential 
backstop for NPLs and adapting it to the new European reality. 
 
Volatility in solvency ratios due to IFRS 17 
The introduction of the new accounting rule for insurance contracts (IFRS 17), that will enter 
into force on 1 January 2023, represents one of the biggest regulatory challenges insurance 
sector will have to face. This new regulation also has significant implications for banking groups 
with insurance subsidiaries, i.e. financial conglomerates.  
 
Although certain initial limitations of IFRS 17 have been solved during the development of the 
accounting regulation, some persistent relevant problems can be addressed through prudential 
regulation. 
 
Given the characteristics of life insurance products placed in the market, a relevant problem for 
Spanish financial conglomerates is the volatility that the application of IFRS 17 will cause in 
solvency and markets ratios (CET1 ratio and book value per share, respectively). This volatility is 
generated by differences in how the insurance contracts are valuated in comparison to the 
valuation of financial investments that support those contracts.  It means that an asymmetry 
occurs between discounted assets and liabilities due to a set of factors. For example, the 
existence of differences in the discount rate used for measuring assets and liabilities and the 
fact that a significant proportion of insurance contracts are not value in current terms. 
 
This relevant issue affects in particular to Spanish financial conglomerates due to the differences 
in life insurance products  with respect to others applied in other jurisdictions  and also for the 
scale of the insurance companies in some Spanish financial conglomerates. Although there exist 
some practical difficulties to obtain concrete estimation s, some calculations suggest that the 
volatility range caused by the introduction of IFRS 17 may achieve 20 b.p. in solvency ratios  
(besides, the one-off impacts of the first application of IFRS 17 should be added to the impact in 
volatility). In this sense, it should also  be mention that these impacts are very difficult to 
anticipate and there exist no management technics available that allow their prevention and 
mitigation. 
 
Considering the explanations given above and, considering that in the short term there is no 
available way to introduce amendments in the accounting framework  (its review is foresee in 
2027), we believe that an amendment in CRR should be introduced to allow banks to not deduct 
gains and losses (registered in their own funds or in the profit and loss account) generated by 
changes in the valuation of insurance contracts and in the fair value of the financial assets. This 
exception would be applicable only for those contracts value under the general model of IFRS 
17. In other words, we propose to include an amendment in the prudential regulation to tackle 
a structural weakness in the accounting framework. 
 
Therefore, we propose the introduction of the following amendments in CRR: introducing a new 
Article 33a and modifying Article 35:

MEDEF position on the Commission’s proposition 
to finalise the implementation of Basel III 
 
In its Banking Package 2021, the Commission proposes a review of EU banking rules to finalise the 
implementation of the Basel III agreement in the EU. While we recognise the Commission’s efforts 
to take EU specificities into account, its proposal is not yet in line with the mandate given to the 
Basel Committee to finalise the Basel III fra mework without significantly increasing overall capital 
requirements across the banking sector.  In fact, we are concerned that some of the propositions 
might have an adverse impact on the real economy.  
In our view, the finalisation of the Basel III accords should be: (1) fair, i.e. recognise the specificities 
of the EU banking sector ( banks’ use of internal models to calculate risks, companies’ reliance on 
derivatives for edging purposes, etc.); (2) reasonable, i.e. pragmatically apply the output floor at the 
consolidated level; and (3) definitive, i.e. mark the final step in the reform of banking rules. Only this 
would ensure sustainable financing from the banking sector, and therefore a growing economy. 
The impact of Basel III on banks and the economy 
Finalising Basel III will further raise banks’ capital requirements… 
 In the aftermath of the global financial crisis, financial regulation has been tightened, fostering 
the soundness of our banking system without hampering the distribution of credit . Since 2010, 
the capital ratios of French banks have more than doubled  and annual credit growth has 
averaged 4%. This is an achievement that must be preserved when finalising the Basel III accords.  
 Admittedly, the Commission’s specific adjustments for the EU would considerably reduce the 
short-term impact of the Basel III finalisation on banks.  According to the Commission, strict 
alignment with Basel III standards would lead to an increase in capital requirements for banks by 
18.5% by 2028. With these specific adjustments for the EU, the increase would be between 6.4% 
and 8.4%, and the cumulative capital requirements would be of the order of €26.3bn. More than 
two thirds of this increase would be due to the introduction of the output floor (see below). 
 However, most of the adjustments proposed by the Commission are only transitional. They would 
end within the next ten years  and would therefore span over a shorter period than the average 
maturity of the funding arrangements they target. 
 EU b anks will also  certainly have to increase their own funds well beyond the estimated 
cumulative amounts.  The capital requirements calculated by the Commission represent the 
additional capital that banks will need just to comply with minimum regulatory requirements. 
However, banks now have comfortable margins over these minimum thresholds. They will need a 
lot more additional capital if they want to maintain them. The total capital needed to restore these 
safety cushions could thus amount to more than €200bn, according to a study based on figures 
from the European Banking Authority. 
 Moreover, the impact of the finalisation of Basel III on US banks will certainly be much lower.  
Maintaining a "level playing field", that is to say equal competition, with the banks of other 
continents is a key element in ensuring European strategic autonomy. However, according to a 
Basel Committee study from September, even a strict application of the Basel III standards would 
lead to a limited increase in capital requirements (only 2.5%) for American banks (United States, 
Canada, Mexico, Brazil). This is because Basel III standards  have been designed for the US  
banking model and is not suited for the European model.

… potentially constraining their financing to the real economy 
 Additional capital requirements for banks could reduce their ability to finance the economy. In a 
study published in July 2021, the ECB analysed the impact that the finalisation of Basel III would 
have on the economy under several scenarios. In the scenario including the specific adjustments 
for the EU which have been proposed by the Commission, the credit would be in the few years 
following the finalisation of Basel III 0.5% lower than it would have been without it. 
 A reduced ability for banks to finance the economy, or some sectors thereof, would be particularly 
concerning in our view. First, the EU economy is still in the process of recovering from the Covid 
crisis. Second, investment needs are considerable in every sector of the economy. To succeed in 
the twin transition of the economy, the ECB estimates that investments of up to €330bn per year 
for the ecological transition and €125bn per year for the digital transition will be needed. 
MEDEF position on the Commission’s Banking Package 2021 
Output floor 
 Banks’ capital requirements are determined by their assets which are weighted according to their 
risk. To calculate their risk-weighted assets  (RWAs), banks can either rely on a standardised 
approach or use their own internal models (as long as they have been approved by the regulator). 
 One of the key measures of the Basel III reforms is the introduction of an “output floor”, which 
aims to reduce the va riability of banks’ capital requirements calculated using internal models, 
and thereby enhance the comparability of banks’ capital ratios. It sets a non-risk-sensitive lower 
limit to the capital requirements that are produced by institutions’ internal mode ls, at 72.5% of 
the requirements that would apply on the basis of the standardised approaches. 
 MEDEF position: 
- The output floor should be applied narrowly , which means that it shouldn’t apply for the 
calculation of Pillar 2 requirements and of EU specific buffers . In any case, the output floor 
should be applied at the highest level of consolidation only to allow banks to allocate capital 
and liquidity where it is needed. 
Financing of unrated corporates  
 Close to 80 % of companies in the EU are not rated by rating agencies and often they draw most 
of their funding from banks that use internal models to determine their risk. However, the Basel 
III standardised approach to measuring credit risk is particularly severe with unrated companies. 
This means that the output floor would lead to a significant rise in EU banks’ capital requirements 
for these exposures.  
 With that in mind, t he Commission proposes to limit these increases , albeit o nly during a 
transitional period. The output floor will gradually increase from 50% in 2025 to 72,5% in 2030, 
and until 2032 banks will be allowed to apply a preferential risk weight of 65% to their exposures 
to corporates that do not have an external rating, provided that those exposures have a probability 
of default of less or equal to 0.5% (this corresponds to an ‘investment grade’ rating).  
 MEDEF position: 
- It is key to maintain risk sensitivity for low risk exposures and a prudential treatment of unrated 
companies that does not penalise  EU banks and companies. In our view, the t ransitional 
arrangements regarding unrated corporate financing should therefore be made permanent.

Real estate lending  
 Unlike US banks, which offload much of their mortgage loans by selling them to state-backed 
entities or through securitization, European banks largely keep them on their balance sheets until 
maturity. Moreover, the dual recourse to both the borrower and the property is a central element 
of mortgage lending in Europe, which significantly reduces losses on mortgages compared to the 
US where non-recourse lending is more common.  
 However, the Basel standardised approach has been built for the US model (in which banks 
mainly retain high-risk residential mortgages), so it is not suited for the European model (in which 
banks mostly detain low-risk residential mortgages). The output floor could therefore have a big 
impact on European banks’ capital requirements.  
 In order t o reduce capital requirements for these low -risk exposures, t he Commission has 
provided for transitional arrangements , albeit again only until 2032. For instance, banks would 
be able to apply a lower risk weight to the part of their residential mortgage exposures that is 
considered secured. 
 MEDEF position: 
- MEDEF supports reducing capital requirements for low -risk exposures . The transitional 
arrangements that are proposed for real estate exposures should be made permanent 
Counterparty credit risk 
 The Standardised Approach for Counterparty Credit Risk (SA -CCR) is a methodology to calculate 
the capital requirements of derivative contracts, which are broadly used by non -financial 
counterparties to hedge their risks. The formula includes a so-called alpha factor, which is set at 
1.4 and increases the cost of capital of derivatives by 40% . This significantly increases the cost 
of hedging, including for non-financial corporates that use derivatives to manage risks arising 
from their business operations (“commercial end-users”). 
 Keeping the alpha factor for such operations would penalise European banks as it has already 
been removed in the US in this case. Also, compared to those in the US, firms in the EU are more 
exposed to currency risks and rely on banks to cover them. The Commission therefore proposes 
to remove the alpha multiplier until the end of 2029 for banks using internal models and 
constrained by the output floor.  
 MEDEF position: 
- As there is no convincing risk -based argument for such a flat -rate surcharge on corporate 
exposures, we strongly advise to follow the US in neutralising the alpha factor (i.e. setting it at 
1) without a time limit, and to neutralise it in the standardised approach (SA-CCR) for all banks 
to benefit and not only for those constrained by the output floor.  
- Otherwise, the SA-CCR will have a significant impact on the price of derivatives for non-financial 
counterparties. This could discourage EU corporates to hedge their risks, which would in turn 
deteriorate their risk profiles and further affect their cost of equity and debt financing . Given 
that the t win ecological and digital transition of the European economy will certainly modify 
and increase non-financial firms’ hedging needs, there is a clear need for a long-term solution. 
SME financing 
 Given the fundamental role of SMEs in growth and job creation, the Commission proposes to 
perpetuate a measure reducing banks’ capital requirements for the financing of SMEs relative to 
that of large companies (the so-called “SME supporting factor”).

- MEDEF supports this proposition to maintain the SME supporting factor. This is essential given 
the key role of SMEs in the EU economy. What’s more, the supporting factor mitigates the

Infrastructure and specialised lending 
 Banks play a key role in infrastructure financing in the EU, while in the US infrastructure projects 
are mainly financed by capital markets. The Commission therefore proposes to maintain a 
favourable treatment reducing the capital requirements for this type of financing (the so -called 
“infrastructure supporting factor”).  
 However, this treatment would only be temporary for non-infrastructure specialised lending such 
as project, object and commodities finance . Specialised lending comprises three different 
categories of finance which play a key role in the EU economy: project finance (e.g., sustainable 
infrastructure investment, supply-chain investment), object finance (e.g., a ship or an airplane) 
and commodity finance (e.g., finance for the import and export of agricultural goods).  
 MEDEF position: 
- Infrastructure and specialised lending will play a key role in the twin ecological and digital 
transition of the EU economy. As such, MEDEF believes that the EU needs to encourage 
investment in infrastructure projects and therefore supports the Infrastructure Supporting 
Factor as it reduces the cost of lending for such projects.  MEDEF also suggests making the 
supporting factor permanent for non-infrastructure specialised lending, and to create a stable 
low risk category based on quality criteria for these types of financing to avoid any significant 
increases in regulatory costs in the future. 
Trade finance – Technical guarantees 
 Technical guarantees are a traditional trade finance product widely used in the real economy. 
These guarantees are required by any company, large or SME, whenever they aim to secure a 
commercial contract. Through technical guarantees, a financial institution guarantees to the 
buyer that the seller will perform their contractual obligations as agreed. Therefore, this product 
is essential to facilitate commercial transactions and reduce the execution risk. 
 The Commission plans an increase from 20% to 50% in Credit Conversion Factors (CCFs) . CCFs 
are used to determine the amount of a bank’s exposure – such as technical guarantees – to be 
risk-weighted. The increase will thus raise capital requirements for banks  underwriting such 
products. 
 MEDEF position: 
- MEDEF is concerned about the proposed changes in the treatment of trade financing 
guarantees, as the planned increase in CCFs would imply a sharp deterioration in conditions 
for export firms (higher prices, lower volumes). A CCF of 50% is overly punitive, and historical 
data show that a CCF of 20% is adequate. The new treatment will increase the cost of technical 
guarantees by 150%, heavily impacting corporate business  – especially SMEs who already 
have limited access to financial markets.  
- This is particular ly concerning given the leading role of European banks on trade finance is 
essential for the EU, as this ensures the supply of key commodities and products and the 
autonomy of production. What’s more, the ecological transition will inevitably increase demand 
for technical guarantees in the coming years.  
Trade finance – Effective maturity recognition 
 Banks using internal models will have to use a specific approach (Foundation Internal Rating -
Based, F -IRB) to measure risk factors for some exposures to large corporates (those with a 
turnover above €500m). However, it is not clear whether banks using this approach will be able

to apply the effective residual maturity to trade finance instruments when calculating their risk-
weighted assets. Instead, they might have to apply a default 2.5-year maturity to these products, 
whereas trade finance deals have, by nature, very short maturities. 
 MEDEF position: 
- Applying an average 2.5-year maturity to short term products would create a significant price 
increase for European corporates, which are the main users of trade finance. This would put 
EU exporters in a weake r position than their competitors outside the EU  – for instance, they 
would need to pay for a letter of credit with a 30 or 90-day maturity the equivalent for the same 
product with a 2.5-year maturity. Not allowing the use of effective maturity would also reduce 
the risk sensitivity of banks’ models and create a negative incentive to move into longer-dated 
and riskier assets.  
- MEDEF therefore urges the authorities to confirm that banks using the F -IRB approach can 
apply the effective maturity for trade finance products. 
Export finance 
 The CRR II requires banks to provision their guaranteed non-performing loans after eight years if 
the loan is covered by an official Export Credit Agency guarantee or after f our years if the loan is 
covered by a private credit-insurer, even if the bank does not expect any loss on these guaranteed 
loans.  
 MEDEF position: 
- MEDEF is concerned that the requirement for banks to provision non-performing loans that are 
guaranteed might reduce banks’ financing of important export contracts with long maturities. 
This in turn could harm the competitiveness of some European exporters. This would also harm 
the competitiveness of EU banks, as the requirement is not part of the Basel III agreement and 
will therefore not be applied to banks in other jurisdictions. 
- MEDEF proposes that the request for regulatory provisions on the guaranteed portion of a non-
performing loan should be waive d if the guarantee is extended by an eligible guarantor, as 
defined in the CRR, and if the bank does not expect any loss on the guaranteed portion.  This 
would be in line with the position of the ECB in its Reporting Instructions on NPLs published in 
November 2019. 
Strategic equity holdings 
 The Commission proposes a favourable treatment for strategic equity holdings, however this 
would not apply to all banking groups.  
 MEDEF position: 
- The favourable treatment for strategic equity holdings should be extended to all categories of 
banks whatever  their prudential treatment, according to the Treaty principle of neutrality.  
Ensuring a “level playing field” between all banking institutions in the EU is essential to 
maintain an appropriate funding of the real economy in all Member States, especially at a time 
when the Banking Union is not yet completed , and the single banking market is still not a 
reality.

        Brussels, 23 February 2022 
EDFI’s response to the EU’s public consultation on the alignment of EU rules on 
capital requirements to international standards 
EDFI, the Association of European Development Finance Institutions (DFIs), has comments with regards 
to parts of the proposal released by the European Commission to align the E uropean Union(EU)’s capital 
requirements’ rules to Basel standards  (the “CRR3 Proposal”). Parts of this proposal are expected to have 
major implications on the activities European DFIs, especially the regulated ones, of supporting the private 
sector in developing and emerging economies. European DFIs, which are state -owned institutions or have 
a government mandate, are major providers of capital, in the form of debt and equity, as well as guarantees, 
towards sustainable and long -term operations in developing and emerging economies, including in the 
Least Developed Countries (LDCs). In 2021, combined new investments of European DFIs reached EUR9 
billion over the year, of which EUR4.1 billion was in Africa, EUR 2.1 billion in climate finance, EUR1.8 
billion in gender-lens investments and EUR2.3 billion towards smaller businesses.  
Credit risk framework – standardised approach 
Equity exposures 
On behalf of the 15 European Development Finance Institutions (DFIs), which are delivering their mandate 
through, inter alia, equity investments in Africa and other developing countries, EDFI welcome s the 
amendments made to Article 133, especially with regards to the following:  
- The differentiation between long -term and riskier speculative equity investments, with the 
classification of long-term exposures referring to the holding period approved by the  institution’s 
senior management as central criterion 
- The recognition of the need for a lower risk weight of 100% for national legislative programmes  
(NLPs) 
However, we see a need for further specifications on both of these options to work for DFIs. 
While the conditions set under Article 133 appear to be adequate for European DFIs to deliver on their 
mandate, the introduction of the requirements set with regards to the Market Risk Framework (FRTB) may 
defeat the purpose of the arrangements proposed under this Article 133, and risk s bringing additional 
burden, both in capital requirements and admi nistrative costs, as explained below  in section “Market risk 
framework – Fundamental review of the trading book (FRTB) ”. In particular, we would suggest excluding 
long term equity investments – as defined in article 133(4) –from the trading book. 
Regarding the second solution for equity exposures  via NLPs, we generally welcome the European 
Commission's proposal to implement the option granted by the Basel Committee for the privileged 
recognition of equity exposures in the context of NLPs with a risk weight of 100%. However, the criteria 
created for US banks for the inclusion of equity investments in legislative programmes only fit the European 
landscape, shaped by public development banks, to a limited extent.  From the pers pective of a DFI, as a 
publicly backed institution , this creates a discrepancy between the criterion limiting the equity exposures 
classified under NLPs to 10% of the institution’s own funds (cf. Art. 133(5) c) CRR-E) on the one hand, and 
DFIs’ activities, being completely aligned with the development mandate from the respective government, 
on the other hand .  To make th is NLP option  also accessible to DFIs, we propose a corresponding 
amendment to Article 133(5) CRR-E, where for equity exposures of public development credit institutions 
as defined in Article 429a (2) and their subsidiaries conditions a) and b)  are satisfied, c) is not applicable 
and the permission of the competent authority is deemed to be granted. 
With European DFIs having diverse institutional set -ups, we would like to highlight the  need for both 
solutions to be effectively practicable (i.e. long-term financing without additional burden from a trading 
book, and NLPs).

Market risk framework – Fundamental review of the trading book (FRTB) 
Under the CRR3 Proposal, a number of instruments are presumed to be given the potential to fall in the 
trading book category, although currently in the banking book. While for some instruments are irrefutably 
to be assigned to the trading book (an example being instruments resulting from securities underwriting 
commitments) another set of instruments is under a general presumption of having a trading intent, 
however with a possibility to refute this intent and assign these instruments to the banking book. T his 
concerns listed equities, embedded derivatives, assets with trading intent under IFRS, and CIUs with 
trading intent, where a look-through can be performed.  
While European DFIs do not have trading activities, with the proposed standards, some of their investment 
activities, in development finance, currently in the banking book, would fall under the category of 
instruments with a general trading book presumption and probably have to be classified as trading book, if 
the refutability of the trading intention was not insured and applicable. This change of classification would 
result in (i) a material increase in capital requirements of the investment activities recognized now as being 
in trading book, and (ii) the need to create additional processes and to establish dedicated trading desks for 
exposures incurred exclusively in their development finance activities that will fall under the trading book, 
and for non -trading book exposures that are subject to foreign exchange or commodity risk. Foreign 
exchange risk is notably significant for equity operations in developing countries with depreciating local 
currencies.  
EDFI is convinced that there is no material ground for DFIs, as state-owned institutions with a mandate to 
support long -term and sustainable inv estments in the private sector in developing and emerging 
economies, to record some of their activities under a trading book, or to establish trading desks.  
The attached presentation from FMO, the Dutch DFI and member of EDFI, outlines in more details the  
implications of the proposal with regards to market risk framework on the DFIs.  
Sustainable Finance 
EDFI welcomes the overall plans the EU has brought together of putting sustainable finance at the top of 
the agenda globally. We believe it is crucial that the EU plays a leadership role in this domain, and EDFI is 
very keen to contribute to the efforts of bringing international finance to greater standards of accountability 
and transparency with regards to ESG aspects.  In this context, EDFI welcomes the CRR3 Proposal of the 
Commission in so far as it addresses the urgent need to include a better integration of environmental, social 
and governance (ESG) risks into the EU prudential framework.  
As European DFIs are exclusively active in financing customers in emerging and developing economies  
(EMDEs), outside the EU, EDFI has been actively looking into the latest developments with regards to the 
EU Sustainable Finance framework and its usability for developing and emerging countries. Beyond 
monitoring regulatory developments  such as the present CRR3 proposal, EDFI is regularly engaging with 
the European Commission, namely  DG INTPA and DG FISMA, on the topic, and has organised exchanges 
with the members of the EU Platform for Sustainable Finance (EUPSF) of the data & usability group . 
EDFI is also an observer on the International Platform for Sustainable Finan ce (IPSF), through which our 
members get valuable insight of the developments happening at EU and at the global level. Finally, we see 
emerging several requests from key stakeholders, such as regulators and governments in emerging and 
developing countries, soliciting European DFIs for their advice on how to replicate and comply with the EU 
ESG regulatory framework outside the EU to attract international investment capital. 
In this context, EDFI is mostly looking at how to address two questions, namely (i) how the EU framework 
can effectively adapt to the reality of developing countries and (ii) what these efforts imply in terms of the 
EU’s role in the global financial architecture.  
EDFI’s assessment to date is that these initiatives remain very much EU -centric, and are not considerate 
enough of the specific role of European investors regulated in the EU that are investing in EMDEs. The EU

Sustainable Finance framework is currently not systematically taking into account non-EU exposures. For 
example, the EU Taxonomy refers to E U regulations and directives to set standards , safeguards and data 
reporting requirements. Most importantly in our view, when non-EU exposures are concerned, the overall 
framework treats these non-EU exposures equally, whether they are located in developed countries (US, 
UK, Canada etc) or located in developing countries (Latin America, Africa): This is a major issue for 
European DFIs and their development mandate, as the levels of technology , ESG reporting and ESG risks 
highly depend on the local economic and social context of each country.  
In this context, we are convinced there is a need to build a specific sustainable finance framework for EMDE 
partner countries, to ensure that the EU Sustainable Finance efforts do not steer European investors 
towards green investments in more advanced countries, at the cost of sustainable investments in LDCs that 
may have difficulties to formally qualify as aligned with EU ESG standards. 
With regards specifically to the integration of ESG risks’ requirements into the CRR3 proposal, we observe 
that the EU-centric approach is maintained , where there is no mention or r eference to the specific case of 
European investors regulated in the EU that invest massively in EMDEs  in pursuing their goals in 
development and sustainable finance . With EMDEs representing circa 40% of the global gross domestic 
product (GDP) and about 75% of global growth according to the IMF, it is of crucial importance that the EU 
adapts its policies and regulations related to sustainable finance and ESG to the context of these economies. 
Conclusions 
Based on this assessment of the CRR3 Proposal, EDFI would conclude the following: 
• It is of crucial importance that the approach outlined for equity exposures  under Article 133  is 
not put into question by other parts of the proposal. As a result, we would recommend:  
o thatthe FRTB approach allows for a derogation for long -term investments 
and national legislative programme s, i.e. Articles 104 paragraphs 2(g) and 3(c) 
should be modified to exclude long term listed equity holdings as defined in article 
133(4) from the trading book (as also outlined by the European Banking Federation) 
o a specification in article 133(5), stating that: for equity exposures of public development 
credit institutions as defined in Article 429a(2) and their subsidiaries, conditions a) and 
b) should be con sidered as satisfied, condition c) should not be applicable and the 
permission of the competent authority should be deemed to be granted (as also outlined 
by the Association of German Public Banks) 
• The CRR3 Proposal, together with other regulatory and policy initiatives of the EU for 
sustainable finance, should recognise the need to find a comprehensive approach of the 
EU to ESG investing in EMDEs, and especially in EU partner countries . EDFI stands 
ready to engage further with EU institutions to contribute to this agenda.  
 
EDFI – the Association of European Development Finance Institutions – was established in 1992 to

INTRODUCTION TO CRR-3 TRADING BOOK REQUIREMENTS
• In CRR-3, as well as in the original Basel standard, a number of instruments are presumed to fall in the trading 
book category, although currently in the banking book. This concerns :
➢ Listed equities
➢ Embedded derivatives 
➢ Assets with trading intent under IFRS
• Once e.g. a listed equity exposure is re-classified to the trading book, the risk weights under the regular credit 
risk capital requirements no longer apply (e.g. 250% if long-term business relationship). 
• Instead, the market risk treatment will apply to this exposure, where the capital requirements will be based on 
the more complex FRTB1 calculation taking into account all the risk types that apply to a trading book exposure.
• It is expected that, due to the nature of the listed equities that DFIs invest in, the market risk treatment for the 
majority of the above-mentioned exposure types will likely be more severe than the current treatment.
• Additionally, process-wise institutions will have to set up dedicated trading desks for exposures that will fall under
the trading book, and even for non-trading book exposures that are subject to FX or commodity risk.

MATERIALITY OF THE PRESUMED TRADING BOOK CATEGORIES
• In terms of materiality, an overview is given of the current size of the exposures that will be
presumed to be trading book according to the draft CRR-3. Since no FRTB calculations for trading
book instruments have been implemented at FMO (only for FX risk in the banking book), it is not yet
possible to predict the capital impact at this stage. 
• Given the materiality, it is deemed not possible that FMO can make use of the ‘small trading book’ 
derogation under art. 94 of the CRR, since the threshold of EUR 50 mln will be exceeded.
• Based on consultations with other regulated DFIs, it is expected that the impact on their portfolio 
will also be material, as it will not be possible either for them to use the small trading book derogation 
of below EUR50m.

CONCLUSIONS
• All in all, in the view of FMO the creation of a trading book by DFIs should be avoided at 
all costs. 
• While the negative impact on capital is not seen as major as the 400% RW for PE, the impact 
is currently unknown (requires full FRTB calculation for the presumed exposures). Given the 
conservative nature of the FRTB calculations, the impact may be very punitive as well.
• In addition to the capital impact, the increased operational implications of setting up a 
trading book should be taken into account as well, which will be huge burden for the Risk and 
Treasury functions.
• As a result, we would encourage the co-legislators to identify solutions that can allow DFIs to 
avoid the creation of trading books, which seem unjustified given the long-term and strategic 
nature of the investment operations.

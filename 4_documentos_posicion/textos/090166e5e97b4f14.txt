General remarks  
UNIFE welcomes the European Commission's proposal regarding the Anti -Coercion Instrument  (ACI). We 
believe that in the current world in which the European rail supply companies operate, often influenced by 
protectionist tendencies or even unexpected actions from third countries interfering with the daily businesses  
of European companies , it is essential that the European Union has a mechan ism to balance this state of 
affairs.  
Nonetheless, the Anti -Coercion Instrument must be compatible with the principles of the World Trade 
Organization, in order to ensure the legality of possible countermeasures to be applied to third countries. 
The most recent crisis between the European Union and China, as a consequence of the deepening of 
relations between Lithuania and Taiwan, only demonstrates this very need to have a tool available that can be 
applied in case of need and as a last resource.  
UNIFE takes note that the ACI is part of the priorities of the French Presidency of the Council of the European 
Union, and calls for swift progress as it is an important part of the EU toolbox of autonomous instruments . 
However, to avoid any effect contra ry to what the Anti -Coercion Instrument wishes to achieve, i.e. revoke 
coercion measures from third countries through deterrence and, if need be, countermeasures, UNIFE would 
like to bring the following considerations that should be taken into account in t he current discussions. 
 
 
 
Scope of Union response measures  
The European Commission’s proposal focuses on coercive measures against the sovereignty of the Union 
and its Member States  and does not directly refer to sanctions against European companies . Even though 
coercion is exercised towards EU authorities and Member States, it ultimately has concrete economic 
implications on EU companies. Therefore, the appreciation of whether a mea sure is deemed to be coercive 
should not only focus on the intended effect on EU and its Member States policies  but also on the expected 
effect on EU companies and their market position. It is particularly true for sectors such as rail supply, which 
are heavily reliant on public procurement and particularly exposed to coercion because of the link with public 
authorities. 
 
At the same time, UNIFE applauds the scope of the proposed countermeasures that, in our view, is sufficiently 
broad and allows a case-by-case approach. In any case, countermeasures should always be coordinated with 
the EU sectors affected, only be implemented as a last resort and in situations where no solution has been 
found through other mechanisms, be it negotiations, mediation , international cooperation  or other types of 
deterrence actions. These measures must be implemented always respecting the principles of international 
law and when necessary to protect the interests of the Union, the Member States and European companies.   
 
UNIFE is particularly grateful to see in the Commission’s proposal the possible suspension of applicable 
international obligations concerning the right to participate in tender procedures in the area of public 
procurement and the imposition of measures aff ecting foreign direct investment or direct investment  
made within the Union and owned and controlled by persons of the third country.  Nevertheless, such 
possibilities and inherent implications shall be always evaluated on a case by case -basis with the concerned 
sectors and industries to avoid additional negative impacts on businesses.  
 
However, there is no mention of extraterritorial sanctions or measures  in the proposed European 
Commission’s proposal. Given the recent example of US sanctions in Iran and th e consequences of such 
sanctions on European businesses, we believe that the Anti -Coercion Instrument needs to include 
extraterritorial sanctions in its scope and avoid similar problems that occurred recently. In fact, the accessibility 
of Iran’s rail market has dramatically fallen and is now considered completely closed to European rail suppliers 
due to these sanctions1. Besides massive losses of business opportunities, this has significantly increased the 
industrial and export imprint of competitors from countries not exposed to these very sanctions. The large

The link between the Anti-Coercion Instrument and the revision of the Blocking Statute Regulation remains 
to be clarified. It seems that the Anti -Coercion Instrument applies when there is a coercion measure , 
irrespective of the manner in which coercion applies. Coercion translated into extraterritorial sanctions would 
fall under the scope of this instrument. At the same time, the same extraterritorial s anctions would also fall 
under the Blocking Statute Regulation which seems to continue  being the main instrument to deal with it. It 
remains to be seen how both policy instruments will co-exist in practice. 
 
Finally, another important point has to do with the likely costs – administrative and/or financial – that may 
arise following the use of the instrument in individual cases, in particular when the Uni on deploys 
countermeasures. These costs must be extremely well measured so that the benefit resulting from the 
deployment of such countermeasures outweighs the harm resulting from the coercive measures of third 
countries. 
 
 
 
Examination of information from third countries 
The proposed regulation foresees the examination of the measures deployed by third countries. However, 
there is no indication on the duration  of this examination nor on the duration of the deterrence actions that 
shall be conducted prior to possible countermeasures. 
 
Although we advocate for  the use of the Anti -Coercion Instrument only as a last resource, the  lack of a 
timeframe may undermine the efficiency of  the applicability  of the Anti -Coercion Instrument, as well as 
prolong the negative impact (economic or other) on the Union, Member States and European companies. 
 
Furthermore, the proposed text lacks certainty on what leads the European Commission to start the process 
of examining the coercive measures imposed by third countries. The wording of article 3(1) – “The European 
Commission may examine...” –  seems too discretionary and could take the form of a purely political decision 
that ultimately may not always or entirely take into account the negative impact of coercive measures 
imposed by third countries. 
 
 
 
Consultation process and role of industry stakeholders 
We welcome the various provisions in the proposed European Commission’s text regarding the consultation 
process and the role of industry associations, in particular of those affected by coercive measures of third 
countries and also possible European countermeasures. 
 
This consultation process must be balanced in order to give stakeholders enough time to provide the necessary 
information, but at the same time it must not prevent the European Commission from developing possible 
scenarios and solutions that could be adopted. 
 
It should also be noted that certain coer cive measures may not be easy to demonstrate and the information 
provided by stakeholders may be limited, as economic operators may fear retaliation and significant impacts 
on their businesses. It  also remains to be seen how the Anti -Coercion Instrument ca n deal against coercive 
measures that are not made public either by the responsible entity or the impacted. 
 
Nevertheless, and in any circumstance, the information made available during the consultation processes must 
be treated confidentially and anonymously by the European Commission services.

Banks’ exposure  
The issue of banks' exposure should also be considered since coercive measures can have a significant impact 
on access to funds (overcompliance requirements, question of the sovereignty of the eu ro vs. dollar, etc.). 
More recently, examples of export controls regimes also raise the issue of the EU's capacity to act in the event 
of coercive measures by a third country.

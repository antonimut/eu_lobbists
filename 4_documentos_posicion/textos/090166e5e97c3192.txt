Introductory remarks 
1. MEDEF (Mouvement des Entreprises de France) is committed to open international trade, governed 
by common rules which allow free and undistorted competition, and preserve freedom of trade.  
2. While MEDEF is in favour of establishing, at EU level, legal and technical instruments more suited to 
current international challenges, it is however firmly opposed to so -called “protectionist” policies 
capable of escalating tensions. 
3. MEDEF is calling on the EU to support the autonomy of companies internationally, by protecting 
them from economic coercion, preserving their right to establish or terminate business relations in 
compliance with WTO rules and general principles, and current legislation. 
1. Summary of the Anti-Coercion Instrument  
• BACKGROUND - Proposed by the European Commission on 8 December 2021, the new Anti-Coercion 
Instrument (ACI) is intended to enable the EU to respond to acts of economic coercion targeting the 
Union or its Member States, if a third country:  
1. Interferes in the legitimate sovereign choices of the Union or a Member State by seeking to 
prevent or obtain the cessation, modification or adoption of a particular act by the Union or a 
Member State.  
2. Applies or threatens to apply measures affecting trade or investment.  
• OBJECTIVE – The ACI, as proposed by the European Commission, is designed as a  deterrent to be 
used as a last resort when all the options for dialogue and cooperation have failed. If this were the 
case, the EU must have at its disposal  sufficiently strong countermeasures to  end the eco nomic 
coercion exercised by a third country. It is strictly a trade defence tool, which aims to put an end to 
a problem, rather than solve it.  
• SCOPE OF APPLICATION  – Boycotts, punitive tariffs  and import o r export restrictions , could be 
covered by the ACI. The tool would therefore make it possible to sanction legal or natural persons 
involved in third countries’ acts of economic coercion. 
➢ However, extraterritorial sanctions, as well as export control regim es, extraterritorial criminal or 
administrative investigations, or legislation ensuring access to company data, which target economic 
operators without directly interfering in an act or decision of the Union or Member State under the 
proposed regulation, remain in a grey area. They could, in theory, remain outside the scope of the 
instrument, which would be regrettable.  
 
• TRIGGERING – The European Commission occupies a central role in terms of deploying  the 
instrument and the adoption of countermeasures; this is to ensure that the system is responsive and 
credible. Member States retain the capacity to block and control. They can prevent the Commission 
from imposing countermeasures (by a qualified majority).

2. MEDEF general observations concerning the Anti-Coercion Instrument 
• MEDEF welcomes the Commission's proposal for the establishment of an Anti-Coercion Instrument. 
This is a new step towards developing technical and legal instruments capable of protecting our 
companies from the effects of geopolitical tensions on their comm ercial activities. Trade 
opportunities and companies’ resilience should not be conditioned by the foreign policy objectives 
of third countries. 
• MEDEF is satisfied with the spirit of the instrument as presented by the European Commission in its 
communication:  
▪ Full compatibility with public international law and trade law; 
▪ A deterrent tool, used as a last resort (negotiated solution vs. retaliation); 
▪ A broad scope that encompasses foreign laws including “disguised coercion”; 
▪ Clear priority given to negotiations with the third country – prior to its use; 
▪ A raft of strong, impactful, diversified and non-protectionist measures; 
▪ A two-step response by the Commission (DG TRADE):  
1. Deployment of non-interventionist measures (negotiations) 
2. If these are unsuccessful: graduated, modular and effective countermeasures that 
seek to limit collateral costs for EU economic operators. The Procedure includes the 
capacity for the European Council and the European Parliament to object within a 
period of two months. 
 
● However, MEDEF considers that the text could be improved: the scope covered could be broader, 
and consideration could be given to  a compensation mechanism , or  a compensation fund , for 
European companies impacted by the ACI.  
 
● MEDEF intends to exercise particular vigilance in terms of this instrument, since its application has 
numerous implications. It is a question here of finding an appropriate balance between being able to 
put an end to measures of economic coercion and controlling the level of risk associated with its use. 
 
● Together with related regulations such as the anti-subsidy regulation, the International Procurement 
Instrument (IPI), the EU foreign investment screening mechanism or revision of Regula tion 2271/1996 
against extraterritorial sanctions (known as the “Blocking Regulation”), the ACI could be subject to 
specific, cross -cutting, political and technical governance within the Commission. This structure was 
referred to in preliminary work as the “EU resilience Office”. Medef remains convinced of the value of 
oversight involving several European Commission Directorates General, to gather information on acts of 
economic coercion, assist companies and coordinate a European response using these tools. The 
establishment of such a structure, even in a development phase, should be reconsidered. 
Ways to improve the instrument 
▪ Provide a compensation mechanism for EU companies affected by the ACI. 
▪ Implement explicit articulation with the EU Blocking Regulation (2271/1996).

▪ Include coercion and threats against companies / business sectors  within the scope  of the 
instrument. 
▪ Include restrictions on access to a foreign market within the scope of the instrument. 
▪ Include extraterritorial sanctions within the scope of the instrument. 
▪ The instrument could cover retaliation by a third country in response to EU countermeasures. 
▪ Specify the conditions for attributing economic coercion to a country’s actions: 
➔  How are cyber attacks viewed? Or refusals to issue export licenses? 
➔ What about direct or indirect pressures on companies (e.g. Lithuania vs. China)? 
 
3. MEDEF detailed recommendations concerning the Anti-Coercion Instrument 
Article 1 – It should be specified in Article 1 that economic coercion is also exercised by applying (or 
threatening to apply) measures affecting trade or investment (similar to the wording used in Article 2). 
This article should specify that the instrument aims to protect the interests of the Union and its 
Member States, including the economic interests of operators. Moreover, it should make it possible 
to defend the legitimate sovereign choices of the Union or a Member State. 
Consequently, we suggest amending Article 1 as follows:  
“This Regulation lays down rules and procedures in order to ensure the effective protection of the 
interests of the Union and its Member States, where a third country seeks to coerce the Union or a 
Member State into adopting or refraining from adopting a particular act or into ultimately taking a 
sovereign decision [or] impacting gravely its strategic interests, through measures affe cting trade or 
investment including measures targeting EU economic operators  in the conduct of their business 
activities.” 
 
Article 2 – In line with the amendment to Article 1, we suggest the scope of the rules as defined in Article 
2 be as follows:  
“This Regulation applies where a third country interferes in the legitimate sovereign choices of the Union 
or a Member State, for instance by applying or threatening to apply measures affecting trade or 
investment, or the strategic interests of the Union and its Member States.” 
The regulation should also specify in Article 2 that by “third country” it also understands all physical and 
legal persons representing or owned by the said “third country”. This would make it possible to include, 
in particular, action taken by third country State-owned enterprises, the administrative services of the 
third country exercising the coercion (including customs services and intelligence services), including 
when these are controlled from other countries and are acting from within a separate geographical area 
to the sovereign territory from which the coercive action is being exercised.  
Paragraph 2 – (d) During the process to determine the act of economic coercion, the legislator should 
pay particular attent ion to abuse of the national security exception provided for in  Article XXI of the 
GATT Agreement. Recourse to Article XXI of the GATT Agreement by a third country or the initiation of 
a WTO procedure should not prevent the Anti -Coercion Instrument being triggered, particularly when 
this recourse turns out to be abusive.

Article 3 – The procedure for reviewing coercive measures taken by a third country should be swift (a 
few weeks at the most) , in order to limit the impact of the coercion on economic operators by quickly 
exerting a deterrent effect on the third country . The Commission should receive complaints and alerts 
from economic operators via secure channels, which would also enable reliable feedback from Member 
States, businesses and employer’s organisations.  
➢ Hence, wording similar to the following could be introduced here: “A Member State 
or an interested party affected by a potential act of coercion , in accordance with 
Article 2 paragraph 1, can notify the Commission of all releva nt information. The 
Commission shall duly take into account the information reported and may start the 
review of the measures reported. In such a case, the Commission shall inform in good 
time the Member States and the interested party about its decision to initiate or not 
a review”. 
Article 4  – The procedure for cooperation with the third country is fundamental. It should make it 
possible to end the economic coercion as soon as possible, without resorting to countermeasures likely 
to cause an escalation. The possibility of the European Commission inviting the third country to submit 
observations should not enable it to “stall for time” , particularly in cases where there is obvious bad 
faith on the part of the foreign party. Speed and flexibility are crucial; the period between notification 
of the third country and the adoption of the delegated act enabling countermeasures, should remain 
within a reasonable period which is not fixed in advance . Furthermore, the European Council should 
be informed in real time of Commission action falling within the framework of this procedure, in 
particular in order to determine the strategy to be adopted.  
Article 5 – The Commission must indeed be able to enter into direct negotiations with the third country 
and be able to obtain cessation of the economic coercion by filing a request / filing a procedure within 
any competent international organi sation. The filing of an international procedure must not unduly 
delay the process or prevent the initiation of the countermeasures provided under the instrument.  
Article 6  – MEDEF strongly supports t he possibility for the Commission to enter into international 
cooperation with other countries affected by the economic coercion  exercised by  a third country. 
Whenever appropriate, the Commission should involve the other EU Member State(s) affected by the 
act of economic coercion.   
Article 7 – Paragraph 1 – The Commission should be able to adopt measures by engaging all the legal 
tools at its disposal, including the revised EU Blocking Regulation (2271/1996).  
Paragraph 7 – (b) MEDEF strongly supports maintaining the proposed wording, making it 
possible to provide relief for  economic operators affected by the measures of economic 
coercion. It might be appropriate here to refer to a compensation mechanism, notably to 
strengthen adhesion of the economic fabric to the instrument ’s application in the event of 
coercion.  
Article 8 – MEDEF is in favour of all natural or legal persons that facilitate or enable third countries’ acts 
of e conomic coercion being subject to sanctions and recovery measures , on behalf of European 
economic operators harmed not only by the measure of economic  coercion itself but also by the 
effects of EU countermeasures. All natural or legal persons directly or indirectly controlled by the third 
country, including when they act on behalf of the third country outside its territory, should also be 
targeted by the EU.  
In addition, all natural or legal persons should agree to cooperate with the Commission within the 
framework of the anti-coercion procedure. In the event of a refusal, or of fraudulent cooperation

(transmission of false information, abuse of procedure, act of bad faith, etc.), they would be presumed 
to have unduly participated in the economic coercion. Consideration should be given to including these 
persons on the list of entities and persons subject to EU international sanctions. 
➔ Paragraph 2 – We suggest amending the text as follows: “The Commission may designate 
a natural or legal person where it finds (a) that such person is linked to the government 
of the third country concerned; or, (b) that such person has caused or been involved in or 
connected with the economic coercion .” Notably for the purpose of sufficiently 
encompassing actors allowing (or having allowed) the economic coercion. 
 
Article 9 – This is first and foremost a deterrent; the EU response should be commensurate with but not 
greater than the injury suffered. A graduated response should be preferred, capable of putting an end 
to the economic coercion without fuelling an escalation , and taking into account the im pact and 
specificity of certain sectors of activity that may be affected. Limiting the negative impacts on the sectors 
concerned is a major issue, as is anticipating various reprisals from the third country targeted by the 
Anti-Coercion Instrument, which certain sectors of activity may be subject to.  
Article 10 – Paragraph 4(d) – The phrase “where it is appropriate in light of the Union’s Interest” needs 
to be clarified, as it implies that the EU could lift its countermeasures without obtaining cessation of the 
coercion, without a negotiated solution or a decision by a third party capable of settling the case of 
economic coercion.  
➔ A lifting of the counterme asures, leading in some way to an “acceptance” of the 
economic coercion, should therefore give rise to compensation for the injured EU 
economic operators: the EU having characterised the injury, but having withdrawn its 
countermeasures without putting an end to the injury.  
Article 11 – MEDEF strongly supports paragraph 3, which provides for  the consultation of European 
industry associations  affected by the envisaged countermeasures. The most representative 
organisations should be systematically consulted as part of discussions on triggering the instrument. This 
consultation could be in writing and then oral, as in the United States ; it should provide feedback for 
companies as part of a framework for monitored/constant and ongoing dialogue. 
Paragraph 4 – The possibility for the Commission to be given inspection powers in the EU, 
notably in order to access information held by natural or legal persons having initiated or 
enabled economic coercion, should be considered.    
Article 12 – MEDEF believes that confidentiality and the protection of exchanges with the authorities is 
essential and necessary for ensuring  the proper functioning of the Anti-Coercion Instrument. Any 
disclosure of information should be subject to sanctions, as provided in the  European directive on the 
protection of trade secrets for example.   
Article 13 – With regard to Annex II, referred to in this article: the Commission, as part of its efforts to 
determine the origin and nationality of the instigator(s) or facilitators of the economic coercion, should 
take into account the cases of direct or indirect “cross-investments and property” for application of the 
50% rule: if two natural or legal persons separately hold 25% of a legal entity or an investment security, 
this legal en tity or this investment should be considered  as being “ controlled” or “owned” by such 
persons. This approach would allow the Commission to gain a better insight into/deal with  cases of 
economic coercion with multiple geographical origins.

Article 14 – Operation based on delegated acts should enable the Commission to move forward quickly 
when a case of coercion is observed. Cross-checking by the European Parliament and the European 
Council, within two months of the adoption of the delegated act, enables a better grasp of the foreign 
policy effects resulting from the Anti -Coercion Instrument  which remains, fundamentally, a tool 
comparable to other trade defence instruments.  
Articles 15 and 16 – No comments at this stage.  
 
Suggested introduction of an additional article  
The Anti-Coercion Instrument could provide for an emergency procedure, for prevention purposes. 
As soon as  the Commission becomes aware of a major risk to the interes ts of the Union, it should be 
able to trigger the mediation procedure provided in Article 5, as well as the cooperation procedure 
provided in Article 6. Thus, in the event that mediation fails, the Commission would have the ability to 
initiate the raft of measures provided in Annex 1, and immediately apply these measures on Day 1 of 
the act of economic coercion. 
These measures could be lifted within seven days at the request of the European Parliament or the 
European Council. Within the framework of the emergency procedure, the Commission would engage 
in negotiations with the third country exercising the economic coercion, prior to the application of  
countermeasures, notably in order to benefit from leverage to avoid this economic coercion.

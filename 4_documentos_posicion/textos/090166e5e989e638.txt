Bayer contribution to the Call for Evidence for an impact 
assessment for an EU Soil Health Law 
 
11 March 2022 
 
We welcome the possibility the European Commission offers to provide input in the Call for Evidence for 
an impact assessment for an EU Soil Health Law. Bayer shares the Commission’s view in the EU Soil 
Strategy for 2030 and the Call for Evidence that soil health is important. Soil provides the basis of our 
production systems and healthy soils do not only benefit the production of goods and services but 
deliver co-benefits such as climate change mitigation, adaptation and biodiversity conservation. 
Managing soils sustainably, restoring degraded soils and remediating contaminated sites to bring all soil 
ecosystems in a healthy state by 2050 is a vision we support.   
 
When it comes to agriculture it is our experience that soil health is top of mind for farmers. Soil is a 
crucial factor in farm productivity, affecting both the quality and quantity of farm produce. Managing 
soil health is a challenging exercise influenced by soil type, climatic conditions, market realities, soil 
ownership, and the regulatory environment. Concrete challenges for farmers are nutrient, pest, disease, 
and weed management and prevention of soil degradation due to erosion, compaction and salinisation. 
Farmers need to be able to address these challenges. 
 
Bayer provides farmers with tools, practices, and knowledge they can use to create integrated crop 
management (ICM) systems that provide in the social, environmental, and economic sustainability needs 
of society. These tools and practices concern seeds, pesticides, digital solutions and any combinations of 
these with additional tools and practices (tailored solutions). Bayer services all farmers, from 
conventional to organic, including regenerative, circular, conservation, agro-ecological, protected and  
agroforestry farming.  
 
Soil health is a key consideration when we develop products for farmers. Pesticides are thoroughly 
tested for potential effects on soil biodiversity and health, seeds are developed for performance under 
different soil conditions and digital solutions offer new opportunities for farmers to manage soil health. 
The tailored solutions we work on, such as integrated weed management (IWM) and carbon farming, 
have soil management at their core. We support farmers in the responsible use of our products. We are 
committed to supporting farmers in reducing their greenhouse gas emissions and to reducing the 
environmental impact of our pesticides by 30% in 2030. Pollution prevention is the standard for our 
production locations and we remediate soils that were contaminated in the past1.  
 
We share the Commission’s analysis on the importance of technological drivers. Innovation is key when 
it comes to soil health. Nurturing established and stimulating new innovations is essential to maintain 
and increase the sustainability of farming and other production systems, including sustainable soil 
management. Developments in digital farming, genome editing and pesticide innovations look 
promising. Farmers need access to the necessary tools and practices, both established and new, to 
maintain and improve the ICM systems that deliver the soil health and sustainability performance 
Europe seeks.

Bayer would like to contribute the following considerations to the work of the European Commission: 
▪ Support farmers 
A soil health law should support farmers to manage their soils in a sustainable way. It should 
address farmers’ challenges with regards to soil management and farm sustainability performance. 
An effective soil health law should strive to ease regulatory and administrative burden for farmers, 
enable their access to the necessary tools and practices for sustainable soil management, ensure 
adequate training and advice, and incentivise farmers to deliver on soil health and farm 
sustainability.  
 
▪ Link soil health to farm sustainability performance 
Farmers are the stewards of the soils they farm. Farmers implement soil health practices to increase 
soil and farm sustainability performance. Soils in farming are a non-renewable resource that needs 
protection, but also a means to deliver sustainability results. Soil health provisions for farming 
should therefore cover both soil health and farm sustainability. For all soils, the setting of clear and 
measurable soil health indicators and their range of values should be aligned with social, 
environmental and economic sustainability indicators. It should be avoided that soil health 
indicators and their management negatively impact the sustainability performance of farming.  
 
Within the farm sustainability framework, the indicators for soil health should at least capture 
erosion, soil organic carbon, nutrient availability, acidification, compaction, salinisation, 
contamination, and soil (ecosystem) services.2 
 
▪ Go for impact 
A Soil Health Law should be result-based. Farmers are, with access to the right incentives, tools and 
practices, and knowledge and advice, very well capable of soil management that delivers the social, 
environmental, and economic sustainability needs of society. Choices in soil management differ 
from farm to farm due to farm structure, soil type, climatic conditions, markets, and the farmers’ 
specific landscape and communities context. A Soil Health Law should therefore refrain from setting 
specific action-based requirements for the sustainable management of soils. It can set or regulate 
the setting of goals, targets, soil health indicators, and their range of values, but should leave the 
practice of ICM that delivers on these to the farmer. Moreover, a results based approach will ensure 
inclusivity towards all farmers, the tools and practices they use, and the systems they farm, 
capturing the contribution of these to farm sustainability performance.  
 
▪ Harmonise across the EU  
There is value in the harmonisation of EU soil policy when it comes to the Soil Strategy objectives for 
soil health indicators and criteria for monitoring and reporting. The development of innovative soil 
health practices and the implementation at scale could benefit from harmonisation of these basic 
soil health management requirements. Harmonisation should however recognise that many soil 
health issues are location specific and need local flexibility for effective management. 
 
▪ Avoid regulatory and policy overlap 
The current EU regulatory framework addresses or touches upon soil health in several directives,

Take for instance the pesticide regulatory framework. This framework already addresses soil health 
effects of pesticides and their sustainable use. Current risk assessments for pesticides have been 
developed to be protective of potential long-term impacts on the structure and function of soil 
communities and addresses protection of soil services (e.g., decomposition and cycling of organic 
matter and nutrients). Taxa that are tested in chronic studies include detritivores (e.g., earthworms), 
predatory soil organisms, and soil micro-organisms responsible for nutrient cycling. In addition, 
exposure is comprehensively evaluated by assessing the environmental fate of crop protection 
products in soil (i.e., the potential for persistence and accumulation, movement, photolysis).3,4,5  
Currently, the existing EU pesticide legislation is the most comprehensive testing and assessment 
framework to achieve protection of soil organism communities in the world. 
 
▪ Manage the key aspects of soil health 
Soil health is multidimensional consisting of biotic and abiotic aspects. Challenges to soil health are 
generally erosion67, compaction, salinization, sodification and contamination8, the latter with a 
special focus on heavy metals and other non-degradable compounds. It is difficult to define specific 
metrics for healthy soils due to the high variability of soils, but also because of the different 
geographies and uses of soils which themselves have created different soil conditions. In agriculture 
for instance strong differences exist between e.g. arable and grassland field soils concerning the 
composition and abundance of soil inhabiting invertebrate species. Agricultural use requires a 
different focus on soil health properties than an industrial, residential or nature conservation use. 
Moreover, both chemical as well as biological soil monitoring to assess soil health is complex and 
costly. A Soil Health Law should manage these key aspects of soil health, for instance when it comes 
to creating a soil health certificate for land transactions. A science and risk- based monitoring and 
certification strategy could help to manage this complexity.

▪ Enable knowledge and innovation 
Knowledge and innovation are key to protect, conserve and restore soils. A Soil Health Law should 
enable knowledge and innovation. When it comes to understanding soil health there are still 
important knowledge gaps on what determines a healthy soil and the effects of many soil 
management practices thereabout. A Soil Health Law should avoid new regulatory hurdles for 
research, development and implementation of soil health tools and practices and help streamline 
existing policy and regulation. To do so it should be science-based. This is especially true for setting 
of soil health indicators, their range of values and the monitoring and reporting of soil condition as 
mentioned by the Commission.  
 
As interesting example of development of soil protective practices in agriculture is adoption of 
conservation tillage9. Environmental benefits of conservation tillage primarily include increased 
carbon sequestration, soil and water conservation, improved functional soil biodiversity, decreased 
soil erosion, improved water quality, and habitat provision for birds and small mammals.  
 
▪ Link to Carbon Farming 
In the Soil Health Strategy for 2030 and the Communication on sustainable carbon cycles the 
Commission acknowledged the importance of soils for climate mitigation and adaptation. We share 
the belief of the Commission that carbon farming can contribute significantly to the EU’s efforts to 
tackle climate change, but also to bring co-benefits beyond this goal such as the restoration and 
protection of soils. Please refer to the Bayer submission on the Roadmap for restoring sustainable 
carbon cycles of 6 October 2021. A Soil Health Law should be aligned with the regulatory action the 
Commission is preparing withs regard to the certification of carbon removals.  
 
▪ Integrate risk-based approach to contaminated site remediation 
In the Soil Health Strategy for 2030 and the Communication on restoration of degraded soil and 
remediating contaminated sites the Commission acknowledged that all Member States should 
identify and maintain a register for contaminated sites, assess the risks and finally remediate these 
sites in case of unacceptable risks. We share this view, but would also like to bring to attention the 
following aspects: 
 
• A contaminated site can still be used for specific purposes (e.g., for industrial use) if the risks 
associated with the contamination are contained. In this case, the site does not necessarily need 
to be fully remediated. 
• Establishing regulations and threshold limits for chemicals must be based on a risk approach, 
considering the toxicological characteristics and complex behaviors of chemicals in different 
environmental compartments.  
• Remedial actions also need to be assesse d on a risk-based approach taking into consideration 
the source of the contamination, pathways of exposure, and the environmental receptors. 
Based on this data different remedial options, if possible, need to be established and assessed, 
considering the costs as well as the feasibility of applying the different options and the related 
potential for a significant risk reduction.

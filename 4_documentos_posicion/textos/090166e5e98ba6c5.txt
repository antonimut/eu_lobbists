Feedback on the European Commission’s proposal to recast the EU Energy 
Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD) 
VELUX A/S welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on the proposed revision of the Energy Performance 
of Buildings Directive (EPBD). An ambitious EPBD revision, which will enable the green transition of Europe’s 
building stock and create healthier living conditions for all Europeans, is an essential part of the EU achieving 
its aims of becoming climate neutral by 2050 and meeting the 2030 CO2 reduction target. 
To ensure a coherent and well-functioning policy framework for buildings and thus the overall energy system, 
a close alignment with other relevant files from the Fit for 55 Package will be crucial, particularly the Energy 
Efficiency Directive (EED), the ETS for buildings, the Effort Sharing Regulation and the Renewable Energy 
Directive (RED). It is also essential to take the ‘Energy Efficiency First’ principle as the starting point : the less 
energy we consume, the less we need to produce, hence allowing for the cheapest, fastest and most efficient 
transition. 
Seeing that energy renovation of buildings is a key European flagship for national recovery plans, the European 
Commission has already – and rightly so – underlined the importance of this sector in terms of creating jobs, 
growth and moving forward with  the green tr ansition. It is now critical that the EPBD sets an ambitious 
framework to ensure the vast sums invested into renovating the building stock will lead to energy efficient , 
sustainable and healthy buildings. But this means changing the way we look at buildings today, moving beyond 
energy performance requirements, to addressing all relevant building parameters, i.e. energy, environment 
and health: The EPBD revision offers a unique opportunity to do so by broadening its scope and including a 
stronger focus on CO2 emissions and health aspects alongside energy performance. 
For the upcoming inter-institutional negotiations, we therefore call for: 
1) A more ambitious definition of zero-emission buildings to address scope 1, 2 & 3 emissions from buildings; 
2) Faster roll-out to calculate life-cycle assessments (LCA) for all new buildings by 2025 and mandatory CO2 
thresholds based on LCA by 2030; 
3) A definition of a  ‘healthy indoor climat e’, which should be factored in the  zero-emission buildings 
definition and in the design and roll-out of Energy Performance Certificates and Renovation Passports; 
4) Better factoring in the benefits of ‘passive building systems’ to improve the energy performance of 
buildings and optimise indoor climate parameters; and 
5) Incorporating direct health externalities into the new methodology for cost-optimal levels & widening of 
the scope of ‘deep renovations’ beyond energy performance. 
Stronger provisions on operational and embodied CO2 emissions in buildings 
Since its introduction the concept of nearly Zero Energy Buildings (nZEB ) has been a key instrument of the 
EPBD to improve the energy performance of new buildings. VELUX supports the Commission’s proposal to 
broaden the scope of the EPBD by introducing a new carbon emission reduction dimension via a new definition 
for zero-emission buildings (ZEB) and the calculation of life-cycle Global Warming Potential of new buildings. 
In our view the proposed definition for ZEBs does not sufficiently address CO2 emissions specifically, however, 
it primarily re-affirms the need to limit the energy consumption of buildings and to supply them with 
renewable energy. The focus thus remains on requirements for operational ZEBs. 
The ambitious energy reduction targets for nZEBs mean that we have now reached a tipping point in Europe 
where the environmental load of construction materials for new buildings is comparable to the CO2 emissions 
associated with the e nergy consumption of buildings.  It is therefore crucial to start regulating the 
environmental load of new buildings, not just for the use phase but also for the embodied CO2, by introducing 
mandatory life-cycle assessments (LCA) for all new buildings as soon as possible.

All buildings account for 37% of global CO2 emissions, with 27% stemming from heating and cooling in the 
use phase and the remaining 10% from construction materials1. But when looking at emissions from new 
builds only, there is a rather different picture: Danish numbers show that the nZEB ambition has successfully 
led to a reduction of CO2 emissions from the use phase, representing now only ~25% of the total CO2 
emissions from the building stock. The remaining ~75% come from the sourcing and manufacturing of 
materials, transport and construction work, maintenance as well as end of life and reuse processes2. This 
shows the urgency to set requirements on the buildings’ life-cycle.  
However, the current proposal will not generalize the use of LCA s until 2030, de facto preventing efficient 
target setting until 2033 -2035 (based on experience from Member States where LCA and associated CO2 
thresholds were introduced). This is not in line with recommendations from among others IEA and UNEP 
Global Build ing Alliance, which request mandatory zero‐carbon‐ready building energy codes for all new 
buildings by 2030 to meet the Paris Agreement. They also state that such energy codes should cover building 
operations (scope 1 and 2) as well as emissions from the manufacturing of building construction materials and 
components (scope 3 or embodied carbon emissions)3. 
The proposal therefore creates a major barrier towards meeting the EU’s  2030 and 2050 climate goals and 
thus needs addressing. We therefore propose to: 
o Revise the definition of ZEBs in line with the IEA definition of zero-carbon ready buildings, covering 
Scope 1, 2 and 3 emissions for buildings. All sources of renewable energy should also be allowed to 
cover the low energy demand of ZEBs (incl. on-site, nearby and grid renewables). The focus of an 
ambitious ZEB definition should remain on the building level; 
o Introduce a more ambitious timeline to apply the ZEBs definition and calculate LCA  for all new 
buildings by 2025; and 
o Introduce mandatory CO2 thresholds based on LCA for all new buildings and request Member States 
to develop a clear timeline for the rollout of such thresholds between 2025-2030. 
A sustainable and healthy European building stock for all Europeans 
Human health is one of the most important co -benefits of energy efficiency, as identified by the  recent 
recommendations by the European Commission on the Energy Efficiency First Principle 4. At the same time, 
there is a distinct lack of available data and methodologies to assess the wider benefits of energy efficiency 
improvements. This limits the possibility to quantify these benefits and to ensure an appropriate cost-benefits 
analysis. The new building tools to be introduced by the EPBD revision, i.e. Energy Performance Certificates 
and Renovation Passports will provide a good basis to gather and build up data and better track progress  in 
this regard. 
After all, people spend up to 90% of their time indoors and many existing European buildings suffer from poor 
daylight and indoor climate with adverse effect on health, well -being and productivity. Many Europeans are 
currently living in unhealthy buildings (1 out of 6 Europeans and 1 out of 3 European children5) - an issue the 
EPBD revision must tackle  by better factoring in  Indoor Environmental Quality (IEQ) indicators. Such co-
benefits also represent important incentives for  consumers to  undertake deep, or staged deep,  energy 
renovation projects6. 
The long-term performance of our buildings will rely on their resilience , but also their  ability to match the 
residents’ or users’ needs and expectations . A building should not just perform well on its own  in terms of 
energy efficiency . It should also provide a healthy and comfortable living and working  environment. We

We believe there is scope to go further to ensure that zero-emission buildings are also healthy by: 
• Introducing a ‘healthy indoor climate’ definition in Article 2: “Healthy indoor climate” is mentioned 
several times in the EPBD recast but is not captured within the 57 definitions. It is important to address 
this to  ensure a common understanding of what this term entails , not least  in light of the new 
provisions on IAQ in the proposal . It should be noted in this context that IAQ is  only one of many 
aspects that constitute a ‘healthy indoor climate’. Such a definition should therefore include essential 
parameters as described in the EPB Standard EN 16798 -1 for achieving an indoor environment that 
enables health, comfort and well -being of occupants : daylight, indoor air quality, thermal comfort 
(especially overheating mitigation and reduction) and acoustics7. 
• Factoring in a definition of ‘healthy indoor climate’ within the scope of ZEBs, nZEBs, EPCs and 
Renovation Passports: 
o The focus of the EPBD so far has primarily been on improving the energy performance of new 
buildings with little attention paid  to systematically improving the indoor climate. It follows 
that existing rules have led to the prioritization of energy aspects over health and comfort. 
This needs to be addressed with ensuring that ‘healthy indoor climate’ is included as part of 
the ‘zero-emission building’ definition in Article 2. 
o Indoor climate improvements represent a strong incentive for building owners to conduct 
energy renovations. This must be better taken into consideration  in the design and 
implementation of EPCs – and to a certain extent Renovation Passports. We therefore call for 
the introduction of the indicators listed in the ‘healthy indoor climate’ definition (see above) 
into the list of mandatory indicators to be present on EPC documents in Annex V. 
• Better factoring in the benefits of ‘passive systems’ : In addition to specific recommendations on 
heating and cooling systems, article  16 on EPCs should also include recommendations on how to 
improve the energy performance and optimi se indoor climate parameters through passive building  
systems i.e. design principles or building elements, which require little or no assistance from an energy 
source. Recital 52 highlights  passive systems but more emphasis on their benefits is needed when 
considering renovation.  
o We therefore call for including a definition of passive systems in Article 2 and to better 
address passive elements such as, solar sha ding, daylight and energy gains within the EPC 
template in Annex V8.

About the VELUX Group 
For more than 75 years, the VELUX Group has created better living environments for people around the world; 
making the most of daylight and fresh air through the roof. Our product programme includes roof windows 
and modular skylights, decorative blinds, sun screening products and roller shutters, as well as installation and 
smart home solutions. These products help to ensure a healthy and sustainable indoor climate, for work and 
learning, for play and pleasure. In 2020, we launched our 2030 Sustainability Strategy , our 10 -year 
transformational journey in which we pioneer climat e and nature action, innovate sustainable products and 
secure a responsible business. By 2030, we are committed to become a 100 percent carbon neutral company 
(scope 1 and 2) and halve the carbon emissions across our value chain - suppliers included - (scope 3). We 
work globally – with sales and manufacturing operations in more than 40 countries and around 11,500 
employees worldwide. The VELUX Group is owned by VKR Holding A/S, a limited company wholly owned by 
non-profit, charitable foundations (THE VELUX FOUNDATIONS) and family. For more, information, visit 
www.velux.com.

Hydrogen Denmark’s response to the consultation 
on the review of emission standards for heavy-duty 
vehicles. 
Hydrogen Denmark ( Brintbranchen) would  first and foremost  like to thank the 
European Commission for the opportunity to provide feedback on this topic. 
Hydrogen has a key role in the green economy, especially in the harder -to-
decarbonise sectors such as heavy road transport . At the same time, hydrogen 
production via electrolysis supports the optimisation of the energy system of the 
future. We therefore welcome the review of the emission standards for heavy -duty 
vehicles. 
Hydrogen Denmark is the national association for hydrogen and Power -to-X and  
represents more than 30 members across the entire value chain: from technology 
manufacturers to energy producers and consumers, as well as  hubs, knowledge 
centres and consultants in the field. 
In the below we present an overview of the status of the hydrogen-based technologies 
needed to decarbonise heavy -duty road transport and the relevant costs, as well as 
our view on the timing to reach an entirely green road transport sector. 
 
Technology development trends indicate that (much) higher ambition is 
possible. 
While direct electrification is in m any cases the most energy -efficient solution for 
decarbonising road transport, it is not always a logistically or technically viable option. 
This is where hydrogen and other hydrogen -based fuels come in: clean vehicles 
running on Power -to-X fuels offer longer  driving ranges, more flexibility in cases 
where electricity grid connection is not possible or practicable (e.g. remote or off-road 
locations and sites under development) , higher payload weights, and shorter 
refuelling times than for fully electric vehicl es. Direct and indirect electrification will 
therefore complement each other in decarbonising heavy road transport.

Clean vehicles that run on  clean e-fuels are already on the road, and more are being 
developed and tested. In Table 1 and Table 2 further below we show an overview of  
some of the  heavy-duty hydrogen and methanol  fuel-cell trucks and buses 
(respectively) that are most advanced towards commercialisation (including some 
that are already on the road). The vehicles presented in these tables are the ones for 
which there is public information available, but our members report of several other 
vehicle manufacturers that are also working on e-fuel trucks and buses but are yet to 
announce them . Similar developments are beginning to flourish in other truck 
segments, as well as buses and coaches.  For example, the International Energy 
Agency, in its report The Future of Hydrogen (2019) identified that “globally at least 
11 companies currently manufacture fuel cell electric buses”.

 In any case, a decarbonised heavy road 
transport ecosystem will consist of a

Hence, the target must be able to 
include technology options that run on 
renewable fuels, also when they include

but a gCO2/km approach will continue

forward. For example, in Denmark we 
also have an e-methanol truck  on the 
road, a carbon-neutral technology (when using biogenic CO2 or DAC) that paves the 
way for decarbonising other transport segments such as shipping and aviation. 
Refuelling technology is also catching up with the developments on the clean vehicle 
market. Current hydrogen refuelling technology offers 350 bar pressure, but next 
generation refuelling modules for 700 bar are well underway. The expected time -to-
market of these refuelling modules is 2024 /2025. Consequently, vehicle 
manufacturers are also developing trucks that can tank at 700 bar, which will 
significantly increase the driving ranges of new vehicles  by potentially 40 -50%. 
Ongoing work  on refuelling with liquid hydrogen  for trucks  will also significantly 
increase the driving ranges.

Hydrogen will soon be competitive in some heavy-duty applications, but policies 
that bring up scale are key to realise the cost-out. 
As the vast majority of studies on the cost of hydrogen  stress (from production to 
consumption in specific application), the key to costing out e-fuels is scale. Ambitious 
mandates, such as the CO2 standards for heavy -duty vehicles, are essential to push  
the scale of hardware production upwards, and thereby pushing costs down. 
McKinsey & Co. found in a report for the Hydrogen Council (20 20) that the learning 
rate for fuel cell stacks for commercial vehicles (over total cumulative production) is 
around 11%. Together with the impact of scale on hydrogen production, distribution 
and utilisation, the total costs of hydrogen in heavy -duty transport are expected to 
drop by 50% between 2020 and 2 030. With this, hydrogen c ould already be cost -
competitive with other low-carbon options for heavy road transport before 2025, and 
with conventional options by 2030. Roland Berger (2020) also expects fuel-cell heavy-
duty trucks to be competitive with diese l from 2027, in a report for the Fuel Cell and 
Hydrogen 2 Joint Undertaking. 
While CO2 emission standards for heavy -duty vehicles directly support the cos t-out 
journey of the vehicles themselves, they also drive demand for hydrogen and fuelling 
infrastructure, thereby reducing the total cost of ownership in a wider sense. For 
example, in the case of hydrogen refuelling stations, even if the technology did not 
decrease in cost (which is  in any case  hugely improbable), higher utilisation rates of 
the stations would still result in lower cost of the hydrogen delivered at the pump. In 
this sense, an ambitious target has the power to drive down costs and stimulate 
decarbonisation across the entire value chain for heavy-duty vehicles. 
The cost of operating a fleet is , however, not exclusively related to the cost of 
ownership of the individual vehicle. Operators must also consider the refuelling times, 
the driving range, the load that vehicle can carry, vehicle performance under different 
climate conditions, etc. Therefore, it is important that targets for decarbonising 
transport allow for a variety of solutions, even when these are not necessarily the 
most obvious solution from a single -parameter persp ective (be it cost efficiency, 
energy efficiency or anything else). 
 
Hydrogen Denmark views an entirely green heavy road transport sector by 2035 
as a realistic target, with a positive impact on market development and cost 
reduction. 
As indicated in the previous section s, an ambitious target for CO2 emissions 
standards for vehicles , by creating scale,  drives cost reductions beyond the vehicles 
themselves. The technology is well underway and in a path to cost -competitiveness 
before 2030. In line with the level of ambition already proposed for light-duty vehicles,

Hydrogen Denmark believes that heavy duty road transport could be entirely  green 
by 2035.  
Specifically, we would encourage the Commission to propose a  0 gCO2/km limit for 
long-haul lorries by 2035 and expanding the scope of the regulation  to urban buses, 
coaches, and urban and regional delivery lorries  with a 0 gCO2/km limit already  by 
2030. At this level , the target  should be achieved through renewable fuels only , 
including renewable hydrocarbons based on climate-neutral CO2 (biogenic and direct 
air capture).

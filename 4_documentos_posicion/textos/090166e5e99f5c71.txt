Soil health – protecting, sustainably managing, and    
restoring EU soils 
Humankind has largely lived beyond the biocapacity of its planet. The use of natural 
resources such as water and soil exceeds the earth's regenerative capacity and we 
already experience the effects such as climate change, species extinction and the 
scarcity of natural resources. The rising world population exacerbates this situation. 
 
The consumption needs of a growing and more demanding world population have led 
to changes in our ways of land use and management in recent decades. To meet the 
needs of humanity, agricultural yields have had to be greatly increased. This has been 
accompanied by the ever-increasing use of artificial fertilizers, chemical pesticides, 
machinery and technical processes, which results in devastating consequences for the 
ecosystem. Biodiversity is decreasing, pesticide residues in soils and waters are 
ubiquitous, and the ecological balance is disturbed. A rapid change of land 
management is crucial.  
 
With its pioneering technologies and methods, the bioeconomy offers sustainable use 
and, at the same time, conservation of natural resources such as soil. The bioeconomy 
focuses on the development and convergence, i.e., integration of biological, 
biotechnological and digital innovations. This includes the recovery of valuable and 
important nutrients as well as the use of fertile soils. To use and preserve soil as a 
resource in a sustainable manner, EU-wide quality standards for healthy soils must be 
established. In addition, strategies for recording current and potential threats to natural 
soil functions due to anthropogenic influences must be further developed. Ecological 
monitoring is an important component in determining the ecological status of a soil: 
the structure of biotic communities and the abundance of individual species is a good 
indicator of current but also past pressures on a site. It is also important to address the 
aspect of bioavailability, for example, by analytically recording bioavailable fractions of 
substances. The development of valid and easy-to-use biomarkers and bioassays is 
essential for monitoring soil quality. The scientific development of technologies and 
products for soil analysis, which have been developed in close cooperation with 
farmers, should be promoted in a targeted manner to put them into practice in a ti mely 
manner. 
 
Especially in today's world, resilience to crisis events is essential to safeguard 
agricultural systems and food production. The development of sustainable resource-
efficient high technologies and transformation processes with regard to climate change 
and sustainability aspects on farms must be supported and promoted. Changing 
climatic conditions as well as the use of pesticides influence the quality and operations 
of agricultural processes. An important tool to enable more sustainable processes is 
digitalization, for example with cloud or AI technologies. AI technologies in particular 
can help to make adaptation to changing conditions more efficient, accelerate it in all 
areas of agriculture, and thus make the overall ecosystem more agile and future-proof. 
Together, science, industry and agriculture need to develop systems that are practical 
to use. These should help farmers analyze plant and soil conditions on large areas of 
land, reorganize nutrient and crop protection processes such as irrigation, fertilization, 
and pest control, and thus ensure sufficient crop yields in terms of quality and quantity, 
reduce emissions, and preserve biodiversity.

is the conservation and restoration of our ecosystems. Desertification, largely man-

The measures for the protection of peatland soils already laid down in various national 
programs and the promotion of site-adapted and climate-friendly management set out 
in them must be further expanded at international level within the framework of the 
EU Soil Protection Strategy. To reduce greenhouse gas emissions, the rewetting of 
drained peatlands primarily used for agriculture is of crucial importance. To avoid a 
complete abandonment of use, it is necessary to adapt the use to the raised water 
levels and to establish alternative value chains for the biomass produced. Research and 
development in the areas of rewetting and paludiculture must continue to be 
supported and implemented on a model basis in the field.  
 
The Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft, Europe's leading organization for applied research, 
supports the goals and measures of the "EU Green Deal" and the "EU Soil Strategy". 
Fraunhofer's ambitious research agenda in the areas of bioeconomy as well as circular 
economy and resource efficiency benefits the EU’s ambitions to achieve a healthy state 
for all soils and to make sustainable use and restoration of soils the norm.

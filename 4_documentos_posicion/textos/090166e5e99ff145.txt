EDISON’S CONTRIBUTION TO THE PUBLIC CONSULTATION FOR THE REVIEW OF EMISSION STANDARDS 
FOR HEAVY-DUTY VEHICLES  
 
Edison welcomes the opportunity to share some preliminary views on the revision of Regulation (EU) 
2019/1242 which establishes the CO2 emission standards for heavy-duty vehicles (hereinafter ‘HDV 
Regulation’). 
 
As a leading energy company with more than 13 0 years of history and an ambitious and forward -looking 
strategy centered around innovation and sustainability, Edison has been very receptive to EU initiative aimed 
at decarbonizing the transport sector , with a proactive endeavor in developing enabling vi able and high 
impact solutions for alternative fuels. In particular, Edison is promoting and investing in solutions for the 
increase in the use of alternative fuels not only in the heavy-duty transport with the use of LNG and bio-LNG, 
but also in urban e-mobility and maritime transport. 
 
Last year Edison, together with Petrolifera Italo Rumena (PIR) and Enagas, launched the Ravenna small-scale 
LNG deposit. This project is based on a sustainable mobility development plan in the transport sector, both 
for heavy-duty and maritime transport, contributing to the reduction of CO2 and other emissions (as SOx, 
NOx and particulates).  In fact, the deposit has the capacity to handle more than 1 million cubic meters of 
liquefied gas a year, making LNG available in Italy to fuel at least 12,000 trucks and up to 48 ferries a year. 
We estimate that the project will avoid at least 6 million tons of CO2 emissions all over its life cycle.  
 
Furthermore, Edison is off-taking 110 Mcm of biomethane from GSE (Gestore Servizi Energetici) and 
supplying it to filling stations across Italy. Edison has developed a valuable partnership with the heavy vehicle 
manufacturing company IVECO to promote the use of LNG and CNG mobility, including the creation of new 
fleets running on  bio-LNG and biomethane. In the medium term, Edison will launch  new biomethane and 
bioLNG production plants to respond to the increasing clients’ demand of green gas  in the transport, retail 
and industrial sectors.  
 
Considerations on the HDV regulatory framework 
The HDV Regulation has had a solid impact in providing signals and mobilizing investments for manufacturers 
to decarbonize their heavy -duty transport fleets . Nevertheless, we urge to underline that the Regulation 
should be based on a framework which , while aiming at  decarbonizing the heavy -duty transport sector 
towards 2050 , also incentivizes the most cost -efficient and cost -effective technologies that are available 
today with the objective of cutting emissions immediately.  
 
It is fundamental that the revision of the HDV regulation continues to respect the principle of technology 
neutrality, not only as an objective but also in terms of concrete measures. Indeed, all energy vectors capable 
of reducing emissions should be favored relative to the status quo, which is dominated by oil products. The 
principle of technological neutrality is essential to deliver a cos t-effective energy transition and to avoid 
postponing decarbonization by waiting for breakthroughs in the lowest carbon options that might materialize 
much later than currently anticipated. Supporting the most cost-effective and ready-to-use technologies will 
lead to a speedy and substantial reduction in the sector’s CO2 footprint, while laying the ground for a deeper 
transformation once technologies that are not yet economically viable will be ready to be adopted at scale.  
 
As the recital (21) of the current HDV Regulation states: “ Liquefied natural gas (LNG) is an available 
alternative fuel to diesel for heavy -duty vehicles. The deployment of current and upcoming more innovative 
LNG-based technologies will contribute to meet ing the CO2 emissions reduction targets in the short and 
medium term as the use of LNG technologies leads to lower CO2 emissions when compared to diesel vehicles”.

Indeed, we believe the use of ready technologies, such as LNG, is certainly a solution that  guarantees a 
significant reduction in CO2 emissions and that contributes to the achievement of intermediate targets. It is 
hence fundamental that the value of these technologies in the decarbonization of the heavy-duty transport 
sector is reflected in the Regulation and that their reduction potential is counted for the purpose of vehicle 
manufacturers’ emission targets. Furthermore, technologies such as LNG and CNG do not create a carbon 
lock in effect. Renewable gases hold the same characteristics as natu ral gas, allowing gradual blending and 
enhancing the GHG reduction effects while relying on the same infrastructure. Indeed, LNG engines are 
technologically ready for bio-methane traction, which can reduce CO2 emissions by up to 90%, making them 
a valuable technology to contribute to the full decarbonization of the heavy-duty transport sector to 2050.  
 
Another important aspect that should be taken into account in the revision of the aforementioned Regulation 
is the potential of the different technological solutions to reduce other pollutant emissions (in addition to 
CO2). In fact, in addition to greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, we emphasize that heavy-duty vehicles are also 
responsible for substantial emissions of local pollutants (SOx, PM and NOx)  caused by the use of traditional 
fossil fuels. We feel that the scale of this problem is often overlooked in the political and public discourse . 
Emission reductions of such pollutants should therefore be taken into account in the revised Regulation as a 
way to consider the full value of different technologies. The use of LNG allows for a reduction of up to 55% 
of NOx and up to 95% of PM10 compared to the emissions of a Euro VI diesel engine. 
 
The uptake of zero and low-emission vehicles will have to come in parallel with highly needed infrastructure. 
Until a network of alternative fuel re fueling stations in Europe is in place, consumers will not switch to 
alternative propulsion vehicles on a large scale. The number of stations is constantly growing in the EU but 
their distribution is uneven. Today there are still  significant differences be tween countries in terms of 
infrastructure (70% of electric recharging points are concentrated in France, The Netherlands and Germany; 
of the 4000 gas stations, two thirds are in Italy and Germany). Therefore the revision of emission targets for 
heavy-duty vehicles will have to take into consideration this key infrastructural need which currently 
represents a barrier  to the scale up of low -carbon technologies. In particular, consistency with other EU 
legislative proposals, such as the proposal for a Regulation for alternative fuel infrastructure (AFIR) has to be 
ensured.

Introduction:  
 
Bosch is committed to the de-fossilization of the road transport and supports the EU’s 
objective to achieve a 55% net reduction in greenhouse gas emissions by 2030 
(compared to 1990 levels) and to become climate neutral by 2050. The company aims 
to contribute to these targets with its innovative technologies and by doing its own 
share. With its more than 400 locations worldwide, the Bosch Group has been carbon 
neutral (in scope 1 and 2) since 2020.  
 
The focus of future mobility in Europe will be primarily electric and electromobility 
becomes a core business for Bosch. With upfront investments of more than 5 billion 
Euros, and the most efficient electrical powertrain solutions in the market, Bosch is 
contributing to its breakthrough. Bosch is preparing for increasing demand for electric 
vehicles in Europe and across the globe.  
 
Mobility must be climate-friendly and nevertheless remain affordable and reliable. To 
bring this about, Bosch is continuing to pursue a technology-neutral approach, offering 
climate-friendly solutions worldwide for all types of mobility. Beside its investment in 
battery-electric powertrains, Bosch invests in hydrogen fuel-cell powertrains, hydrogen 
engine and in further efficiency increases of conventional combustion engines that can 
contribute to reaching climate targets by running on renewable and low carbon fuels.   
 
To follow the path to climate neutrality, a broad market uptake of vehicles with 
alternative powertrains (BEV, FCEV, (P)HEV & H2-engine) across all commercial 
vehicle segments is required. Electrification is key, but cannot be effectively applied in 
all locations and use cases. A broad range of varying vehicle & mission specifications 
requires different technological solutions tailored to various mission-profiles. In doing so, 
vehicle availability is only one aspect of a complex system. Others like infrastructure 
deployment and the affordability and competitiveness of new technologies must develop 
hand in hand with the same level of ambition. For the development of different 
measures usually addressed by different regulations, it is important to obtain 
consistency.

Feedback on the upcoming review of CO2 standards for  
Heavy Duty Vehicles (HDV) 
 
Bosch supports stringent CO2 reduction targets for Heavy Duty Vehicles (HDV) to 
contribute to the objective of the EU to reduce its overall CO2 emissions by 55% by 
2030 and to become climate neutral by 2050.  
By setting targets for HDV, potentials of cost-efficient CO2 reductions, technology 
availabilities and lead times as well as freight transport specific requirements to ensure 
reliable logistic chains over long distances must be taken into account. Freight and 
passenger transport must remain functional, affordable and continue to cover all 
necessary use cases.   
A significant effect of new or more stringent CO2 standards on the total CO2 emissions 
(of new vehicles and vehicles in use) can only be expected in the next decade due to 
necessary lead times to comply with new emission standards and expected market 
penetration of ZLEV1 in the existing fleets. New technologies become available in the 
market mainly in the 2nd half of the 2020s.

The current 2025 target should remain untouched as the new regulation will 
not likely be adopted before 2023/ 2024 and an adequate lead time is necessary 
for industry to adapt.  
For the period after 2030 a clear pathway with increasingly stringent CO2 standards is 
recommended as this provides long-term investments signals and regulatory 
predictability.  
Electrification potentials in the freight and passenger transport sector, the challenges 
in battery range, grid development and electricity supply, infrastructure and costs etc. 
should be closely monitored and targets re-assessed by the end of the 2020s.   
All contributors (segments) should be covered by CO2 standards, which should be 
adequate to reflect their potential to contribute to the overall required CO2 reductions.  
Urban buses and delivery trucks have much greater electrification potentials as they 
face less challenges in terms of battery range, charging capacities and use times than 
regional delivery lorries and long-haul trucks or coaches. For urban buses and urban 
delivery lorries, zero emission achievements are feasible in the next decade, whereas 
no reliable assumptions can be made today for long-haul lorries and coaches. 
It is important that the CO2 regulation follows a technology neutral and Life Cycle 
approach to combine the endeavor for a climate neutral mobility & transport with the 
needs of the transportation and logistics sector (long distances, reliability, affordability).  
Incentives in ZLEV are still important to push new technology into the market to reach 
self-sustaining as quick as possible. However, incentive schemes need to be clearly 
defined regarding phase-in and phase-out. H2 trucks including the H2-engine shall be 
considered as Zero Emission Vehicles.  
Renewable fuels play a decisive role for defossilizing the existing fleet but should also 
be accepted for applications/use cases of new vehicles where electrification cannot be 
effectively applied (to provide for technology neutrality and as safety net). A broad range 
of varying vehicle & mission specifications requires different technological solutions 
tailored to various mission-profiles. 
CO2 fleet targets alone will not ensure that the EU reaches its climate targets. The 
deployment of a recharging and hydrogen refueling infrastructure must go hand in 
hand with the same level of ambition. 
The EU will need to put forward higher targets for public recharging infrastructure 
than those currently foreseen in the AFIR proposal, to accommodate the large 
number of electric vehicles coming onto the European market. Likewise, higher 
targets are needed for the hydrogen-refueling infrastructure; especially to meet 
future demands in HDV.  
A CO2 price signal is needed to incentivize consumers to opt for low-carbon 
technologies (electric vehicles, H2 trucks) and for renewable fuels for the 
existing fleet. It is important that the Commission’s proposal of an EU ETS

for transport fuels remains an effective instrument throughout the co-decision

Revenues from fines for excess emissions shall be used to support investments in 
ZLEV, the deployment of recharging and hydrogen refueling infrastructure, R&D for zero 
and low emission technologies and to support the transformation process in the 
automotive measures (e.g. requalification programs).  
Co-benefits in terms of better air quality are limited. Modern vehicles (Euro VI) have 
already only a small impact on urban air quality. The current Real Driving Emissions 
(RDE) legislation has proven to be effective as real world emissions dropped sharply for 
vehicles of the Euro 6d/VI norm according to measurements published in the Handbook 
for Emission Factors (HBEFA) 4.1.2. The effect is already noticeable in improvements of 
ambient air quality as recent air quality data of the German Environmental Agency 
show3. Therefore, significant co-benefits are not expected. Furthermore, a switch to 
electric vehicles will not prevent pollutant emissions from road transport entirely as zero 
emission vehicles also emit brake & tire particulate emissions.  
Positive economic benefits can only be expected if the CO2 regulation remains open 
for a variety of ZLEV technology options and respects necessary lead times; and if 
overall conditions (EU-wide alternative infrastructure deployment, availability of 
renewable electricity/H2, CO2 pricing for fuels and other consumer incentives e.g. via 
CO2 differentiation in toll systems) support an ZLEV market-uptake to achieve the CO2 
standards. 
CO2 standards for road transport involve a deep transformation of the entire 
automotive industry which affects jobs and employment. A great effort must be put in 
requalification and supporting measures. Also, an adequate and predictable timeline for 
new CO2 emission standards is key for industry to ensure a just transition. 
According to a CLEPA study, over 500,000 jobs - 84% of the current powertrain 
workforce - risk becoming obsolete in LDV (developments will be comparable in 
the HDV sector) during the transformation. New jobs will be created but they are 
often not interchangeable with those lost, as they will involve different 
companies, skill sets, regions and timelines.

About Robert Bosch GmbH  
 
The Bosch Group is a leading global supplier of technology and services. It employs 
roughly 395,000 associates worldwide (as of December 31, 2020). The company 
generated sales of 71.5 billion euros in 2020. Its operations are divided into four 
business sectors: Mobility Solutions, Industrial Technology, Consumer Goods, and 
Energy and Building Technology. As a leading IoT provider, Bosch offers innovative 
solutions for smart homes, Industry 4.0, and connected mobility. 
 
Bosch is pursuing a vision of mobility that is sustainable, safe, and exciting. It uses its 
expertise in sensor technology, software, and services, as well as its own IoT cloud, to 
offer its customers connected, cross-domain solutions from a single source. The Bosch 
Group’s strategic objective is to facilitate connected living with products and solutions 
that either contain artificial intelligence (AI) or have been developed or manufactured 
with its help. Bosch improves quality of life worldwide with products and services that 
are innovative and spark enthusiasm. In short, Bosch creates technology that is 
“Invented for life.”  
 
The Bosch Group comprises Robert Bosch GmbH and its roughly 440 subsidiary and 
regional companies in some 60 countries. Including sales and service partners, Bosch’s 
global manufacturing, engineering, and sales network covers nearly every country in the 
world. 
 
With its more than 400 locations worldwide, the Bosch Group has been carbon neutral 
since the first quarter of 2020. The basis for the company’s future growth is its 
innovative strength. At 129 locations across the globe, Bosch employs some 73,000 
associates in research and development, of which nearly 34,000 are software 
engineers. 
 
The company was set up in Stuttgart in 1886 by Robert Bosch (1861–1942) as 
“Workshop for Precision Mechanics and Electrical Engineering.” The special ownership 
structure of Robert Bosch GmbH guarantees the entrepreneurial freedom of the Bosch 
Group, making it possible for the company to plan over the long term and to undertake 
significant upfront investments in the safeguarding of its future. Ninety-four percent of 
the share capital of Robert Bosch GmbH is held by Robert Bosch Stiftung GmbH, a 
charitable foundation. The remaining shares are held by Robert Bosch GmbH and by a 
corporation owned by the Bosch family. The majority of voting rights are held by Robert 
Bosch Industrietreuhand KG, an industrial trust. The entrepreneurial ownership 
functions are carried out by the trust.

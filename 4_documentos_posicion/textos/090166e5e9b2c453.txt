Contribution of Wageningen University and Research to the Soil Health call for evidence. 
Soils provide the basis; they have a fundamental role in the functioning of ecosystems and provide immense 
ecosystem services to society. Wageningen University and Research (WUR) holds Europe’s largest soil science 
cluster, performing both fundamental and applied science, leading and contributing to many H2020 projects 
like RECARE, SOILCARE, LANDMARK and EJP SOIL. Furthermore, WUR supports the ministry of agriculture, food 
and environment by providing a knowledge base for the implementation for the Dutch soil strategy.  As such, 
Wageningen-UR acknowledges and supports the vision of the new European Soil Strategy to have all soils in 
healthy conditions by 2050 and to make protection, sustainable use and restoration of soils the new normal. 
To reach healthy soils by 2050, it is crucial that the EU develops and adopts a coherent framework, preferably 
to be legally implemented at national scale to protect, restore and sustainably manage soils. As a contribut ion 
to this call for evidence, the scientists of WUR identified selected points on which they already provide 
evidence in relation to the objectives and policy option of a Soil Health Law perspective.  
Soil health definition  
Soil Health has been defined by the Mission “A soil deal for Europe” as the continued capacity of soils to 
support ecosystem services (Bonfante et al. 2020, Veerman et al. 2020). We do support this definition, where 
in terms of ecosystem services provision, soil health revers to the actual capacity of soil to supply ecosystem 
services, while the widely used concept of soil quality refers to the capacity of soils to provide the desired 
ecosystem services in potential.  However, we would like the definition to be expanded, such that it also 
includes and reflect the human aspects; as soil health should refer to the actual capacity of soils to supply 
ecosystem services for human wellbeing. The capacity of soils to deliver ecosystem services across an array of 
seven soil functions has been emphasized and these functions are critical. However, for a formal definition in 
legal terms (the Soil Health Law in preparation) we recommend that ‘soil health’ would not be limited to these 
soil functions and associated ecosystem services, but we widened, e.g. including cultural ecosystem services, 
and whilst balancing human wellbeing and societal needs in order to facilitate considering potential trade-offs 
between ecosystem services 
Consequently, the definition of soil health should be coupled with the definition for soil quality. The definition 
for soil quality that is widely accepted in the Netherlands: the capacity of the soils to, under varying conditions, 
sufficiently execute the desired soil functions for a combination of purposes like food productions, efficient 
nutrient cycling and maintenance of biodiversity. 
Indicators of soil health and their range of values 
Defining indicators of soil health and their range of values is critical for monitoring soil health and to conceive 
and support a policy aiming at rendering European soils healthy. At European level, the EJP SOIL SIREN project 
provided a stocktake of soil ecosystem services indicators and reference values in 23 European countries. 
While a unique indicators framework is needed, the reference values of soil health indicators need to be 
context-specific (climate, soil type, land use). Within the Netherlands , measuring the soil quality of agricultural 
fields in a uniform and integral manner is possible with the set of indicators ‘Soil indicators for agricultural 
fields in the Netherlands’ (Bodemindicatoren voor Landbouwgronden in Nederland, BLN for short). Measuring 
the soil quality is important for the sustainable management of agricultural soils. An overview of its reference 
and ambition values is given in https://edepot.wur.nl/553470. Here we would like to stress that it is a nice 
start to have reference values, yet what is actually needed, is to have context specific target values for the 
indicators.  Hence soil quality assessments require a nuanced measurement approach and soil biology should 
be measured in conjunction with chemical and physical properties. WUR has developed a flexible selection

Requirements for the sustainable use of soils 
Assessing requirements for the sustainable use of soils must be accompanied with the definition of a set of 
sustainable soil management practices that allow for the delivery of soil related ecosystem services without 
hampering the functions that enable those services nor biodiversity (FAO, 2017).  
Regarding the agricultural use of soils it remains important that no “ one solution for all” exists. Sustainable use 
of soils depends on the specific targets, i.e. do you want to produce food, store carbon, facilitate water 
infiltration, and the extend to which one seeks to realize a set of the beforementioned targets and obviously 
on the specific location characteristics: soil type, climate and land use. When using soils, not everything is 
always possible at each location. The H2020 Landmark project (https://landmark2020.eu/)  was very clear 
about this statement and developed the Soil Navigator tool to combine soil targets and ambitions with soil 
functions and management suggestions. Furthermore, the SoilCARE project ( https://www.soilcare-project.eu/) 
has identified a set of soil improving cropping systems; addressing soil improving crops, fertilizer amendments, 
compaction alleviation, cultivation and soil improving technologies. In the Netherlands we are currently testing 
and validating the line of thought from these H2020 projects in the  Dutch program PPS beter bodem beheer 
(https://www.beterbodembeheer.nl/nl/beterbodembeheer.htm). Some first results (in Dutch)  
https://edepot.wur.nl/536510; later in 2022 WUR will provide an update of this document.  
Measures to contribute to reducing nutrient losses 50% and fertilizer use 20%  
Reducing nutrient losses, which is an important ambition in the Dutch soil strategy, starts with providing good 
fertilization recommendations and making sure that these are followed. Often  (at least in NL) this doesn’t 
completely solve the problem. Further optimization of fertilization on top of current fertilization measures  can 
be considered to be Good Agricultural Practice, yet it often yields only a few percentage gain in terms of 
limiting losses or fertilizer use, and this is too little for a 50 or 20% reduction.  
Actually reducing nutrient losses should be sought in a combination of adapting/or drastically changing the 
crop rotation, preventing yield losses through optimal management of other factors like water supply 
(irrigation) and prevention of damage from disease, pests and weeds and precision agriculture (see 
e.g.https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agwat.2020.106316). 
Monitoring and reporting the condition of soils  
While Europe is relatively soil data rich there is an urgent need to harmonize soil information, to increase soil 
data sharing and to align National Soil Monitoring systems, between themselves and with the LUCAS Soil. 
Within EJP SOIL we are tackling these issues and performing stocktakes on soil data management and sharing 
in Europe and on the existing National soil monitoring systems. 
An important element regarding harmonizing soil information is the need to develop standardized methods for 
soil analyses. At this moment not all important methods have suitable ISO standards, and often laboratories 
measure important parameters differently (lack of standardization), or measure different parameters (lack of 
harmonization). To bundle expertise and effort WUR launched in 2017 the SO PHIE initiative: "Soil Program on 
Hydro-Physics via International Engagement" to work on 3 pillars: 1. harmonize soil hydro -physics properties, 
2. standardize the measurement methods in field and laboratory, and 3. innovate towards cost -effective soil 
data generating methodologies. More than 200 participants, and more than 14 soil hydro -physics laboratories 
are collaborating within Europe to work on sufficient and harmonized hydro -physics data for EU policy making. 
It should be noted that SOPHIE focusses on soil-water relationships. Initiatives like these are at the root of the 
data chain, and are crucial for the realization of reliable soil data and soil monitoring systems, as also indicated  
in for example the RECARE project and the ISRIC and JRC institute s.

Additional comments to the survey 
 
Introduction:  
 
Bosch is committed to the de-fossilization of the road transport and supports the EU’s 
objective to achieve a 55% net reduction in greenhouse gas emissions by 2030 
(compared to 1990 levels) and to become climate neutral by 2050. The company aims 
to contribute to these targets with its innovative technologies and by doing its own 
share. With its more than 400 locations worldwide, the Bosch Group has been carbon 
neutral (in scope 1 and 2) since 2020.  
 
The focus of future mobility in Europe will be primarily electric and electromobility 
becomes a core business for Bosch. With upfront investments of more than 5 billion 
Euros, and the most efficient electrical powertrain solutions in the market, Bosch is 
contributing to its breakthrough. Bosch is preparing for increasing demand for electric 
vehicles in Europe and across the globe.  
 
Mobility must be climate-friendly and nevertheless remain affordable and reliable. To 
bring this about, Bosch is continuing to pursue a technology-neutral approach, offering 
climate-friendly solutions worldwide for all types of mobility. Beside its investment in 
battery-electric powertrains, Bosch invests in hydrogen fuel-cell powertrains, hydrogen 
engine and in further efficiency increases of conventional combustion engines that can 
contribute to reaching climate targets by running on renewable and low carbon fuels.   
 
To follow the path to climate neutrality, a broad market uptake of vehicles with 
alternative powertrains (BEV, FCEV, (P)HEV & H2-engine) across all commercial 
vehicle segments is required. Electrification is key, but cannot be effectively applied in 
all locations and use cases. A broad range of varying vehicle & mission specifications 
requires different technological solutions tailored to various mission-profiles. In doing so, 
vehicle availability is only one aspect of a complex system. Others like infrastructure 
deployment and the affordability and competitiveness of new technologies must develop 
hand in hand with the same level of ambition. For the development of different 
measures usually addressed by different regulations, it is important to obtain 
consistency.

Chapter 1: Objectives  
The answers only concern the question whether the objectives mentioned should be 
envisaged by the HDV CO2 regulation. Bosch makes no statement here about the 
value of the objective as such or as part of any other regulation. 
 
Question 1: Objectives for the future HDV CO2 Regulation 
Reducing CO2 emissions from new HDV in a cost-effective way, in line with 
the 2030 overall climate target of at least -55%: 
• It is important to align CO2 standards for HDV with EU-target of -55% by 
2030. While setting targets for HDV, potentials of cost-efficient CO2 
reductions, available technologies, and lead times for new technologies 
as well as freight transport specific requirements (reliable logistic chains, 
timely deliveries) must be considered. 
• A significant effect of new or more stringent CO2 standards on the total CO2 
emissions (of new vehicles and vehicles in use) can only be expected in the 
next decade due to necessary lead times to comply with new emission 
standards and expected market penetration of ZLEV in the existing fleets.   
 
Promoting the market uptake of ZEV by making them more affordable: 
• Targets itself do not make the necessary technologies affordable. In case of 
too short periods, vehicle costs may increase considerably as no cost-
efficient technologies are available/in the market yet. But rightly set targets 
together with other regulatory incentives as e.g. a CO2 price signal (ETS, 
ETD, Eurovignette Directive), binding targets and support for infrastructure 
deployment and ZLEV purchase incentives can stimulate the market-uptake 
of ZLEV and reduce cost impacts for vehicle owners and transport users. 
 
Fostering Innovation 
• Binding targets as such foster innovation in zero emission technologies. 
Accompanying framework conditions are needed to enhance the effect, e.g. 
R&D&I support and creating demand by CO2 price signal.

Strengthening technical and industrial leadership and stimulating 
employment: 
• It is to be considered as an important objective, however, stimulating 
employment also requires corresponding lead time for the transformation 
process to cover re-qualification measures and setting up adequate 
accompanying programs for employment. 
 
Contributing to the reduction of air pollution and other environmental 
problems: 
• Every regulatory instrument should focus on one specific target to be 
efficient. Air pollutants are best addressed by Euro6/VI and the 
upcoming Euro 7/VII regulation. 
 
Reducing EU energy consumption and import dependence on fossil fuels:  
• The objective is to reduce and replace fossil fuels consumption. A switch 
to electromobility and hydrogen can create new (import) dependencies, 
e.g. for battery components. Reducing energy consumption is an 
important target but it should not be the only determinator. The 
technology solutions must fit for purpose. 
 
Chapter 2: Future CO2 emissions targets for new HDV 
Bosch supports the objective to render road transport carbon neutral and to set 
ambitious standards for new vehicles. Nevertheless, hurdles for a zero-carbon road 
transport are considerably higher for HDV compared to PC, caused by different 
requirements e.g., regarding durability, long driving distances, loading capacities, 
special purpose use cases, etc.. Freight and passenger transport must remain 
functional, affordable and continue to cover all necessary use cases. To address the 
existing challenges, adequate lead time for the development, market introduction as well 
as market penetration of cost-effective and affordable zero carbon technologies is 
required. The time for the market penetration of ZLEV can be shortened if standards are 
defined timely an responsibilities are clearly addressed, e.g. by clear and adequate 
infrastructure requirements in the Alternative Fuel Infrastructure Regulation.  
Too stringent targets and too short timelines can have the impact that the existing fleet 
remains even longer in the market with an opposing effect on CO2-emissions. 
Therefore, a clear path of ambitious but feasible target values are key. A revision clause 
shall ensure that the target setting for the time after 2030 remains in line with technology 
development, technology costs and infrastructure deployment in the next coming years. 
The revision shall consider all factors that contribute to a cost-efficient progress towards 
climate neutrality by 2050; especially the following: 
a) the cost of zero- and low-emission vehicles 
b) consumer take-up of zero- and low-emission vehicles 
c) the specific characteristics of heavy duty vehicles

Question 2: Setting new targets for other types of vehicles 
• All contributors (segments) should be covered by CO2 standards, which should 
be adequate to reflect their potential to contribute to the overall required CO2 
reductions. Exceptions for some vehicle types could lead to segment shifts. 
• The leverage effect of CO2 standards for new trailers is important. But short term 
effects cannot be expected as trailers stay increasingly long in the market1.   
• Urban busses are sufficiently addressed by the Clean Vehicle Directive. 
Therefore, there is no regulatory need to include them in the regulation for CO2 
standards for HDV. Overregulation shall be avoided. 
 
Question 3: Setting target for all new vehicles to be zero-emission 
Should the CO2 emission standards become so strict that all new HDV be zero-
emission vehicles? If so, by when? 
• Freight and passenger transport must remain functional, affordable and continue 
to cover all necessary use cases. To address existing challenges adequate lead 
time for the development, market introduction and market penetration of cost-
effective and affordable zero carbon technologies is required.  
• Bosch supports stringent and technically and economically feasible targets. 
Overly strict targets respectively too short timelines risk that the existing fleet 
remains longer in the market with a negative effect on the overall CO2 emissions.  
• A clear pathway beyond 2030 is recommended and contributes to secure the 
investments needed. 
• For urban buses and urban delivery lorries zero emission targets and more 
ambitious timelines can be set, as they have shorter use times and battery range 
as well as charging capacities are less challenging compared to long-haul trucks 
and coaches. For urban busses, the ambition should be implemented via the 
Clean Vehicle Directive. 
• For long-haul lorries and coaches, no reliable assumptions can be made today of 
the adequate time frame for a zero emission target. Besides, to generate a real 
contribution to a climate neutral transport & mobility, a well to wheel approach 
must be followed (by either recognizing the contribution of renewable fuels or 
setting CO2 standards that permit a remaining percentage of vehicles / use 
cases to resort to renewable fuels where electric drives are not feasible or 
effective). The potentials of the electrification in the freight and passenger 
(coaches) transport sector, the challenges in battery range, grid development 
and electricity supply, infrastructure and costs etc. should be closely monitored 
and targets should be re-assessed by the end of the 2020s.

Chapter 3: Incentivising zero- and low-emission HDV 
Incentives are crucial to push new technology into the market to reach self-sustaining as 
quick as possible. However, incentive schemes need to be clearly defined regarding 
phase-in and phase-out.  
 
Question 1: main barriers for market uptake of ZLEV  
From the barriers mentioned, the availability of recharging and refueling infrastructure 
present the greatest challenge.   
 
Question 2: evaluation of current ZLEV incentive scheme 
It is difficult to evaluate the current scheme because there is no experience to draw 
upon and the impact is not yet sufficiently measurable/visible due to the time delay of 
(available) monitoring & reporting data. 
 
Question 3: vehicles eligible for the incentive system 
H2 trucks including the H2-engine shall be considered as Zero Emission Vehicles.  
 
Question 4: alignment of ZLEV benchmark levels with target ambition   
Benchmark levels should be in line with the ambition of the CO2 reduction target; but 
benchmarks must also consider technology readiness.    
 
Chapter 4: Contribution of renewable and low-carbon fuels 
 
Question 1: effects of an accounting (crediting) mechanism for renewable and 
low-carbon fuels 
• The survey suggests that a crediting system is to be seen as an incentive to 
promote renewable and low-carbon fuels. This is not the case; a crediting 
scheme serves rather as an instrument to secure the availability of renewable 
fuels as back-up solution.  
o It will be less cost-intensive for vehicle manufacturers to bring alternative 
powertrains to the market than to commit themselves to purchasing 
credits from Renewable Fuel Suppliers over an entire vehicle lifetime. 
This is especially because development costs decrease and electric 
heavy duty trucks are generally forecasted to reach cost parity within this 
decade  (depending on a robust EU Battery Supply Chain and envisaged 
battery ranges).   
o A crediting scheme would secure that a possible resort to 
renewable fuels (to a certain extend) serves as a back-up in 
case of market failure, e.g. in case of lacking customer

vehicles must also in future meet customer requirements (timely delivery, 
reliability, costs…), otherwise new efficient trucks become unattractive

Question 2: design of an accounting mechanism 
• A crediting system is preferable as it provides more predictability and 
transparency on the manufacturers strategy in regard to its vehicle fleet 
composition. If manufacturers decide to make use of carbon credits, they must 
ensure the provision of credits by long-term contracts with fuel suppliers. In 
parallel, fuel suppliers still need to comply with the RED and ETS T&B. 
• Carbon correction factors dilute the responsibility between car manufacturer and 
fuel supplier. Besides, it is unclear how a carbon correction factor can be 
determined annually.   
 
Chapter 5: Other elements 
 
Pooling provisions:  
• Flexibility measures are a valid option to support the overall mechanism but 
should not lead to market distortions.  
 
In-/exclusion of small-volume manufacturers 
• All manufacturers should in generally contribute to CO2 emission reductions, but 
targets should not result in market exclusion of small-volume manufacturers due 
to additional expenditure and associated costs (balanced cost-benefit ratio).  
• If necessary, certain niches / special vehicles could be excluded in case of 
unjustified effort or the availability of alternative solutions. 
 
Allocation of revenues from excess emission premiums  
• Revenues from fines for excess emissions shall be used to support investments 
in ZLEV, the deployment of recharging and hydrogen refueling infrastructure, 
R&D for zero and low emission technologies and to support the transformation 
process in the automotive measures (e.g. requalification programs).

Chapter 6: Impacts 
 
Expected co-benefits in terms of better air quality: 
• Modern vehicles (Euro VI) have already only a small impact on urban air quality. 
The current Real Driving Emissions (RDE) legislation has proven to be effective 
as real world emissions dropped sharply for vehicles of the Euro 6d/VI norm 
according to measurements published in the Handbook for Emission Factors 
(HBEFA) 4.1.2. The effect is already noticeable in improvements of ambient air 
quality as recent air quality data of the German Environmental Agency show3. 
Therefore, significant co-benefits are not expected. Furthermore, a switch to 
electric vehicles will not prevent pollutant emisions from road transport entirely 
as zero emission vehicles also emit brake & tire particulate emissions.  
 
 
Macroeconomic benefits, effects on competitiveness, jobs and SMEs: 
• Positive impact on global competitiveness and macroeconomic benefits can only 
be expected if CO2 regulation remains open for a variety of ZLEV technology 
options and respects necessary lead times; and if overall condition (EU-wide 
infrastructure deployment, availability of renewable electricity/H2, CO2 pricing for 
fuels and other consumer incentives e.g. via CO2 differentiation in toll systems, 
…) support an ZLEV market-uptake to achieve the CO2 standards. 
• If favourable conditions are not in place, technologies are not ready in foreseen 
timeframes or are still too costly, this can result in shortcomings in freight 
transports & logistics (longer delivery times, supply disruptions) and lead to 
overall increases of transport costs which would negatively affect the robustness 
of European supply chains. 
• High upfront investments (grid connection, charging points, higher vehicle 
prices) & long amortization will have direct cost impacts on freight logistics costs. 
Cost increases will be especially difficult to handle for many haulage companies 
(which have hardly any capital reserves) [and require public support].    
• With a large-scale switch to BEV electricity prices can differ considerably for 
large vs. small electricity consumers, depot charging vs. public charging, low-
cost connection to medium-voltage power grids or PPA power prices vs. grid

About Robert Bosch GmbH  
 
The Bosch Group is a leading global supplier of technology and services. It employs 
roughly 395,000 associates worldwide (as of December 31, 2020). The company 
generated sales of 71.5 billion euros in 2020. Its operations are divided into four 
business sectors: Mobility Solutions, Industrial Technology, Consumer Goods, and 
Energy and Building Technology. As a leading IoT provider, Bosch offers innovative 
solutions for smart homes, Industry 4.0, and connected mobility. 
 
Bosch is pursuing a vision of mobility that is sustainable, safe, and exciting. It uses its 
expertise in sensor technology, software, and services, as well as its own IoT cloud, to 
offer its customers connected, cross-domain solutions from a single source. The Bosch 
Group’s strategic objective is to facilitate connected living with products and solutions 
that either contain artificial intelligence (AI) or have been developed or manufactured 
with its help. Bosch improves quality of life worldwide with products and services that 
are innovative and spark enthusiasm. In short, Bosch creates technology that is 
“Invented for life.”  
 
The Bosch Group comprises Robert Bosch GmbH and its roughly 440 subsidiary and 
regional companies in some 60 countries. Including sales and service partners, Bosch’s 
global manufacturing, engineering, and sales network covers nearly every country in the 
world. 
 
With its more than 400 locations worldwide, the Bosch Group has been carbon neutral 
since the first quarter of 2020. The basis for the company’s future growth is its 
innovative strength. At 129 locations across the globe, Bosch employs some 73,000 
associates in research and development, of which nearly 34,000 are software 
engineers. 
 
The company was set up in Stuttgart in 1886 by Robert Bosch (1861–1942) as 
“Workshop for Precision Mechanics and Electrical Engineering.” The special ownership 
structure of Robert Bosch GmbH guarantees the entrepreneurial freedom of the Bosch 
Group, making it possible for the company to plan over the long term and to undertake 
significant upfront investments in the safeguarding of its future. Ninety-four percent of 
the share capital of Robert Bosch GmbH is held by Robert Bosch Stiftung GmbH, a 
charitable foundation. The remaining shares are held by Robert Bosch GmbH and by a 
corporation owned by the Bosch family. The majority of voting rights are held by Robert 
Bosch Industrietreuhand KG, an industrial trust. The entrepreneurial ownership 
functions are carried out by the trust.

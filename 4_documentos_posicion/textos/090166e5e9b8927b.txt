Concawe feedback to Call for Evidence for an Impact Assessment 
Soil health – protecting, sustainably managing and restoring EU soils 
(Ares(2022)1132884) 
 
GENERAL FEEDBACK 
Concawe are principally focused on the application of a Soil Health law to the identification, 
management, remediation, and disposition / beneficial reuse of contaminated land. We provide direct 
comments and useful Evidence to support our comments on Soil health – protecting, sustainably 
managing and restoring EU soils in the second part of our submission. In this first part, we confirm our 
members would like to see a framework which evolves to be: 
RISK BASED 
• Founded on risk-based management principles, which requires development of a conceptual 
site model (e.g., ISO 21365:2019) that identifies source-pathway-receptor linkages.  
• The advantage of a risk-based perspective is that it is the only way to make an EU wide 
framework relevant to the multitude of soil environments that can be present across Member 
States, and risk-based assessment and action can preserve the potentials of a specific 
environment, by always keeping land use in consideration.  
 
PROPORTIONAL 
• Requires remediation actions proportional to the risk(s) to human health / environment, and 
does not require remediation where there is no unacceptable risk  
• Allows remedy selection in line with impact significance – recognising that high risk, recalcitrant 
chemicals may require high energy, destructive technologies to reduce loading to acceptable 
levels.  
• Gives due consideration for taking remediation action in a reasonable amount of time, 
considering a total net benefit argument, noting that regulators must be able to require the 
‘fastest’ action when there is a clear and immediate risk.  
• The perpetuation of remediation obligation from a compliance point of view needs 
consideration. Where impact might have occurred at a facility (which remains in operation), and 
that impact does not pose a significant risk it is reasonable per legislation to perform post 
operation remediation – until that time, or the risk profile of the site changes then impact that 
does not pose a risk should simply need to be contained within the site boundary.  
 
SCIENCE LED 
• The implementation of a soil strategy that could drive regulatory led assessment and 
remediation programs of properties to a non-risk-based end point, could incur disproportionate 
effort – any framework should be based on scientifically defensible principles, risk-based action 
which considers sustainability and cost/benefit elements.  
• In this area particularly, the assessment of ecological ‘receptors’ would benefit from greater 
common scientific research to better understand what constitutes an intact vs. impaired 
environment, and what are the risk indicators of soil biodiversity. 
Ref. Ares(2022)1924093 - 16/03/2022

• Shallow 'perched' water, or smaller localised bodies of water below the ground have limited 
local, and regional value, and often have low interconnectivity. As such these bodies of water 
should not be classified as groundwater. Definitions and classifications need care alignment. 
Simply by being water 'below ground' - should not identify these waters as 'Groundwater' a 
usable resource - and should not always therefore be considered an ‘impaired environment’. 
This should be reconsidered in alignment with the definition of controlled waters / parts of the 
Groundwater Directive. A site-specific conceptual model (CSM) is acknowledged under many 
Member State organisations and ‘best practises’ to be a tool which can well represent the 
overall risk posed to or from impacted soils, and can demonstrate the most effective 
remediation strategy for a local site and its environs. A non-rigid but harmonised ‘site specific’ 
technical-scientific approach as demonstrated in a CSM should form part of EU Soil Health 
considering actions that maybe required on a local scale  
 
SUSTAINABLE 
• Incorporates sustainability assessment (e.g. ISO 18504:2017, Sustainable Remediation) into 
remedy selection and implementation – expanding the range of ‘Green’ tools / technologies 
available for deployment in Member States with a clear ‘net environmental benefit’ framework.  
• Encourages re-use of contaminated land for future beneficial uses, and does not place new 
barriers to the successful regeneration of sites  
• There is a clear desire for soil Total Organic Carbon (TOC) protection. There are also high risk, 
recalcitrant chemicals that require high energy, destructive technologies to reduce contaminant 
loading to acceptable levels. Sometimes to sustainably develop and reuse land carbon 
consumption has to be balanced for the greatest good. There should be a recognition that a lack 
of flexibility could see changes in remediation tactics - where technologies like Soil washing, 
thermal desorption etc. (which remove TOC AND contamination) may be less preferred – or 
worst case not available for selection.  
 
ALIGNED WITH STAKEHOLDERS 
• Recognises voluntary remediation (e.g. through a land-use planning system) can deliver land 
regeneration / remediation.  
• Enforcement or register identification (e.g. through a contaminated land system) should focus 
on high risk sites where risk is not acceptable and for which no voluntary action is forthcoming.  
• Allows the transfer of land ownership and liabilities in a manner that facilitates both responsible 
business and brownfield regeneration. Should consider ‘polluter pays principles’ as valuable – 
but not indefinite obligations after remediation, sale and contractual transfer of land in a 
modern, well-regulated framework, with clear principles of due diligence, it should now be 
explicit that in property transfer the buyer becomes liable for the remediation if deemed 
required.

B. Objectives and policy options 
The EU Soil Strategy for 2030 already 
indicated a number of aspects that 
should be considered in the impact 
assessment: […] 
• Indicators for soil health and 
their range of values 
• Soil has multiple end-uses in Europe where few soils are 
free of anthropogenic input. Soil Health Indicators should 
not be assumed to simply be fully natural / agricultural 
systems. It should be recognised in an EU Soil Health Law 
that soils on industrial facilities or in man-made 
environments may be healthy – but perform different 
services. 
• There is growing recognition that for contaminated land 
management, the definition of soil health is relative to the 
human health and ecological risk contaminants within soil 
represent, and the value of the soil is considered within 
the broader assessment. 
• Assuming contaminants within soil present no risk based 
on the site setting, then a more appropriate definition of 
soil health might look at wider indicators – the most 
sustainable action should be considered. The concept that 
the ‘greater good’ might be served by allowing soils to 
remain untouched should be appreciated within the range 
of soil health values that will be proposed. Remediation 
requirements should address unacceptable risks to human 
health and the environment. Various risk-management 
approaches can be used to achieve a sustainable outcome. 
In addition, within the ranges, it should be understood 
that soil health can be ‘improved’ without requiring action 
to remove impacts that prove no discernible risk.

B. Objectives and policy options 
The EU Soil Strategy for 2030 already 
indicated a number of aspects that 
should be considered in the impact 
assessment: […] 
• options for a soil health 
certificate for land transactions 
to provide land buyers with 
information on the key 
characteristics and health of the 
soils in the site they intend to 
purchase; 
• Such a certificate is most viable where it documents the 
condition of a property and facilitates the transfer of land 
ownership and liabilities. With such a soil health 
certificate, both responsible business (ability to transact 
impacted land on a ‘Buyer Aware’ basis) and brownfield 
regeneration (re-use of the soils at a standard acceptable 
for the new end-use) will be facilitated.  
 
 
 
• The disadvantage of a rigorous ‘polluter pays principles’ is 
that an eternal obligation can arise after contractual 
transfer – soil health certificates could be used as a 
mechanism to allow the transfer of responsibility to occur. 
If a soil health certificate is used as a tool to prevent 
transfer until a new soil standard is met, sites could be left 
– reducing the likelihood for sustainable management as 
called for in the concept of the Soil Strategy scope. 
EVIDENCE – an example of suitable  NICOLE liability 
transfer:  NICOLE liability transfer booklet (NICOLE 2021) 
(link)

B. Objectives and policy options 
In addition to the policy options 
listed above, the impact assessment 
will also consider alternative, non-
legislative policy instruments, such 
as self-regulation, voluntary 
agreements and stewardship, 
standardisation, financial incentives 
and economic instruments 
• As noted earlier - the system should recognize voluntary 
remediation (e.g. through a Land-use Planning system) 
can deliver land regeneration / remediation. In the UK,

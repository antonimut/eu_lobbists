Soil health – protecting, sustainably managing and restoring EU soils. 
The Views of the Enel Group 
 
Land take and soil degradation are a serious problem in Europe and require urgent action. Increasing 
land use change for industrial and agricultural purposes, urbanization and soil sealing have led to soil erosion, 
pollution and degradation, as well to the rising scarcity of a limited reso urce that constitutes a platform for 
human activities and habitat. A harmonized regulatory framework at EU level for soil protection is therefore 
paramount to reach no net land take by 2050 and land degradation neutrality by 2030, as stated in the  EU 
Biodiversity Strategy for 2030.  
Enel sees with perplexity that the EU 2030 Soil Strategy mainly focuses on soil protection  and 
management related to agricultural activities and urbanization, without a more holistic approach to 
land use by other sectors, such as industry. A new Soil Health Law should instead provide for a broader 
scope, including all type of land uses that might impact soil quality as well as its ecosystem services and 
interactions with other environmental aspec ts such as water and air . The Law should explicitly address all 
anthropogenic activities and clearly define the sectors covered by its provisions. Land use by industrial sectors 
is partially regulated by other directives such as the Industrial Emissions Di rective and the Environmental 
Liability Directive. To avoid overlapping and create coherence, the Soil Health Law should include specific 
criteria and provisions on soil and land use also for the industrial sector. 
To be effective and ensure a coherent regulatory framework, Enel believes that the new Soil Health 
Law should consider the following recommendations: 
- Land degradation neutrality should be clearly defined upon soil threatening factors, in function 
of the desired land use. In contrast to water and air, soil quality is not limited to the degree of soil 
pollution, but also related to the different purposes that i t might be used for. Beyond chemical, 
biological and physical conditions, s oil quality needs to be defined with respect to the desired use of 
land. Soil and land  degradation is caused by threats such as erosion, decline in organic matter, 
contamination, sealing, compaction, decline in biodiversity, salinization, and floods and la ndslides. It 
is important to establish conceptual linkages between soil quality indicators and soil threats , taking in 
consideration the potential for remediation and desired use of land.  
 
- Regarding the definition of soil health indicators and the characterization, risk assessment and 
remediation of soil pollution, homogenous criteria should be set up at EU level, including 
thresholds for certain pollutants. EU standardized criteria and a uniform methodological approach 
towards characterization and remediation of soils would enable the implementation of a EU soil quality 
certification, which beyond transactional purposes could facilitate fast track permitting for circular uses 
of land, such as the repurposing and re -use of brownfields. A EU soil quality certificate could foster 
investments in brownfields , by addressing critical issues such as the risk and liability for soil 
contamination, defining the corresponding liabilitie s and remediation requirements for both the seller 
as the buyer. It should consider the possibility to introduce indemnity clauses that protect the parties 
from undue responsibilities, also towards third parties and competent authorities.  
 
- In the same line , the creation of a soil quality passport for excavated soil , based upon the 
established soil health indicators, might bring a valuable tool to stimulate the re-use of soil and 
avoid the application of the waste directive.  Overall, such a passport would enable the 
implementation of circular economy principles, by creating a tracking system that at the time would 
simplify the management of excavated soil.

- A definition of sustainable and circular land management, beyond the concept of sustainable 
use of soil  is desirable, considering the different potential uses of land.  Sustainable land 
management envisages a broader and more holistic scope than soil use and would open the possibility 
for new, optimized land use s and new business models that incentivize the coexistence of different 
activities that create mutual synergies and benefits . Moreover, such definition would align better with 
the no net land take goal by 2050, and the land take hierarchy as set out in the 2030 Soil Strategy.  A 
good example is agrivoltaics, an innovative and sustainable model to foster a multipurpose land use, 
where agricultural and/or pastoral activities are integrated and can coexist with solar power plants. 
Generally, photovoltaic plants and agricultural activities are considered competitive business and 
antagonist, but  the adoption of the agrivoltaic business model allow s to combine solar energy 
production with agricultural or other activities preserv ing the biodivers ity and improv ing ecosystem 
services. 
 
- Regarding the goal of no net land take by 2050, and to effectively implement the land take 
hierarchy, the definition of land take should consider suitable criteria and metrics, including 
the quality of land, the level of possible remediation and the desired land use .  The no net land 
take ambition should be rolled out gradually , accompanied with guiding lines and best practices for 
the different levels of the land take hierarchy. 
The land take hierarchy should: 
• Prioritize practices that avoid additional land take  such as multipurpose land us e, providing 
for the proper incentives and rewards for the preservation of the eco-systemic soil functionalities. 
• Foster the re-use of land, through the repurposing of decommissioned industrial sites, creating 
the adequate economic incentives, and simplifying permitting procedures  for land recovery and 
re-purposing. The re-use of brownfields can bring many co-benefits in terms of jobs creation, jobs 
allocation, social cohesion, environment and land use, waste reduction and circular sustainable 
economy. Incentives should be granted to favor financing the reuse of brownfield sites against 
greenfield ones.  
• In those cases where additional land take can’t  be avoided, ensure minimization by providing 
for simplified permitting procedures and financial support for the use of degraded land.  
Irreversibly degraded or poor-quality land might offer the possibility to investors to deploy certain 
industrial projects, without laying hand on additional green field. Expedite permitting and funding 
support or fiscal incentives could be a key driver thereto, by ensuring the projects’ viability.  
• Guide the transition towards sustainable land use, setting priorities and intr oducing 
indicators related to habitat transformation and land use change . Such guidance  would 
enable a gradual reduction of land take and the preservation of soil services, protected areas and 
valuable or endangered biodiversity habitats, without precluding the needs of development, 
especially infrastructural, in regions without previously occupied areas. 
 
- Finally, to ensure a cross sectorial and efficient roll out of the land take hierarchy, the expected 
level of soil remediation and sustainable land management, it is crucial to provide for the right 
incentives and permitting procedures. Funding and permitting are key to investments in R&D on 
soil management, development of new business models or the remediation of polluted soils for their 
re-use. Existing funding mechanisms mainly target agricultural activities and should be expanded 
towards all the sectors that might positively impact soil quality.

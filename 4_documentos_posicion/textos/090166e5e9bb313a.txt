 
 
 
 
 
EU SOIL HEALTH LAW 
CALL FOR EVIDENCE FOR AN IMPACT ASSESSMENT 
IUCN European Regional Office 
The EU Soil Health Law will be a crucial instrument to improve the health of soils  in the EU. Healthy 
soils provide essential ecosystem services such as cleaning and storage of water, carbon storage and 
erosion prevention. Moreover, agriculture productivity and the quality of the food produced depend 
on them. This is even more relevant considering the threat of climate change.  
Soil degradation is mainly caused by  land-use change  and unsustainable  land management . 
Agriculture is the main contributing sector globally1, and one of the main drivers in  the EU: intensive 
practices and contamination from agriculture, urbanisation, and industry and household pollution are 
serious threats to soil health2. Currently, European soils are being affected by erosion, organic matter 
decline, biodiversity loss, compaction, contamination, salinization, sealing and desertification3. 
Protecting and enhancing soil biodiversity is key to keep soils healthy: from bacteria to rodents, soil 
organisms provide nutrients, contribute to the circulation of air and water and help in pest control.  
Sustainable agriculture approaches such as agroecology, nature-inclusive agriculture, permaculture or 
organic farming, have as a principle to preserve and enhance soil health by implementing practices 
that promote functional biodiversity, as well as improve soil structure and organic matter, e.g., no or 
minimal tillage, the reduction of synthetic pesticides and fertilisers, the use of cover and companion 
crops, crop rotation, mixed crop and intercropping, low livestock densities and managed grazing4.  
However, farmers face challenges to implement these approaches and practices. According to a recent 
IUCN report 5, redirecting incentives will be  needed to accelerate the transition to sustainable 
agriculture. It is crucial that current and future EU policies incentivise the uptake of sustainable 
agricultural practices by supporting farmers for the conservation of land and landscapes, also reducing 
the risks for farmers associated with the transition to sustainable agriculture. Also fundamental is to 
eliminate disincentives, redirecting them to sustainable practices. 
As mentioned, t he support to farmers through EU policies is crucial in this transition. N ature-based 
Solutions (NbS) would also be recommended, since they aim to  preserve and restore soils while 
enabling thriving and resilient farmers’ livelihoods, ensuring food security and tackling climate change; 
in this context it is recommended to use the IUCN Global Standard6, which provides a framework with 
specific criteria for NbS.  
The Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) is a key policy to ensure the widespread use of sustainable 
agricultural approaches and practices that contribute to preserving soil health. This is already reflected 
in the CAP itself; one of its key objectives is ‘Eff icient soil management’. This objective needs to be 
translated at  the national level through the CAP Strategic Plans, which are being agreed at the 
moment. As a recent evaluation study published by the European Commission concluded, although 
the CAP has the potential to improve soil management, there has been little enforcement of existing

protection of soils by setting targets on soil health (soil biodiversity, physical and chemical state) and 
sustainable land management, which should be integrated into the CAP Strategic Plans to ensure their

Coherence with other EU policies that already have specific targets on soil and/or agricultural practices 
is also very important. The EU Farm to Fork and Biodiversity Strategies already target the reduction of 
pesticides and fertilisers or the increase of farmland under organic farming and landscape features. 
The Zero Pollution Action Plan also ai ms to reduce soil pollution to levels that are not harmful to 
ecosystems and health by 2050. The Forest Strategy and the future EU deforestation -free products 
legislation are also important legislations for soil since deforestation is one of the main causes for soil 
degradation globally, and it is leading to poor soils that no longer provide ecosystem services. The new 
Soil Health Law could ensure such targets are included in legal provisions, making them legally binding. 
Other upcoming EU legislation, such as the ones on EU nature restoration targets, Sustainable Food 
System Framework and EU taxonomy for sustainable activities should also be coordinated and 
coherent with the Soil Health law, also including these targets in their provisions. 
Furthermore, collaboration with global communities’ efforts to better understand soil biodiversity and 
to improve its monitoring would enrich the development of the Soil Health Law. There are several 
initiatives, networks and organisations such as the Soil Biodiversity Observation Network (Soil BON)8, 
the Global Soil Biodiversity Initiative (GSBI) 9, the Intergovernmental Technical Panel on Soils (ITPS) 10 
and the FAO Global Soil Partnership11. Also, international initiatives promoting sustainable agriculture 
could bring useful insights, such as t he "4 by  1000"12 International Initiative, which aims to tackle 
climate change and food security by implementing sustainable agricultural practices adapted to local 
conditions. 
To conclude, setting legally binding targe ts on soil health and soil management practices and strong 
coordination with other relevant EU policies is crucial for the success of the new Soil Health law.

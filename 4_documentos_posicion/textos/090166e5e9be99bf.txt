Why do the northern forest soils store big amounts of carbon? 
Soils vary greatly across the EU. In the northern boreal forest, the soils are mainly podzolic. Such soil 
has a fully organic uppermost layer of coarsely decomposed plant material resting on mineral soil 
layers, which can be of moraine or sediment types, formed by the glaciation. Also, there are fully 
organic soils, the peatlands.  
  
On the left: The natural weathering of podsoils releases dissolved organic matter and ions of iron and aluminium. The formed organo-
mineral complexes (chelates) moves from the upper to the lower part of the mineral soil causing the “bleached” colour of the upper part 
(the eluvial horizon) and the darker colour of the lower part (the illuviated horizon). On the right: An organic peatland soil profile. 
The boreal forest soils contain a significant store of organic carbon originating from the 
photosynthesis of trees and other vegetation. As much as two-thirds of the total carbon stock in a 
boreal forest can be found below ground. Carbon accumulation has been occurring since the end of 
the glaciation c. 10 000 years ago and is caused by the slow decomposition of the plant material due 
to the cold climate and the recalcitrant chemical composition of plant material from coniferous trees. 
Saprotrophic fungi and bacteria process the soil organic matter, i.e. decompose it, and release it to 
the atmosphere via their respiration (breathing). The other main process releasing soil carbon to the 
atmosphere is wildfires raging through forest landscapes, but under most circumstances, only the 
uppermost organic soil layer is lost, while carbon is stored further down in the mineral part of the soil 
profile is not affected by fires. 
How do the boreal forests differ from forests in the south?  
The boreal forest soils of the north differ substantially from soils of the temperate or tropical forests. 
Due to the warmer climate of the south, trees grow faster and produce more litter than the trees in 
the north. The litter however decomposes faster in the south causing carbon turnover to be faster 
the warmer the climate. The highest net carbon accumulation (i.e. forest growth) is normally found 
in conifer-dominated forests in the temperate region1.

Forest harvesting does not cause irreversible carbon losses from the soils2 
Forest harvesting is sometimes criticised as turning forestland from a carbon sink to a source of 
carbon emissions from the forest to the atmosphere. Such conclusions are often based on short-term 
studies (< c. 20 years) of a particular forest stand, dismissing the long-term carbon balance of boreal 
forest landscapes. A harvested site becomes a net carbon source as the trees are removed since they 
are responsible for the main carbon input to the soil via their photosynthesis. Left at the harvested 
site are the soil fungi and bacteria continuing to decompose the organic matter emitting carbon as 
part of their normal life process. As trees and other vegetation grow back their carbon uptake will 
soon exceed the carbon emissions from the soil organisms. How long it takes before a harvested site 
turn from being a carbon source to a sink depends on the fertility of the site. The more fertile the site 
the better the new trees grow and the faster the forest becomes a net carbon sink. Although this is 
the general pattern seen following harvesting in the forest, there are also sites that never become a 
net carbon source (due to extraordinary slow soil decomposition processes) or stay a carbon source 
for longer than 10-20 years (due to slow tree growth). It is important to consider that the balance is 
site-specific between the processes sequestering carbon and emitting carbon.

Nitrogen addition to boreal forest enhances soil carbon accumulation4 
Increased carbon stocks of soils are often encountered in forests amended with nitrogenous 
fertilizers or nitrogen deposition. The mechanisms through which nitrogen addition to forests 
increases amounts of soil carbon include increased input of dead plant material (litter) as the trees 
grow more when having access to more nitrogen. Also, nitrogen additions reduce the decomposition 
of the soil’s organic matter. This is due to that additional nitrogen reduces rates of late-stage 
decomposition of soil organic matter by suppressing the activity of wood (lignin)-degrading 
enzymes5. 
Forest soil’s nitrogen supply progressively limiting tree growth due to climate change6 
The balance between carbon and nitrogen is fundamental in directing the productivity of boreal 
forest soils. Climate change shifts the balance between carbon and nitrogen in boreal forests (as well 
as other cold-climate ecosystems) in two important ways. Firstly, the warmer climate causes the 
growing season to start earlier and end later, thereby increasing the vegetations’ growth and need to 
take up nitrogen. Secondly, as the CO2-concentration of the atmosphere is increasing, the 
photosynthetic rate of the vegetation is increasing. Photosynthetic capacity is linked to nitrogen 
supply as it is needed for building the Rubisco enzyme (protein) capturing the CO2 from the 
atmosphere. Although nitrogen availability has always been limiting to plant growth in northern 
boreal forests, the warmer climate combined with increasing CO2 concentrations turns it into 7 
limiting￼. This effect is much stronger in the north of Europe than further south due to the long-
term atmospheric nitrogen deposition that has been a major environmental problem for many 
decades although the situation has improved significantly for the most recent decades.

What are the relevant impacts of forestry on soils? 
Land-use impact soils, but as already argued, potential negative impacts are difficult to generalise 
across ecosystems. In the northern boreal forest, the main challenge for forestry regarding soils is 
local damages by harvesting machinery. Although driving in the forest takes place only two to three 
times in a century in the rotational forestry most practiced, local damages to soils and connected 
waters can be severe and remain for long if not sufficient precautions are taken. Wheel tracks may 
cause damming and silting of waterways damaging the water quality and biodiversity connected to 
waters. Tracks may also cause local emissions of methylmercury. The source of methylmercury is the 
inorganic mercury that is spread worldwide via the atmosphere from the burning of fossils. 
Methylmercury is formed from inorganic mercury by the action of anaerobic organisms that live in 
e.g. aquifers in soil. This methylation process converts inorganic mercury to highly poisonous 
methylmercury.  
There are national regulations in place to ensure the avoidance of soil and water damages in forestry, but still, severe 
damages do occur. Also, the warmer climate is challenging to forest harvest operators since the length of the soil frost 
season becomes shorter and sometimes forest soils do not freeze at all in winter. It is hence important to develop 
machinery that is less prone to causing damage to soils and to use digital tools and maps to better keep track of waterways 
underneath the ground not visible in the terrain.

Managing and restoring peatlands to improve forest landscapes  
Peatlands cover a large portion of the land area in the Nordic countries. In Finland about 30% of the 
land area is peatlands, in Sweden 15%. In Finland about half of the almost 10 million hectares of 
peatlands have been drained for forestry use. In Sweden two million hectares have been drained out 
of the in total 6.4 million hectares. Main threats to all peatland habitat types from draining are 
hydrological effects, including the effects of existing and cleared ditches in the surroundings of the 
mires and in their catchment areas.  
The restoration of drained peatlands aims to restore peatland ecosystems damaged or destroyed by 
drainage to a natural like condition8. This is achieved by filling ditches and removing trees to raise the 
water level. In most cases, the water level in peatlands is rapidly restored to that of undrained mires, 
but natural successional processes in peat and vegetation are triggered at a slower pace. In the short-
term restoration has potential adverse impacts on the environment. Disturbances in nutrient cycling 
caused by the blockage of ditches increase the nutrient loading to nearby waters. greenhouse gas 
emissions may also increase in the short term. Little information exists so far on the long-term 
restoration success. It is therefore not known whether restoration returns the slow processes typical 
for mires or whether it forms totally new mire types. 
Boreal forest soils are difficult to consider in some current frameworks for product 
environmental footprints (PEFs) 
In the EU, there are ongoing activities to come up with frameworks for PEF calculations common to 
all Member States. The LANCA model is an example of such a framework proposed by the EC for 
general use in EU. The model has been developed to link the classic LCA of products to the land use 
that takes place to produce the products' raw material. The central input to LANCA is various soil 
properties such as erosion susceptibility, cation buffering capacity and the land use impact on water 
supply and quality9. For boreal forest soils, these factors are relatively irrelevant to describe the 
environmental impact of forestry. This is because large-scale erosion is very unlikely (heavy rains are 
uncommon and forestry is not done on steep slopes), leaching of nutrients is limited due to the high 
content of organic material (humus) which effectively binds cations including nitrogen (as 
nitrification hardly ever occurs due to low soil pH) and the combination of annual precipitation and 
relatively low temperatures lead to an excess of water seen over an annual cycle. Although the idea 
of common PEF calculations across Europe may appear attractive from a consumer’s perspective, the 
importance of further regionalization of soil-based models like LANCA is key to a future successful 
application10. To ensure that Nordic forestry meet its standards relevant additions can be indicators 
related to the avoidance of soil damages in connection to forest waters and the restoration of 
peatlands previously drained for peat extraction.

The European Steel Association, EUROFER, welcomes the initiative undertaken by the EU 
Commission on the Soil health-protecting, sustainably managing and restoring EU soils as part of 
the Soil Health Law proposal announced in the EU Soil Strategy for 2030. 
The New Soil Strategy aims at enabling transformative change s in Europe, at the same time,  
promoting horizontal actions and it already indicated some of the aspects that should be 
considered to achieve these goals, among them: 
• improvement of the monitoring of soil quality  
• restoration of degraded soils and secure sufficient EU funding 
• adoption of sustainable soil management practices 
• development of knowledge and research 
• leading EU global action on soil, EU external action and development cooperation 
• adaptation and improvement of the EU policy framework to the context of today 
• acceleration of transition and behavioural change 
 
The Soil Strategy for 2030 already indicated some of the aspects that will be considered in the 
Impact Assessment , which  aims at meeting the existing EU medium - and long -term policy 
objectives for 2030 and 2050, and the vision that all soil ecosystems should be in healthy 
condition by 2050.  
EUROFER members are committed to reduce the ir environmental impact and to support the 
Green Deal through the different on -going Commissions initiatives, such as the Zero Pollution 
Ambition, the Circular Economy Action plan, the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability, and w e 
take the soil and water contamination prevention as one of our highest priorities, along with 
remediation measures in case of accidents or when a site is dismissed.  In this framework, 
EUROFER agrees on the main goals and different aspects that the Impact Assessment will tackle; 
however, we are of the opinion that when considering the following:

 options to identify, register and remediate contaminated sites, and to apply the polluter pays 
principle 
Some MSs already apply remediation measures by requiring that only land that is likely to cause 
a Significant Possibility of Significant Harm (SPOSH) is remediated.  Most other remediation 
interventions are addressed during planning and rely on a change in land use that triggers 
remediation to ensure future users are not affected. In other MSs, the Soil Protection legislation 
defines trigger and action values , which, if exceeded (e.g. due to the presence of a harmful soil 
change or site contamination), lead to an investigation and/or additional remediation measures. 
We believe that it is the responsibility of the National Authorities to identify the best procedure 
for contaminated land and also set which are the priorities, taking into account the local 
conditions, the land use and the risk to human health and environment. 
 
 
 options for a soil h ealth certificate for land transactions to provide land buyers with 
information on the key characteristics and health of the soils in the site they intend to purchase  
 
Soil and groundwater investigations are already conducted across the site in some MSs b efore 
sales. Some other MSs maintain registers of possible contaminated sites based on historical data 
only, which are not necessarily systematic investigations . However, according to Art. 21 of the 
IED, a requirement already exists to ‘’prepare and submit to the competent authority a baseline 
report before starting operation of an installation or before a permit for an installation is updated 
[…], which contains the  information necessary to determine the state of soil and groundwater 
contamination”. 
We are of the opinion that this practice is already happening under the IED and we fear that an 
additional ‘’soil health certificate’’ which reports the soil status would add an extra administrative 
burden on operators and overlap with the baseline report.

 options for a passport for excavated soil 
 
Anything that stops soil  from being a waste should be welcomed. A site should be required to 
adequately characterise any soil that it wish es to give to another site whether this is free of 
charge or a monetary transaction.   As long as it meets the requirements of the receiver site, it 
should be able to be moved and used as a product and not a waste.  For example, if a company’s 
business is to excavate and sell top soil, they are allowed to do so without any 
impediment.  However, if a site has an excess of soil, which it wishes to sell to another site even 
if it is chemically identical, i t is treated as a waste and there are numerous regulatory hurdles 
that must be overcome.  This prevents the sustainable use of soil and leads to some soils being 
landfilled even though they are perfectly suitable for sustainable re-use. The intended “passport 
for excavated soil” should prevent any classification as waste material. 
 
 options for soil literacy 
 
It is essential to launch a soil literacy engagement and awareness initiative. This might help to 
support also private finance and funding opportunit ies to support the soil remediation, stop 
desertification and land degradation. 
 
 
CONCLUSIONS 
EUROFER agrees with the purpose of this initiative as it is an opportunity that helps to achieve 
the goals of a zero-pollution ambition under the Green Deal, and helps to contribute to the post-
pandemic recovery . However, in addition to the comments provided above, we would like 
stakeholders’ participation in the development process of the new soil law to be ensured.

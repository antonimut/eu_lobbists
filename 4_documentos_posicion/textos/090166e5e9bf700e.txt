 
In the framework of its two -year sustainability policy programme, t he European Council of 
Young Farmers (CEJA) welcomes the possibility to contribute to the call for evidence launched 
by the European Commission in the perspective of the preparation of the EU Soil Health Law.  
BACKGROUND 
Because  healthy and well-functioning soils are fundamental to their production activity, but 
also to their capacity to deliver ecosystem services and play their part in the achievement of 
the Union’s climate objectives , young farmers are committed to  improve and maintain good 
soil quality across Europe. While soil has no borders,  and the effects of soil degradation are 
transboundary, soil characteristics differ greatly across Europe and most of the remedies are 
local, sometimes at the very level of the farm.  
As a result, CEJA welcomes the European Commission's strategic approach to soils , in 
particular when it comes to preservation of land against urban artificial development and the 
strong focus on knowledge development. However, some precisions are needed to better take 
into account farmers’ constraints and needs. For young farmers, a primary need remains the 
quality, but also the availability and accessibility of the land, in order  to allow them to 
implement long-term sustainable soil management practices. 
CHALLENGES 
• On-farm constraints: as many soil preservation practices deliver results on the long -
term, farmers are left with acti ng towards sustainability while managing short -term 
constraints on a productive, technical and regulatory front, but also from an economic and 
social point of view. 
 
• Generational renewal challenges : the difficulty  for young farmers  to secur e land 
ownership or long-term leases does not create adequate condition s to implement long -
term, time-consuming practices for soil preservation. 
 
• Knowledge gaps: the complexity of soils and their great variability from one area to 
another makes it difficult to identify soil degradation processes and target the appropriate 
set of measures. Knowledge gaps have led to soil preservation and enhancement seeming 
out of reach, or to the implementation of unsuccessful practices at farm-level.  
 
• Technological drivers : despite the potential of new technologies to a ccess soil 
information and implement nature-positive practices, their uptake is impeded by a lack of 
digitization, a gap between research and innovation and their on-field implementation, 
but also their high cost.

• Regulatory failures : the gre at differences existing between national legi slation as 
regards soil preservation and enhancement have led to a risk of distortions within the 
single market. Additionally, the focus on treating land management as a national and local 
issue has led to a lack of strategic vision at EU level on the preservation of agricultural land 
in the face of increased urban and artificial pressure. 
 
• Market failures and economic competition: the delivery of ecosystem services is not 
properly compensated by the market  currently, while the cost of soil degradation is not 
taken into account. This adds up to increased competition between farmers, land 
managers and other activities for land use . For example, the utilisation of soils for the 
production of renewable energies, in  particular photovoltaic and wind -power energy 
plants, also needs to be taken into cons ideration. Whilst suited to some land types and 
business models (e.g. urban areas, abandoned lands,  polluted areas, obsolete industrial 
zones and industrial roofs, private roofs), they should not  compromise the economic, 
social and environmental value of agricultural soils.  
 
PATHWAYS FOR ACTION 
Prior to elaborating on the framework initiative, the European Commission will be presenting 
an impact assessment. Such assessment is of great importance to address the potential trade-
offs and risks which may emerge in the impl ementation of sustainable soil management  
practices. Because soils are very diverse , due to different pedo -climatic conditions  and 
biological profiles, any regulatory approach on soils should avoid putting forward a one-size-
fits-all framework.  
1) Consider the multifunctionality of soils 
Soils have five main functions : primary productivity, climate mitigation, habitat for 
biodiversity, water regulation, and nutrient provision and cycling. In the context of a result -
based approach, it might be tempting to require from farmers to optim ise their performance 
in every single one of those five functions. However, as research has shown, each farm cannot 
achieve top performance in all five indicators. Different pedo-climatic parameters, histories 
with parent materials and specific biological processes give each soil different challenges and 
opportunities, making it impossible to opt for a one -size-fits-all approach. Instead, policy 
should aim to increase the different services and help farmers optimise the strengths of their 
soils while addressing the weaknesses and potential trade -offs emerging between different 
functions.  
2) Support farmers in preserving and enhancing soil quality 
In order to face  soil degradation processes, such as  erosion, compaction and desertification, 
and tap into the potential of soils when it comes to climate mitigation, a set of tools must be 
provided to farmers. Advisory services funded by public authorities should be made available 
to establish a soil diagnosis at the level of the far m, accompanying farmers towards the most 
adapted practices. Additionally, agri-environment schemes, such as eco-schemes and AECMs 
in the ne w CAP, are considered the most relevant instrument to avoid the one-size-fits-all 
approach. Their design must be characterised in the f irst stage  by an obl igation of means 
rather than results, and cover implementation costs and income fore gone in order to 
encourage farmers  to subscribe to them, as well as cover tailored practices providing 
advantages for the environment. 
 
 
3) Preserve land against urban and artificial development 
Agricultural land is under pressure  due to urba n sprawl, urbanisation and artificialisation , 
ultimately detrimental both to production capacity and s ustainable soil management.  Soils 
being a finite resource, taking hundreds of years to form  and bei ng easily degr adable, the 
effects of urbanisation and artificialisation appear long-term and complex to address. In this 
perspective, CEJA is fully supportive of the objective of no net land take by 2050, for which a 
definition will need to be set up at EU level.  To preserve agricultural land and its capacity to 
deliver on production and eco-system services, as well as to reduce competition land access, 
the land planning hierarchy proposed by the Commission appears as a useful tool. It should 
be accompanied by concrete actions to address those subsidies at national and lo cal levels 
encouraging to turn agricultural land into built land. It is also important to monitor land use 
change on the basis of publicly available data.  
4) Invest in knowledge and research  
Because there cannot be a research er on every farm, knowledge and skills are required for 
farmers to asses s the specific needs of their soils, implement the most -fitted practices and 
measure results. Initiatives such as the “Test your Soil for Free” appear promising and need to 
be deployed on a larger scale , so that farmers can reinforce the knowledge of their soils and  
decide what the most adapted practices are to achieve sustainable soil management. Peer-to-
peer learning , through which farmers share  local knowledge among themselves,  and 
collaborative approaches, including with science practioners, are powerful levers to be used to 
promote tested and profitable management practices . Research and innovation  must be 
further supported to ensure a science-based approach to soils at farm level and better inform 
decision-making at policymaking level. In particular, is essential to develop reliable tools and 
unlock technological drivers. A prior ity, from this perspective, is to  identify and reinforce 
alternatives to mineral fe rtilisers which are both environmentally safer and economically 
viable. The valorisation of organic matter an d regional management systems should also be 
further promoted.  
5) Success drivers for policymaking 
An EU framework to soils requires first and foremost the involvement of all levels of 
power, from local to EU authorities. This is particular ly true for certain key soil functions, 
such as water purification and management, which go far beyond the sole environment of the 
farm, involving neighbouring industries, communities and regional and local authorities. It is 
also true for land planning , which is mostly a local competence , with wide impacts on the 
Union’s capacity to maintain sufficient agricultural land.  
Secondly, the Soil Health Law shoul d enable sustainable soil management while 
empowering farmers to maintai n a sustainable economic activity  and fully 
taking account their constraints. This requires to raise obstacles to investment and land 
access, but also to make sure that the law is comprehensive, adaptative, practically applicable 
and not adding administrative burden on the shoulders of farmers. Thus, in the context of the 
restoration of peatlands, those farmers cultivating on such land should be compensated if that 
possibility is taken away from them. 
Last but not least, given the increasing number of priorities and instruments to be financed by 
the CAP, it will be essential to reflect on additional streams to remunerate ecosystem 
services delivered by farmers. From this perspective, both the use of public and market tools 
must be considered.

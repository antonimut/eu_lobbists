Call for evidence for an Impact Assessment 
Soil health – protecting, sustainably managing and restoring EU soils 
 
Yara would like to thank the E uropean Commission for the opportunity to contribute to the preparat ory 
work for the EU Soil Health Law proposal. Yara takes a long-term view on soil health. By providing farmers 
with tools and services enabling a precise and balanced nutrient management in combination with suita-
ble fertilizing products, we help them preserve and improve the fertility and health of their soils, a benefit 
that carries forward to future generations and ensures food security. 
 
1. The potential legal framework for soil protection at EU level needs to ensure policy 
coherence and avoid one-size-fits-all methodologies 
 
Soil is a very complex subject, due to its heterogeneity, multifunctionality and its relations with other en-
vironmental aspects, like water and air, which leads to the sensitivity of the soil protection discussions. 
Soils have diverse chemical, physical and biological properties. Therefore, they differ in their responses 
to management practices, their ability to deliver ecosystem services, as well as their resilience to disturb-
ance and vulnerability to degradation 1. Due to t his variability and constant evolution of soils, an all -en-
compassing and scientifically standardized framework to define a “healthy soil”  does not yet exist. The 
future proposal for the EU Soil Health Law, therefore, needs to avoid one-size-fits-all and one-out-
all-out methodologies. Instead, it should enable the implementation of measures, which must be 
best suited for national/local situations (e.g. incentivize best practices, provide access to advice 
and enable knowledge sharing).   
It is important that the future EU Soil Health Law proposal ensures policy coherence and avoids double 
regulation as well as additional administrative burden. For example, when it comes to industrial sites, 
existing EU environmental legislation provides sufficient mechanisms to ensure the protection of soil/land. 
Adequate safeguards are already in place in the EU, a.o. via the Industrial Emissions Directive (IED), the 
Environmental Impact Assessment Directive (EIA) and the Seveso Directive. In addition, the current revi-
sion of the IED should be considered as prevailing. The same should apply with regards to the presence 
of heavy metals in fertilizing products, which are regulated by the Regulation (EU) 2019/1009. The EU 
Soil Health Law proposal should, therefore, focus on integration with existing EU environmental and ag-
ricultural legislation and build upon the subsidiarity principle, regardless of the chosen policy option.  
Possible direction could be to encourage EU Member States to develop National Soil Health Plans 
where the main threats on national and/or regional level would be identified, and actions estab-
lished to remedy those via existing legislation. National Soil Health Plans could be designed in close

2. The interlinkages between soil health and nutrient cycling must be recognized 
 
As part of the impact assessment, plant nutrients, either from CE-marked fertilizing products (accord-
ing to EU regulation 2019/1009) or from manure and recycled nutrients, should be recognized as major 
and positive contributors to the nutrition of soil organisms, the improvement of soil organic matter 
content and the sequestration of soil carbon, as well as to maintenance of the physical soil prop-
erties. Plant nutrients are essential for food production, to feed crops to nourish humans, guarantee qual-
ity yields and to ensure soil fertility in the long run. They are not per se a source of diffuse soil pollution; 
it only happens when plant nutrients are not applied according to good practice  and to the needs of the 
crops. 
Empirical evidence suggests that applying optimal amounts of required nutrients, through e.g., a combi-
nation of mineral and organic fertilizers, promotes plant growth and thus increases the amount of residues 
and root biomass added to soil 2. Especially roots have a key role in promoting soil health, as they are 
increasing the carbon content in deeper soil layers and feeding microorganisms via root exudates . The 
extra biomass produced based on nutrient inputs into the cropping system enables to increase soil or-
ganic carbon and to improve soil structure, nutrient supply and moisture retention, thereby improving the 
conditions that increase crop yields. The Carbon/Nitrogen ratio of organic matter in soils is about 10:1 3, 
to sequester 1t of additional carbon in a soil therefore requires about 100kg additional nitrogen. In addi-
tion, a meta-analysis of data from long-term trials from around the world concludes that the long-term 
effect of mineral fertilizer on microbial  biomass is pH dependent and in the long run  mineral fertilizer 
increase microbial biomass in cropping systems while responses of specific microbial groups depend on 
environment and crop management 4.

• Reducing nutrient losses by Improving Nitrogen Use Efficiency (NUE)  
 
Nutrients are exported from the field when crops are harvested. To maintain soil fertility for sustainable 
crop yields and quality, nutrients exported from the field with the harvest and lost to the environment must 
be replaced by other organic and/or mineral sources to avoid soil nutrient mining. 
The concept of Nitrogen Use Efficiency (NUE) describes the relationship between nitrogen supply to a 
crop and nitrogen removal from the field by the harvest. NUE can be described as the percentage of the 
nitrogen contained in the crop (kg) of the nitrogen applied (kg). The nitrogen applied is essentially com-
posed of mineral and organic fertilization, biological nitrogen fixation, the nitrogen deposition and nitrogen 
residues from cultivation in the previous year. The European Nitrogen Expert Panel, a network of 
European scientists, decision makers, and representatives from the agricultural sector and indus-
try, does not recommend a general reduction in nitrogen quantities as a solution to effectively 
prevent nitrogen losses. In fact, less is not always more. It is seen as more appropriate to optimize 
the ratio between crop yield and nitrogen supply. A high rate of nitrogen fertilization that produces a 
high yield can be just as efficient and have limited losses compared with a low amount of nitrogen with a 
low yield.  
If we look at the current situation in the EU, there is still possibility to improve Nitrogen Use Efficiency. 
The Nitrogen Use Efficiency indicator in EU -28 in 2014 was 65% 7, while the European Nitrogen 
Expert panel8 recommends a NUE target of between 75% and 90%9. The most recent research also 
shows that to achieve surface water quality targets without crop production losses, average NUE needs 
to increase from 64% to 78%, whereas achieving groundwater targets only requires a modest increase 
from 64% to 67%. In hotspot areas, however,  redistribution of N inp uts and improved N management 
might need to be complemented by other strategies, such as reducing crop demand by e.g. cutting food 
waste10.  
Yara’s own research shows that by using best practices and solutions that exist today, European farmers 
can already improve nutrient use efficiency by at least 20% , increase yields and incomes by 5 -7% and 
reduce their carbon footprint related to mineral fertilization up to 20%.

highest-producing regions, Europe's agricultural production will probably need to increase in the future 
for several reasons11:  
1) The first reason is geo-political: since 1990, Europe has shifted from food self-sufficiency to import 
dependency with many European countries currently producing less than 70% of their domestic 
demand. This import dependency makes Europe vulnerable in case of scarcity on global crop 
markets12.  
2) Secondly, the current crop yield growth rates are  likely insufficient to meet th e increasing world 
demand and if growing demands cannot be met by domestic production and pressures on global 
food markets increase, it is likely that trade with Europe will play a role in meeting demands13.  
3) The third reason is to avoid spill-over effects: at constant global demand, a reduction in European 
food production would shift production to other regions with potentially less strict environmental 
regulations14, thus, effectively relocating environmental damage. In addition, the recent Joint Re-
search Centre (JRC) report15 on “Modelling environmental and climate ambition in the agricultural 
sector with the CAPRI model” stated that meeting the Farm to Fork target for reduction of gross 
nutrient surplus is one of the two main drivers behind the reductions in production and that there-
fore special attention needs to be paid to how these targets are implemented and accompanied 
by Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) and other measures. 
 
Due to the above , reducing nutrient losses in the EU should be approached through improving 
Nitrogen Use Efficiency (NUE) in order to avoid yield losses, outsourcing of production to regions 
with lower standards and deterioration in soil fertility. The Soil Health Law proposal should avoid 
any general targets on reduction in nutrient use. 
 
• Enhancing phosphorus efficiency to protect water and biodiversity 
 
Next to nitrogen, phosphorus is the other essential plant nutrient covered by the Farm to Fork target of 
halving losses by  2030. This is particularly relevant for reducing excess nutrients’ surface runoff from 
fields. A strategy to minimize phosphorus losses at field level is the proper incorporation into 
soils. The application method of mineral phosphorus sources, combined with a better utilization of phos-
phorus in on-farm organic sources, contributes to increasing phosphorus use efficiency and to reducing 
losses.  
Yara’s R&D hubs in Germany and Finland have con ducted field trials comparing different application 
methods. Banding phosphate containing fertilizers close to seeds at planting increases phospho-
rus use efficiency, in comparison with surface application (followed by soil incorporation). Place-
ment of phosphorus in a band can reduce losses by reducing its exposure to soil microorganisms and

4. Balanced crop nutrition is one of the key building blocks of the sustainable use of soil 
in agriculture 
 
Best practice crop nutrition management is at the core of all good agricultural practices. Farmers need to 
be enabled to further improve their practices via adequate toolbox ranging from agronomic advice to 
financial support, as well as farm management systems and tools (such as precision farming), in order to 
sustain the different soil ecosystem functions.

delivers a more sustainable food value chain. 
To achieve our ambition, we have taken the lead in developing digital farming tools for precision farming

economy, driving a green transition of shipping, fertilizer production and other energy intensive industries. 
Founded in 1905 to solve the emerging famine in Europe, Yara has established a unique position as the 
industry’s only global crop nutrition company. We operate an integrated business model with around 
17,000 employees and operations in over 60 countries.

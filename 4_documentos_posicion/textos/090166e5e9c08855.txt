16 March 2022 
 
 
 
This note is intended for t he European Commission in its Call for Evidence  regarding the new legislative 
proposal on soil health.  
It presents concrete proposals and applications to develop a passport for excavated soil.  
The European Commission has identified the issue of excavated soils in several Communications “The New 
Circular Economy Action Plan”, “The Zero Pollution Action Plan” and “The EU Soil Strategy for 2030”. In 
the latter, the Commission states it will “assess the need and potential for legally binding provisions for a 
passport for excavated soil”.  
Hesus welcomes the Commission’s awareness on this issue and considers that regulatory changes are needed 
at the EU level to reach this objective.  
- Hesus believes a passport for excavated soils is necessary in order to map soil resources and improve 
soil tracing.  
- Hesus calls the Commission to r evise the Waste Framework Directive to set a binding  material 
recovery target for excavated soils within the objectives regarding C&D waste.

2. A passport for excavated soils 
 
Hesus welcomes the Commission’s objective announced in the Soil Strategy to investigate the 
streams of excavated soils in the EU and benchmark the market situation by 2023.  
Moreover, Hesus strongly supports the Commission’s policy option to introduce a passport for 
excavated soil in the new legislative proposal on soil health. 
 
Hesus’s suggestions 
A passport for excavated soils is necessary to promote their circular, safe and sustainable 
use in the EU. Such passport should encompass :  
 
1) Soil diagnosis 
 
A lack of soil diagnosis leads to a situation where the majority of polluted soils are not properly 
characterised before their reuse. Diagnosis is essential to assess the quality of soils and ensure 
their compatibility with receiving sites, whether they are treatment centres, storage centres or 
reuse sites. Such diagnosis must include:  
1. background history of the site  to identify the potential sources of pollution 
linked to its past and current activities;  
2. soil sampling and their analysis; 
3. interpretation of the analysis to identify potential for reuse.  
 
This would allow the passport to include the chemical characteristics of soils and their origins.  
Soil diagnosis matches three policy options that the Commission is considering in its Call for 
Evidence :  
- Indicators for soil health and their range of values  
- Monitoring and reporting on the condition of soil. 
- Options to identify, register and remediate contaminated sites.  
 
2) Soil tracing 
 
The lack of tracing of non -hazardous waste leads to sub-optimal management of excavated 
soils as the latter are regularly sent to unsuitable waste storage facilities or even illegal 
5  
embankments. This results in additional environmental and financial costs , such as 
unnecessary CO2 emissions and spreading soil pollution. 
A passport with soil tracing would ensure that soils are actually transferred to the correct 
destination while giving the precise location of the land deposit.   
A concrete application would be to make site operators indicate the destination of excavated 
soils that it manages. Such information would then be indicated in the passport.  
 
Soil tracking matches two policy options that the Commission is considering in its Call for 
Evidence :   
- Monitoring and reporting on the condition of soil.  
- Requirements for the sustainable use of soil.  
 
3) Digital dimension  
 
Monitoring and tracing excavated soils is complex because of the volumes involved and a lack 
of common framework for soil data collection. For now, this is carried out using paper 
documents which do not allow live -tracing and cause several problems (loss o f papers, more 
limited reliability).  
 
The use of digital tools can help industrial and institutional players overcome those difficulties. 
The outputs include live-tracing and monitoring, data sharing among stakeholders through 
online platforms and optimised logistics. 
 
Hesus’ case study 
 
 
Hesus has developed several digital tool s to optimise excavated soil management while 
ensuring their traceability and sustainable use.  
 
These digital tools are based on 3 pillars. 
- Characterization 
Hesus establishes a soil reuse plan. It is based on an automated chemical analysis of soils, on 
the basis of the data transferred by the user, which is then converted into a generic reading 
grid. A final summary by use classes allows to easily distinguish the appropriate solutions and 
the possible synergies with other worksites.  
- Compatibility 
Based on the characterization of the soils from the donor site, Hesus certifies the compatibility 
of the added soils physico-chemical quality with the receiving site and the considered usage. 
6  
- Traceability  
Once the best solutions have been identified, Hesus ensures the traceability of inflows/outflows 
from connected IoT sensors installed on each vehicle  conveying soils. This guarantees that 
soils are actually going to the correct destination  as the sensors geolocate the vehicles and the 
precise location of the land deposit. 
Adequate information is sent in real time to site operators, allowing them to remotely supervise 
the flows of excavated soils.  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Example from Member States 
 
 
Several Member States have adopted a set of regulatory measures on excavated soils, both from 
a waste management and an environmental protection perspective.   
 
Those measures share a joint approach that focuses on the monitoring of excavated soils from 
the excavation to the application site, while ensuring they do not constitute a threat to the 
environment and the population.

3. Excavated soils  
 
Hesus believes excavated soils should be at the heart of the EU’s legislations  (the new 
legislative proposal on soil health and the Waste Framework Directive), both for their recovery 
potential and their climate impact. Several elements support such a request: 
 
1) It is the largest waste stream in the EU 
 
Excavated soils are the largest waste fraction in the European Union. In 2018, they accounted 
for more than 530 million tonnes 1, meaning 4 times more than municipal waste! The 
European Commission confirms this figure in its Call for Evidence2.  
 
2) Excavated soils have a high recovery potential  
 
The construction industry is the largest consumer of raw materials as well as the largest 
producer of waste in the EU3. 
Using excavated soils as secondary raw material would constitute a major lever to redu ce the 
construction industry pressure on raw materials. Indeed, excavated soils can be used to:  
- supply aggregates and/or backfill construction sites; 
- create access ramps to infrastructures, such as bridges, instead of using quarry resources; 
- replace polluted soils to rehabilitate polluted sites;  
- rehabilitate agricultural soils to make the land cultivable again.  
Non-polluted excavated soils can be reused dir ectly, as such, on a compatible site.  Polluted 
excavated soils go to a treatment centre. Once decontaminated, they can also be reused. 
To demonstrate the high recovery potential of excavated soils, Hesus subsidiary Soltracing, 
with other partners such as the French Ministry of the Environment, ADEME4, and the BRGM5, 
has developed the GeoBaPa project. It aims at establishing a mapping of the chemical 
characteristics of soils in the Ile-de-France and Normandy regions.

3) Recovery of excavated soils contributes to climate change mitigation 
 
According to the European Commission , GHG  emissions from material extraction, 
manufacturing of construction products, construction and renovation of buildings are estimated 
at 5-12% of total national GHG emissions6.  
A circular management of excavated soils can significantly contribute to decrease these 
emissions as soil management accoun ts for between 2%-3% of a construction site’s carbon 
footprint. 
 
The major optimisation levers are:  
- Empty loads avoidance thanks to logistics optimization. 
- Optimization of route distance thanks to the use of local solutions.  
- Raw material consumption avoi dance thanks to circular economy (soils reused from 
other construction sites). 
 
Most of today’s managements of excavated soils follow non-optimal market practices in terms 
of climate change mitigation ( lack of circular practices ; extraction of raw materials for 
backfilling operations; empty runs from delivery points).

Annex I – Examples from Member States 
 
1. France 
France adopted a Legislation on waste and for a circular economy  (Loi anti-gaspillage pour 
une économie circulaire – AGEC) in February 2020.  
 
One of the objectives of the AGEC Law was to reinforce the French waste traceability system, 
in particular for excavated soils.  
 
Article 117 of the AGEC Law extends the waste traceability regime to excavated soil . As 
such, people who produce, import, export, process, collect  or transport excavated soil s must 
make specific data available to the administration : 
- The quantity, nature, origin of these excavated soils and their destination; 
- If applicable, the means of transport and the envisaged process of treatment or disposal. 
 
 
The decree No. 2021-321 relating to the traceability of waste and excavated soils that specifies 
the regime applicable to the traceability of excavated soils was adopted in March 2021.  
- It created a centralized electronic database , called the National Register of 
Excavated Soil , where p roducers and managers of excavated soil s must register the 
information required in Article 117 for any transfer carried out outside the excavation 
site.  
 
2. Belgium 
 
In Belgium, Flanders and Wallonia have implemented legal measures regarding traceability 
of excavated soils.  
 
Flanders 
 
The Soil Decree  (1996) is the heart  of the soil remediation  policy in Flanders. Its original 
purpose was to remediate contaminated land in Flanders and to prevent new contamination  by 
introducing a soil certificate, with indications on the degree of soil contamination , for anyone 
who wishes to conclude an agreement on the transfer of land.  
 
The soil certificate is delivered by OVAM, the Flemish Public Waste Agency.  
 
This decree was revised in 2006  and enteredinto force in 2008, to introduce regulations  on 
excavated soils. It imposed:  
- A soil quality survey 
- Conditions for the use of the excavated soil, which depend on soil quality.  
- A tracking procedure (permit; statement of receipt after the soil transfer; soil report 
for the client) guarantying the administrative follow-up of the active soil flows. OVAM 
is also acting as the supervising authority in the regulation on the use of excavated soil.  
 
 
 
 
13  
Wallonia 
 
The decree of 5th July 2018, relating to the management and traceability of soils  (the so -
called BWR Terres) entered into force on 1st May 2020. It introduced several requirements :  
- A sampling (by a soil expert or a registered sampler) and a systematic control of the 
soil must be carried out on the site of the excavated area 
- Traceability of excavated soil, which allows it to be tracked from the site of origin to 
the receiving site, passing if necessary through temporary storage sites, collection 
centers or land treatment. 
- It also created Walterre, the concession holder of a public service under the supervision 
of the Walloon government , as the operator in charge of quality control missions for 
soils and soil movements.  
- Finally, the decree stipulate d that all steps must be taken digitally. As such, Walterre 
uses an IT platform that centralises all requests and allows the soil to be traced.  
 
3. The Netherlands 
 
The Soil Quality Decree (2007) regulates soil management in the Netherlands. It foresees that 
an environment declaration is mandatory regarding the quality of excavated soils.  
 
A person who uses excavated soil must know the qualities of these soils determined by an 
authorized institution. Proof of the quality of these soils and other information (ownership; 
excavation and application sites; amount) are indicated in the environmental declaration and 
reported in a national register.  
 
4. Germany 
 
Germany adopted an Ordinance introducing a Substitute Building Materials Ordinance in July 
2021. It will come into force in August 2023.  
 
This Ordinance, which amends several core legislative texts related to soil protection and 
waste management (Federal Soil Protection ; Contaminated Sites and Landfill Ordinances), 
regulates the use of C&D waste nationwide (in the past, this competence was divided between 
the 16 Länders).  
 
The Ordinance primarily aims to protect people and the environment (in particular the 
protection of soil and groundwater ) from pollutants  while ensuring the highest possible 
recycling rates for C&D waste.  
 
It introduces requirements for the monitoring  of excavated mate rials, including excavated 
soils, such as :  
- Obligation to investigate the excavated material 
- Assessment of the findings of the investigation 
- Classification of soil material and excavated material 
- Indicate the above information into a document to be kept for 5 years from the time it 
is issued.  
 
It also mandates the party which uses excavated soil to indicate its whereabouts from the 
initial placing on the market to the installation in a technical structure via a delivery note 
14  
containing several information (material class ; distributor ; volume ; delivery date ; etc.).  
 
5. Poland 
 
According to the Act on Waste (2012), soil excavated during construction works  constitute 
waste and an appropriate classification of waste should be made by the waste producer.  
 
Poland´s regulation (2016) on the assessment of contamination of land surface of the minister 
of the environment lays down detailed requirements to monitor the quality of the earth’s surface, 
including: 
- Activities that are likely to cause historical pollution of the earth’s surface. 
- Reference methodologies for ground and soil pollution testing. 
 
6. Spain 
 
In Spain, the National Waste Management Framework Plan ( PEMAR) for 2016 -2022 set 
quantitative objectives and harmonized criteria regarding the recovery of excavated soils.

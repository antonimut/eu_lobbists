In response to the Call for Evidence: ‘Soil health – protecting, sustainably managing and 
restoring EU soils’

The European Biostimulants Industry Council (EBIC) welcomed the new EU Soil Strategy for 2030 
adopted in 2021, including developing a legislative proposal for EU targets to protect, ensure the 
sustainable use of, and restore soils in Europe. The call for evidence correctly outlines a number of 
the critical services delivered by soil ecosystems and how much of our agricultural production and 
environmental services rely on healthy soils. 
However, EBIC urges the European Commission to consider wildly varying soil properties – not just 
across Europe, but sometimes within the same Member State – and the importance of tailoring soil 
protection and restoration measures to different physical, chemical, and biological compositions of 
soils. In particular, any legislative proposal should treat agricultural soils separately from other soils as 
they are subject to substantially different uses and applications than other soils. 
Plant biostimulants help to enrich soil biodiversity while reducing soil degradation and erosion. As the 
European organisation representing the biostimulant industry in Europe, EBIC would like to offer its 
insights on this Call for Evidence on Soil Health, including where and how plant biostimulants may 
contribute to the impact of such a legislative proposal. 
EBIC will continue to engage with the following steps in the consultation stage and is eager to 
contribute to this legislative process over the coming months. Please do not hesitate to contact us for 
more information and details relating to plant biostimulants and soil health, particularly in improving 
the nutrient use efficiency of plants. 
Plant biostimulants help to improve the health of agricultural soils 
 Plant biostimulants help farmers reduce nutrient losses in the environment, contributing to one 
of the aspects outlined in the call for evidence for the Soil Strategy (i.e., “measures that can 
contribute to reducing nutrient losses by at least 50% without deterioration of soil fertility…”). 
o EBIC strongly supports highlighting this vital consideration for soil health within the 
legislative proposal for this strategy and identifying agricultural practices that could 
contribute to this. 
 
 Plant biostimulants enhance soil biodiversity by adding beneficial microorganisms to the soil 
environment. By strengthening the growth of roots that remain in the soil after harvest, some 
biostimulant products add carbon to soils as this biomass breaks down. 
 
 Many plant biostimulants enhance root growth, which helps reduce soil erosion from both wind 
and run-off and improves soil structure and, therefore, water retention capacity of soils. 
 
 Plant biostimulants increase tolerance to abiotic stresses, such as extreme and volatile climate 
conditions, enhancing plant resilience and crop quality in the context of climate change.  
Improving the climate resilience of crops will reduce soil erosion, desertification, and land 
degradation. 
EBIC would call for the EU legislative proposal on soil health to include concrete policy options to 
promote and support innovative plant nutrition solutions such as plant biostimulants which could deliver 
some of the benefits listed above to agricultural soils.

However, for plant biostimulants to contribute to the objectives outlined in this call for evidence, the

Coherence crucial across EU policies 
There must be coherence between all relevant policy frameworks that govern agricultural production, 
inputs, and land use to preserve and enhance soil health across Europe. This includes the Fertilising 
Products Regulation (FPR), Common Agricultural Policy (CAP), Organic Production Regulation, Farm to 
Fork Strategy, Biodiversity Strategy, Circular Economy Strategy, the new EU soil strategy, and more. 
But this coherence needs to go beyond soundbites and include impact assessments of specific measures 
to understand the trade-offs and make informed decisions.  
Agricultural soils are regulated by different regulatory frameworks, including those which cover 
agricultural inputs (e.g., fertilising products). The technical regulatory frameworks also concern soil 
health: in the specific case of fertilising products, the Regulation (EU) 2019/1009 defines safety limits 
in terms of contaminants and pathogens and quality provisions to preserve agricultural soils. These 
provisions should be considered when elaborating all legislative activities on soil health to avoid 
incoherence, overlaps, barriers to uptake, or conflicting regulatory requirements. 
Ensuring a Single Market for all relevant products should also be central to policy coherence. This is 
currently not the case for organic production, where acceptance of agricultural inputs varies from one 
Member State to another and from one certifying body to another. Such discrepancies across the EU 
currently represent a hindrance to practical measures for soil fertility management, particularly in the 
context of the EU’s Farm to Fork objectives for increased organic agricultural land. 
Foster innovation 
The Commission also needs to consider fostering innovation and ensuring that innovative products are 
accessible to farmers. In this light, we would like to call attention to the particular needs for beneficial 
microorganisms in agriculture  for enhanced soil biodiversity . The Fertilising Products Regulation 
recognises microbial plant biostimulants for the first time. However, at the moment, there is no clear 
pathway for having additional microorganisms – beyond a very short list – approved for use in EU 
Fertilising Products. Considering the vast potential of beneficial microorganisms and rapid developments 
in this area of R&D, it would be a missed opportunity for enhanced soil health and sustainability if the 
EU did not find a way for companies to bring these products to market soon. Therefore, we strongly 
suggest the rapid development of safe and efficient pathways to add new microorganisms to this list to 
unlock the full potential of microbial plant biostimulants for soil health and achieve the ambitious Farm 
to Fork Strategy objectives. 
Training and Education needed 
The CAP should also ensure adequate training, advisory, and incentives for farmers to learn about and 
adopt sustainable, site-specific practices to improve soil health, such as integrated plant nutrition and 
soil fertility management, within conventional and organic farming. This is especially important in light 
of recent research showing that farmers have more demanding standards for bio-based fertilising 
products fertilisers than for mineral fertilisers, yet are not willing to pay as much for bio -based 
fertilisers.

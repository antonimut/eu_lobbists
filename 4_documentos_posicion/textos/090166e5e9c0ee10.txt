Euromines acknowledges the main goals and concerns that the initiative “New Soil Strategy” aims to tackle, 
especially to address potential soil degradation in a comprehensive way.  
The soils in Europe are characterized by a great variety of different soil types in different  geographical regions 
of Europe with differing natural characteristics such as soil fertility, storage of water, providing home for flora 
and fauna and natural mineral contents  or a buffer for anthropogenic substances. Also, the anthropogenic 
pressures on soils are often very different per geographical regions and countries. It is our opinion that creation 
of uniform EU soil standards will be a highly difficult and controversial matter. Currently, many Member States 
have already developed their specific (risk based) soil legislations since the eighties.  
For consideration of an EU soil regulation, we believe that a proactive dialog between the mining sector and EU 
decision makers is vitally beneficial to better define opportunities and challenges in fulfilling the objectives of 
the EU Soil Strategy for 2030. 
In consideration of this background, we would like to share some comments about an  emerging EU Soil  
Legislation: 
▪ Soil protection in most EU member states is also already comprehensively regulated by specific laws and 
regulations. A coherent and integrating European Soil Protection  Framework must take this fact into 
account. Therefore, any overlapping or double regulation (both National and EU) should be avoided, e.g. 
existing national regulations for remediation have to be acknowledged. 
▪ As far as issues are already covered by other specific environmental EU legislation s (i.e. Water 
Framework Directive, Natura 2000 Directive, Mine Waste Directive 2006/21/EC), there is no need and 
no gap for including them into the scope of a new EU soil regulation. This would lead to overlapping , 
unclear or even contradictory regulations. 
▪ The extraction of raw materials can by nature also have diverse impacts on soil. These impacts are today 
already regulated on EU member states level by national soil, environmental and mining legislations. 
These regulations are the basis for carrying out raw material´s extraction when at the same time 
ensuring environmental and soil protection.  
▪ Any EU soil legislation needs to consider the demand for the extraction of raw materials in the EU – at 
present and in future.   
▪ With a still growing population, need for growing residential/industrial land use, and a crucial demand 
for European raw materials, a “no net land take” concept cannot be a target to aim for. In our opinion, 
the focus should shift to the goal of “sustainable land use”. 
▪ Stakeholder participation in any development of an EU priority list for pollutants in soils and soil health 
indicators is strongly recommended.

▪ A targeted and proportionate risk-based approach should be considered to enable the full (re)use of 
(restored) degraded soil at industrial used lands. The question if a contaminated soil should be 
remediated and to what extend or just to be secured to protect human health and environment 
should be based on a risk-based impact assessment. This is especially relevant for brown field sites. 
Besides environmental and technical aspects, also economic ramifications should be considered when 
planning any remediation. 
▪ For handling of contaminated soils, a risk-based approach is needed that also takes the 
respective/intended land use into consideration and cost-benefit assessment of chemical clean-up 
residues to be accounted for.  
▪ A clear legally/technically sound distinction must be made between remediation and restoration. 
▪ “Soil biodiversity” is a very complex system that needs to be better understood and scientifically and 
legally clarified to define applicable biodiversity protection measures. 
▪ Description of limited/nihil responsibility of environmental legacy sites (brownfields)  should be clearly 
defined for circumstances where current site operators are not responsible for ancient and/or naturally 
occurring soil degradation.  
▪ The importance of spatial variations in local and regional natural background levels should be taken into 
consideration in assessments of anthropogenic contributions and bioavailability of pollutants in soil. 
▪ The past experiences in implementation of land remediation in Europe and USA should be taken into 
detailed review for relevance and efficiencies in development of the emerging EU Soil Legislation. 
▪ The assessment of soil contamination should also consider the future use of the site. So-called "brown 
fields", i.e. previously used sites, should be considered differently than use of new sites, so-called "green 
fields".  
▪ Regarding the sustainable use of agricultural land, “balanced fertilisation” should be included in the 
considerations. Besides over-fertilization, soils degradation can also be caused by nutrient depletion 
(when nutrient extraction by crops is not replaced by fertilisation). Moreover , nitrogen losses can 
minimised by a balanced supply of all plant nutrients. Mining plays an import ant role in obtaining a  
balanced fertilisation by providing potassium, magnesium and other nutrients which often have limited 
availability in soils.  
▪ As a responsible stakeholder, E uromines is ready and willing to contribute its expertise on the subject 
matter in development of the emerging EU Soil Legislation

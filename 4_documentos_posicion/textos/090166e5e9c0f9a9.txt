Syngenta contribution to the Call for Evidence for an impact assessment for an 
EU Soil Health Law 
At Syngenta we warmly welcome this opportunity to provide our perspective and input to the Call of 
Evidence for an impact assessment for an EU Soil Health Law. For Syngenta, soil health is a key priority as 
healthy and productive soil is essential for life on earth.  
Soil health is defined as ‘the ability of soil to sustain productivity, diversity, and environmental services of 
terrestrial ecosystems (ITPS, 2020). Healthy soils provide public goods and beneficial ecosystem services: 
food and feed production; habitat for plants and wildlife; water storage, purification and regulation; 
greenhouse gas regulation; natural products to combat disease (e.g., antibiotics).  
Soil is the fundamental resource that our customers, the farmers, are stewards of, and rely on, to produce 
food, energy and fibre. Syngenta is committed to playing a full part in protecting and enhancing soil health 
around the world, sustaining soils for the generations to come and regenerating soil where possible.  
Syngenta shares the  European Commission’s view on the importance to bring soil in a healthy state by 
2050.  
Below we outline some key elements which we believe should be addressed in the EU Soil Health Law. 
EU Soil Health Law to incentivize Good Agricultural Practices in building on existing various regulations  
Good agricultural practices that are broadly recognized as being key to good soil management include:  
• reduced tillage and disturbance of soil (including min-till, strip-till and no-till); 
• cover of soil throughout the year (including cover crops, catch crops and mulching);  
• Maximize functional biodiversity;  
• Rational and optimized fertilization programs, and 
• Crop diversification.  
An EU Soil Health Law should encourage these practices by  building on the various EU directives, 
regulations and policies touch ing on soil health,  while avoiding regulatory overlap. We recommend the 
use of result-based approaches within soil laws, which permit a higher degree of flexibility and adaptation 
to local conditions than action-based requirements. 
EU Soil Health Law to support farmers  
Growers are the center of soil health for agriculture, and they must be adequately supported to help them 
restore soil health . Policy, regulatory and subsidy considerations, as well as private and philanthropic 
investment can be utilized to assist growers with the transition to good soil management practices . This 
should include: 
• the provision of loans for purchase of specialized equipment,  
• providing services, training and advice to help growers to incorporate new agronomic methods; 
• linking subsidy payments to soil health parameters; 
• better finance and insurance offers for growers transitioning to practices key to healthy soils.

EU Soil Health Law to leverage Science and technology  
Science and technology will be critical in improving  soil he alth. In the area of soil biodiversity, the 
developments in genetic sequencing mean that it is now possible to identify the micro-organisms that are 
associated with the healthy function of soil, and that knowledge can be used to manage and improve soil 
health.  
Recent research into the symbiosis between plants and fungi in the soil has shown the importance of 
chemical signaling that helps improve plant access to soil nutrients, water, and enhance defenses against 
pests and diseases. Crop breeding and small molecule technologies show promise in applying this new 
understanding in agronomy.  . Advances in chemical ecology and microbial chemical ecology represent 
promising venues to drive new and sustainable innovation targeting particularly soil health (Kellogg et al., 
2020;   Kang et al., 2021; Screpanti, 2021).   
Data capture and soil health metrics are growing significantly, enabling the development of modelling 
approaches that will better inform management decisions. Automated sensors and probes are beginning 
to collect high-resolution data in real time that can be used by growers to apply inputs more precisely and 
tailor management practices. 
With regard metrics it will be extremely critical to standardize the metrics within the EU Countries and 
align with other initiative across regions. There is an urgency to agree how to measure soil to be used later 
for broad policies. 
Legislation should prioritize the approval and widespread use of technologies that contribute to soil health 
sustainability. Such an approach would encourage private investment in new approaches and solutions, 
which will also require public-private collaborations. 
EU Soil Health Law to link with Carbon farming 
The important role of soils in capturing carbon to mitigate climate change has been recognized by the 
European Commission. There is a pressing need to adequately incentivize growers to adopt soil carbon 
capture techniques, with adequate certification methods to assure long-term carbon storage.  
Further policy and operational effo rts are needed to develop an effective soil carbon economy and 
facilitate soil carbon trading (Keenor et al., 2021).  Indeed, the wider implementation of the carbon market 
would have knock -on benefits for soil health generally, since carbon storage leads t o soil improvement 
from a number of perspectives.   
The Soil Health Law should include a strong focus on the importance of carbon farming. 
EU Soil Health Law to link with Water legislation 
Soil and water are crucial environmental resources  that interact in many ways. Water cycle is one of the 
most important processes operating on our planet, sustaining life and controlling our weather and climate. 
Functional soils play a key role in the water cycle, supplying clean water and improving our re silience to 
floods and droughts.

Soil hydraulic properties play an important role in determining the soil and environment quality and the 
capability of the soil to serve the ecosystem  (Encyclopedia of Soils in the Environment, 2005). The soil 
hydraulic properties are useful in predicting the plant–water relationship, water movement and storage, 
irrigation and drainage, leaching of nutrients, runoff and catchment management, crop breeding planning, 
and assessing the productive potential of soils and hydraulic and climate models. 
Knowledge about soil water relationships can  inform the decision -making process in agricultural  
operations or natural resource management. 
In this context , soil management should include the concept of soil hydrological properties taking in 
consideration the co-relation water/soil and agriculture/environment 
Syngenta Group contribution 
The agricultural industry also has an important role to play to promote good practices and assist growers 
with the transition to better soil management.  
Syngenta Group, in collaboration with The Nature Conservancy, has identified three priority areas against 
which it will focus its efforts to promote the healthy management of soils : promote soil biodiversity, 
promote soil resources, and mitigate climate change (Screpanti, 2021; Atwood et al., 2022). 
We are committed to working together with all stakeholders to help deliver on the Commission’s goal of 
healthy soils by 2050.

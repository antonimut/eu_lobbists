ARD final comments on the MFA consultation 
 
We would like to make the following final comments: 
• We do understand the background of the Commission’s intention. We have read 
carefully the media chapters in the respective national parts of the last rule-of-
law report and it cannot be ignored that in a couple of EU Member States 
problems with media freedom and media pluralism do exist.  
• Despite understanding these important shortcomings in these Member States, 
we have serious doubts whether the overall statement by the Commission 
regarding the alleged obstacles in the “European media market” qualify as an 
appropriate starting point for introducing a new legislative instrument on the EU-
level. Quote: “… The internal media market is affected by (i) different national 
rules on media pluralism, (ii) insufficient structures for cooperation between 
independent media regulators, (iii) instances of public and private interference 
in the ownership, management or operation of media outlets, and (iv) lack of 
media pluralism safeguards, including online”). 
• From our point of view the creation of a genuine “internal media market” in the EU 
is not among the tasks of the European Commission and not part of the 
competencies conferred to the Union by the Treaties. That - as stated - “the 
internal media market is affected by different national rules on media pluralism” 
is not a problem to be addressed in the context of the MFA,  but rather an 
inevitable consequence of the allocation of competencies between the EU and 
the Member States. And while the principle of conferral does not impede per se 
EU media regulation from the outset and it is true that media regulation is not 
formally excluded from the Treaties, though media are not mentioned at all, it 
has to be reiterated that the EU, and thus the European Commission, is obliged 
by the Treaties to respect national and regional cultural diversity, of which the 
media are an important part, and thus is restricted to only contribute to the 
flowering of the cultures of the Member States, while any harmonisation of the 
laws and regulations of the Member States is excluded (art. 167 TFEU). It seems 
that the European Commission has not even considered making art. 167 TFEU a 
legal basis for its intended action. By not even referring to this article, the 
Commission also risks to ignore entirely the subsidiarity principle in its approach 
known so far. While the European Commission by the EU Treaties is not 
competent in initiating genuine media regulation, which is under the sole 
prerogative of the Member States, it seems to attempt to redefine media 
regulation matters as internal market matters to construe itself a legal basis for 
its intended action. This is legally highly doubtful and politically inacceptable. 
• In view of art. 4 TEU media regulation is expressly not a shared competence of 
the EU and the Member States. According to art. 5 TEU the EU can only support 
the Member States in their cultural policies; this excludes, however, any 
harmonisation of Member States’ legal frameworks (art. 167 para. 5 TFEU; see 
also art. 2 para 5 TEU stating that where the EU supports Member States 
measures, this does not create a genuine competence of the Union replacing the 
competence of the Member States).  
• The European Commission can also not draw upon art. 2 TEU (values of the EU 
including ‘freedom’ and ‘pluralism’) and easily deduct competencies therefrom. 
• At the same time and already mentioned above, it is true that there are problems 
with media freedom and media pluralism in some EU Member States. And it can 
be argued that this needs appropriate action. But for action on EU level this 
demands evidence, the proof of necessity and proportionality in approach.

• It is equally true that the provision of media services has an internal market 
dimension, given its manifold cross-border characteristics. This results into a 
natural tension between national cultural policies, including media policies, of 
the Member States on the one side and internal market policies on the other side 
which needs to be dissolved in a meaningful way. The EU is obliged to handle 
the Member States prerogatives with care and to restrict itself to the minimum 
necessary in securing the Union’s interest. If the EU acts on the legal basis of its 
(exclusive or shared) competences, the EU must take cultural aspects in 
accordance with art. 167 TFEU into account. The intention to “establish a 
common framework for advancing the internal market in the media sector, 
thereby safeguarding media freedom and media pluralism in the market” is by 
systematic interpretation of the TFEU not a defined EU interest and thus no 
genuine task of the European Commission. Media pluralism cannot be the main 
objective of EU rules. 
• The very fact that “media actors are confronted with different national 
substantive rules and scrutiny procedures over media market operations” was a 
conscious decision by the Member States in creating the EU Treaties and has been 
explicitly included in Art. 167 TFEU. 
• In line of the above, we have strong doubts that choosing as a legal basis for the 
future Media Freedom Act Art. 114 TFEU. Safeguarding and fostering media 
freedom is first and foremost a competence of the Member States in order to 
fulfill public interest objectives and is widely reserved for action by the Member 
States, only to be enhanced by Union’s action subject to proof of necessity. This 
is in particular true for public service broadcasting in line with the so-called 
Amsterdam Protocol. 
• We therefore urge the Commission to consider another legal concept as the 
fundament of the Media Freedom Act. 
• We strongly remind the Commission of the fact that both the Amsterdam Protocol 
and the established European case law confers sole competence on the Member 
States to define the public service remit for public service broadcasters, to entrust 
suitable broadcasting organisations with its fulfilment and to determine their 
funding. These principles, accompanied by wide discretion in respective action 
not only on the Member States’ side, but also on the public service broadcasting 
organisations’ side translate into organizational and programming autonomy as 
much as into editorial independence. As a consequence there is a wide spectrum 
of national approaches. National safeguards securing pluralism and 
independence of the media need to be assessed in a national context. Taking 
into account the diversity of EU Member States’ constitutional, legal, political, 
economic and cultural systems, there is no one-size-fits-all model. The 
Amsterdam Protocol must not be undermined by the future Media Freedom Act 
under any circumstances. 
• It is deeply concerning that the Commission tries to construct a direct link 
between the “fulfilment of a public service remit that serves all viewers” and 
“distortion of competition in the internal market”. If this logic is taken to the 
extreme, even in Member States which are currently not considered of breaking 
the rule-of-law principle, opponents of public service broadcasters, e.g. populist 
parties or competitors, could use the European Media Freedom Act to undermine 
and weaken public service broadcasters. The Commission’s call for evidence as 
well as its online consultation document seem to generalize problems in some 
Member States in view of all Member States. However, the very fact that in some 
Member States media freedom and media pluralism is endangered (each in a 
specific form and to a different extent) does not give sufficient evidence for the 
need for EU-wide action nor can the EU create a competence-competence 
regarding media pluralism and freedom. In most Member States these 
fundamental principles are effectively secured and promoted. The annual rule-
of-law reports clearly show both: the problems in some Member States as well 
as well-working systems in the majority of other Member States. 
• We believe that the Commission should rather focus on elements which are 
already laid out and can be found in the AVMS Directive by ensuring elements that 
secure free and pluralistic media: due conduct, independence from governmental, 
political, economic or other vested interest; “money flows” etc. Those rules would 
need, however, to apply to all media: public service broadcasters, commercial 
broadcasters and press publishers and – where necessary to secure media 
freedom and media pluralism – also to online platform providers. 
• Should the European Commission nevertheless consider to carry on with its 
plans for an EU Media Freedom Act it should choose a legal instrument that does 
not interfere with the Member States competencies and does not damage well-
functioning and balanced media regulations. Even allegedly minor requirements 
through EU-legislation could have massive and ripple effects in well-functioning 
systems. Given the competencies, we consider a Recommendation of the 
European Commission as the most appropriate tool.  
• Should that not be an option we strongly argue for a Directive, setting European 
minimum standards while leaving Member States sufficient leeway. 
• Whatever the Commission will propose in regard to substantive rules, there is a 
clear need for appropriate monitoring and a graded mechanism for action only 
being triggered after systemic failures in securing media freedom and media 
pluralism and overstepping well-defined thresholds.  
• With regard to a possible mechanism for action we disagree that ERGA should 
become the actor responsible for such mechanisms. The group has been set up 
for other purposes (under the AVMS Directive) and is not equipped with sufficient 
competencies and resources. We would rather advocate for a targeted expert 
group. 
• Overall our skeptical approach towards the European Commission’s intentions 
is not at least motivated by the fact that deficits in supporting media freedom and 
pluralism in some Member States is very often only a symptom of the fact that 
these Member States disrespect the values of the EU as enshrined in Article 2 TEU 
and the EU’s Fundamental Rights Charter on a more fundamental level. The 
problem is more of a “constitutional” nature and can ultimately only be solved 
in that very constitutional context, thus cannot be transferred to a lower level. 
Handling of this overall problem by limited secondary law action does not reflect 
the severity of the general matter and will thus remain insufficient. Member 
States in question have already shown willingness to successfully resist 
subordination under certain EU law or to circumvent it. At the same time, an EU 
Media Freedom Act could cause harm in Member States where there is no need 
to intervene due to appropriate conduct faithful to EU values and principles and 
furthermore where media freedom and media pluralism is protected sufficiently 
and constitutionally established in a balanced system.

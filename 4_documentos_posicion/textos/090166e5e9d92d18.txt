The Leibniz Institute for Media Research | Hans-Bredow-Institut welcomes the opportunity to 
contribute to the EC consultation regarding the Call for evidence for an impact assessment - 
Ares(2021)7899801: “Safeguarding media freedom and pluralism in the internal market (Me-
dia Freedom Act). This statement is based on the input for the ERGA Academy on 18th February 
2022. Its structure is based on the guiding questions received for that event.  
Q1: Would you see a need for common European standards for  
national scrutiny rules and procedures for media market 
transactions/operations, notably based on existing national 
approaches? Should this include commo n criteria for 
assessment, and if so, what such criteria could be? 
The initiative for a Media Freedom Act (MFA) certainly pursues important goals. Diverse and 
independent media are among the fundamental European values that are repeatedly invoked. 
 
Preliminary remark on competencies for rule setting  
With each new challenge, the basic question of the delimitation of competencies between the 
EU and the Member States in the area of public communication arises again. The EU's internal 
market competencies do not give it the pow er to harmonize the law in the area of media 
diversity protection. Since competenc ies will always overlap in case of commercial media, 
there is a need for sustainable and reliable coordination mechanisms that respect Member 
States’ rights as recognized by the ECJ. 
In order to a void unnecessary conflicts and to ensure coherence it seems advisable to agree 
on a European level on basic principles for the further development of the communication 
sector as well as the separation of powers of member states and EU institutions in this regard. 
This could entail templates for opening clauses and for formulating exemptions from the coun-
try-of-origin principle as well as good practices for an impact assessment regarding freedom 
of communication and media on a national and European level.1 
 
Background: Changing communication ecosystem 
Research as well as regulation needs to broaden their perspectives: The adequate regulatory 
object is a functioning communication system in a democracy, not just a diverse media system. 
There are at least three preconditions for such a communication system to function: 
A. No undue influence on that system by the state (or by EU institutions);

If we start with the aims given for the MFA process, we n eed to assess all regulation s on the 
national and EU level as to whether it guarantees those preconditions given the current com-
munication ecosystem. This might mean retracting EU regulations in some cases (possibly con-
cerning the powers of public service media providers) and establishing additional regulations 
in others.  
As for (A) we see an option for uniform European criteria for the independence of media and 
media supervision from the state – the latter partly already addressed in the AVMS Directive. 
The consultation text rightly refers to the fundamental values of the EU stipulated in Article 2 
of the Treaty on European Union; clear, justiciable criteria would be helpful here for legal cer-
tainty. Borrowing from the work of the Council of Europe might be helpful .2 Here, the narra-
tive of the EU Commission backing the MFA that the single market requires stable democratic 
institutions makes sense. 
As for (B) the assessment is much more complex:  
Challenge of defining the scope  
It is becoming increasingly difficult to delineate the “media”, both as an economic market and 
as a specific type of actor in the formation of public opinion. There is still evidence that jour-
nalistic-editorial communication has a special function here, but this is not i dentical with the 
notion of “editorial responsibility” in the AVMS Directive. Thus, the definition of the scope of 
the MFA calls for a new approach. 
Moreover, when the EC’s call for evi dence speaks of “media” regulation, it addresses areas 
that historically have had comp letely different governance structures in the Member States, 
i.e., regulated broadcasting on the one hand and unregulated press and online offerings  on 
the other hand, for which only a general legal framework applies. 
The biggest challenge that different member states have addressed differe ntly is how to up-
date traditional regulation for the internet age, with its new media consumption practices and 
diversified media landscape. To name but one example: RT Deutsch, Russia’s German -speak-
ing TV station, has experienced difficulties both in operating as a broadcaster in Germany, and 
in continuing to disseminate content using its YouTube channel, from which it was banned by 
YouTube for, inter alia, spreading disinformation. The regimes under which RT Deutsch has 
fallen under online and offline, in order to disseminate the same content, are very different.

If the MFA seeks to contribute to achieving media freedom, then the question becomes es-
sential what the scope of this media is. Is it just television broadcasting, all audiovisual media, 
all media or even all services relevant for public communication?  
Some Member States are responding to new threats to the freedom of public communication 
by expanding their media regulation, such as Germany by (partially) including intermediaries 
in media regulation. Building on that, German media policy already explores ways to combat 
manipulation of public communication comprehensively  and not restricted to broadcasting. 
European harmonization should not hold back such effort but, the other way round, the Eu-
ropean institutions should support the Member States in their efforts to mutually learn. 
 
Challenge of defining the aim  
While Member States could previously assume that a diverse range of media would make 
public communication work, changes in communication practices mean that th ere are many 
more potential threats. Diversity on the side of providers is just one issue among others. We 
suggested under Germany’s EU Council Presidency 2020 to rethink the concepts of plurality 
and diversity fundamentally.3 
 
Challenge of harmonizing the instruments  
If the attempt to harmonize the law s combatting media concentration should be made, the 
elements of the regulatory concept that could profit from standardization within Europe need 
to be parsed, especially: 
- the models of measuring media concentration (e.g. audience share model), 
- the criteria for such measurements, and 
- the thresholds for undue influence. 
There is the risk of interfering with the national constitutional frameworks (again, Germany is 
an example where the Federal Constitutional Court does not only have an elaborated under-
standing of a free and open exchange of opinion s which is reflected in the German law, but 
also a specific view on the relation of the constitution and European law). Furthermore, the 
specific structures of media markets – national, regional, local and sub-local diversity and their 
interdependencies – are often unique and might be seen as obstacles for harmonisation. 
Therefore, the scope for implementation of option 2 (Legislative instrument) seems to be ra-
ther limited in this area.  
 
Other options for action  
It should be in the interest of the Member States and the EU to have a better overview of the 
state of freedom of opinion formation and possible threats. Member States can also learn

Moreover, clear conflict -of-law rules in European law should safeguard the Member States'

Transparency-related standards would be conceivable and helpful, i.e., an EU-wide database

In consequence, this contribution rather backs Option 1 outlined by the European Commis-
sion. 
Q2: Do you see a role for the (potentially reinforced) ERGA in ensuring 
convergence in this area, for example through a peer review of 
national decisions/opinions? 
As the new European digital services and digital markets order is being debated, the future 
role of ERGA is indeed up for discussion. Here, a number of different scenarios for ERGA’s 
future are considered by the Commission with regard to the MFA.  
In view of the distribution of competencies described earlier, the possibilities of formally 
strengthening ERGA along the lines of the regulatory setting in the telecommunications sector 
or the GDPR, to take two prominent examples, are rather limited. There are strong arguments 
against Option 2 outlined by the Commission with ERGA developed into a European Regula-
tory Compound:  
- This would place the regulatory bodies closer with the Commission and at arm’s 
length to the Member States in matters where the Member States actually have reg-
ulatory primacy (see above). 
- The regulatory bodies have a legacy as broadcasting regulators that does not always 
ideally fit the needs for convergent media markets, even though great efforts to gain 
new expertise can be observed with many regulators.  
- The democratic legitimation of decisions might be weakened in a field where protect-
ing democratic discourse is key.  
Thus, soft instruments to ensure compatibility seem to be more fitting here. 
To this end, it may be useful for national decisions to be informally reviewed by other regula-
tors with regard to their basic assumptions and with regard to their impact. This would align 
ERGA’s role with that of a high-level coordination forum.  
However, the circums tance that even states like Germany do not require the regulators to 
publish the reasons for a decision makes mutual learning in this field much more difficult. In 
our view the rule of law requires the publication the reasoning at least of decisions of national 
importance. Here, there might even be room for European regulation requiring the publica-
tion of decisions of regulators including the reasoning ( having in mind , of course, that parts 
of the text would might need to be blacked out  where information would infringe with the 
rights of third parties).  
The joint development of regulatory concepts and reactions to major changes in the media 
sector can also enable a soft de-facto-coordination in areas where EU law is not in place. This 
is likely to be permissible as long as the Member States and their regulatory bodies retain their 
margin of discretion.  
In this vein, ERGA could further develop into a think tank that informs not only the Commission 
but also the Member States about relevant changes in the media markets and the structures 
of public communication.  
In contrast to that, a ssigning formal rights could even weaken ERGA, especially if unanimity 
were required, as Member States with questionable levels of rule of law and not fully inde-
pendent regulation would then gain veto power.

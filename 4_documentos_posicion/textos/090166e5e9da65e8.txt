1.  Executive summary
5GAA considers the proposed revision of the Intelligent Transport Systems (ITS) 
Directive (2010/40/EU) as a step in the right direction of establishing a future-proof 
framework to deliver cooperative, connected, and automated mobility in Europe. We 
welcome the consideration given to the transition towards new technologies for ITS 
services.
In this paper, 5GAA proposes constructive suggestions to help improve the proposal, 
namely:
1. Strengthen the legislation by including technology neutrality as one of the 
guiding principles for deployment of ITS in Annex II 
2. Recognise the evolutionary path of technologies by removing the counter-
productive principle of backward compatibility in Annex II
3. Ensure that the introduction of any future technology mandate for ITS services 
deployment is subject to appropriate democratic control via ordinary legislative 
procedure
4. Clarify the interoperability definition to achieve safety and traffic efficiency 
at service-level and guarantee continuity of services without discriminating 
between the various technologies
5. Establish an inclusive C-ITS definition, including both mobile network  
communications and direct communications
6. Develop a holistic EU C-ITS security and trust model including but not limited 
to PKI
By design, no regulatory instrument can keep up with the rapid pace of technology 
evolution. 5GAA therefore calls for a flexible and holistic system approach, combining 
network-based and direct communication technologies, and allowing the seamless 
integration of their respective evolution into the ITS system.

I.  Technology neutrality must be a key 
principle for deployment of ITS services
The ITS regulatory framework should offer market certainty by enshrining the principle 
of technology neutrality in Annex II. 
5GAA supports a regulatory approach where services and their related benefits are 
benchmarked according to their end-user and ecosystem value (regardless of the 
underlying technology). The focus should be on service delivery, and the regulatory 
framework should remain technology- neutral to foster market-led innovation.
The principle of technology neutrality has been an important guiding principle in 
Union telecommunications law since at least 2002 and was reinforced in 2009, with 
particular regard to spectrum licences. In recital (25) of the Directive (EU) 2018/1972 
on the Electronic Communications Code, the legislator set out a definition of that 
principle: "The principle that Member States should apply Union law in a technologically 
neutral fashion, that is to say that a national regulatory or other competent authority should 
neither impose nor discriminate in favour of the use of a particular type of technology, does 
not preclude the taking of proportionate steps to promote certain specific services where 
justified in order to attain the objectives of the regulatory framework, […]"
Member States also reaffirmed their attachment to technology neutrality when 
rejecting the C-ITS Delegated Regulation in 2019.
The current Recital 8 already recognises ‘’that requirements for ITS systems neither impose 
nor discriminate in favour of the use of a particular type of technology ’’. To strengthen 
this commitment, 5GAA propose to list it explicitly among the guiding principles in 
Annex II. This will ensure that technology neutrality is appropriately reflected in future 
EU (secondary) legislation related to ITS.
Text proposal for Annex II Principles for specifications and deployment of ITS
(n) Be technology neutral – specifications should describe the result to be 
achieved, but neither impose nor discriminate in favour of the use of a particular 
type of technology to achieve the result’

II.  “Backward compatibility”  
is incompatible with 5G-V2X  
technology evolution
The future ITS Directive provisions should pave the way for 5G-V2X, the new-generation 
technology that enables more advanced ITS services. 5G-V2X will reach mass market 
deployment worldwide by 2025-2027. It will constitute a key element of Europe’s 
competitiveness on the way to connected and automated driving as the first 5G car 
models are now commercialised e.g. BMW iNext.
The principle of backward compatibility in Annex II will hamper 5G-V2X adoption in the 
EU. Unlike interoperability, which focuses on service delivery, backward compatibility 
requires radio-level compatibility i.e. to tweak the wireless air interface of 5G technology 
(its core design) to understand and communicate with an older technology like Wi-Fi.
However, if newer technology offers higher performance and enables new ITS services, 
it is precisely because its core design offers a break-through compared to previous 
ones. 
In addition, this principle creates legal uncertainty and is self-contradicting: the full 
detriment would be incurred by 5G-V2X, yet this must be done “without hindering the 
development of new technologies”.
5GAA fully supports the principles of interoperability and continuity of (legacy) services 
to ensure that different ITS technologies exchange seamlessly to deliver ITS services 
across the EU. Backward compatibility, however, only jeopardises the EU’s capacity to 
fully embrace 5G-V2X and will hurt our automotive industry’s ability to compete with 
China and the USA, that are currently accelerating their 5G deployment plans.
Therefore, 5GAA suggests removing the principle from Annex II to focus on achieving 
ITS service interoperability, regardless of the communication technologies powering 
them.
Text proposal for Annex II Principles for specifications and deployment of ITS
Annex II
(f) support backward compatibility – ensure, where appropriate, the capability for 
ITS systems to work with existing systems that share a common purpose, without 
hindering the development of new technologies and while supporting, where relevant, 
the complementarity with or transition to new technologies;

III.  Only the EU co-legislators  
should decide on technology  
or service mandate  
5GAA reiterates its position that the revised ITS rules should focus on achieving safety 
and traffic efficiency at the system level and not overregulate by requiring a specific 
technology implementation for the delivery of services. 
The ITS Directive Impact Assessment Report suggests mandating the deployment of 
C-ITS services by 2028-2030. The proposed revision of the ITS Directive foresees that 
such a mandate could be adopted simply by means of delegated act amending Annex 
IV.
We believe that imposing a technology and/or the mandate for the delivery of ITS 
services is a critical political decision that can only be made by the EU co-legislators 
via the ordinary legislative procedure. Such a decision cannot be considered as 
“non-essential elements” of the legislative act, as per Art. 290 TFEU. Granting such 
discretionary power to the Commission would limit legislators’ scrutiny to a right of 
objection without power to review or amend the regulatory provisions.
5GAA suggests removing the delegation of power granted under Article 7(1b) to the 
Commission to amend Annex IV and the list of C-ITS services by means of delegated 
acts.
We observe that technology always evolves faster than legislation: 4G-LTE is now 
widespread and triggers a wide range of non-safety critical C-ITS services whilst in 
parallel, 5G network deployment, a key objective for Europe, progresses rapidly.
As the automotive industry gears up towards 5G-V2X, the pending threat of a 
technology and/or service mandate, adopted without the appropriate democratic 
scrutiny, and possibly based on older technology, risk stalling or delaying all on-going 
5G-V2X investments in Europe.
In addition, a mandate would mean reopening the debate over the C-ITS Delegated 
Act in 2019, to which the Council of the EU objected, to preserve technology neutrality.
Text proposal for Article 6b Deployment of ITS services
(6) The following Articles 6a and 6b are inserted:

Article 6b

for the geographical coverage set out in that Annex by the date specified therein.

IV.  Ensuring continuity of services by 
securing interoperability at service-level
The proposed ITS Directive interoperability definition could be improved by stressing 
the importance of service-level interoperability to guarantee continuity of services 
without discriminating between the various alternatives. 
To be future-proof, the overall ITS framework must remain focused on requiring 
"open interfaces" between the various implementation alternatives for a given service 
to achieve service-level interoperability, when and if appropriate (some services may 
not require interoperability). However, the regulation should not prescribe how these 
"APIs" are designed or implemented. This should be left to Standards Developing 
Organisations (SDOs) as per Article 8 of the Directive. 
Regardless of the underlying technology, continuity of (legacy) services should be 
privileged over backward compatibility. In contrast, service-level interoperability could 
still be achieved despite being powered by different generations of communication 
technologies.
It should be noted, that it is perfectly possible to achieve a certain level of interoperability 
in the short term, in a cost-beneficial way by using a common language, i.e. mobile 
network communications. This is already the preferred language and the most widely 
spoken by the existing connected car fleet.
Text proposal for Article 4(2) Definitions
(2) ‘’interoperability ’’ means the capacity of systems and the underlying business 
processes to  exchange data and share information and knowledge at service-level 
to guarantee the continuity of ITS services

V.  Inclusive definition of C-ITS
Today, there are already many C-ITS services deployed over different technological 
means: e.g., Bosch's wrong way driving, or BMW's Rescue Lane Assistant (safety-
related use cases) are both powered by mobile network communications.
To improve, we recommend the proposed C-ITS definition, which is currently vague, 
to be explicitly inclusive of all technologies, otherwise this will lead to parallel yet split 
streams of C-ITS deployments whilst the services aim at the same objective (e.g. direct 
short-range communications vs network-based communications). This would cause 
further fragmentation of the ITS ecosystem resulting in lower market penetration and 
reduced societal benefits. 
Text proposal for Article 4 (19) Definitions
(19) “cooperative intelligent transport systems” or “C-ITS” means intelligent transport 
systems that enable ITS users to cooperate interact by exchanging secured and 
trusted messages; data or information between vehicles and other elements of 
the transport ecosystem (including but not limited to vehicles, infrastructure 
and vulnerable road users) via wireless communication technologies.

VI.  Developing a holistic C-ITS security 
and trust model
To realise the societal and economic benefits that ITS offers, it is paramount that 
the system architecture ensures not only the principles of security and privacy but 
also those of deployability and practical operation. Security plays an essential role in 
building trust in C-ITS, protecting users' privacy, and enabling safety, efficiency, and 
comfort.
The current text implies that a single means of authentication, i.e. Public Key 
Infrastructure (PKI), should be used system-wide. However, this means of authentication 
is best suited to situations where the sender and the receiver do not have an existing 
trust relationship. There is not sufficient clarity about the meaning of "single trust 
model", as there are clear instances of activities that could affect the trustworthiness 
of received C-ITS messages but for which it is not appropriate to protect with the C-ITS 
PKI.
The current content of Recital 11 establishes a narrow focus on the messages that are 
authenticated by PKI, which implies that the other issues of trustworthiness inside the 
system are not important. In fact, for a receiver to trust the C-ITS messages, all data 
sources and management activities that affect those messages must be trustworthy.
Furthermore, the language of the current text could imply that even in scenarios 
where a non-PKI based approach is the natural way of implementing trust, the PKI-
based approach has to be used. In practice, the PKI approach is appropriate for many 
applications, including key day 1 C-ITS applications, but it is not a one-size-fits-all 
solution. 
Therefore, the ITS Directive should allow for the use of non-PKI solutions where this 
is appropriate. The existing language could be read as implying that once a PKI is in 
place, that's all that needs to be done to guarantee trustworthiness. A better solution 
is to refer to PKI as a key technology but explicitly acknowledges that it is not the 
only way to establish trust. In practice, this is valid for cases such as cloud or network 
service vehicles’ upload of data, downloading high-resolution maps, OEM-originating 
software updates, and others.

Text proposal Recital 11
11) C-ITS use technologies that enable road vehicles to communicate with each other 
and with roadside infrastructure including traffic signals. C-ITS services are a category 
of ITS services based on an open architecture that enables a many-to-many or peer-to-
peer relationship between C-ITS stations. This means all C-ITS stations should securely 
exchange messages with each other, as  and should not be limited to exchanging 
messages with pre-defined stations.
Most services require ensuring the authenticity and integrity of C-ITS messages 
containing information such as position, velocity and heading . Therefore, one 
common European C-ITS trust model should be created to establish a relation of trust 
between all C-ITS stations, regardless of communication technologies used.  That 
trust model should be implemented by the adoption of a policy on the use of a Public 
Key Infrastructure (PKI). The highest level of that PKI should be the European certificate 
trust list, consisting of entries of all trusted root certification authorities in Europe. 
To ensure the smooth functioning of that C-ITS trust model, certain tasks should be 
carried out at a central level. The Commission should ensure that those essential 
tasks are carried out, in particular as concerns the roles of the C-ITS certificate policy 
authority, the trust list manager, and the C-ITS point of contact.
It is well established that for many C-ITS services, the use of a Public Key 
Infrastructure (PKI)  is the natural way to authenticate C-ITS messages, providing 
assurance to the receiver as to the trustworthiness of these messages. For 
this PKI to be used successfully there must be a policy adopted for its use, to 
ensure consistency across trust statements. An important tool to provide this 
consistency is the use of central components, identified in standards as the 
C-ITS certificate policy authority, the trust list manager, and the C-ITS point 
of contact. The Commission should ensure that these central components are 
established and can operate successfully. This does not rule out the use of other 
trust mechanisms for types of C-ITS data exchange which must be trusted but 
for which the use of the C-ITS PKI is not suited.

Additionally, the text should imply that it is expected that operators closer to the 
ground have the responsibility, and are expected, to plan for emergencies, with the 
European Commission more clearly positioned as a remediator of last resort, instead 
of having  time-critical technical responsibility for remediating system issues.
Text proposal for Recital 14
(14) Compromising the integrity of ITS services could thus have a severe impact on 
road safety, for example when the wrong speed limit is communicated, or a vehicle 
makes an emergency stop due to a non-existing danger. In order to ensure uniform 
conditions for the implementation of this Directive, implementing powers should 
be conferred on the Commission in emergency situations where the integrity of ITS 
services is compromised, to adopt countermeasures to address the causes and the 
consequences of that situation. Those measures should be taken as quickly as possible 
and be immediately applicable. The powers should only be used in emergency 
situations when other forms of remediation by other authorities have not been 
successful. It is expected that appropriate local and system-wide authorities 
will have emergency management plans in place to address a range of possible 
failure modes of the system and can act on those plans if necessary.
The current proposal focuses only on authentication; however, authentication only 
partially addresses threats to GNSS and, although its use should be encouraged, from 
the perspective of overall robustness of the system, it is appropriate for the language 
to address robustness mechanisms more broadly.
Text proposal Recital 21
(21) The provision of secured and reliable timing and positioning services is an essential 
element of the effective operation of ITS applications and services. Therefore, it is 
appropriate to ensure their compatibility with the authentication mechanism provided 
by the Galileo programme, in order to mitigate Global Navigation Satellite Systems 
(‘GNSS’) signal spoofing attacks, and also use other mechanisms to ensure the 
reliability of position and time information, which mechanisms may include 
plausibility checking and use of GNSS services from multiple providers.

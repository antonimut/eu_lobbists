 11 March 2022  EMMA and ENPA position paper on the European Media Freedom Act     The European Newspaper Publishers’ Association (ENPA) and the European Magazine Media Association (EMMA) represent together the majority of European press publishers with a variety of magazine and newspaper offerings, both digital and print.   The press publishing sector is one of the most culturally diverse and pluralistic in Europe. It ensures pluralism of information and opinions across the European Union (EU) and therefore represents an essential asset for the formation of opinion and for democracy. In every Member State, both magazines and newspapers are present locally, regionally and nationally in printed and digital formats. In this way, the press is fulfilling its vital function of providing a diverse and trusted source of news, views and analysis as well as stimulating an open and critical democratic debate on all platforms.  The associations have taken note of the public consultation launched by the European Commission on the European Media Freedom Act. With this position paper, EMMA and ENPA would like to provide some general comments on this consultation and the issues raised in it around three main points.   I) Media pluralism is better preserved at Member State level Press and media freedom in Europe is built on national media markets, which are very diverse and have been shaped by historical, cultural and societal developments. As such, each Member State has its own approach to media freedom and pluralism, as well as to possible initiatives to safeguard it. This is warranted as market conditions, media consumption and reading habits vary considerably from one country to another, with the press, public and private TV and radio sectors holding very different local market shares in different territories. Furthermore, it would not be appropriate to apply the same rules to linear media such as TV, and radio and the press. These media are essentially different, for example the barrier to entry for the digital press is relatively low, while that is not the case for radio and television.  Apart from this, due to the richness and diversity of the media landscape in the EU Member States, it is hardly possible to define a “European media market“. Instead, the media markets are characterised by a mix of national, regional and local realities that are best equipped to effectively address any specific situation. In this light, media pluralism can only be assessed by national authorities who can adapt their legislative framework according to their national, regional and local characteristics.   In addition and in light of the democratic, social and cultural importance of a free media for Member States, the EU does not have the competence to regulate media freedom and pluralism, other than through the provisions of competition law, notably the EC merger regulation, antitrust rules set out in Articles 81 and 82 of the EU Treaty, which prohibit anti-competitive horizontal and vertical agreements, the Treaty’s public service provisions in Article 86, and EC state aid control in Article 87 (TFEU). Rather than introducing new regulation that would further complicate the regulatory landscape, the EU can support media freedom and pluralism in different ways through legislative initiatives with an impact on both the economic and editorial freedom of the media, as well as the distribution thereof. For example, the European Commission could help the press by monitoring the implementation of the Copyright directive and the subsequent negotiations with the platforms using and distributing press content, as well as their payments.  Another way to indirectly support media pluralism, with regard to data protection rules, would be to avoid advertising restrictions beyond the already high standard set in the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the e-Privacy regulation under discussion. Advertising is an essential source of funding for many media outlets and allows users to access content with little to no direct payment. On the one hand, commercial advertising should be clearly safeguarded from cumbersome obligations such as those described in the political advertising regulation proposal. On the other hand, very large online platforms should not be enabled to abuse their market position, either by setting unilateral restrictions to the use of cookies or by becoming gatekeepers of users’ consent.   Adding to the topic of imbalances in the media markets and the overwhelming power of gatekeeper platforms, all gatekeeper platforms must be obliged to provide fair and non-discriminatory conditions of access to all press publishers (in the Digital Markets Act - DMA) and not be allowed to interfere, demote or remove legal press content on the basis of their terms and conditions (in the Digital Services Act – DSA). Unfortunately, the Commission proposals fall short on these key aspects and have not sufficiently considered the concerns of the free media.   II) No EU-level rules on freedom of expression In order to safeguard freedom of expression, EMMA and ENPA oppose the regulation of the press sector at EU level, including printed and digital press as well as editorial and journalistic video and audio content, which is not the principal purpose of the relevant publication and therefore is not a non-linear audiovisual media service under the Audiovisual Media Services (AVMS) Directive. Hence, no media regulation beyond the scope of the AVMS Directive is legitimate and justifiable. Any such regulation would threaten press freedom and could impose new limitations to editorial freedom and to what content can be published and disseminated online. In order to protect democracy, the limits to press freedom must strictly correspond to the general rules on freedom of expression.  In this context, we oppose in particular the extension of the European Regulators Group for Audiovisual Media Services (ERGA) competences over the press, the creation of a new EU body or agency and the assimilation of the press with audiovisual media services and their rules. This would lead to a centralisation of rules to the detriment of editorial freedom and pluralism of information. In addition, press and media councils can play an important role. However, this can only be the case if press and media councils are truly voluntary and purely self-regulatory, in particular neither directly or indirectly regulated, co-regulated or supervised by laws, regulators or other authorities. In case national situations require different arrangements, this should only be decided at national level and not by the EU and press council must have the right to remain truly voluntary and purely self-regulatory.  We view especially critically the references to possible actions to ensure balanced and impartial coverage, as such actions would be diametrically opposed to our understanding of press freedom and would undermine the editorial freedom of press publishers. of press freedom and of the editorial freedom of press publishers means that the state cannot under any circumstance perform a qualitative assessment of editorial content. Imposing equal coverage of political actors in the press, or marginalising or prohibiting (political) opinions of the fringes of society, is inherently in opposition with media pluralism. In essence, it is absolutely impossible to ensure an objective and unbiased decision over what should be considered “balanced” or “impartial”. A decision to this effect by the state or a public authority would constitute an infringement of fundamental rights, the freedom of expression and the freedom and pluralism of the media.  III) Media Ownership, Concentration and Pluralism  As each Member State, its regions and areas therein, differ significantly, it is impossible to determine at EU level what should be considered a pluralistic media market. Regarding ownership, it is important to distinguish between who owns the media and who has editorial responsibility for the media. While both can be of interest to readers for the purpose of full transparency, the crucial safeguard and guarantee is whether there is a person with editorial responsibility, who can be held liable for the content. In other words, in all Member States it is ensured that the publisher and the person responsible for the content are known. Should this not be sufficient in some Member States, then it should not be excluded that the Member State imposes additional obligations on ownership transparency, but it should not be a pan-European obligation.  With a view to media concentration, it should be noted that many newspapers and magazines have benefited from cooperation and concentration in the context of a declining market. Indeed, cooperation and consolidations have allowed them to be more resistant to the financial crisis and the downturn in advertising revenues and helped for one keeping editorial teams while also accelerating the digital transformation. In fact, some titles would have closed down in recent years if they were not able to cooperate or merge with other publishers and media companies. In other words, it would be erroneous to see media concentration in a negative light. On the contrary, in times where financing editorial teams and reaching readers becomes increasingly difficult, many national media markets are dependent on concentration processes to ensure the survival of newspapers and magazines, and therefore to ensure plurality on the market.   Media concentration and pluralism have always been subject to lively debate at European and national level, since these issues touch upon fundamental values of democracy and upon freedom of expression. In this respect, national authorities and the EU play a role in ensuring that different media have the opportunity to develop and in preventing anti-competitive behaviours. However, when it comes to evaluating pluralism in a particular national and local market, this can only be done by the national authorities. In addition, European and especially national competition law has been adjusted and liberalized over the years in this regard and has been and still is effective with regard to media concentration and consolidation. While some specific issues such as state advertising and physical, judicial and cyber-attacks against journalists may and should be monitored by the European Commission in the framework of the rule of law mechanism, such monitoring shall not turn into EU-level regulation.  IV) Conclusion  In light of the above, EMMA and ENPA are strictly opposed to any legislative intervention on behalf of the EU to regulate media freedom. While we are aware of problematic situations in a minority of Member States, we fear that that an EU-level regulation will do more harm in the Member States that have a healthy media freedom situation, than good in the Member States

where media freedom is at stake. Some EU Member States such as the Scandinavian countries, on the contrary, are models for press freedom worldwide. In fact, those Member States are global blueprints for press freedom because of the lack of media regulation or state intervention. On the other hand, there are legitimate concerns that member states will use this instrument in order to interfere with editorial freedom and pluralism in their territory.  Instead of regulating media freedom, the EU should provide valuable contributions to press and media freedom, as well as plurality in the EU by focusing on the impact of legislative and regulatory initiatives on the free press and media. This includes initiatives such as the DSA and DMA; but also, data protection law, terrorist content online and e-evidence, as well as avoiding regulation or co-regulation on legal but allegedly “harmful” content.   Therefore, even as issues in certain Member States persist, the EU should focus on political pressure and recommendations to find political solutions while having have a clear and comprehensive picture of the many challenges facing the professional media today. This must not lead to a centralisation of media legislation that would ultimately decrease the level of media freedom and pluralism in the EU as a whole.

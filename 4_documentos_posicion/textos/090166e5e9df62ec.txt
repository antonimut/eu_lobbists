Attachment briefing for the European Commission’s consultation on strengthening 
national equality bodies in the EU 
Prepared by ILGA-Europe 
March 2022 
 
 
Introduction  
We welcome the European Commission’s plans to draft an initiative that aims to strengthen 
equality bodies in the EU by setting minimum standards for their functioning. Any initiative 
foreseen by the European Commission needs to include  a) a widening of the scope of the 
mandated grounds and b) an intersectional approach to collecting and analysing information 
(inclusive of data) on discrimination that would allow equality bodies to see, understand and 
therefore respond more effectively to experiences of multiple and intersectional 
discrimination.1 It is important to note that the term “intersectionality” is not a ground of 
discrimination, but rather an approach to analysing and responding to discrimination in a more 
complete and holistic way, that better reflects the reality of people’s experiences. Regarding 
data collection, equality bodies should be obliged to collect data that is disa ggregable, on all 
the grounds, to allow for this intersectional approach. It is important to have binding minimum 
standards to ensure EU equality legislation is enforced equally across the Member States, as 
this would improve the independence and capacities of equality bodies. 
The European Parliament’s position on the Commission’s proposal on the Pay Transparency 
Directive, adopted 17 March 2022, calls on equality bodies to “take due account of any 
situation of disadvantage arising from intersectional discrimination, in particular for substantive 
and procedural purposes, including to recognise the existence of discrimination, to decide on 
the appropriate comparator, to assess the proportionality, and to determine, whe re relevant, 
the level of compensation awarded or penalties imposed” and in the case of receiving data 
from employers equality bodies should “analyse that data in a way that takes account of 
multiple and intersectional forms of discrimination”. The Parliam ent’s position defines 
intersectional discrimination as “a situation in which grounds of discrimination prohibited under 
Directive 2006/54/EC and one or more grounds of discrimination prohibited under Directive 
2000/43/EC or 2000/78/EC - interact with each other at the same time in such a way as to be 
inseparable, producing distinct and specific forms of discrimination”.2  
At the minimum, the European Commission  should require that all prohibited grounds of 
discrimination listed in Article 19 of the TFEU (s ex, racial or ethnic origin, religion or belief,

However, w e also need to go beyond this, in order to properly address the reality of 
discrimination. Limiting equality bodies’ mandates to working only on grounds of discrimination 
explicitly named in  the EU Treaties means that many causes of discrimination are left out, 
remaining unrecognized and therefore not properly addressed. In the case of LGBTI people, 
who face discrimination based on their sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression 
or sex characteristics (or a combination of these grounds), not including gender identity, 
gender expression and sex characteristics  in the mandates of equality bodies means that 
equality bodies cannot properly address discrimination faced by trans and intersex people, in 
a time when discrimination is on the rise against LGBTI people across the continent.  
We encourage the European Commission to interpret the Charter of Fundamental Rights in a 
way inclusive of all LGBTI people, as Article 21 contains an open -ended list of grounds of 
discrimination to be protected in the EU, allowing for the EU to update its anti -discrimination 
laws and policies over time in accordance with the reality on the ground and emerging needs. 
Indeed, the European Court of Human Rights interpreted Article 21 CFR as inclusive of gender 
identity and sex characteristics in Identoba and Others v Georgia ([2015] 39 BHRC 510.). 
On 17 March 2022, the European Parliament adopted its position on the Commission’s 
proposal on the Pay Transparency Directive. It highlights that the definition of “sex” in Article 
157 TFEU is dated, and that social and legal changes, as well as research in the medical and 
biological field have led to the recognition of sex and gender as being more diverse than 
binary, which can be interpreted as covering non-binary and intersex people. It states that the 
Pay Transparency Directive “applies a human rights-oriented approach that seeks to protect 
persons from pay discrimination irrespective of their sex, gender, gender identity, gender 
expression or sex characteristics and therefore interprets its legal basis, discrimination on the 
grounds of sex, in the broadest and most inclusive way to foster gender equality and 
encompass people in all their diversity”.3 
Inclusion of gender identity, gender expression and sex characteristics in the mandates of EU 
equality bodies would fully reflect the objectives of  the work on EU equality that has been 
conducted over the last years, and  is in line with the commitments made in the EU LGBTIQ 
Equality Strategy which acknowledged that there are gaps in the legal protection of LGBTI 
people and committed to find ways to b etter protect trans and intersex people.  The strategy 
calls on equality bodies to work to achieve these objectives.4

Inclusion of gender identity and gender expression 
The t erm “gender identity” is increasingly enshrined in EU law and policy, reflecting 
international developments. Reflecting this, in her State of the Union Address 2020, European 
Commission President Ursula von der Leyen envisioned the EU as a Union of equality , 
acknowledging a person’s gender identity is an intrinsic part of their identity.6 Equality bodies 
should be mandated to work to tackle discrimination based on gender identity and gender 
expression in order to better understand and serve a highly marginalised part of society. Trans 
and non-binary people, although a minority group, still make up a significant proportion of the 
world’s population, and therefore should not be excluded from equality bodies’ mandates.  
 
Currently two pieces of EU secondary legislation cover the grounds of gender identity, one of 
which also covers gender expression. In the case of the Victim’s Rights Directive, Member 
States must regularly report on how the rights set out in the directive have been accessed as 
regards gender identity and gende r expression. 7 The Recast Qualification Directive also 
recognises gender identity as a ground for which certain social groups may be subjected to 
persecution.8 These Directives build on the already existing CJEU case law, which interpreted, 
in P v S and Co rnwall County Council , that the EU prohibition of sex discrimination in 
employment9 encompasses also those trans people who are intending to, undergoing or

According to ILGA-Europe’s 2021 Rainbow Map, 25 EU Member States already have equality 
bodies whose mandates include combating discrimination based on gender identity.14 Equinet 
recorded in 2020 that of its 37 members, 35 have mandates covering sexual orientation in 
employment, 33 of which covering sexual orientation beyond employment. 31 have mandates 
covering gender identity in employment, 30  of which  covering gender identity beyond 
employment.15 This shows very clearly that Member States are already going beyond the 
limited grounds set out in the EU Treaties, addressing discrimination faced by trans people 
based on their gender identity. 
Inclusion of sex characteristics 
The term “sex characteristics” refers to the physical sex characteristics of an individual, 
including their chromosomes, hormones, gonads, and genitalia. The EU is paying increasing 
attention to individuals with variations of sex characteristics (also known as intersex 
individuals) and their life experiences. 16 For example, the FRA LGBTI II Survey of 2019 
disaggregated the experiences of intersex people, finding that intersex respondents were 
among the most marginalised, including b y finding almost two thirds (62%) of intersex 
respondents felt discriminated against in at least one area of life because of being intersex in 
the 12 months before the survey.  By comparison, this figure was 42% for the average of all 
LGBTI respondents.  Of all the interse x respondents who experienced an incident of

Many European Parl iament resolutions have advocated for ensuring (legal) protection to 
intersex people, also in the framework of gender equality (2020/2035(INL); 2021/2035(INL); 
2020/2215(INI); 2016/2249(INI); 2018/2878(RSP)). The European Commission has recently 
commissioned a study on the lived experiences of intersex people within the EU, with results 
expected at the end of 2022. 
In order to fully and adequately protect intersex people from discrimination, “sex 
characteristics” should be added as a protected ground and as a ground included in equality 
bodies’ mandates. Currently, according to ILGA-Europe’s 2021 Rainbow Map, already five EU 
Member States have equality bodies whose mandates include this ground (Malta, Belgium, 
the Netherlands, Denmark and Finland). 18  Even in countries where equality bodies are not 
mandated in regard to sex  characteristics, intersex issues have been on the radar: for 
instance, earlier this year the Polish  Commissioner for Human Rights asked the Minister of 
Health and the President of the Natio nal Health Fund  with questions about the situation of 
intersex people in Poland, after receiving several applications concerning severe human rights 
violations. In addition, “sex” (covered in the EU Treaties) can also be interpreted in an inclusive 
manner to cover the discrimination faced by intersex people on the basis of their sex 
characteristics, though explicit mention of the grounds is significantly more likely to ensure 
both protection and access to justice.

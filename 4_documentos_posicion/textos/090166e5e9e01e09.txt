 
 
TGEU position paper 
European Commission public consultation on 
equality bodies 
18 March 20222 
Key points: 
• Discrimination against trans people in the EU is significant and increasing. 
• Only 20 Member States are currently working on discrimination relating to 
gender identity and gender expression, yet equality bodies have great 
potential to protect trans people’s rights.  
• To address this gap, a new directive on equality bodies must contain explicit 
reference to gender identity and gender expression, either as stand-alone 
protected grounds, as in the Victims’ Rights Directive, or as an explicit in-text 
interpretation of the ground “sex", as in the Pay Transparency Directive. 
TGEU strongly supports the introduction of binding legislation to govern the 
mandates, services, and minimum standards of national equality bodies. It is essential 
that equality bodies both have minimum standards and that these are accessible to all 
EU citizens, especially the most marginalised, in the same way in all EU countries. 
TGEU considers it essential that the new equality bodies directive includes: 
1. Explicit protection for trans people against discrimination. This can be 
achieved through either listing gender identity and gender expression as 
explicit grounds for discrimination, or through making explicit in the text of 
the directive that the ground of “sex” includes gender identity and gender 
expression, reflecting CJEU case law. Both options would be in line with 
existing EU law.  
2. Explicit reference to addressing intersectional discrimination and the 
inclusion of intersectionality as a cross-cutting principal. 
3. Minimum standards for staffing and financial resources, independence, and 
services provided, so that equality bodies can effectively protect the most 
marginalised.  
Protection of trans people already has a strong legal basis in EU law. 
● Article 19 of the TFEU and Article 21 of the Charter for Fundamental Rights 
already prohibit discrimination on the ground of sex. These laws, as well as

directives that prohibit discrimination on the basis of sex are consistently 
interpreted as including protection for trans people against discrimination.  
● Since P v S and Cornwall County Council in 1994, the Court of Justice of the EU 
determined that the EU prohibition of sex discrimination in employment 
compasses also those trans people who are intending to, undergoing, or having 
undergone gender reassignment. 
● The Pay Transparency Directive text, voted on and adopted by the European 
Parliament on 17 March 2022, notes that “The Court of Justice also has held that 
the principle of equal treatment for men and women cannot be confined to the 
prohibition of discrimination based on the fact that a person is of one or other sex. 
In view of the purpose and the nature of the rights which the principle of equal 
treatment seeks to safeguard, it also applies to discrimination arising from the 
person’s gender identity.” 
● Gender identity and gender expression are increasingly explicitly mentioned in 
secondary EU law as specific grounds for protection: 
a) The Victims Rights’ Directive1 lists gender identity and gender 
expression as not only grounds under which a person may not be 
discriminated against when seeking protection and support as a victim 
of crime, but reasons for which a person may need specific and 
sensitive treatment.  
b) The Recast Qualification Directive2 includes gender identity as possible 
grounds for persecution and seeking asylum. 
c) The Pay Transparency Directive3, notes that it “applies a human rights-
oriented approach that seeks to protect persons from pay discrimination 
irrespective of their sex, gender, gender identity, gender expression or sex 
characteristics and therefore interprets its legal basis, discrimination on the 
grounds of sex, in the broadest and most inclusive way to foster gender 
equality and encompass people in all their diversity”  
● Any new EU directive covering standards for equality bodies must represent a 
‘levelling up’ of existing legislation that covers all forms of discrimination and 
doesn’t leave any gaps.4 Further, it must support and strengthen existing EU 
legislation such that e.g., the rights outlined in the Victims’ Rights Directive are 
directly reflected in the mandates of equality bodies, which are a channel for 
implementation.

Including protection for trans people is coherent with the EU LGBTIQ Strategy… 
● Protection of trans people in the equality bodies directive is also coherent with 
Commission President von der Leyen’s vision for a ‘Union of equality’ and the 
EU LGBTIQ Equality Strategy focus on mainstreaming of LGBTIQ concerns 
into “all EU policies, legislation and funding programmes.”5 
● It follows naturally that any new directive protecting against discrimination on 
grounds of sex covers trans people. However, it must be explicitly and clearly 
written into the text of the directive. 
…and with the findings and demands of the EU Fundamental Rights Agency. 
● In its 2016 report ‘Legislating for stronger more effective equality bodies’, the 
FRA recommended that “EU Member States should ensure that competent public 
authorities, such as equality bodies, national human rights institutions, and 
children’s ombudspersons, are properly mandated, resourced and encouraged to 
deal with bullying and discrimination on grounds of sexual orientation and/or 
gender identity in education.”6  
● It further notes that “trans persons are seen by some public officials as facing 
particularly severe discrimination in many EU Member states” and that “robust EU 
anti-discrimination legislation and relevant directives” are main legal tools of 
public service officials dealing with equality and non-discrimination.7  
● In addition, when it comes to major drivers for LGBT persons’ fundamental 
rights work across all countries, “the work of equality bodies was also seen as 
important”.8

There is a significant need for protection against discrimination that strong equality 
bodies can help with. This protection must be intersectional in its approach and must 
address intersectional discrimination experienced by trans people of further 
marginalised communities. 
● TGEU’s 2021 Trans Rights Map shows that only 20 EU Member States have 
equality bodies that are explicitly mandated to work on gender identity or are 
systematically working on issues covering gender identity.9 
● At the same time, and as noted in the EU LGBTIQ Equality Strategy10, there is 
a significantly high prevalence of and therefore need for protection against 
discrimination against trans people in the EU, which is only increasing over 
time.  
● 60% of trans respondents to the FRA 2019 LGBTI Survey indicated they had 
experienced discrimination in at least one area of life in the previous 12 
months. 40% of trans people experienced discrimination when at work or 
looking for work, one-third by health care or social services personnel.11  
● Trans people are more likely to be unemployed (8%) or out of the workforce 
(43%) than the average population (6-7% and 27%, respectively).12  
● Trans people who experience intersectional discrimination fair even worse. 
Amongst trans migrants, only 27% indicated having a paid job, contrasted with 
40% of their native trans counterparts who reported being in paid 
employment. Having a migrant or ethnic minority background makes it more 
likely for a trans person to be in an unpaid occupation. Disabled trans people 
and, in particular, disabled trans women, are more likely to be unemployed. 
Trans men are more likely to be amongst the working poor.13 
● Discrimination against trans people in the EU appears to be increasing. The 
share of trans respondents who experienced discrimination when looking for 
housing increased from 17 to 21% between 2012 and 2019. In 2012 30% of 
trans respondents indicated they experienced discrimination when looking for 
work, in 2019 this share was 32%. 
● Equality bodies have demonstrated they can be instrumental in helping trans 
people achieve their rights. For example, the Greek Ombudsman, in reaction to 
a complaint by an adult trans student about discrimination based on their

Explicit reference to inclusion of gender identity and gender expression in the text 
of the directive is needed. 
● The language used in the directive needs to be clear and unambiguous if it to 
be truly protective of trans people; other alternatives will not be sufficient.  
● Gender identity and gender expression must be explicitly referenced in the 
text of the directive; a description in the preamble will also not be sufficient. 
This is essential to avoid legal uncertainty and possibilities for 
misinterpretation regarding protection of trans people. 
● Relying on other grounds or phrasing is inadequate. For example, “gender” 
alone is insufficient: trans people may face discrimination due to their gender 
identity and gender expression that is separate to and in addition to 
discrimination they face due to their gender. It is important to make this 
distinction clear. 
●  “Gender reassignment” is not only outdated and inaccurate, but it also misses 
those trans people who don’t intend to undertake medical or other transition. 
○  It also frames gender as a binary construct and therefore does not 
include the majority of trans people who identify outside the binary: 
62% of respondents to the FRA 2019 LGBTIQ survey identified outside 
the gender binary of ‘men’ and ‘women’.15 The EU LGBTIQ Strategy also 
specifically acknowledges the unique discrimination experienced by 
non-binary people throughout its text. 
 Intersectionality as a cross-cutting approach must also be explicitly written into 
the text, following the standard now set by the Pay Transparency Directive.  
● The Pay Transparency Directive is the first ever piece of EU legislation to 
include a definition of intersectional discrimination; any new directive on 
Equality Bodies must also reflect this standard.  Intersectional discrimination is 
defined in defined in the Pay Transparency Directive in this way: 
“’intersectional discrimination’ means a situation in which grounds of discrimination 
prohibited under Directive 2006/54/EC and  one or more grounds of discrimination

To successfully address discrimination against trans people and other 
marginalised groups, equality bodies must be truly independent, sufficiently 
funded, staffed, accessible, and aware of trans people’s needs, and this must be 
written into binding legislation. 
● There are currently numerous ways that national governments can influence 
the running of equality bodies, through interfering with staffing, budgeting, 
and taking away powers of litigation. Minimum standards in these areas would 
ensure that equality bodies are truly independent and effective.  
● Minimum standards for the services provided by equality bodies can only be 
achieved with sufficient resources, both financial and staffing. We have seen 
during the pandemic, a time when marginalised groups have suffered severely 
in many aspects of life and need protection most, that equality bodies had to 
cut services due to lack of staff.  
● These minimum standards are especially important in countries like Hungary, 
Poland, and Bulgaria where democracy and justice for all are under threat and 
where the rights of trans people and other marginalised groups are being 
violated by their national governments.  
● The staff of equality bodies themselves must also be protected against 
victimisation and harassment so that they are not afraid to investigate cases of 
inequality and discrimination.  
● The 2020 European Commission study ‘Legal gender recognition in the EU: the 
journeys of trans people towards full equality’ argued that “[i]t should be 
ensured that equality bodies are competent to handle complaints from all 
transgender people in accessing their rights and can fully support them.”16 The 
report further notes there is a need for Member States to “[e]nsure equality 
bodies are competent and have the adequate resources to support transgender 
individuals in accessing their rights in line with other protected grounds.”17

Strong equality bodies can play many vital roles in protecting marginalised groups 
and strengthening cohesion in society. 
● Equality bodies need to be able to provide legal advice, legal support, and 
representation as essential services. Where equality bodies have provided 
legal advice, it has helped trans people to get their rights, often without having 
to go to court. Where equality bodies have the mandate and resources for 
legal representation, the likelihood of trans people turning to it increases.  
● Equality bodies play an important role in bringing together public authorities 
and marginalised groups. Establishing a dialogue is an important precondition 
for participation in public and policy making processes.  
● Moreover, equality bodies are essential for informing public opinion on 
marginalised groups, and for increasing awareness of rights and respect for 
diversity among the general public. In times of targeted disinformation 
campaigns, it is even more important that equality bodies are mandated to 
uplift the voices of trans people and counter any attempts to attack 
marginalised parts of society as early as possible.   
● Equality bodies can be leaders in setting standards for speech during political 
campaigns and issue recommendations that focus public attention towards 
what is appropriate conduct.  
● Equality bodies can produce important bodies of knowledge and research on 
the experiences of marginalised groups and thus make an important 
contribution to societal discourse.  
● Strong equality bodies, when given appropriate mandate and powers, can play 
a significant role in addressing hate speech, a key issue on the European 
Commission’s agenda. Through empowering victims to report hate speech; 
providing referrals to police, psychological support and other service providers; 
and opening legal proceedings equality bodies are well positioned to defend 
victims’ rights in line with the Victims’ Rights Directive. 
● The current war in Ukraine and the significant number of refugees fleeing to 
the EU for safety – trans and other LGBI people being among the first – 
further highlights the role equality bodies can play in awareness raising and 
building strong community. Citizens should be informed that everyone has the 
same rights with the aim to create empathy, understanding, and pride in EU 
values in a situation that could otherwise easily be exploited by populists.

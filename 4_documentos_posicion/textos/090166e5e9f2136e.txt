IntroductionTwitter International Unlimited Company (“Twitter”) welcomes and appreciates theopportunity provided by the European Commission to participate in the consultation on theupcoming European Media Freedom Act (“EMFA”), published on 10 January 2022.
Twitter fully endorses the rationale behind the EMFA, which is to improve the functioning ofthe media market and increase media pluralism, editorial media independence and theprotection of freedom of expression across media actors.  Indeed, Twitter’s mission is toserve the public conversation and help ensure the freedom of expression of media servicesand individual platform users alike. Allowing online platforms like Twitter to preserve thesetenets as this initiative develops is crucial and can be achieved by ensuring that theobligations of all concerned stakeholders are clear in the text and guidance. It is equallyimportant that there is proportionate understanding of the technical realities that onlineplatforms face, their own rights, and the avenues realistically available to them in bolsteringmedia freedom.
In this consultation response, Twitter makes seven recommendations for a balanced,consistent, and eﬀective legal framework governing media freedom and freedom ofexpression on the internal media market:1. Include a more nuanced distinction between diﬀerent types of in-scope mediaservices, especially online platforms;2. Ensure the freedom of expression of online platforms;3. Adhere to the country-of-origin principle;4. Avoid counterproductive media safeguarding obligations;

Clarifying the scope
Deﬁning media servicesThe consultation questionnaire on the EMFA distinguishes between two general types ofmedia services. On the one hand, it refers to online and oﬄine editorial media, namelymedia companies – ranging from audiovisual to radio and press – that have editorial controlover the content they publish. On the other, it addresses online platforms, speciﬁcally socialnetworks and video-sharing platforms, as presenters and curators of “public media contentfor which they do not exercise editorial control.”
1
Twitter agrees that it is important to distinguish between diﬀerent media actors in order toconceptualise an eﬀective legal Act. However, it urges the European Commission to adoptmore nuanced distinctions. In particular, the EMFA should contain a nuanced and granularunderstanding of diﬀerent types of online platforms and their very diﬀerent roles andcapabilities in achieving the objectives of the EMFA.
Twitter started with the idea of an individual using an SMS service to communicate with asmall group. Its service is also often referred to as “microblogging”. Content on Twitter, frompurely text-based Tweets, to images, and audio(visual) content, is mostly user generated.Partly due to this low-threshold yet eﬀective concept, a diverse range of editorial mediacompanies and individual journalists turn to Twitter to promote their content, increaseexposure, and engage in conversations with media and news consumers. As such, Twitteris a ﬁerce endorser of media pluralism and independence. At the same time, we do nothave the reach or ability to regulate the operation of editorial media, and should not besubject to rules intended for online platforms with more inﬂuence on such matters.
Twitter expects one of the challenging aspects of the EMFA to be the ambiguity regardingwhich companies are intended to be caught by diﬀerent new provisions. This is comparableto concerns which we have with other EU legislation touching on the regulation of onlineplatforms, whose use of diﬀerent but overlapping deﬁnitions cause considerable legaluncertainty. Many conceivable deﬁnitions relevant to media freedom and media pluralismare ‘open’, and prone to providing hesitancy in the market and stiﬂing investment andinnovation as an unintended result. We would encourage the European Commission tocommit to providing as much clarity and certainty as possible as to the scope of the newrules.

Ensuring the freedom of expression of online platforms
The aforementioned binary distinction between editorial media and online platforms runs anon-trivial risk of qualifying the latter exclusively as portals for the dissemination of mediaby editorial media actors. The conclusion from this distinction might be that onlineplatforms themselves do not “express” themselves or “receive and impart information”within the meaning of article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights, and have norights of their own stemming from fundamental-rights laws. This would be incorrect. Forexample, the European Court of Human Rights (“ECtHR”) has ruled that the transmission ofcontent by an intermediary (tele)communications provider also constitutes an “expression”protected under art. 10 ECHR. Indeed, it is settledECtHR case law that the art. 10 ECHR

Legal imperatives about the organisation and transparency of media are therefore alsosquarely an issue of the freedom of expression of online platforms. It is vital that the EMFAactively supports this fundamental right. Twitter invites the European Commission tothoroughly consider how freedom of expression for all actors in online spheres can berespected, both under the EMFA as under a potential discrete legal instrument for theprotection of (online) freedom of expression.
Adhering to the country-of-origin principle
The EMFA is intended to introduce rules on important yet abstract concepts surroundingmedia. Twitter agrees that the fragmentation and inconsistency caused by national laws andinterpretations can be to the detriment of media freedom, pluralism, and independence, aswell as limiting the exercise of treaty and Charter freedoms. To avoid that the EMFA furtherexacerbates these issues with the well-intentioned introduction of rules to combat threats ofEU pluralism at an EU level, it is crucial to keep in mind the country-of-origin principle fromthe outset. Indeed, this principle lies at the heart of EU media law, like the AudiovisualMedia Services Directive (“AVMSD”), and is integral to the proper functioning of an internalEU media market.

Avoiding onerous and ineﬃcient safeguarding obligationsTo ensure that online platforms are able to continue providing healthy and safe serviceswhere media companies and consumers go to diﬀuse and engage with content, it is crucialthat the EMFA does not impose burdensome media plurality safeguards on onlineplatforms. Although attractive in theory, obligations such as self-regulation or obligations onmonitoring or fact-checking on online platforms are unlikely to achieve the intended result.They might inadvertently force Twitter, with relatively little means, to divest from theimportant and eﬀective work it is already doing. What is more, such obligations are prone toinadequate distinction between violating and non-violating content, with the result ofblocking lawful communications and thereby violating users’ freedom to receive and impartinformation.
An example of an ineﬀective and indeed counterproductive measure to promote mediafreedom is the recently debated exemption for media and/or news outlets from removal orother content moderation decisions by online intermediary service providers. Such speciﬁc

Support for online platforms in safeguarding media pluralismWe ﬁrmly believe that strengthening independent and pluralistic media in the internal marketmust coincide with oﬀering suﬃcient support to online platforms in maintaining anddeveloping aforementioned eﬀorts by online platforms.
Speciﬁcally in this context, the space which the EMFA intends to make for testing innovativesolutions, including by way of media sandboxing schemes, is welcome. Indeed, oﬀeringonline platforms the means to innovate and cultivate quick and eﬀective processes for thecreation and maintenance of a healthy online platform is crucial. Therefore, when furtherdeveloping these options, it is important to ensure that their deployment and managementis not discriminatory towards online platforms.
Transparency obligationsThe EMFA consultation addresses issues surrounding the transparency of audience sizesand state advertising. Twitter agrees that obligations regarding these topics can bebeneﬁcial in enhancing the competitive sphere for diﬀerent media companies. However, toensure that any such obligations provide a solution to the problem identiﬁed by theEuropean Commission, it is important that they are formulated meticulously and addressedat those services that will be able to eﬀect change. We elaborate below with regard toaudience measurement and state advertising.
Audience measurements
Twitter believes that any obligation with respect to audience measurement should betailored to the type of media service. While many editorial media companies may have anoverview of their diﬀerent audiences that is instrumental to the planning of contentproduction, calculating advertising prices, and understanding market dynamics, this ismaterially diﬀerent for many online platforms.
For instance, it is ineﬀective – if not impossible – to further segment parts of Twitter’splatform for the identiﬁcation of distinct audiences which are meaningful for thosepurposes. One Twitter user may follow the oﬃcial Twitter account of multiple news outlets,the oﬃcial account of a sporting competition, fan accounts of television shows from severalonline video platforms, and the private account of a recreational cinema enthusiast. Thefollower count of those media services’ Twitter accounts as well as the likes and Retweetsthey received are public. Further audience segmentation and measurements are unlikely toeﬀectively serve the purpose of the proposed requirement.
Twitter invites the European Commission to be cautious with introducing further audiencemeasurements obligations to online platforms. They will likely all be subject to graduatedlevels of transparency obligations, including on the user base, under the upcoming Digital

State advertisingWe agree that transparency on the allocation of state advertising is key in informing mediacompanies and consumers in a democratic society. The eﬀectiveness of this transparencyis best harbored by placing the obligation with the governmental and public institutionswhich decide on the allocation. Here, the primary responsibility for reporting on theallocation of state advertising, the criteria for allocation, the amounts allocated, and thebeneﬁciaries of state advertising should lie with governmental bodies. Insofar as theseobligations extend to media players, they should be proportionate to the service’s focus onadvertising as well as the frequency and/or amount of state advertising allocated.
Enforcement and governanceThe EMFA is a new legal instrument with the potential to apply horizontally across a verydiverse and dynamic media sector. Indeed, the EMFA has the ambitious and laudableobjective of providing coherence among Member States with diﬀering legislation regardingmedia freedom and plurality. In the same vein, it is important that the EMFA ensures a cleardelineation of rules it may partially overlap or interact with – notably the Audiovisual MediaServices Directive, the Digital Services Act, the Digital Markets Act – and their respectiveenforcement.  The EMFA should be drafted with due account to the existing rules, and withas much clarity as possible on how the EMFA interacts with those legal imperatives.
Although precise drafting is vital in achieving consistency, the ultimate enforcement of theEMFA will play a decisive role. Regardless of the ﬁnal division of competences, it isimportant to ensure that there is a clearly carved out competence for the regulator in thecountry of origin, and that any EU-level governance body is able to coordinate with thatregulator.
ConclusionTwitter thanks the European Commission for the opportunity to share our views and we areavailable for a more detailed discussion. We stand ready to work with the EuropeanCommission as it continues to explore options to ensure that the above principles areadhered to during the legislative process. We hope our feedback is helpful in ascertainingthat the EMFA is drafted in a balanced and consistent manner that respects and protects allrights and interests involved.

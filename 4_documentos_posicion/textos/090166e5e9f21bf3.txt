Background 
The Chemical Agents Directive (CAD) (98/24/EC) relates to the protection of the health 
and safety of workers from the risks relating to their safety and health arising, or likely 
to arise, from the effects of chemical agents that are present at the workplace or as a 
result of any work activity involving chemical agents.  As the Directive relies on 
indicative and binding occupational exposure limit values as well as biological limit 
values, these occupational exposure limits can clearly fluctuate with the trend of 
increasing the variety of substances that can be used in workplaces or work activities. 
To deal with this issue we are conscious that the Commission is running through a 
robust exercise having so far proposed limit values for a significant amount of cancer-
causing chemicals (e.g., legislative amendments  covering a total of 26 priority 
chemical agents which saw new limit values  introduced under the Carcinogens  and 
Mutagens Directive (CMD) and the 41 new or revised ones under the Chemical Agents 
Directive (CAD) ). While there is growing awareness  concerning hazardous 
substances at workplaces across the  EU generally , as well as significant and 
substantial provision of information, training, specialist services, and support  tools in 
many countries and sectors , the Chemical Agents Directive still incu r in a series of 
deficiencies and difficulties caused by problems in understanding the complex matter 
of handling hazardous substances, low risk awareness, lack of expertise and 
occupational safety and health knowledge1. 
Reviewing binding occupational exposure limits (OEL) and  biological limit values for 
lead and its compounds and setting an OEL for di-isocyanates in the CAD  does not 
only come as part of the mandate from the Commission to protect workers from risks 
related to exposure to chemical agents at work but also through frameworks such as 
the  Regulation on the Classification, Labelling and Packaging of Substances and 
Mixtures (CLP)  or the Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of 
Chemicals (REACH). It is also part of a bigger agenda as seen from th e European 
pillar of social rights - which makes a very clear reference to a healthy, safe, and well 
adapted work environment – and the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability, which is a 
key commitment of the European Green Deal . On a higher level the 2030 Agenda

When it comes to lead  and derivatives the existing EU limit values (150 µg lead/m³

setting limit values 
On the shorter term, this is unsustainable for workers, consumers and the 
environment, with latest figures indicating that hazardous substances , such as lead 
exposure, accounts for 1.06 million deaths and 24.4  million disability -adjusted life 
years (DALYs) due to long-term effects on health4 5 
While positive progress has happened, as seen on the opinions of the Committee for 
Risk Assessment (RAC) of the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA), the Scientific 
Committee on Occupational Exposure Limits (SCOEL) , The Working Party on 
Chemicals (WPC), or the  recent opinions of the Advisory Committee on Safety an d 
Health at Work and consultative debates with the social partners, there is still an urgent 
need to improve policies and practices . There were expectations for the European 
Commission's Strategic Framework on Health and Safety at Work 2021-2027 to define 
occupational exposures to  substances for which no exposure limit without harmful 
effects c ould be defined , namely endocrine disruptors or skin and/or respiratory 
sensitisers6. 
A stronger focus on the r isk factors associated with reproductive and 
developmental health 
Despite societal and technological advancement many workers are still exposed to 
lead and its compounds. Workers are exposed to lead because of the production, use, 
maintenance, recycling, and disposal of lead material and products. Lead exposure 
occurs in most industry secto rs including construction, manufacturing, wholesale 
trade, transportation, remediation and even recreation. 
Certain jobs and industries are more likely to come in contact with lead.  Jobs and 
activities linked to auto repairers, battery manufacturers, construction workers or glass 
manufacturers have been known to put workers at risk of lead exposure . Workers in 
shipbuilding and ship repair 7 are also known to be exposed to work-related chemical

From a gender -sensitive perspective, l egislation is also very much focused on 
chemical substances, but barely addresses reproductive factors 8 such as physical, 
biological. Women can suffer impacts on reproductive and pregnancy outcomes long 
after exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals. It is estimated9 that about 2 million 
workers in the EU member states are exposed at work to at least one toxic substance 
that adversely affects reproduction. Exposure of pregnant women to high levels of lead 
can cause miscarriage, stillbirth, premature birth, and low birth weight.  Women may 
be more vulnerable to health damage from toxic chemicals “at different life stages such 
as pregnancy, lactation, and menopause10. 
While the EU Directive on the protection of pregnant workers and workers who have 
recently given birth or who are breastfeeding (Council Directive 92/85/EEC) 
recognises a broad range of conditions that could present a risk for new and expectant 
mothers, r egarding reprot oxicants and OELs for reprotoxic substances, there are, 
however, limitations and gaps that need to be considered and addressed.  
Good examples of performance of lead management as a reproductive and 
developmental toxicant  can be  seen in countries such as Germany 11, that 
distinguishes between workers in general (max. blood lead levels of 40 µg/dL) and 
women of childbearing age (i.e.  less than 45 years of age) in particular (max. blood 
lead levels of 10 µg/dL) to minimise the potential risk to the foetus . That said, these 
examples tend to be an exception to the general rule or practice from Member States.

A risk-based management approach 
Further efforts regarding approaches to chemical risk assessment and risk 
management at the  workplace are required . It is well -evidenced that a risk -based 
approach allows a fair comparison of the risks posed by several chemicals althoug h 
there is still a need for improved transparency and Member States working towards a 
collective and harmonised methodology. Chemical risk assessment and management 
practices have been  accepted as a crucial  and essential starting point in chemicals 
management at  workplaces (apart from the principles that prevention followed by  
substitution must always come first). In many cases these practices are of a mandatory 
nature, but according to the latest evidence13, there is still a weak connection between 
risk assessment and the necessary risk management measures  derived from it , in 
European workplaces.  
Approaches from countries could be considered. Such as the UK in which the general 
duty of care r equires employers to ensure that the exposure to these hazardous 
substances from any route is either prevented or, where this is not reasonably 
practicable, adequately controlled as per the hierarchy, leaving employers to consider 
and adopt the best practi cal measures for achieving the overall protection of 
employees’ occupational health. 
This of course needs to be monitored by regulators to ensure those legal requirements 
are being implemented and that the control measures are suitable, examples of which 
include, suitability of the personal protective equipment, the availability of occupational 
health services where needed , the hygiene and rest facilitates , and the quality of 
training and awareness from workers on the use and e ffects of these substances or 
how to recognise the symptoms of over-exposure or misuse. 
Recommendations 
This response highlights a number of recommendations for better protecting workers 
from exposure to chemicals  (lead and di -isocyanates), considering that legislative 
developments in the form of OEL’s will not be enough to reduce workers’ exposure to 
hazardous chemicals.  
• Enforcement and implementation at the workplaces, in particular in micro-SME 
companies, constantly has to be improved. This needs to be well-supported by 
other initiatives that seek to improve  the waySMEs assess and control  risks

• Something that IOSH and other organisations such as EU -OSHA have

• From a technical perspective , experts working on the field still struggle with 
occupational health and safety issues arising from the interrelationship between 
European occupational safety and health requirements and those coming from 
other legislative developments such as REACH and CLP. We believe there is 
still a need for bett er alignment to bring much needed consistency, 
understanding and comparability between Member States practices and 
perspectives. At the same time  wider e xpertise needs to be taken into 
consideration in key decision-making initiatives from not only toxicologists, but 
also epidemiologists, occupational health and experts, and experts in public 
and occupational health. 
 
• More evidence-based research is needed to understand the health effects of 
exposure to toxic chemicals to women and to analyse the global burd en of 
disease related to women and chemicals . As such, this evidence should be 
used in the identification of OELs and limit values.  A stronger collaboration 
between the Commission key regulating bodies and the ILO, the WHO and the 
International Commission on Occupational Health should be stimulated (ICOH) 
is also suggested. 
 
• Raising-awareness initiatives that inform each worker who could be exposed to 
dangerous substances about the hazards related to the chemicals, and other 
on-site employers whose workers could be exposed, about chemical hazards 
and appropriate protective measures . We believe the European Agency for 
Health and Safety at Work 2018-19 campaign that aimed to raise awareness of 
dangerous substances in the workplace, the risks associated with them and 
how to prevent harm to workers was an excellent example of translating a 
technical issue into a wider audience. The campaign clearly tackled a structural 
issue, the low levels of literacy from workers and employers on this matter. We 
recommend building on this awareness initiatives to not only increase 
awareness of relevant EU legislation and but to provide businesses and 
workers with practical guidance on how to ensure compliance, prevention, and 
an effective management.  
 
 
 
IOSH welcomes this inquiry that looks to assess the work -related health risks of 
occupational workplace exposure to chemicals, from lead and its compounds and di-
isocyanate exposure. 
IOSH would be keen to see stronger measures introduced, with a collective effort by 
policy makers, government, regulators, employers, and worker representatives.  For 
that reason, we would be pleased to provide further input on the current risks posed 
by chemicals in the workplace.

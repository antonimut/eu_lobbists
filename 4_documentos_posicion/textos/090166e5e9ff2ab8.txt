Vodafone is a communications technology company connecting over 334 million people, and 
organisations of all sizes, to the digital society. Through our business, we aim to build an inclusive 
digital society that enhances socio-economic progress, embraces everyone and does not come at 
the cost of our planet.   
As one of Europe’s largest converged TV operators, we provide TV services via cable, IPTV, mobile

Vodafone welcomes the recent initiative by the European Commission to support and protect free 
journalism and pluralism of media and detect obstacles in the EU media market. Nothing has 
shown more t he relevance of media freedom than the Ukrainian crisis and the  importance of 
ensuring a safe, fair and diverse audiovisual landscape in the EU. 
We appreciate the possibility to comment on the questions raised by the European Commission in 
the consultation on the Media Freedom Act. This  paper sets out our views on the topics raised in 
the consultation which are particularly relevant to Vodafone in its capacity as a large pan-European 
TV provider in the media sector.  
 
1. Safeguarding the EU internal media market, media independence and 
Pluralism
  
 
The media landscape in European markets (but also internationally) is currently massively 
evolving, driven by increasing digitisation and the accompanying technological convergence. This 
has made the internet the central media distribution channel, also for the various forms of content 
that were previously clearly separated by means of distribution. This has led to a  change in user 
expectations among recipients, who are now able to access a much greater variety of content, 
entirely according to their individual preferences. 
The interaction of the different players in the media landscape is changing, both in economic terms 
as well as in the way information and communication services interact.

The aim of the Media Freedom Act should therefore be to achieve a  harmonization in the EU in 
order to strengthen and promote media pluralism.  
Vodafone welcomes the initiative taken by the European Commission to revise the AVMS Directive 
in order to harmonise national legislation on audio-visual media services while contributing to the 
promotion of cultural diversity, providing an adequate level of consumer protection, and 
safeguarding media pluralism.  
In our experience, however, this goal of harmonisation has unfortunately not yet been achieved in 
all European countries. This results on the one hand from the different levels of implementation in 
the Member States (some Member States have not implemented the AVMS directive yet) and on 
the other hand  from the diverging interpretations of regulatory concepts relevant  to media 
pluralism. Examples in this context are the different rules for prominence and public value offers. 
Prominence 
Recital 25 of the AVMS Directive states as follows:  
“Directive 2010/13/EU is without prejudice to the ability of Member States to impose obligations 
to ensure the appropriate prominence of content of general interest under defined general interest 
objectives such as media  pluralism, freedom of speech and cultural diversity. Such obligations 
should only be imposed where they are necessary to meet general interest objectives clearly 
defined by Member States in accordance with Union law. Where Member States decide to impose 
rules on appropriate prominence, they should only impose proportionate obligations on 
undertakings in the interests of legitimate public policy considerations.“ 
Some EU Members States (e.g. Germany) have taken advantage of the possibility given by R ecital 
25 of the AVMS directive to implement in national media regulation provisions for the easy 
findability of media offers classified as content of general interest or public value.  
Other Member States plan to do this e.g. it has recently been announced that the French regulator 
Arcom is in charge of defining ways how to make general interest content visible on the main user 
interfaces installed on a television set, on devices intended to be connected to the television set, 
on connected speakers, on service distributors or within an app stores). 
European works 
Recital 35 of the AVMS Directive states as follows: 
„Prominence can be ensured through various means such as a dedicated section for European 
works that is accessible from the service homepage, the possibility to search for European works in 
the search tool available as part of that service, the use of European works in campaigns of that 
service or a minimum per-centage of European works promoted from that service's catalogue, for 
example by using banners or simi-lar tools.”  
Another example relates to the provisions for the highlighting European works in VOD offers, which 
require that providers of on -demand audiovisual media services should promote the production 
and distribution of European works by ensuring that their catalogues con tain a minimum share of 
European works and that they are given sufficient prominence . Recital 35 of the AVMS directive 
specifies how prominence for European works can be ensured by various means. 
While certain Member States (e.g. Netherlands) have very general requirements on how to secure 
prominence of European works in VOD offers, other Member States, e.g. Germany, have very

Public value offers 
A further example resulting from the  implementation of Recital 25 AVMS directive concerns the 
prominence of public value  offers. We note that German y provides an application and selection 
procedure in which TV and radio broadcasters , as well as content providers , can apply to be 
classified by state media authorities as being of public value or general interest. Currently over 300 
content providers applied in Germany to be classified as public value offers.  
For platform operators the German  regulation provides the obligations to highlight and to list 
public value offers  in a special section on the user interfaces , as well as to install the function to 
search particularly for the keyword public value offer. As this detailed regulation does not apply in 
any other EU Member State in which we are active, the user interface of our platforms needs to be 
adapted only for one member state causing significant implementation costs. In our opinion these 
costs are not justified as prominence can also be ensured by other means already provided by our 
platform e.g. filtering in the search function.  We would like to see ‘ more room for manoeuvre ’ in 
this respect, consistent with the goals of the regulation. 
In summary, t hese d iverging concepts to safeguard media pluralism , combined with the above 
deviations from the country-of-origin principle, can make it exceptionally challenging to provide a 
harmonised pan-EU or cross-border media service. 
Provisions to foster media pluralism (like the above mentioned on public value, prominence) at 
Member State level should focus on measures that contribute to building the single market. 
Requirements for user interfaces and devices should be harmoni sed (e.g. a “Public Value Button”) 
so that they are consistent across the single market. As a result, even if the general interest content 
is different in all Member States, the user interfaces and platforms design would be harmonised. 
The aim of European media regulation should be to create the equ ivalent conditions which allow 
pan European providers to create competitive offerings in an increasingly globalised competitive 
environment. The main focus of regulation should be to secure competition and to create a 
common level playing field.

obviously, it is relevant to advertising  and marketing. However, it also underpins requirements for

We also observe very different approaches to audience measurement across Member States, which 
can vary depending on the measurement of linear or non-linear content (VOD).  
For example, in Hungary the Nielsen reports are used as a measurement too l for TV broadcasting. 
The Netherlands, Germany and the Czech Republic, use small panels of participants to track the

Smaller panels have the risk of offering less scalable and representative data for the overall 
advertising market. Also, the increased use of TV offerings in the i nternet leads to distortions in 
audience measurement, especially for linear TV . Therefore, the advertising industry is asking for 
more representative, detailed data. 
In our opinion audience measurement must depend on consumption of types of media and that 
differs heavily by generation and education, and also from M ember State to M ember State. In 
addition, not all Member States have measurement tools for all different kinds of content e.g. for 
VOD usage or the measurement of multiscreen and OTT. This needs to be developed in the future.  
We do not propose that one advertising measurement concept should be required for all markets 
or media categories across the EU. However, they should be suitably harmonised. The key objective 
should be that the required measurement criteria are transparent, i nclusive, and proportionate of 
all relevant media categories as well as accessible for both stakeholders and competent 
authorities.  
We do not see the need to install a European tool for audience measurement to safeguard 
competition. To ensure fair competition where audience measurement is relevant for the business 
models, national competition authorities can look at the audience measurement standards from 
an anti-trust perspective, as they have done already in certain cases to remove entry barriers (e.g. 
the Federal Cartel Office in Germany investigating  Google). Competition law provides a solid basis 
to address this. A newly introduced provision under the German Competition Act, that entered into 
force in January 2021, enables the Fe deral Cartel Office in Germany to intervene earlier and more 
effectively, in particular against the practices of large digital companies and to take action against 
specific anti-competitive practices also so caused by processing of personal data and dealing with 
them.

Vodafone strongly supports media diversity and media pluralism: Vodafone distributes a variety of 
free-to-air TV channels and pay channels as well as in 10 European markets among others a large 
number of smaller local and regional operating TV and radio channels.  
Obstacles that we are facing in the pan -European media market result from fragmentation and 
different level of harmo nisation in the regulation  introduced a t across EU Member States ,, 
examples of which were already set out in section one above.

4. Governance options 
In our opinion there is no need to strengthen ERGA’s current institutional set- up or to install an 
independent secretariat to assist ERGA. Regulators on national level even if there are diverse 
responsible in one country (14 state media authorities in Germany) cooperate in an effecti ve and 
sufficient way together. We welcome the cooperation on national level within ERGA.  
Obstacles in the cooperation between national regulators result in our opinion more from the lack 
of enforcement agreements between EU M embers States e.g. regarding the protection of minor s 
(Blocking of illegal content), rather than from a lack of cooperation between national operators. 
We therefore would propose that the European Commission continues to drive better coordination 
between the individual regulatory authorities of the EU Member States.

Lumos’ recommendations on the initiative: ‘Preventing and combating trafficking in 
human beings- review of EU rules’ 
Introduction  
Lumos is fighting for every child’s right to a family by transforming care systems around the world. We are an 
international charity striving for a future where every child is raised in a safe, loving home, supported by family 
to help them thrive.  Lumos has been sitting for years on the EU Civil Society Platform against Trafficking in 
Human Beings, and is a founding member of the European Expert Group on the Transition from Institutional to 
Community-based Care. Lumos is also Co-Chair of the Child Rights Action Group, and a member of the EU 
Alliance for Investing in Children. 
 
An estimated 5.4 million children ar ound the world live in institutions – including so -called “orphanage s”, 
residential special schools and reception centres – that undermine their human rights and cannot meet  their 
needs.1 There are numerous definitions of what the term ‘institution’ means when referring to children.  The 
Common European Guidelines on the Transition from Institutional to Community-based Care define institutions 
as any residential setting where an ‘institutional culture’ prevails. Children living in an ‘institutional culture’ are 
isolated from the broad er community and are compelled to live with children to whom they are not related. 
Crucially, the requirements of the organisation tend to take precedence over the children’s individual needs. 2  
Over 80 years of research from across the world has demonstrated that institutional care can harm children’s 
wellbeing and development. 3 Evidence also points to institutional care as both a driver and outcome of 
trafficking, playing what appears to be a significant role in many instances of child exploitat ion and abuse .4 
Children in institutions are not only highly vulnerable to being trafficked 5 but institutions can also serve as a 
driver of child trafficking and a destination for children who have been trafficked. 6 Therefore, by adopting a 
deinstitutionalisation strategy, one will also be reducing a demand that fosters traffickin g. At the same time, it 
is important that strategies aimed at combatting trafficking also cover trafficking in to and out of institutions.  
 
The current war in Ukraine has once again demonstrated how vulnerable children in institutions are.  7 They are 
being daily exposed to multiple risks, including being left completely without care if staff are forced to flee. 
The crisis in Ukraine has also resulted in millions of Ukrainians fleeing the country, of which hundreds 
of thousands are children. Amongst those fleeing, many are unaccompanied or have been separated

The links between children’s institutions and trafficking 
There is growing evidence of links between institutionalisation of children and child trafficking  - 
‘institution-related trafficking’. The following linkages have been identified: 
 
1) Children are recruited and trafficked into institutions for the purpose of financial profit and other forms 
of exploitation – also known as ‘orphanage trafficking’; 
2) Children are trafficked from orphanages/institutions into other for ms of exploitation  including sexual 
abuse, forced labour and forced criminality; 
3) Child trafficking victims and unaccompanied children are placed in institutions for ‘protection’, which 
can put them at risk of trafficking and re-trafficking; 
4) Care-leavers are more vulnerable to exploitation and trafficking.9 
 
Based on evidence assessed for Lumos’ Cracks in the System Report10, all forms of institution-related trafficking 
are evident in Europe with the last three forms being particularly prevalent. Nevertheless, at present there is no 
legislation in Europe that refers to the trafficking of children into and out of institutional c are specifically.  
 
Orphanage Trafficking 
In recent years, the phenomenon of ‘orphanage trafficking’ has become more widely recognised. Orphanage 
trafficking is generally defined as the trafficking of children from vulnerable families into residential institutions 
for the purpose of financial exploitation.11 In some cases, children are actively ‘recruited’ into orphanages, often 
using (false) promises of education and food. Once inside, children can be further exploited, whether sexually 
or by being forced into labour such as begging on the streets and dancing for tourists to earn money, or through 
illegal adoption. Some ‘orphanages’ are profit -making ventures and exist to attract the lucrative international 
flows of volunteers, donations and other funding.  
 
For the purposes of this initiative , it is important that EU Member  States recognise their role in tackling 
orphanage trafficking globally, by informing their citizens about the harms of supporting orphanages, and 
banning the practice of orphanage volunteering within the EU.   
 
Onward Trafficking 
Children in institutions are at high risk of becoming victims of onward trafficking. Aware of the ext reme 
vulnerabilities of children living in institutions, human traffickers across Europe are known to approach potential 
victims directly inside or in the close vicinity of the institution, often capitalising on their wish to run away, often 
for the purpos e of sexual  exploitation.12 Some conditioning tactics include the cultivation of romantic 
relationships with institutionalised children or the provision of financial support.  For example, children and 
young adults who ran away from local orphanages and pen al institutes in Romania and Albania have ended up

Re-trafficking 
Child victims of trafficking are often placed (back) in institutions by the responsible authorities, creating a vicious 
cycle for trafficked children and additional risks to their peers in institutions. The reasons for placing them in an 
institution are threefold: (i) children might be placed in an institution as a protection measure; (ii) not considered 
a victim  by law enforcement and the criminal justice system , children are placed in the institution a s a  law 
enforcement measure; or (iii) children are placed in an institution simply because there appears to be no viable 
alternative. In some cases, victims of trafficking are placed in the same institution from which they were 
trafficked, re-exposing them to the risks of being re-trafficked and re-traumatisation. The institutionalisation of 
victims of trafficking thus magnifies vulnerability to future exploitation, perpetuating the intricate cycle of 
institutionalisation and trafficking.15 
 
Care Leavers 
Care leavers are particularly vulnerable to trafficking even if they are adequately supported to reintegrate into 
society once they have left an institution. Care leavers can be categorized into two groups: (i) those who ‘age 
out’ of the system and (ii) those who run away from the institution. Due to the lack of financial means to sustain 
themselves and lack of knowledge and information about trafficking, 16 care leavers are particularly vulnerable 
to trafficking. Care leavers can find themselves in situatio ns where they may be forced into lives of sexual 
exploitation, begging or robbery to survive.17 Furthermore, research has shown that care leavers can sometimes 
also assume the role of perpetrators of institution -related trafficking, having themselves been recruited and 
coerced by friends who sometimes used to live in the same institution, before going on to recruit and coerce 
others.18 
 
The EU’s Role 
The EU has recognised the harms of institutionalisation and taken various steps to transition from institutional 
to family - and community -based care. W hilst w elcoming these developments, it is now time for the EU to  
recognise that child trafficking presents an additional risk faced by children living in institutions, and can also be 
the reason why children end up in institutions. This reality needs to be urgently addressed. In this regard, Lumos 
calls on the EU to expand the scope of the Anti-Trafficking Directive to also cover institution-related trafficking. 
 
The recognition by European countries of institution -related trafficking finds footing in the 2019 UN Resolution 
on the Rights of the Child, which urges states to take action to ensure the enjoyment of human rights for all 
children without parental care, i ncluding by: “Taking appropriate measures to protect children who are victims 
of trafficking and are deprived of parental care, as well as enacting and enforcing legislation to prevent and 
combat the trafficking in and exploitation of children in care faci lities, and supporting children who are victims 
of human trafficking in returning to their families and in receiving appropriate mental health and psychological 
assistance that is victim-centred and trauma-informed, and taking appropriate measures to prevent and address

Recommendations 
As pointed out in the European Commission’s First, Second and Third progress reports on the EU Anti-Trafficking 
Directive, trafficking in human beings has further evolved and the available means are not sufficient to fight it 
effectively. 
Lumos welcomes the Commission’s initiative to revise the  EU Anti-Trafficking Directive and to address possible 
gaps and shortcomings of the current legislative framework.  The objective of the below recommendations and 
amendments to the  current EU Anti-Trafficking Directive  (2011/36/EU) is to address a serious lacuna in the  
current legislative framework on trafficking in human being s. Presently, perpetrators involved in the chain of 
trafficking of a child into, within and out of an institution can continue their criminality with  limited fear of any 
interference, criminal investigation or prosecution. The current crisis in Ukraine which makes children deprived 
of parental care even more vulnerable is adding to the urgent need to close the loopholes which enables those 
exploiting the vulnerability of children to operate with almost impunity.  In order to enhance awareness on this 
issue and offer better protection to the victims of institution-related trafficking we recommend the following 19: 
1. Broaden the definition of trafficking, to include orphanage trafficking 
To ensure that a revised Directive can address the phenomenon of children being trafficked into orphanages for 
the purpose of financial exploitation , this form of trafficking should be explicitly covered . Lumos proposes the 
following changes/additions to the Directive 2011/36/EU: 
Recital 11: In order to tackle recent developments in the phenomenon of trafficking in human beings, this 
Directive adopts a broader conce pt of what should be considered trafficking in human beings  (…). The 
definition also covers trafficking in human beings for the purpose of the removal of organs, which constitutes 
a serious violation of human dignity and physical integrity, as well as, for  instance, other behaviour such as 
illegal adoption or forced marriage in so far as they fulfil the constitutive elements of trafficking in human 
beings. The definition also covers any act where a child is being removed from its parents or caregivers to 
be accommodated in a n orphanage or any other residential childcare institution for the purpose of  
(financial)  exploitation. 
Article 2: Offences concerning trafficking in human beings 
3. Exploitation shall include, as a minimum, the exploitation of the prostitution of others or other forms of 
sexual exploitation, forced labour or services, including begging, slavery or practices similar to slavery, 
servitude, or the exploitation of criminal activities, or the removal of organs , or the recruitment, 
transportation, transfer, harbouring or reception of  children in orphanages or other residential childcare 
institutions for the purpose of financial exploitation. 
  
2. Protect child victims of trafficking from being placed in institutions by the responsible authorities to 
prevent re-trafficking 
As explained in the ‘re -trafficking’ section, child victims of trafficking are often placed in institutions by 
responsible authorities, which puts them at increased risk of being re-trafficked. This can create an ongoing cycle

3. Includes offences committed against a child living in institutional care as an aggravating 
circumstance 
Article 4 of 2011/36/EU states that Member States shall take measures to ensure that an offence referred to in 
Article 2 is punishable by a maximum penalty of at least 10 years  of imprisonment, where that offence was 
committed against a victim who was particularly vulnerable  (when the victim is not particularly vulnerable, a 
maximum penalty of at least 5 years of imprisonment applies) . It is explicitly stated that  child victims are 
‘particularly vulnerable’. However, there is no provision for victims who suffer different types of vulnerabilities, 
such as children in institutional care, who are even more vulnerable as they are not protected by a family 
environment. To ensure that perpetrators who exploit the extreme vulnerability of children in institutions are 
adequately punished, this should be accounted for in the Directive. Lumos therefore proposes the following 
changes to the Directive: 
Article 4: Penalties 
[New point to be added] 4. Member States shall take the necessary measures to ensure  that an offence 
referred t o in Article 2  which was comm itted against a child victim living in an orphanage or any other 
residential childcare institution up until or when  being trafficked  by the perpetrator , is seen as an 
aggravating circumstance. 
4. 5. Member States shall take the necessary measures to ensure that an offence referred to in Article 3 is 
punishable by effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties, which may entail surre nder. 
 
 
4. Call on member states to discourage their citizens to undertake any volunteering activities in 
orphanages

ANNEX I: Recommendations stemming from Lumos’ Panel Discussion on 27 October 2021 
Time to Strengthen the Anti -Trafficking Directive: How can a revised Directive better protect ALL 
children at risk of trafficking, including those vulnerable to the different forms of institution-related 
trafficking? 
 
This online event started with a presentation delivered by Lumos, followed by a panel discussion on 
how a revised Directive can better protect all children at risk of trafficking. On the panel were Parosha 
Chandran - Professor in Modern Slavery Law; Juan Fernando López Aguilar - Member of the European 
Parliament; Soraya Rodríguez Ramos - Member of the European Parliament ; Ivanka Kotorova - 
National Member for Bulgaria at Eurojust; and Gabriëlle op ‘t Hoog – Research Consultant at Ecorys. 
 
Lumos’ research20 identifies four ways in which trafficking is linked with institutions for children  (for 
which we use the umbrella term “institution-related trafficking”): 
1. Children are recruited and trafficked into institutions, solely for the purpose of financial profit 
and other forms of exploitation (this is also referred to as ‘orphanage trafficking’). 
2. Children are trafficked from institutions into other forms of exploitation. 
3. Child trafficking victims and unaccompanied children are placed in institutions for 
“protection”, putting them at risk of re-trafficking. 
4. Care-leavers are more vulnerable to exploitation and trafficking. 
Despite these proven links between child trafficking and institutions, there is currently no legislation 
in Europe that refers to the trafficking of children into and out of institutional care specifically.  
We therefore asked our panellists: How can institution -related trafficking be addressed  at the EU 
level, and how can the EU Anti-Trafficking Directive be Strengthened? This resulted in the following 
set of key messages: 
• Targeted laws are transformative, and in the absence of such, criminals can act with impunity 
and victims remain undetected. To date, trafficking laws have not given adequate attention 
to the forms of trafficking which centre around institutional care for children. Exploiters 
operate by finding these cracks in the system, and can use them to identify and traffic 
vulnerable children. Laws, policy, research and practice need to work together as a framework 
to address institution -related trafficking, better pr otect vulnerable children and hold 
perpetrators to account. 
• The common element of victims of trafficking is vulnerability. Children are  particularly 
vulnerable,21 and therefore child victims of trafficking require special attention, with the best 
interest of the child being paramount. Those who do not have the protection of a family are 
especially vulnerable. If the public administrations in charge of children without parental care 
fail to significantly enhance their role of offering protection and safeguarding, these children 
will remain at increased risk of trafficking.

• Effective coordination (including cross -border) between child protection agencies, judicial

• European policy- and decision-makers can play a role in tackling all forms of institution-related

• Orphanage tourism and volunteering play a role in driving family separation and 
institutionalisation. Citizens across the EU take part in these activities, often without 
knowing the harmful consequences.  
 
Based on the outcomes of the panel discussion, these are  Lumos’ recommendations for a new 
Directive:  
i. Broaden the definition of trafficking so as to cover all forms of institution-related trafficking;    
ii. Strengthen the capacity of the different bodies which  come in contact with  victims of 
trafficking such as child welfare officials with the aim to ensure that child victims of trafficking 
are not placed (back) into institutions as a protection measure.   
iii. Improve collabora tion and information -sharing between child protection actors, law 
enforcement and the courts for children deprived of parental care;  
iv. Reinforce data collection  mechanisms to ensure that institution -related trafficking is 
recorded and included in national referral mechanisms.  
v. Enhance accountability by implementing  systems that monitor and regulate funding  that 
flows into orphanages.   
vi. Raise awareness about institution -related trafficking and ban the practise of unskilled 
volunteering in children’s institutions.

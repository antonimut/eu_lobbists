Trafficking in human beings - Evaluation and Revision of the Directive 
 
The Red Cross EU Office (RCEU) represents the 27 National Red Cross Societies in the European 
Union, the Norwegian Red Cross, The Icelandic Red Cross and the International Federation of the 
Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies (IFRC).  
 
National Red Cross Societies in Europe assist all people in need, without discrimination and 
irrespective of their legal status both throughout their journeys and once they are settled. Several 
of our members across the European Union (EU) provide psychosocial, integration and legal 
assistance to identified victims of trafficking in human beings (THB). 
 
The document below aims at complementing the RCEU contribution to the Consultation ‘Fighting 
human trafficking – review of EU rules’ by providing some more comments to our answers.  As a 
member of the EU Civil Society platform against THB, the RCEU welcomes the opportunity to 
respond to this consultation, and remains committed to further contribute to the efforts of the 
European Union in the fight against trafficking.  
 
Introduction 
Trafficking in human beings is prohibited by the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union as a grave 
violation of fundamental rights. It is also addressed in Article 83(1) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the 
European Union as a particularly serious cross -border crime that can only be effectively tackled by common 
minimum rules across the European Union. 
 
Nearly half of the victims of trafficking within the EU are EU citizens, and a significant number of them are 
trafficked within their own Member State. EU and non-EU victims are also trafficked from their country of origin 
via transit countries to their destination, both within and to the European Union. The EU Anti-Trafficking Directive 
(‘Directive', 2011/36/EU)has been the backbone of the EU’s efforts in combatting human trafficking since 2011.  
 
The Directive: 
 
▪ establishes minimum rules concerning the definition of criminal offences and sanctions in the area of 
trafficking in human beings; and 
▪ introduces provisions, taking into account the gender perspective, to strengthen the prevention of this 
crime and the protection of the victims as well as to reinforce investigation and prosecution.  
▪ The Commission First, Second and Third progress reports and the results of the EU-wide data collections 
demonstrate that trafficking in human beings has further evolved and the available means are not 
sufficient to fight it effectively. According to the latest available data, between 2017 and 2018, there 
were more than 14 000 registered victims within the European Union. The actual number is likely to be 
significantly higher as many victims remain undetected. 
 
The Commission acknowledged in the EU Strategy on combatting trafficking in human beings 2021- 2025 adopted 
on 14 April 20211 ( 'Strategy') that various reports indicate that the decade old Directive may not be entirely fit 
for purpose any longer in all relevant aspects. As a consequence, the Strategy sets out as a key action to evaluate 
the implementation of the Anti -trafficking Directive and if necessary, based on the outcome of this evaluation, 
propose revising it to make it fit for purpose, including by assessing the possibility of having minim um EU rules 
that criminalise the use of exploited services from victims of trafficking.  This public consultation will inform the 
evaluation and a possible revision of the EU Anti -trafficking Directive, providing citizens and stakeholders with

Comments 
Human trafficking is connected to all the enlisted criminal actions above, although at different extents. 
Victims of trafficking can be involved/exploited in drug trafficking and money laundering. Document 
fraud can facilitate the action of traffickers, while corruption has a negative impact on rule of law and 
law enforcement, which conditions the effectiveness of the anti-trafficking policies. 
 
The facilitation of irregular migration is  not necessarily based on the intention to exploit persons, 
neither it is necessarily combined with practices  producing harm to the migrant , although this can 
happen. As per definition, ‘smuggling’ is based on a commercial relationship which ends after irregular 
border crossing. Conversely, trafficking entails an exploitative relationship, which continues in order to 
maximise profits from the exploitation of the victim.

Comments  
There is no evidence proving the reduction of the ‘demand’ for these forms of human trafficking in the 
years following the introduction of the directive, however the reported increase in the number of 
victims detected does not necessarily refer to an increase of trafficking actions, but could correspond 
to a better detection and identification of victims.  
 
While the demand for trafficking is hard to define, there is a clear demand for cheap and exploitative 
products, services and labour. At the same time, it is possible to identify a growth in the number of 
people that are vulnerable to trafficking and would therefore constitute the potential ‘offer’. This is 
due to the increased vulnerabilities created by economic insecurity and health emergencies in the last 
years, as well as the increasing number of migrants with irregular or precarious status, that are more 
vulnerable to exploitation due to lack of access to regular labour contracts. Ensuring regularisation of 
trafficking survivors, and in general investing in regularisation campaigns of undocumented migrants 
can protect persons irregularly residing in the EU from being trafficked or re-trafficked.

Comments  
As a neutral humanitarian actor, the Red Cross refrains from engaging in prosecution and law 
enforcement, including in cooperation in judicial processes related to investigating crime. 
Nevertheless, a number of actors have analysed this measure, which has a number of potentially 
harmful effects on the victims.  
 
• First, the criminalisation of the knowing use of exploited services of victims is extremely hard 
to prove, and risks to lead to extremely long trials and criminal prosecutions rarely conducive 
to conviction. 
 
• Second, criminalising all those that knowingly use services  which involve exploitation is 
dangerous and impractical, as it would oblige all citizens to examine working conditions under 
which goods are produced or services offered. Such a provision may create a situation of legal 
uncertainty, where anyone can be held criminally liable for buying goods, products or services 
for everyday use, that are produced within the global supply chain, by workers in extremely 
precarious and exploitative conditions (and may include trafficked persons). 
 
• Third, in the case of victims of sexual exploitation, domestic servitude or other forms of labour 
exploitation, criminalising the knowing use of exploited services risks to worsen the condition 
of victims, which end up being more likely to experience violent behaviours of exploiters. Some 
evidence has been provided by specialised organisations on  the case of buyers of sexual 
services. In the countries where clients’ criminalisation has been approved, sex workers have 
been directly stigmatised and marginalised, increasing the dependence between victims of 
trafficking and their exploiters. 
 
• Last, the criminalising the use, will not reduce the demand on cheap or exploitative services: 
only by ensuring that the demand of services is met by workers who are well informed about 
their rights, and who are enabled to enforce their rights through safe  and effective reporting 
mechanisms it is possible to limit exploitation.

Comments  
The articles 11 and 12 of the Directive 2011/36/EU provides ground for ensuring that victims of 
trafficking have access to assistance, support and protection measures. Articles 13 and 14 provide for

2011/36/EU have been transposed in the national legislation. The report reflected a good but 
incomplete level of implementation, with most of Member States having included provisions on 
assistance and support for victims of trafficking in human beings (both for what concerns assistance 
and support, and for criminal investigation and proceedings). Nevertheless, considering that more than 
5 years have passed from this report, conduc ting a second report to assess the  extent to which the 
directive has contributed to ensu ring victims ’ protection could help in evaluating the directive’s 
effectiveness.

Comments 
Compensation remains one of the least implemented provisions of the Directive 2011/36/EU, and 
among the most difficult ones to be enforced.   
 
While it is positive that the Directive includes the right to compensation, already embedded in the 
Council of Europe Convention of 2005, the practical application of this right is challenging , and many 
victims have still no access to this right. The transposal of the directive ensured that all Member States 
provide the possibility for such compensation, available to all victims irrespective of  
nationality, nevertheless the provision is rarely enforced.  
 
In addition to various procedural obstacles, victims have no access if assets are not recovered, and even 
if compensation is awarded in court, it is very difficult to ensure that the compensation order is 
executed, as victims have no access to legal support after the court procedure has concluded.

The Directive 2011/36/EU – together with the CoE Convention – has ensured that EU member states 
have specific legislation in place to prevent the punishment of victims of trafficking. Nevertheless, 
Implementation of the national provisions is still seriously lagging behind.  
 
Despite international and European legally binding standards on non-punishment, and the adoption 
of specific legislation in several countries, trafficked persons in Europe are often still wrongly 
detained, prosecuted and punished for offences they have been compelled to commit in the course, or 
as a result, of having been trafficked.  
 
In most European countries there are no specific legal provisions on non-punishment of the victims of 
trafficking. However, general provisions, such as ‘the state of necessity’ – which is often invoked as a 
reason for not needing an explicit reference to non-prosecution trafficking victims – are not adequate 
to reflect the complex trafficking scenarios that victims face and are often too narrow to be 
effectively used to shield victims of trafficking from unjust punishment. 
Ensuring that the directive is implemented with specific provisions on non-punishment of victims of 
trafficking could bring to a more systematic application of the non-punishment principle.

the objectives of the directive in terms of victims’ protection and safe reporting.

Comments  
Strong continued action is required, including strong commitment of government actors and other 
relevant stakeholders, in addition to sufficient resources and capacity. Education and awareness-
raising, community mobilisation, professional training and socio-economic support are key actions to 
reduce vulnerability to trafficking, as potential victims will be able to identify risks themselves. 
 
Nevertheless, to ensure that prevention measures are durable, the specific vulnerabilities of at-risk 
groups need to be addressed. Specifically, third country nationals constitute half of trafficking victims 
in the EU: European action to reduce vulnerabilities to human trafficking (and ensure therefore better 
prevention) should include drawing conclusions between how immigration  
regulation and control, as well as the functioning of the Common European Asylum System can in fact 
create conditions that foster trafficking. 
 
Asylum seekers and irregular migrants are particularly vulnerable to being trafficked once they arrive 
in Europe as a result of particular policies that make environments hostile or difficult to live in. The 
inability to move freely and in a regular manner around the EU once someone is granted protection in 
one country, as well as existing restrictions of the right to work can push people to seek a livelihood in 
precarious conditions. An in-depth prevention strategy of trafficking should therefore explore the 
impact of migration and asylum policies on trafficking and identify other legislative changes that 
could better support preventive work. 
 
Firewall provisions enabling irregular migrants to access services, including the possibility to report 
labour exploitation and other violations, can support victims of labour exploitation in emancipating 
from abusive employers.

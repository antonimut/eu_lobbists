Fighting human trafficking – review of EU rules 
Missing Children Europe contribution 
 
 
1. Introduction 
Every year an estimated two hundred and fifty thousand children slip through the net of Europe’s 
child protection systems and disappear. Trafficking is both a driver and consequence of children 
going missing. The most vulnerable children at high-risk of trafficking are children that are victims of 
family violence, abuse, neglect and poverty, children on the move and children left alone.1 Out of 
all victims of trafficking, a significant proportion these continue to be children.2 
 
As recognized in the recently adopted EU Strategy on the Rights of the Child and EU Strategy on 
Combatting Trafficking in Human Beings , it is especially important to take into account the 
protection of child victims of trafficking. In addition, the Commission identified room for improvement 
of measures taken by Member States to prevent, protect and support child victims of trafficking. 3 
Missing Children Europe thus welcomes the Commission’s initiative to evaluate the implementation 
of the EU Anti-trafficking Directive. In this paper, Missing Children Europe presents a brief assessment 
and recommendations for the EU’s possible revision of the Anti-trafficking Directive.

If a child or young person has been exploited, or if there is an intention to exploit the child, early 
identification is critical, wh ich is not an easy task. 7 In addition, child victims may be suffering from 
psychological, physical or emotional trauma and being subjected to fear of those who trafficked 
them, so their accounts can be contradictory and confused.8 Children and young people will only 
open up about the details of their experiences when they have a trusted and secure relationship 
with the professional they talk to.9 Therefore, cases involving child victims of trafficking require trained 
professionals taking into account the particular vulnerabilities of child victims. and who are well 
trained on children’s rights and protection needs during criminal proceedings.10 
 
Child victims of trafficking are also often punished for offences they might have committed while 
being under pressure by traffickers, including by imprisonment.11 This does not correspond with the 
victim-central approach as adopted in the Anti -Trafficking Directive and Strategy. Also required 
under the Directive, children should be provided with legal a ssistance, e.g., in the case of 
unaccompanied minors by a guardian, however , this is still lacking in multiple Member States , as 
reported by the European Parliament.12  
 
2.2. Children in migration 
A group of children specifically at risk of being trafficked are children with a migration background.13 
Children and young people arriving in Europe face different situations: some are being exploited 
to pay back the costs of their travels, some face abuse in detention centres, while others end up 
on the streets, vulnerable for traffickers to recruit them into labour or criminal exploitation. 14 The 
European Commission has already acknowledged the particular protection needs of children in 
migration15 and gave special attention to them in the Strategy.16 However, children in migration

Several factors contribute to increasing the risk of children and young people on the move being 
exploited: travelling alone, a lower level of education, the length of their travel , and facing 
xenophobia and racism.19 In addition, traffickers are known to frequently use reception centres to 
identify their potential victims.20 The RADAR study shows that children in migration are increasingly 
at risk of being groomed for trafficking. By improving the timing of procedures upon arrival as well 
as speeding up integration into society, the risk of children’s exposure to groomers can be 
decreased.21 In addition, research shows that being identified as a potential victim of trafficking is 
a key risk factor for young people going missing from care.22 This risk increases when the child does 
not speak English, has an unclear asylum status and lacks documents.23  
 
Also linked to this is the issue of residence permits for victim of trafficking. For the implementation of 
the Anti -Trafficking Directive, Member States need  to take into account Council Directive 
2004/81/EC of 29 April 2004, which states that third-country nationals who are victims of trafficking 
in human beings or who have been the subject of an action to facilitate illegal immigration should 
be provided with support and the ability to stay in the country, when they are willing to cooperate 
with the competent authorities or share useful information.24 Thus, victims of trafficking are only issued 
a residence permit when they participate in criminal proceedings. As mentioned above, however, 
child victims may be suffering from  trauma and fear those who trafficked them , which can make 
them hesitant to open  up to authorities. 25 This is especially the case for children with a migration 
background, who often experience distrust and fear of authorities. 26 The European Commission 
also highlighted in their implementation report the risk of re -trafficking of non -EU victims, as they 
may be transferred to the country where they were exploited when they first arrived, therefore 
becoming more accessible to the traffickers.27

Another key challenge in the case of children in migration is the lack of legal provisions and multi-
agency cooperation in finding a durable solution for the child. The EU Anti -trafficking Directive 
includes the legal obligation to find a durable solution for child victims of trafficking based on an 
individual assessment of the best interests of the  child, and even if they haven’t applied for 
international protection. Member States have not transposed this duty in their national systems28. 
 
2.3. Runaway children and institutional care 
According to Missing Children Europe’s annual data collection29, runaway children constitute the 
largest category of children going missing and are most often reported to have faced violence,  
abuse, neglect and/or exploitation.  There is also evidence that runaways from the LGBTQ+ 
community are significantly underreported. Despite these gaps, runaway and LGBTQ+ children and 
youth are especially vulnerable for trafficking , according to US data. 30 Also linked to this is 
institutional care. Children in alternative care are overrepresented among runaways, as problems 
in the care home where children lived were indicated as primary concerns for children running 
away.31 Moreover, other research has found that institutional care facilities do not always provide 
specialised and appropriate preventive and protective resp onse to child victims of trafficking. 
Instead, institutionalisation can increase risks of trafficking and re -trafficking for all children placed 
in institutions, including unaccompanied children, runaway children and children left alone.32  
 
3. Recommendations 
In order to decrease the number of children going missing in the EU, the possible revision of the EU 
Anti-Trafficking Directive should aim to decrease the number of child victims of trafficking an d 
improve the level of support they are given. The review should be based on the intentions set out 
in the adopted Strategy and should meet its minimum requirements for supporting and protecting 
victims. The possible revision of the Anti-Trafficking Directive should: 
 
• Take a comprehensive approach to trafficking that looks at all different categories of children 
(at risk) of going missing . Given their particular vulnerability, it would be important to explore 
the specific application of the anti -trafficking directive to children in migration and runa way 
children, to be able to tackle their individual needs.  
• Take into account the views of child ren who are  victims of trafficking  and who have gone 
missing, from various backgrounds, including children from migration backgrounds. This may

BACKGROUND 
 
About Missing Children Europe 
Missing Children Europe  is the European federation for missing and sexually exploited children, 
representing 31 member organisations from 27 European countries. Missing Children Europe 
coordinates the network of 116 000 hotlines for missing children, a dedicated service for children 
(at risk of) going missing and their families; and the cross -border family mediators’ (CBFM) network 
that prevents and resolves parental abductions. We provide the link between research, policies 
and organisations on the ground to protect children from any kind of violence, abuse or neglect 
that is caused by or results from them going missing. 
 
Key figures and trends  
 
• Categories of missing children  
Missing Children Europe identifies the following categories of missing children: 
- Runaways: Children who run away from home, from the people responsible for their care or 
from the institution where they have been placed; 
- Abduction by a third person : Abductions of children by anyone oth er than the parents or 
persons with parental authority;  
- International parental abduction: Cases where a child is taken away to, or kept in, a county of 
place other than that of its normal residence by one or more of his/her parents or persons 
having parental authority against the other parent’s will or against the will of the person with 
parental authority;  
- Missing children in migration : Disappearances of migrant children, national or a country with 
which there is no free movement of persons, under the ag e of 18 who have been separated 
from both parents and are not being cared for an adult, who by law is responsible for doing 
so. 
- Lost, injured or otherwise missing children: Disappearances for no apparent reasons of children 
who have got lost or who have be en injured and cannot be found immediately, as well as 
children whose reason for disappearing has not yet been determined.

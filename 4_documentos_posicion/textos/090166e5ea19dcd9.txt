 
 
 
BVI1 position paper on the AIFMD review based on the proposals provided by the European 
Commission – general remarks  
 
In the EU, a total of €12.8 trillion is invested in investment funds by private and institutional investors. 
With assets of €3.4 trillion in UCITS and AIFs, Germany is the largest market with a share of 27 per-
cent. In terms of funds launched in the EU, the German AIF market accounts for EUR 2,218 billion out 
of EUR 6,878 billion in the EU, which corresponds to a share of 32.2 percent. Most German AIFs are 
so-called special funds, which are exclusively open to professional and semi-professional investors. 
These are primarily regulated companies such as insurance companies, banks or pension schemes 
that invest in low-leverage investment funds. 
 
Both the UCITS and AIFM Directives already provide extensive coverage of the activities of manage-
ment companies and include effective and proportionate rules on their authorisation, capital require-
ments, conduct of business and organisational and transparency requirements, delegation of functions 
and reporting obligations to the supervisory authorities. We support the EU Commission's proposal to 
make only specific changes in order to further strengthen investor protection and to reduce any ineffi-
ciencies and resulting inconsistent supervisory practices. For this reason, we propose further improve-
ments. Overall, the position of the German fund industry can be summarised as follows:  
 
Harmonisation of rules for UCITS and AIF: The coexistence of the UCITS and AIFM Directives and 
other EU legislation (e.g., EuVECA and EuSEF Regulations) have proven their worth. We welcome the 
adherence to these frameworks, but also see the need for alignment of key elements of the UCITS and 
AIFM Directives, such as liquidity management and reporting. 
 
Delegation rules and notifications to ESMA: In order to increase operational efficiency within and 
outside a group, reduce costs and, above all, benefit from know-how in certain markets, sectors or re-
gions, 22 percent of German mutual funds such as UCITS and 60 percent of German AIFs with institu-
tional investors have currently delegated portfolio management to companies within Germany, the EU 
and in third countries (in each case measured in terms of fund assets). That is why delegation of portfo-
lio management is of great importance in practice, with German management companies also delegat-
ing other functions (e.g., fund administration) to third parties. The EU Commission is showing a sense 
of proportion by not wanting to significantly change the already strict rules of the AIFM Directive on the 
delegation of functions. The concrete proposed amendments are sufficient overall to prevent letter-box 
entities. In particular, we support specifying the rules for UCITS on letter-box entities and adapting them 
to the more detailed requirements of the AIFM Directive. An EU-wide harmonisation of these require-
ments for managers of UCITS and AIFs prevents regulatory arbitrage. This also applies to the new pro-
posals that a sufficient number of decision-makers must actually work locally in management compa-
nies within the EU in order to be able to hold them accountable in the case of rule breaches. A manage-
ment company should have the required level of management ‘substance’. This means that the man-
agement company can only delegate functions, not responsibilities.

We support a uniform enforcement of delegation rules across the EU. However, the existing Level 3 in-
struments (e.g., ESMA guidelines) are currently not being used to create standards. ESMA therefore 
has a special role to play in harmonising divergent supervisory practices. The proposals for new notifi-
cation requirements for NCAs to ESMA on whether portfolio and risk management functions are pre-
dominantly outsourced to third countries will contribute to a better overview of existing market practices. 
Nevertheless, we believe that further clarification is needed on the importance of a holistic view of the 
proportion of portfolio and risk management functions that are delegated to third countries or retained in 
the EU. In any case, we are against any kind of purely quantitative delimitation criteria for third- country 
delegation of portfolio and risk management functions. The qualitative assessment of the information 
reported to ESMA should be the responsibility of the national supervisory authorities.  
 
Liquidity Management Tools (LMTs): We welcome the approach that a wide range of tools should be 
available for the liquidity management of open-ended funds. Our proposed amendment aims at allowing 
UCITS or AIF managers to use all mentioned LMTs in addition to suspending redemption. However, the 
current text requires the use of a limited selection of instruments, namely redemption gates, notice peri-
ods or redemption fees. From the perspective of competitiveness, this puts ‘non-EU AIFs’ in a better 
position, to which all LMTs should be available. Otherwise, we can accept the approach that the further 
requirements for LMTs are to be defined at level 2. However, ESMA should take existing practices into 
account. 
 
Loan-originating AIFs: A framework with general obligations for managers of loan-originating AIFs in 
the AIFMD makes sense. Nevertheless, there is a need for further amendments, in particular, regarding 
the treatment of loans granted to portfolio undertakings in which the AIF itself is invested for the pur-
pose of assessing whether the fund may be issued as open-ended or closed-ended. 
 
Depositories: We are neutral on this issue. Overall, we think it is reasonable that depositories can also 
be appointed with a registered office in another member state after approval by the NCAs in order to 
prevent concentration risks in individual markets. 
 
Reporting: We welcome the proposal that ESMA should first conduct a feasibility study to find syner-
gies between already existing regulatory reporting obligations of investment funds and make further 
proposals. The transitional periods give sufficient time to establish uniform new reporting for UCITS and 
AIFs, which we explicitly support. However, we miss further explanations on the calculation of leverage, 
in particular for UCITS. This aspect could be addressed in the context of ESMA's Level 2 reporting, alt-
hough this lacks further clarification of ESMA's powers to develop Level 2 requirements. Even if the 
methods by which the fund manager can increase the fund's exposure are different for AIFs and 
UCITS, the metric for calculating leverage (gross and commitment) should in principle be based on the 
same methodology for UCITS and AIFs. Such an approach would ensure a sustainable and significant 
understanding and monitoring of leverage for financial stability purposes. The current practice for 
UCITS with reference to the old CESR guidelines currently leads to different calculation methods in the 
individual member states and is also very hindering in cross-border fund business within the EU. 
 
Other points (disclosure in the annual report): The extension of the transparency obligations to-
wards AIF investors is adequate. However, the frequency of reporting to investors should be uniform for 
all information to be disclosed by an AIF. The proposed shorter (here: quarterly) disclosure only for cer-
tain information like fees and the associated companies is not comprehensible in practice and in ter ms 
of content, as all other information must be disclosed on a regular basis.

The Depositary Banks Council operating within the Polish Bank Association 
(hereinafter the “DBC” or the “Council”) has welcomed with enthusiasm the possibility 
to express its position to a proposal of changes to legal instruments regulating the 
operation of investment funds1. 
 
The Polish Bank Association  (“PBA”) is a self -government organisation of banks, 
established in 1991, founded on the Chambers of Commerce Charter. Membership in 
the PBA is voluntary and open for all banks created under the Polish law as well as for 
foreign branches of credit institutions ope rating in the Republic of Poland. Currently 
100 banks operating in Poland are members of the PBA. The organisation represents 
the interests of the whole Polish banking community against the Parliament, the 
Government and general public. 
 
Within the Associa tion there are numerous consultative committees, councils, task 
forces and other bodies. Their objective is to develop and issue opinions and positions 
in matters determining the correct operation of the Polish banking system. One of them 
is the Depositary Banks Council within the PBA. The aim of the Council is to represent 
banks which deliver depositary and custody services in Poland. The Council takes an 
active role in discussion with national supervision authorities in the area of depositary 
obligations and represents the interests of its members to the public institutions. 
Membership in the Council is voluntary – as in the Polish Bank Association. Currently

In connection with the commencement of works on the amendment of UCITSD 2 and 
AIFMD3, covered by a document published by the European Commission “ Proposal 
for a Directive of the European Parliament and of the Council amending Dire ctives 
2011/61/EU and 2009/65/EC as regards delegation arrangements, liquidity risk 
management, supervisory reporting, provision of depositary and custody services and 
loan origination by alternative investment funds  – COM(2021) 721 final” (hereinafter: 
the “AIFMD & UCITSD Amendment” or the “Amendment”), on behalf of the DBC, we 
hereby present the conclusions from the analysis of the above proposal. 
 
In the Council’s opinion, the most important in the Amendment, from the perspective 
of the Polish market of depositary services, is a proposed change to Article 61(5) of 
AIFMD, according to which it will be allowed, after having obtained an authorisation of 
competent supervisory authorities , to use the services of a depositary established in 
other Member State than the home state of the alternative investment fund (hereinafter: 
the “AIF”),. This change substantially changes the principles of providing depositary 
services to the AIF, especially in the context of the principle expressed in Article 21(5) 
of AIFMD, according to which the depositary shall be established in the home Member 
State of the AIF. The above change will affect directly the market of depositary services 
in Poland and its competitiveness against the more developed markets in the territory 
of the European Union, which is discussed more broadly below.  
 
The proposal of changes in the cross -border provision of depositary services to the 
AIF realises, in principle, two expectations. First, their aim is to increase the availability 
of depositary services on those markets where such availability is limited. Second, the 
proposal in a way materialises one of the fundamental principles of the EU law, i.e. the 
assurance of freedom to provide services, enabling to some extent cross -border 
provision of depositary services. 
 
In spite of relatively positive effects of the proposed changes to the extent of cross -
border provision of depositary services, we may not disregard the fact that such 
solution increases a risk of regulatory arbitrage appearing between various 
jurisdictions. Entities interested in providing depositary services may seek to choose 
those jurisdictions where the provision of such services will be relatively easier 
because of less restrictive local regulations, and also other crit eria, e.g. the costs of 
conducting business activity.  
 
In view of the above, the DBC note s that, starting as of the implementation of AIFMD 
and UCITS V Directive, the regulatory burdens for entities providing depositary 
services to investment funds in Pol and have changed significantly. Fundamental 
changes have been observed on the Polish market of depositary services, and the 
Council indicated the possibility of their occurrence during legislative works connected

Notwithstanding the positive direction of changes and the idea underlying the 
introduction of AIFMD and UCITS V Directive, for actual evaluation what are the effects 
of introducing those regulations, it is also necessary to make an assessment of their 
implementation to national legal regimes. Many times Member States, using the 
freedom available in implementation of directives, clarify such regulations in a way that 
may be considered as conflicting with their objectives or exceeding the scope of the 
regulation (the so called gold-plating). Such approach may be directly contrary to the 
idea of harmonisation of legislations at the European Union level (harmonisation 
should lead to the achievement of the set objective in all Member States). 
Consequently, there may be differences in regulations between individual Member 
States that encourage to the application of regulatory arbitrage, which in consequence 
disturbs the competition on the market. In the DBC’s opinion, the revision of AIFMD 
and UCITSD provides an opport unity to identify the above situations and gives a 
chance to limit or eliminate the same, and consequently to ensure the actual fulfilment 
of objectives indicated in the above provisions, above all to ensure the protection of 
investors. 
 
Below, the DBC will present the situation on the Polish market of depositary services 
in the context of implementation of AIFMD and UCITS V Directive, as well as in the 
context of legal solutions adopted in domestic legislation. In addition, we will present 
proposed suggestions regarding the proposal of amendment to AIFMD and UCITSD. 
 
1. Situation of depositaries in Poland 
 
As indicated above, the situation of depositaries in Poland has changed significantly 
after implementation of AIFMD and UCITS V Directive, which have materially affected 
the manner and conditions of providing depositary services. In particular, there has 
been a significant change with respect to services provided to alternative investment 
funds as well as the opening of depositary services and the admission of investment 
firms to provide depositary services to alternative investment funds. 
 
1.1. Information about the market of alternative investment funds in Poland 
 
In Poland, an alternative investment fund may operate in a dedicated fo rm envisaged 
by legal provisions regulating the operation of investment funds, i.e. as a specialised 
open-end investment fund (SFIO) or a closed-end investment fund (FIZ), or as an 
alternative investment company (ASI) which may operate in the form of a capital 
company or partnership. 
 
SFIO and FIZ must obligatorily designate a depositary. In the case of ASI, designation 
of a depositary is ma ndatory if the manager of ASI is obligated to obtain an 
authorisation for the conduct of activity, the obligation to designate a depositary applies 
to each ASI managed by such entity (see Article 3 of AIFMD, which specifies 
exemptions from the application of directive requirements). 
 
Starting as of 2016 4, we may observe a clearly falling tendency in the number of FIZ 
as well as the value of assets deposited in FIZ (see the table below). In total, between 
2016 and 2020 the number of FIZ went down by approx.  25% (from 834 to 622 FIZ),  
while the value of assets decreased by approx. 15%. At the same time, i.e. from 2016 
onwards, the Polish market saw a noticeable increase in the number of ASIs as well 
as the assets under management. Between 2016 and 2020, sever al hundred ASIs 
were formed, while the increase in assets managed within ASIs was approximately 
2282%.   
 
Data shows univocally that the interest in this form of an alternative investment fund 
has went up significantly in recent years in Poland, which results directly from the 
implementation of AIFMD. Assets of the investors were transferred from entities which 
must have a depositary (SFIO and FIZ) to entities (ASI) not required to have 
a depositary, which affects the protection of investors’ interest. At t he same time, we 
should emphasise that at the moment in Poland there is only one ASI manager that 
operates pursuant to an authorisation and was thus obligated to designate 
a depositary.  
 
The above leads to unambiguous conclusions that a significant portion of assets from 
FIZs (i.e. entities that in Poland are obliged to appoint a depositary) goes to ASIs, 
which in general do not have to appoint a depositary. Therefore, the increasing size of 
assets is subject to lower standards of protection, which leads t o lower protection of 
investors. 
 
1.2. Market of depositary services in Poland 
 
In Poland, depositary services may be provided only by the following entities: 
a) a domestic bank whose equity amounts to at least PLN 100,000,000; 
b) a branch of a credit institution established in the territory of Poland, if the funds 
allocated and placed at disposal of the branch amount to at least PLN 
100,000,000; 
c) Krajowy Depozyt Papierów Wartościowych Spółka Akcyjna (Central Securities 
Depositary of Poland); 
d) investment firms authorised to perform activities within the scope of holding in 
custody or registration of financial instruments, including keeping of securit ies 
accounts, derivatives accounts, and omnibus accounts, and keeping of money 
accounts, if the founding capital of the company amounts to at least the PLN 
equivalent of EUR 750,000 – entities of this type provide depositary services 
only to ASI and FIZ. 
 
At the moment, depositary services in Poland are provided by 12 entities. 10 entities 
are banks or branches of credit institutions, and 2 entities are investment firms.5

1.3. Condition of the market of depositary services in Poland 
 
Based on ongoing observation of the market by the DBC it is evident that the interest 
in FIZ as one of forms of alternative investment funds is falling, inter alia, because of 
regulatory burdens that are significantly higher for FIZ than for ASI. At the same time, 
because of legal regulations prevailing in Poland, banks are less and less interested 
in providing depositary services (which is discussed more broadly hereinafter). 
Consequently, it results in a situation that depositary services are provided to 
alternative investment funds predominantly by investment firms. At the moment there 
are two investment firms operating on the market which provide such services. 
 
Whereas the extension of the catalogue of entities providing depositary services may 
be perceived in a positive way (each change aimed at increasing the competition leads, 
in principle, to better quality of services), the practical effects of such change h ave 
brought about the situation where at the moment we may observe the concentration of 
depositary services for alternative investment funds. The concentration has been 
successively growing over consecutive months that have elapsed from the 
implementation of AIFMD in Poland. 
 
2. “Gold-plating” in Polish legislation with respect to the performance of 
depositary function 
 
First, the DBC wants to emphasise that the phenomenon of “gold -plating”, i.e. over -
excessive regulations and restrictions in the domestic law in relation to the EU law, 
applies also to depositary services. In the DBC’s opinion, it has an adverse impact on 
the market of depositary services, and also indirectly on the market of alternative 
investment funds and their attractiveness to investors and  other capital market 
stakeholders.  
 
Gold-plating in Poland with respect to the performance of depositary function is 
reflected in the excessive regulations and also in the issuance of interpretations by the 
supervisory authority, where those actions tran slate into the increased scope of 
obligations of domestic depositaries and the increased scope of liability of depositaries, 
compared to the standard of performance of the function according to the provisions 
of the EU law. 
 
The implementation of UCITS V D irective and AIFMD to the Polish legal regime has 
resulted in significantly increased obligations and liability of depositaries, whereas the 
application of gold -plating has brough about a number of additional adverse effects, 
among which we should list the following: 
i. additional operating, legal and reputational risks appearing in the operation of 
depositaries, 
ii. weakened competitiveness of domestic depositaries against depositaries from 
other EU Member States, inter alia, by increased costs of their operation, 
iii. increased costs for investment funds and their participants following from a will 
to compensate the above risks by depositaries, increased remuneration collected 
from the investment funds, and so in fact from the investors, i.e. participants of 
investment funds, 
iv. shrinking offer to perform a depositary function for alternative investment funds, 
which, as may b e assumed, results from a necessity to perform additional 
obligations and bear additional liability, 
v. transfer of the performance of a depositary function from banks to investment 
firms, meaning entities with significantly lower equity (the equivalent of EU R 
730,000), which may have an adverse impact on the participants of those 
investment funds in the event of financial claims addressed to investment firms 
in connection with non -performance or improper performance of a depositary 
function. 
 
The examples of gold-plating presented below univocally show, in the DBC’s opinion, 
that implementation of the provisions of the EU law is done in Poland with violation of 
the fundamental principle of proportionality. The adoption of national legal regulations 
which exceed the regulatory framework specified by instruments of the EU law (and 
sometimes conflict with such framework) renders impossible the achievement of 
objectives envisaged by the EU law, and may even lead to the conclusion that it 
violates fundamental freedoms prevailing in the European Union. 
 
2.1. Examples of gold-plating in Poland 
 
a) Extension of control and supervisory obligations 
 
In the course of implementing AIFMD and UCITS V Directive to the Polish legal regime, 
the legislator also changed in unprecedented way the scope of depositary obligations. 
According to the Polish Act on Investment Funds 6, the catalogue of depositary 
obligations was extended to include also verification whether operation of the 
investment fund is compliant with legislati on regulating the operation of investment 
funds or with the articles of association and takes due regard of the participants’ 
interest.7 The above obligation has no authorisation in the provisions of law of the 
European Union, but the initiative to that e xtent was reported by the supervisory 
authority in Poland, i.e. the Polish Financial Supervision Authority. The introduction of 
the subject regulation has caused that we should expect depositaries to take actions 
of control nature over the operation of inv estment funds. In practice, it means actual 
necessity to duplicate the role of the manager, which is contrary to basic regulations of 
the EU law regarding the separation of both roles8.

Concluding, in consequence of the provisions adopted in the Polish Act on Investment 
Funds, depositaries in Poland have become obligated to exercise a comprehensive 
oversight of investment funds’ compliance with broadly understood laws and 
regulations, articles of association of investment funds and due regard to participants’ 
interest, also to the extent that may violate the principle of separation of activities within 
the scope of holding assets in custody and management functions. Under the 
obligation imposed by the Polish Act on Investment Funds, a depositary may be  
required to verify also the compliance of the investment fund’s investment activities , 
including to assess whether specific investment decisions made by the manager of the 
investment fund comply with laws and regulations, articles of association and take due 
regard of participants’ interest. 
 
What is more, this oversight has the features of ex ante oversight, which generally 
conflicts with the basic principle of operation by a depositary, i.e. making ex-post 
control. We should emphasise here that the current supervisory practice, based to 
a large degree on laws and regulations adopted in Poland, indicates that the national 
supervisory authority expects the maximalisation of depositaries’ activity in a way 
exceeding the role of a depositary envisaged by the EU law. Taking as an example the 
valuation of investment fund assets, the Council observes (based on publicly available 
information about sanctions imposed on depositaries and their justification) that in 
practice the supervisory authority expects the depositary to make a valuation of the 
investment fund assets, regardless whether those assets have been valued by the  
investment fund manager or an external valuation entity contracted by the investment 
fund. Such model of operation does not only significantly affect the amount of incurred 
costs, primarily by participants of investment funds, but also duplicates the activities 
for which in principle the manager should be responsible. It is not possible to reconcile 
(both from legal and functional perspective) the role held by a depositary with the 
conduct of activity to which managers are obligated. 
 
This leads to a significant increase in expenditures which must be incurred by 
a depositary in order to satisfy the requirements of domestic law and the expectations 
of the Polish supervisory authority, which in consequence results in higher costs of 
services, additionally the increased scope of obligations effectively discourages from 
providing depositary services. In the longer time horizon it will mean that availability of 
depositary services will be a ctually further restricted, especially for alternative 
investment funds, which will also result in growing concentration of services and 
actually higher costs of services where such costs will be ultimately borne by 
investment fund participants. Progressing regress on the market of depositary services 
may lead to a  material increase of the system risk arising from high concentration of 
services in several entities. 
 
b) Combining activity conducted by manager and depositary function in one 
entity 
 
Having regard to the fundamental principle of the protection of investors’ interest and 
the priority of that interest over depositary ’s interest, the EU legislator provided for a 
general prohibition to combine the function of manager and depositary.15 In view of the 
provisions of AIFMD and UCITSD, we should say univocally that those Directives do 
not authorise Member States to introduce any derogations from the above principle in 
national legislation. In addition, this principle is even more strongly emphas ised in 
Recital 24 to UCITS V Directive, which says that “ In order to ensure consistently high 
levels of investor protection, provisions on conduct and on the management of conflicts 
of interest should be adopted and should apply in all situations (…). Those rules should 
in particular ensure a clear separation of tasks and functions between the depositary, 
the UCITS and the management company or the investment company”. 
 
In AIFMD, the principle of separation of depositary and management company function 
is introduced in Article 21(4)(a). Taking into account a negative impact connected with 
a conflict of interest occurring, the EU legislator has decided to adopt in that provision 
that in order to avoid a conflict of interest in relations between the depositar y, the 
management company or the alternative investment fund or investors of the alternative 
investment fund – “an AIFM shall not act as depositary” . This provision seems to 
indicate clearly that combining the function of manager and depositary creates 
a conflict of interest and is inadmissible. In the DBC’s opinion, the prohibition of 
concurrent conduct of activity  means that a given entity may not perform activities 
falling within the scope of basic operations of the manager and at the same time 
execute depositary activities for those operations. Such combination of roles means 
that the entity exercises oversight over itself, which additionally generates a risk of 
conflict of interest and materially weakens the level of protection of investors’ interest. 
Looking at examples from other Member States, we should note that in neither of them 
does the depositary replace the manager of the investment fund if the authorisation for 
the conduct of activity  by the manager of the investment fund has expired or been 
revoked. Such role is usually held by another entity that has a relevant authorisation 
for investment fund management, and in extreme cases (e.g. in Malta) also the 
competent supervisory authority of that state is involved in the management of the 
investment fund.

In spite of such clearly specified provisions of the EU law, the solutions implemented

manager and depositary of an investment fund in one legal entity. As a result, 
additional obligations have been imposed on a depositary, significantly exceeding the 
supervisory and control role assigned thereto. If the management has been taken over 
by a depositary, the entity is obligated (often overnight) to separate  within its

implement appropriate IT infrastructure required for the provision of accounting and

So the activity of  depositaries in Poland is not limited to basic role specified by the 
provisions of the EU law, but includes the activities falling under the powers of 
manager. We should emphasise here tha t the EU legislation univocally regulates the 
issues of asset manag ement (e.g. UCITSD, AIFMD), and in accordance with those 
regulations the conduct of activity involving asset management requires, in principle, 
an appropriate licence which is granted after specific requirements (in terms or 
organisation, personnel etc.) have been satisfied. In the situation described above, no 
licence is required for the depositary to manage assets of the investment fund, and 
the depositary has to take over the entire liability for investment fund management and 
in fact satisfy all legal requirements that are normally satisfied by the manager. For 
example, the depositary must take into account issues involving risk management with 
respect to the investment fund, investment decision-making, but at the same time there 
is no provision releas ing the depositary from performance of basic control and 
supervisory functions, which leads to univocal conclusion that both roles are conflicting 
with each other. The only way to avoid negative effects of such  status quo should be 
such organisational separation made by the depositary so as to separate the functions 
which are to replicate the manager activities from the control and supervisory functions. 
 
However, undoubtedly it would be contrary to the EU legislation (prohibition to combine 
the roles) and also it would generate a significant increase of costs and risk for 
depositaries. As a supplementation , we should also say that the Polish Act on 
Investment Funds does not envisage a possibility for the depositary, who becomes by 
law an investment fund liquidator or representative if the investment fund manager has 
lost its authorisation to operate, to outsource the performance of activities within such 
scope to another entity (including to another manager). 
 
As a supplementation , we should also note that it is a rule in the Polish law that the 
investment fund liquidator is its depositary. Activities taken by the depositary in 
connection with the investment fund liquidation include, in particular, to make the 
investment fund’s assets liquid (sale of investments is in fact an investment decision), 
to repay the investment fund’s creditors, to prepare appropriate financial statements 
and reports for the supervisory authority, to publish announcements and to pay monies

to investment fund participants. The above activities univocally indicate that the role of 
depositary in the course of liquidation also overlaps with activities performed by 
managers. 
 
The above example of gold -plating results in a situation that each entity interested in 
providing depositary services considers a scenario wherein it will be obligated to 
provide services falling within the scope of investment fund management. Entities 
providing depositary services, before the commencement of such service s, identify, 
assess and quantify the above circumstances and any risks related thereto. One of the 
key elements of the assessment is a risk of depositary taking over the manager’s role, 
which from the point of view of the entity defined to hold control fun ctions makes 
a significant change in the conducted activity. 
  
An obvious consequence of such status quo is limited interest in providing depositary 
services to investment funds that, according to depositaries, are more exposed to such 
situation, which in practice most often means that services are limited to entities with 
a short history of operation, independent (i.e. not being members of large capital 
groups). 
 
c) Special regime of liability for a depositary in the same capital group with 
the manager 
 
The Polish Act on Investment Funds implements a special regime of liability for 
depositaries who are, together with the manager, members of the same capital 
group17. In such case the legislator has introduced the assumption of depositary’s fault 
if participants of an investment fund have suffered damage caused by non -
performance or improper performance of the manager’s obligations within the scope 
of the management and representation of the investment fund, based only on premises 
arising from the structure of the capital group whose member is the depositary. In the 
DBC’s opinion, such univocal assumption is in conflict with fundamental principles 
regulating the operation of managers and dep ositary, and especially the principle of 
role separation and the obligation to act independently and in the interest of investment 
fund participants both by manager and depositary 18. The above regulation is not 
supported in any way by the provisions of the EU law. 
 
In the DBC’s opinion, a special regime of liability hinders development of depositary 
services, because member entities of large financial groups must use external service 
providers in order to mitigate risks. Such situation constitutes per se a hindrance for 
capital groups which pay a special attention (risk estimation) when using services of 
external providers. 
 
d) Special interpretation by the supervisory authority of assets held in 
custody by depositary – extension of the catalogue of assets h eld in 
custody 
 
For the manner of performance of depositary services in Poland significant are also 
positions and interpretations issued by the Polish Financial Supervision Authority, 
which, although they are not the source of law in Poland, have a materia l impact on

18 Which is expressed e.g. in Article 25(2) of UCITSD.  
the situation of depositaries. As an example of the supervisory authority’s interpretation 
affecting the manner of performance of depositary services we may indicate a position 
regarding the obligation to hold in custody cash on bank accounts k ept by depositary 
(while according to the EU legislation, the obligation to hold in custody covers only 
financial instruments). According to the interpretation made by the Polish Financial 
Supervision Authority, assets of an investment fund in the form of cash deposited on 
bank accounts kept by a depositary should be classified under the category of assets 
held in custody for the loss of which depositary is liable. 
 
The above clearly conflicts with the classification of assets under the provisions of the 
EU law and not only extends depositary ’s obligations but also extends the liability of 
depositaries in Poland. First, we should note that the issue of asset classification in 
terms of depositary’s obligations and the scope of depositary’s liability is regulated by, 
respectively, Articles 88-90 in Regulation 231 and Articles 12-14 in Regulation 438. In 
accordance with the provisions referred to above, assets that should be ‘held in 
custody’ by a depositary include: 
i. financial instruments which are able to be physically delivered to the depositary, 
ii. financial instruments which are not able to be physically delivered to the 
depositary, and with respect to which additional requirements specified in the 
provisions of the regulations are met (e.g. they may be registered on a relevant 
securities account). 
 
The analysis of provisions of the EU law leads therefore to a univocal conclusion that 
assets that may (should) be covered by obligations of holding in custody (whether by 
registration on a securities account or by physical keeping) cover only financial 
instruments – it is obvious and undisputed that cash does not constitute financial 
instruments. Classification of cash as assets “held in custody” is directly contrary with 
the provisions of Article 88 in Regulation 231 and Article 12 in Regulation 438. 
 
As a supplementation , we should only note that cash kept by banks and credit 
institutions is subject to protection under the provisions regulating that activity, and 
therefore there is no sense to introduce additional special regulations in this regard. 
 
e) Additional requirements for depositaries outsourcing the keeping of 
investment fund assets 
 
In the course of implementing AIFMD and UCITSD to the Polish legal regime, the 
Polish legislator also extended the catalogue of  legal requirements for depositaries 
outsourcing the asset keeping to third parties. According to excessive regulation in the 
Polish Act on Investment Funds, a depositary may outsource the performance of asset 
keeping function if the law of a country other than the Republic of Poland where the 
entrepreneur or foreign entrepreneur will keep the assets of an inve stment fund, 
regardless of the manner of keeping the assets, ensures that: 
i. only the investment fund will be entitled to those assets, and 
ii. assets of the investment fund: 
− may not be subject to enforcement addressed against any entity other than 
the investment fund, 
− may not be covered by injunction proceeding addressed against any entity 
other than the investment fund, 
− are not making part of the bankruptcy estate of the entity keeping the assets 
of the investment fund, 
− may not be covered by any restructuring p roceedings or other proceedings 
of a similar nature conducted against the entity keeping the assets of the 
investment fund. 
 
The above additional requirements for depositaries materially affect the manner of 
service provision. First of all, a depositary is obligated to implement, in relation to 
assets kept in other countries, an additional verification that the above premises ar e 
satisfied. The market practice and available legal opinions on individual jurisdictions 
cover by their scope, in principle, the assessment of regulatory requirements under 
AIFMD and UCITSD, and not special requirements under domestic laws. It means in 
practice that a depositary has to carry out a costly examination of legal regulations 
applicable in the country of the entity where assets will be kept, in the context of Polish 
regulations. 
 
Notwithstanding the significant costs of examination of legal regu lations in other 
jurisdictions, we may not exclude a situation that an entity from another country will not 
satisfy the requirements specified in Polish regulations, and in consequence it will not 
be possible to keep the investment fund’s assets in such en tity. The above may 
become significant especially if the investment fund acquires a specific asset in a legal 
act other than purchase, e.g. by taking over an object of security. 
 
3. Conclusions from observation of the depositary market in Poland 
 
The regulatory burdens indicated above, existing only in the regulations of the Polish 
law, have brought about a situation in which the interest in providing depositary 
services by domestic banks has been significantly reduced. We may easily observe 
gradual limitation of that service, especially with respect to closed -end investment 
funds which are AIFs. 
 
As a supplementation , we cannot disregard one more effect, unintended in the 
Council’s opinion, of amendments made in recent years to Polish legal regulat ions. 
The implementation of AIFMD has made it possible to provide depositary services to 
closed-end investment funds also for investment firms. Whereas the extension of the 
catalogue of entities may be perceived in a positive way (each change aimed at 
increasing the competition leads, in principle, to better quality of services), the practical 
effects of such change have brought about the situation where at the moment we may 
observe the concentration of depositary services for alternative investment funds i n 
two investment firms. The concentration has been successively growing over 
consecutive months that have elapsed from implementation of AIFMD in Poland. 
 
The concentration has resulted in a higher systemic risk (smaller and smaller number 
of entities keep s a significant number of funds and assets), and therefore any 
extraordinary events affecting those entities will have an impact on a significant portion 
of the market, and thus on the investors/participants of investment funds. Another 
effect of the concentration is also disturbed competition on the market, which may lead 
in longer perspective to a real increase in costs of depositary services. 
 
In the Council’s opinion, the most important at this stage of works is an in -depth 
analysis what are the effects  of the proposed possibility of cross -border provision of 
depositary services for AIF. In particular, in the Council’s opinion, it is key to apply 
solutions that will prevent a risk of further limitation of the Polish market of depositary 
services, including by the assurance of equal principles of conducted operations, which 
according to the DBC is possible only by implementing appropriate legal regulations at 
the EU law level to ensure equal principles for depositary services throughout the entire 
European Union. We should bring about a situation allowing to avoid regulatory 
arbitration, and this is possible only through harmonisation of legal regimes for 
depositary services throughout the entire European Union. So at the moment, in the 
DBC’s opinion, the most appropriate will be to make changes to the Amendment that 
would allow the assurance of equal conditions of providing depositary services 
between various jurisdictions. 
 
Failure to accommodate the above suggestions may lead to progressing concentration 
on the market, and thus the objectives involving development of the market of 
depositary services will not be achieved. In the DBC’s opinion, such status quo may in 
principle lead to negative consequences for ultimate market participants, i.e. 
participants of investment funds. And going further, the Amendment may also have 
such consequences that the Polish market of depositary services for AIF provided by 
banks (credit institutions) will be vanishing, which generally would negate the 
justification for amend ing Article 61(5) of AIFMD, as expressed in Recitals of the 
Amendment19. 
 
Here we should also add that, in the DBC’s opinion, the effects of implementing the 
Amendment will also depend, to a large degree, on the shape of domestic 
implementing regulations a nd supervisory practice adopted in this regard. For 
example, according to the DBC it is possible that regulatory practice of the supervisory 
authority in Poland  (i.e. the Polish Financial Supervision Authority), granting 
authorisations for cross -border pro vision of depositary services, will consist in an 
attempt to unify the standards of depositary services on the domestic market. In 
particular, it may be expected from a foreign entity to satisfy the same conditions as 
domestic entities and to provide depos itary services on the same principles as 
domestic entities. We cannot deny that such expectation is reasonable. We should not 
allow a situation where in the same market area (depositary services) operate entities 
applying so different principles of conduct ing operations. In consequence of such 
expectations, according to the DBC, it is obvious that for entities that could theoretically 
provide cross-border depositary services such condition may be impossible to satisfy. 
As a result, adoption of the Amendment  to AIMFD and its implementation in Poland 
will not lead to achievement of the objective for which the Amendment should be 
adopted (enhanced availability of depositary services for alternative investment funds). 
 
To sum up the above, in the DBC’s opinion, it is key to ensure equal conditions of 
providing depositary services, and such status quo may be achieved only after full

4. Proposal of changes to the Amendment 
 
Having regard to the conclusions presented herein with respect to the Amendment and 
its effects for development of the market of investment funds in Poland, the DBC 
presents below proposals of provisions to be added to UCITSD and AIMFD, which will 
enable t o restrict the actions of Member States in the course of implementing the 
provisions of the EU law and, where reasonable, will bring about  changes in the 
prevailing provisions of Member States so as to assure the harmonisation and 
achievement of objectives specified in the Directives, with respect to the operation of 
depositaries. 
 
If the presented proposal is taken into account by the European Commission, it will be 
possible to create equal conditions for the conduct of activity in all Member States, and 
therefore to realise the postulates specified in the Amendment with respect to 
increased attractiveness and availability of depositary services in some markets. 
 
Proposal of provisions to be added to AIFMD and UCITSD: 
a) AIFMD 
− adding Article 21(2a) reading as follows: 
“In no way shall the depositary be authorised to conduct the activity that 
consists in the management or representation of one or more AIFs.”, 
− adding Article 21(18) reading as follows: 
“Member States are not authorised to establish national regulatory frameworks 
regarding the obligations and liability of the depositary in the scop e broader 
than arising from the provisions of this Directive or implementing provisions 
issued pursuant thereto.”; 
b) UCITS 
− adding Article 22(3a) reading as follows: 
“In no way shall the depositary be authorised to conduct the activity that 
consists in the management or representation of UCITS.”, 
− adding Article 25a reading as follows: 
“Member States are not authorised to establish national regulatory frameworks 
regarding the obligations and liability of the depositary in the scope broader 
than arising from the  provisions of this Directive or implementing provisions 
issued pursuant thereto.”

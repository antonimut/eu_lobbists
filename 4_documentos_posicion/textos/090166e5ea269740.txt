SYNERGIs consultation on the revision of the energy performance in buildings 
directive (EPBD) 
Summary: SYNERGI welcomes the EU Commission's proposal for the revision of the EPBD  and believes that the 
proposal is a good starting point. There are generally good elements in the proposal, including: the increased focus on breaking 
down economic barriers, relieving energy poverty, new provisions to ensure a healthy indoor climate , and strengthening of 
building tools such as B uilding Passports and Energy Performance Certificates (EPCs) , etc. While these elements are an 
improvement, SYNERGI still believes that the directive can be  improved in areas such as the ambition level regarding 
Minimum Energy Performance Standards ( MEPS), the definition of zero-emission buildings and deep renovations, the 
national plans for building renovations, further strengthening of indoor climate measures as well as the focus on smart, digital 
buildings and optimizing passive systems . SYNERGI emphasizes the importance o f the primary focus of the EPBD 
continuing to regulate energy consumption in buildings , ensure a healthy indoor climate and that energy-efficient buildings 
are private - and socio-economically profitable. 
The purpose of the EPBD is to set the framework for achieving a zero-emission building stock by 2050. 
Furthermore, the directive must also ensure that the building stock consists of healthy and safe buildings 
with an affordable energy bill . Considering that European buildings acc ount for 40 pct. of the energy 
consumption and 36 pct. of the energy-related CO2 emissions in the EU, the EPBD is a crucial tool for 
achieving the EU's energy and climate target for 2030 and towards the climate target of 2050. The EU 
Commission points out that 75 pct. of the current buildings in Europe are energy inefficient and between 
85-95% of the buildings will still be in use by 2050. Therefore, it is crucial to have an ambitious approach 
to the revision of the EPBD.  
SYNERGI find the following areas important to address in the revision of the EPBD: 
1) The EPBD must continue to be the driving force for increasing energy efficiency in buildings  
according to the ‘energy efficiency first’- principle and deliver on the multiple benefits of energy 
renovations  
2) Minimum Energy Performance Standards (MEPS) should be more ambitious 
3) The definition of zero -emission buildings and deep renovations must be defined in terms of 
thresholds for both primary and final energy demand and divided in relation to new buildings and 
renovation 
4) National plans for building renovations must show the way towards the 2050-target 
5) The directive should contain an increased focus on smart digital buildings and optimizing passive 
systems

renovations 
 
SYNERGI finds it important to emphasize that the EPBD must focus on reducing energy consumption 
in the building stock. In this relation, the directive must ensure that reducing the energy consumption 
also leads to a healthy indoor climate - both regarding energy renovations, for optimizing installations in

buildings, and for new buildings. Energy efficiency will also help alleviate energy poverty  which is an 
important issue that is  obvious to address in the EPBD – especially on a  long-term basis. A focus on 
more energy-efficient buildings in the EPBD supports the EU Commission's basic principle of 'energy 
efficiency first', which means that one must first reduce the heat demand through e.g., deep renovations, 
better energy management, and using data, and then ensure an energy-efficient and green heat source.   
 
Unfortunately, the EU Commission's proposal does not follow the 'energy efficiency first' principle, as 
the focus has shifted  to decarbonizing the heat supply rather than first reducing the buildings' energy 
needs. This is  illustrated in Annex I regarding the calculation of buildings' energy performance, where 
the requirement to ensure optimal performance of the building envelope using primary energy factors 
has been removed. Similarly in Annex III where the benchmark for zero -emission buildings is set for 
primary energy consumption, even though energy consumption must be supplied with 100 pct. renewable 
energy. Buildings have a lo ng life and typically a building will  change heat supply and function several 
times during its life. Therefore, it is important to improve the building envelope for both new buildings 
and existing buildings. This is most effectively achieved by defining benchmark values independently of 
the energy supply (i.e., as final energy demand). Reduced energy demand in buildings helps to reduce the 
need for investment in renewable energy and hence speed up a cost-effective green transition. The focus 
of the directive should therefore be to reduce final energy demand, as this will  ensure that energy 
consumption is redu ced regardless of the energy production. It is crucial that energy is used wisely – 
regardless of the source - if the EU is to achieve the overall climate goal of climate neutrality by 2050. 
 
SYNERGI also welcomes the new provisions in the proposal to better factor in the multiple benefits of 
energy efficiency (such as health, comfort, and indoor air quality) associated with the optimization of the 
energy performance of the building stock. These include the revision of the methodology for calculating 
cost-optimal levels of minimum energy performance requirements to factor in environmental and health 
externalities, gathering and displaying data on wider benefits as part of renovation passports and EPCs, 
as well as new requirements to monitor and regulate indoor air quality for new buildings. However, more 
efforts are needed to ensure that energy efficiency, safety, and a healthy indoor climate go hand in hand. 
To achieve this, a definition of a ‘healthy indoor climate’ needs to be introduced in Article 2 to ensure a 
common understanding of essential indoor cli mate parameters. Indoor climate parameters are an 
important incentive for building owners to renovate their buildings –a common definition and better 
factoring within the design and implementation of EPCs and Renovation Passports are therefore critical 
to ensure that future zero-emission buildings are also healthy. SYNERGI suggest the following definition 
of ‘Healthy Indoor Climate’ in Article 2: ‘Healthy Indoor Climate’ means an indoor environment that enables the 
health, comfort, and well-being of occupants and is described by a set of indicators and associated target values related to 
Daylight, Indoor Air Quality, Thermal Comfort and Acoustic Quality as described in EN 16798-1.  
 
2) Minimum Energy Performance Standards (MEPS) should be more ambitious 
SYNERGI is positive towards the EU Commission's proposal on ‘Minimum Energy Performance 
Standards’ (MEPS) as an instrument to ensure renovations in the existing building stock but believes that 
the level of ambition is set too low. The E U Commission's proposal for MEPS is only aimed at r aising 
buildings with energy labels F and G to energy label E  and does not provide a plan to improve energy 
labels from E to A. After this relatively low level of renovation, it is left solely up to each member state

to define the further renovation requirements for 2040 and 2050. Instead , SYNERGI believes that the 
EU Commission should ensure that plans are made on how to lift the energy labeling on buildings with 
energy labels F and G, as well as all other buildings over time, to be renovated into zero -emission 
buildings in one or two steps. At the same time, it must  be ensured that short-term renovations do not 
make future renovation initiatives unprofitable because only low-hanging fruits are harvested. This must 
be done by incorporating the energy-efficient solutions that are fast to install into long-term renovation 
initiatives - for example by 'staged renovation' plans which show clear and economically viable paths to 
gradual improvements of energy labels. 
 
Today, there are several examples of countries that already have minimum requirements for their 
buildings. In France, for example, from 2025, all buildings that use more than 330 kWh per. sqm. with 
energy labels, F and G must be renovated so they meet the same standards as new buildings, and in 2050 
all buildings must have energy labels A or B. In the Netherlands, they have introduced minimum 
standards for office buildings, as all offices over 100 sqm. must achieve energy label C by 20231. Finally, 
in Scotland, although this is not an EU member state, all owner-occupied homes are required to have  
minimum energy label C in 2033 - the same year in which the EU Commission proposes energy label E 
as the lowest level2.  
 
Thus, there are several examples of objectives being set for MEPS nationally. Therefore, SYNERGI 
believes that the EU Commission should not only set a framework for MEPS for buildings with energy 
label G and F, but also for energy label E to A by setting an ambitious bar for national renovation targets 
aimed at 2030, 2040, and 2050.  
 
3) The definition of zero-emission buildings and deep renovations must be defined in terms of 
thresholds for both primary and final energy demand  and divided into new buildings and 
renovation 
 
In the EPBD, the E U Commission proposes a new definition regarding both zero-emission buildings 
and deep renovations. The definition is closely linked, as a ‘deep renovation’ in the EU Commission 
proposal is defined as a building that through renovation becomes a ‘zero-emission building’ after January 
1st, 2030.  
 
By applying the same benchmark (in Annex III) for zero-emission buildings for new buildings and deep 
renovations there is a risk that th e level will be skewed. SYNERGI believes that the definition of zero -
emission buildings for particularly new buildings should be strengthened. In addition, the benchmark 
levels should be defined as the final energy  demand to ensure a  high-performing building envelope , 
independent of the type of energy supply, as highlighted in  section 1. Furthermore, when setting 
benchmark levels for deep renovations, the multiple benefits achieved with increased energy efficiency 
such as better indoor  climate and reduced air pollution  should also be considered. The scope of deep 
renovations should therefore be widened to better factor in the multiple benefits.

Furthermore, SYNERGI supports the EU Commission's reporting requirements for Life -Cycle Global 
Warming Potential although it is a key priority to SYNERGI that the calculation method including 
product data is harmonized. A harmonized method will ensure an e qual effort across Europe and 
transparency and clarity for the industry. While the requirements proposed by the EU Commission are 
reasonable, SYNERGI believes that the reporting requirement should be implemented faster as many 
MS already started regulating  embodied carbon at a national level. The method used in Denmark is in 
this case highlighted as a best practice example.  
 
4) National plans for building renovations must show the way towards the 2050-target  
SYNERGI finds it positive that there are increased requirements for reporting from MS in the national 
plans for building renovations (previous long -term renovation strategies). The increased requirements 
include that MS must report a plan with national goa ls, which must ensure that the countries reach the 
goal of a zero-emission building stock by 2050 - including objectives that illustrate the countries' progress 
in order to measure it. The plans must also contain an overview of planned policies so that it is clear how 
the plans will be implemented. Furthermore, the plans must be submitted every five years, which is more 
frequent than before . These changes are  in SYNERGI's opinion positive. The proposal for the new 
renovation plans will thus provide a better overview of the  national efforts. SYNERGI , therefore,  
emphasizes the importance of ensuring that the plans are implemented nationally and that they are 
prepared based on the ‘energy efficiency first’-principle. 
 
In addition, SYNERGIs proposes that  national renovation plans should be combined with  district 
heating planning. For example, district heating in Denmark alone can secure 44 pct. of the CO2 
reductions which are needed towards the 2030 climate target 3. Therefore, MS should examine in which 
areas it is possible to establish or retrofit low -temperature district heating systems and include plans for 
this in the national renovation plans. This can be done, for example, by defining low-temperature zones, 
which must be combined with the degree of renovation in the area, as a transition from 3 rd -generation 
district heating to 4 th-generation district heating (low -temperature) requires buildings with a high-
performing building envelope.  
 
5) The directive should contain an increased focus on smart digital buildings  and optimizing 
passive solutions 
As the EU Commissions’ proposal reflects, digital solutions must play an increasingly central role in our 
building stock. Digital energy efficiency technologies can contribute to 20-40 pct. reduction in greenhouse 
gas emissions from buildings, often with a payback period of fewer than eight years4. At the same time, 
30 pct. savings in buildings' energy consumption can be achieved via digital building technologies, such 
as building management systems, and 14 p ct. of the EU's 2030 energy effici ency target can be achieved 
by optimizing digital building systems5.

Therefore, there should be a focus on technical building systems, Smart Readiness Indicators (SRI), and 
access to building data. For example, the E U Commission proposes that MS set up a national database 
for energy labels and renovation passports. A common database for Europe's building stock is a necessity 
to identify the buildings that perform the worst. Unfortunately, there are many buildings that do not have 
an energy label, so the first step is to ensure that most buildings are energy labeled . At the same time, 
digital solutions should be seen in the context of the important role they can play to improve the indoor 
climate in buildings. For example, digital solutions can monitor daylight conditions, temperature, 
humidity, and CO2 levels and provide ventilation to keep the indoor climate healthy and comfortable. 
Digital solutions can at the same time help to balance the distribution of heat in a building so that there 
is an even temperature  in the entire building . By balancing the temperature, the heat demand in the 
household is reduced , and at the same time , the comfort increases. But to collect these potentials and 
benefits we must ensure that outdated and inefficient technical building systems are replaced. In addition, 
the scope of Technical Building Systems should be extended to include electrical installation as these 
installations can typically lead to increased energy savings. Thus, digital solutions can help improve the 
operation of buildings - both in terms of energy consumption and indoor climate.  
 
Equally critical are assessments and evaluations of interdependent effects of existing buildings’ active and 
passive building systems . Passive systems are prioritized in Recital 52 but a stronger focus on their 
benefits is required when looking at optimizing building systems. Recommendations on how to improve 
the energy performance and optimize indoor climate parameters (e.g. daylight, IA Q, thermal comfort, 
acoustic) through passive building systems should be included in the EPC article. A definition of passive 
systems should also be introduced in Article 2. SYNERGI suggests the following definition: 'Passive system' 
means a design principle or a building element that maintains or improves energy performance and/or one or several indoor 
climate parameters (i.e., daylight, IAQ, thermal comfort, acoustic) with little or no assistance from an energy source.

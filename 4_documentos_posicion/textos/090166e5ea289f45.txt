Educational publishing and media freedom (FEP) 
Many parallels can be drawn  between the media and educational publishing sectors, first and 
foremost the fact that both are made of companies that provide essential services to the European 
society while also representing a valuable part of the economy, and whose integrity and independence 
must be protected and upheld. 
Book publishing is the largest cultural industry in Europe; it  generates around 22-24 billion € of net 
revenues per year, for a retail market value of about 35-38 billion €. Book publishing is a valuable asset 
for Europe, which is a global leader in this sector. According to FEP estimates, educational publishing 
is an important component of the publishing industry, representing around 20% of the market at EU 
level, and up to one third of the total in some Member States  (being the backbone of the local 
publishing sector and underpinning a healthy bookselling sector); including academic and professional 
publishing, the share is around 40%. 
Educational publishers are specialised in meeting the needs of the various educational systems. The 
role of publishers is precisely to provide quality educational materials, fit for the different national  
curricula, in the formats required by teachers and students , while also ensuring their neutrality with 
respect to specific orientations (political, religious, etc.) . Publishers fulfil this role in a competitive  
environment, investing their resources and taking risks, while ensuring the remuneration of authors  
and other contributors. This reinforces the quality of the publishers’ products, as is normally the case 
in a free, competitive marketplace. 
Finally, publishers hold the intellectual property rights on the resources they produce for the 
educational systems, having acquired f rom creators and/or intermediaries the relevant rights in the 
several pieces of content included in any textbook. Educational publishing in this is no different from 
any other sector of the  cultural industries, for all of which copyright is the basic legislative tool that 
ensures the protection of  creation and recouping of investment, thus perpetuating the process of 
creation and reinvestment. 
Educational publishers have embraced the digital transformation of education  by providing not only 
innovative digital learning materials and management systems, but also training opportunities for 
teachers. This relies entirely on the investments that publishers make every day. Furthermore, most 
of the production costs of quality educational material do not decrease when switching from print to 
digital (such as remunerating authors, assessing quality, editing, etc.). 
Just like the media sector, educational publishing should be allowed to work independently and in a 
pluralistic environment, without pressure or interference from the public authorities. 
However, several experiments have been conducted on the public creation of educational resources, 
often within digitalisation programmes (mixing legitimate objectives such as exploring the possible 
benefits of digital learning materials and connectivity in classrooms  and improving digital 
infrastructure and skills with dubious attempts to replace professionally published textbooks). Beside 
resulting invariably in an inefficient allocation  of resources  ( the resulting materials being used 
disproportionately little compared to their cost), such initiatives sometimes involve the imposition of 
state-published materials; these drastically reduce the freedom of choice of teachers, and open  the 
way to manipulation and the pursui t of a political agenda:  this kind of interference  is in fact often 
associated with a deterioration of content from a democratic perspective (with materials being subject 
to censorship or supporting authoritarian ideologies) . What makes it even worse, most of these 
initiatives are financed with EU funds (such as Structural Funds). 
The Commission aims to encourage the establishment of transparent, independent media markets. 
Once again, this should apply to the markets for educational resources . Part of this consists in 
refraining from imposing restrictions related to companies’ market entry and operation  and in 
avoiding discriminatory or disproportionate application of administrative authorisation or control 
procedures. 
The large-scale production of resources with public funds (with a view to substituting textbooks with 
allegedly “free” materials) constitutes  unfair competition to  educational publishers and seriously 
endangers the whole sector, while at the same time being a very  inefficient allocation of resources. 
Public initiatives of this kind negatively affect the capacity of publishers to operate on the market, and 
risk pushing out of the market most private enterprises, with serious consequences also for the 
freedom to publish. 
Certain practices regarding the adoption of educational materials , in parallel, amount to  
disproportionate (if not outright discriminatory) administrative authorisation or control procedures. 
A need for transparency also arises in this domain, not so much with regard to the ownership of 
publishing houses – which is easily verifiable – or their identifiability as producers and providers  of 
educational content. The notion needs to apply to other operators that are increasingly active in the 
education field: big technology providers. In a more digital context, the issue of data or rather the 
potential of data in education becomes much more relevant. First of all, data protection is at the core 
of the educational publishers’ approach in the EU. Educational publishers take care to apply the GDPR 
and its guidelines to protect the personal data of students. They take measures to shield personal and 
learning profiles from third parties. 
In this framework, greater transparency will be needed about collection and usage of data from  
students and teachers in education, as a way to establish a level playing field. This is crucial given the 
increasing involvement of internet giants, moreover largely non -European, in the provision of tools 
for education, with motives and objectives not as clear as those of publishers. 
Several of the conditions for healthy media markets identified by the Commission can also be 
translated into the educational publishing field. For example, educational materials greatly benefit 
from being balanced and impartial – which is part of the publishers’ job to ensure. 
Publishers are at the forefront of innovative practices in the provision of educational materials and 
learning environments, and their ability to innovate depends on their capacity to recover their 
investments. 
Finally, the Commission advocates a fair allocation of state resources  in the media markets. Once 
again, this fits very well the market for educational resources. 
State budgets should reflect the importance of professionally published materials, and  adequate 
allocations should be made for the acquisition of print and digital resources, in an open, competitive 
environment. Too often programmes and projects to step up the digitisation of schools have budgets 
that do not encompass the procurement of learning materials and systems. Quality materials, adapted 
and updated, in all formats, require adequate investments.  
When the state acts replacing publishers, this simply ‘hides’ the cost of publishing without constituting 
a saving: public authorities have to  increase their involvement in the long-term in the publishing of 
materials (or outsource this task), leading to significant, long term  public investment/expenditure, 
increased by the necessary quality assurance, possibly reaching beyond what is normally spent on the 
procurement of professional content. This sort of unfair competition can drive private enterprise out 
of the market, thus resulting in publishers stopping their investments, leading in turn to worse quality 
and service, the end of innovation, b oth digital and  pedagogical, with negative consequences for 
educational systems and ultimately for the future of EU citizens.  
National examples/cases 
Norway 
Norwegian Digital Learning Arena is a joint county enterprise offering open digital learning assets for 
upper secondary education to all high schools in Norway with the exception of Oslo. NDLA was 
founded in 2007, following a budget incentive from the national government imposing the counties 
to provide free educational material in print as well as dig ital for the upper secondary education. 
Today NDLA provides open digital course material in more than 100 subjects. The counties spend 
approximately 25% of their learning material budgets on funding NDLA, the remaining budget funds 
are mainly allocated for printed materials. 
D
ue to NDLA being free and available to all high schools in Norway, except the schools in Oslo, the 
individual schools have very limited additional budgets for acquiring digital learning materials in the 
open market. This situation has led to publishers being reluctant to invest in digital development of 

learning materials for upper secondary education and the range of available products in the market is 
therefore quite limited and simple.  
For the primary and secondary schools, the situation is the opposite. In this market the range of high 
quality innovative digital learning materials is strong and there are several publishers and edtech 
companies competing to deliver ever better products to the market. This development has 
accelerated over the last 2 -3 years following the implementation of a new curriculum reform where 
the government incentivized the development of digital learning materials by supporting publishers 
and edtech companies through grants for development and innovation of digital material for the new 
reform. The government also implemented an incentive system for local cities who purchased these 
digital learning materials on the market once they were available. In sum this has led to growth both 
in the use of digital learning materials and in the variety and quality of digital learning materials offered 
to the market.

Romania 
The current situation does not allow for a well-functioning, free textbook market. There is no free 
competition, private initiatives are strongly discouraged.  
The Ministry is the authority that evaluates, buys and allows distribution in schools of educational 
materials (textbooks, bought by the Ministry and complementary resources , paid by the parents, 
voluntarily). 
The tenders for new textbooks lack organisation : it may happen that you hear in January/February 
that you have a tender for textbooks in March, and you need to deliver the new textbooks in 
September (when school year starts). Tenders are organised, usually, for one grade/year, so you have 
now pupils that study, in 2022, on textbooks published in 2004. Publishers are not allowed to change 
the content of the textbooks, so the pupils read now that Romania is not yet a member of the EU. 

The price for textbooks is utterly inadequate. In 2022, during the tender for 5 th grade textbooks, the 
Ministry decided not  to pay more than 8 .14 lei (1 .6 euro), no matter the size and complexity of  a 
textbook, and including the digital edition. So, you  may have a 240 -page maths textbook, and the 
publisher is forced to sell it to the Ministry for 1 .6 euros, both in physical and digital version . Even 
though paper cost has doubled since 2021, not to mention since 2017. 
As a publisher, you are not allowed to enter the educational market unless the Ministry grants 
permission, through a tender. You are not allowed, for instance, to publish and distribute educational 
materials for Ukrainian refugees, if they choose to attend Romanian schools, because the Ministry of 
Education has not organised a tender in which to evaluate and to acquire these resources. 
As a publisher, you are not allowed to suggest a new complementary material during the school year. 
You must wait your turn to join the tender organi sed by the Ministry. Sometimes these tenders are 
announced just one week ahead.

Transversal issues in Eastern Europe
Approvals 
Since liberali sation of the textbook system  in the 1990s,  most of the countries in Eastern Europe 
imposed different kind s of approval systems. One of the most unusual measures in some of the 
countries, like Slovenia, Bulgaria and a few  others, is that only textbooks are approved , whereas  
workbooks are not, which means that while  textbooks are in school funds and adopted in limited 
numbers, workbooks could be published by a broader range of publishers, and sometimes there is no 
connection between the activity in the workbooks and the textbooks . This is somehow bizarre, 
because the set of the textbook and workbook is basically a unit by itself. Another  anomaly of the 
approval system is the total freeze for new titles to be submitted ; a most egregious  example is 
Bulgaria, where for a decade or more one subject  has been  completely blocked, with two or three 
selected textbooks that cannot get a competitor.

Digitalisation 
Most alarmingly, many countries in Eastern Europe understand the need to digitalise education as a 
way of limiting the number of publishers, which results in curbing the freedom to publish – for instance 
by limiting the introduction new titles and new didactical approaches. Therefore, the markets witness 
anomalies and interferences by the  state through large -scale tenders turn the markets into 
monopolies and stifle fair competition. One of the recent examples was in 2006 to 2008 in Slovenia, 
where the government spent 23 million euros to produce educational materials on an exclusive basis. 
This practice is widespread, with other countries imposing gigantic tenders (Bulgaria, Romania), for 
massive amounts of money in order to produce mediocre content for teachers , without instructions 
or training. Therefore, the materials produced cannot compete with high quality materials produced 
by publishers and generate very little usage (if any), but at the same time publishers face tremendous 
financial obstacles when trying to launch innovative products as they end up competing with  free 
materials.

Scientific publishing and media freedom (FEP and STM) 
The European publishing sector promotes European ideals globally, is world- leading, and provides 
an essential service to European society 
Many parallels can be drawn between the media and scientific publishing sectors, first and foremost 
the fact that both are made of companies that provide essential services to the European society while 
also representing a valuable part of the economy, and whose integrity and independence must be 
protected and upheld. 
Book publishing is the largest cultural industry in Europe; it generates around € 22-24 billion of net 
revenues per year, for a retail market value of about €35-38 billion. Book publishing is a valuable asset 
for Europe, which is a global leader in this sector. According to FEP estimates, academic and 
professional publishing is an important component of the publishing industry, representing around 
20% of the market at EU level, and up to one third of the total in some Member States  (being the 
backbone of the local publishing sector and underpinning a healthy bookselling sector); including  
educational publishing, the share is around 40%. 
According to Outsell Inc. the Scientific and Technical information and Solutions amounts globally to 
$13.8 billion in 2020
6 of which 31% falls to the EMEA region and puts it on almost equal footing with 
the USA. Scientific publishers play a pivotal role in the dissemination of research by investing in editing, 
curating and managing the review of research publications to bring them to their intended audiences, 
thus contributing to the openness of science. The peer review service they provide is a guarantee of 
quality, and competent, independent editorial boards protect the integrity of published research. The 
competitive environment in which they operate continues to foster system investment and 
innovation.  
The sector is made of many players, big and small, from commercial publishers to learned societies 
and university presses, operating in different sectors, such as Science Technical Medical (STM) fields 
and Social Sciences and Humanities (SSH) The sector landscape differs from country to country and 
from market to market in many respects: the offerings of business models, the role of governments 
(especially the availability of funds for Open Access), the language, the readership of publications 
(academic consortia, but also individuals, small research centres, individual libraries, etc.) . Many 
scientific works find their readers on the market, not just in the academic domain. 
The diversity typical of scientific publishing should be recognised ; the differences among disciplines, 
between books and journals must be understood, and the value of language and cultural diversity 
needs to be appreciated. 
Publishers make continued investments for an open and innovative scholarly communication 
ecosystem 
Scientific publishers are key to openness; opening science has been for a long time  in the nature of 
publishing. Curation, editorial work, dialogue with the authors to find the best language to address 
the audience, are some of the key factors , next to many others . Publishers are essential to these

aspects of an Open Science context. Science is open when it is able to engage in a real dialogue with 
society. A publisher’s key task is identifying the audience for each and every scientific publication . 
Publishers’ added value includes thus not only editorial and peer reviewing work,  and substantial 
investments, but also the best strategy to reach audiences. 
Finally, freedom to publish in this domain, like in any other, is underpinned by IPR; and the openness 
of science is underpinned by IPR  as well. Indeed, a strong copyright regime is the pre -condition for 
cultural diversity and freedom expression, which are the wealth of the EU, in that it provides incentives 
for continued creations and a framework for the recognition of human creativity and enterprise. 
Scientific publishers have long enacted the digital transformation of scientific dissemination by shifting 
massively to the digital environment, and by providing a huge array of digital tools to help researchers 
and institutions . This relies entirely on the investments that publishers make ever y day  and their 
continuous innovation efforts. Publishers are continually investing in infrastructure to enable them to 
publish the outcomes of research more rapidly, efficiently, and openly. 
Just like the media sector, scientific publishing should be allo wed to work independently and in a 
pluralistic environment, without pressure or interference from the public authorities. 
However, the drive towards the openness of science has been often misguidedly directed against 
publishers, as an attempt to circumvent  or replace their role and contribution. This is especially 
evident in the framework of the debate around Open Access (OA) to scientific publications. Publishers 
support OA, either by allowing authors to post their articles after a sufficient embargo time for 
publishers to recoup their investment (“Green route”), or by making articles immediately freely 
accessible online thanks to the payment of publication costs upfront by research institutes (“Gold 
route”) and a number of other innovative approaches . In a n online environment where dubious 
information is increasingly available, the role of publishers as trustworthy sources of selected, verified 
and findable knowledge shouldn’t be underestimated and lost in the name of “open” for its own sake. 
Open information has no value for society nor application if it cannot be found, ingested by humans 
or machines, nor trusted.  
Nonetheless, defining OA as a pre-requisite or as the main driver of open science is a misconception, 
since it does not take into account the c omplexity of this domain . The demand for every good, and 
thus also for a publication, depends on the price  and its corresponding value , and thus OA can be a 
way to increase the demand. However, considering this effect out of context is misleading. Assuming 
that Open Sciences and Open Access always go together and defining public policies on the basis of 
such assumption is a misguided approach. Open sciences are a strategic goal; open access is, or can 
be, a tool to achieve it. 
A fundamental misconception is that if research results should be open, especially if publicly funded, 
this applies to publications, too. Some research is publicly funded; publications, however, require a 
set of different infrastructures and substantial operations that are added on top of the research 
results. These are taken care of by publishers, and usually are not publicly funded. The continued 
investment by publishers to transform the raw research output into verified and trusted publications 
scientific information guarantees the maintenance of a working system of scientific dissemination. 
Open is often associated with “for free”, but this is another misconception: scientific publications do 
not come without a cost, it is just a matter of who sustains it and how it is compensated. Since open 
                 
 
does not mean free, any OA strategy must take care not to enable approaches that compromise the 
ability of publishers to invest in peer review, research integrity, and quality control and that make a 
false promise at the expense of the long -term health and sustainability of the scholarly 
communications ecosystem. 
The key issue is the sustainability –  economic as well as cultural –  of scientific publishing, the 
recognition of the work of the publisher and  its adequate remuneration. This in turn underpins the 
diversity and pluralism of the sector and the role of private enterprise within it . Sustainability in fact 
means allowing a diverse and pluralistic landscape of publishers of all sizes and specialisati ons to 
maintain their activity viable in time in different sectors and with different business models. 
The pluralism in the publishing offer that today characterises the scientific publishing market must be 
preserved. Long term s ustainability should be evaluated accordingly. Providing incentive s to 
researchers to publish in OA is much more effective than obliging them. Obligations without resources 
create difficulties for researchers and provide a very bad service to the open access cause, as they  
make OA unpopular in the scholarly environment. 
An increased role for the public sector would undermine the independence of the intermediation 
provided by publishers 
Scientific publishing requires specific competences (editing, managing metadata, taxonomies, subject 
schemes, party identifiers, staying on top of technology developments, etc.) and infrastructures, to 
ensure the efficient dissemination of research in the scientific communities. The publishing mediation 
requires higher investments , since the quality of publication should include additional criteria to 
broaden the audience, and quality is no longer defined as a matter between “peers”. An inclusion 
policy should also pay attention to accessibility: open sciences cannot close the doors to print impaired 
people, and the investment towards born accessible publishing requires investments. 
The need for more resources to recover such additional costs calls for multiple approaches Since the 
search for readers becomes an essential requisite, when revenues only come from the author’s 
institution, the incentive to broadening the audience may disappear. In certain circumstances, this 
may result in a paradox: that OA  decreases the capacity to reach more readers. There is a need, 
therefore, to assess wh ich is the best model to open each publication , case-by-case, in constant 
dialogue between the author and his/her publisher. 
The issue cannot be solved through increasing the role of the public sector, calling it to take over the 
functions of the publishing mediation. In such a case, the publisher’s impartiality and independence 
will be lost, and the publishing function would be impoverished, since its value depends primarily on 
diversity and pluralism. 
The Commission aims to encourage the establishment of transparent, independent media markets. 
Once again, this should apply to the scientific publishing sector, including via refraining from imposing 
restrictions related to companies’ market entry and operation. 
Diverse policies are prefer able to inflexible mandates  to safeguard the freedom to publish, 
competitive markets and cultural diversity 
                 
 
The publishing mediation has an important role in promoting open sciences. The independence of 
publishers is a cornerstone. The market today sees players of various kinds competing with each other: 
commercial publishing houses, university presses, university departments and learned societies. 
Increasing the direct intervention of the public sector is not useful. On the contrary, policies that 
enhance this diversity and support the small and medium-sized enterprises are rather to be preferred. 
Even more importantly, the freedom and independence of researchers must be at the heart of any 
policy for Open Sciences  Incentives must be preferred to obligations and limitations of the freedom 
of choice of researchers. The protection of the authors’ right of every individual researcher and the 
trust in his/her capacity of managing it in a way consistent with the open sciences’ objectives are of 
paramount importance. 
Just as research becomes  increasingly open, barriers to the range of publication options be opened 
rather than closed, with as few controls as possible , and a range of licencing options must remain 
available for researchers to apply to their work. 
State pu blishing should therefore be restricted to cases in which there is a lack of demand or no 
sufficient profitability. Private publishers contribute to a stronger economic efficiency cultural 
diversity and freedom to publish. And yet , private publishers are i ncreasingly facing actions by the 
state, consisting of circumventing them in the field of scientific publishing  and in stifling their 
independence. 
Very often, state strategies on scientific publishing do not seem to be based on a previous assessment 
of the capacity of private operators to fulfil the market demand and on the impact of state initiatives 
on the market.  
For example, the OA Diamond model, much favoured by cOalition S funders, is based on the idea that 
authors, institutions, or funders do not pay an Open Access fee and the reader does not pay for access, 
because for example  costs are subsumed within existing budgets of universities, society presses or 
research institutions and regarded as part of their mission. This model, however, can simply “h ide” 
the real cost of publication, it does not eliminate it – and can thus reduce transparency. In a recent 
report, the Diamond model is revealed as an economy that largely depends on volunteers, universities 
and government: over 60% of journals run on a budget of less than 10,000 EUR, 70% run on 1 -2 FTE, 
40% break even and 25% operate at a loss and many lack knowledge of their own financial situation. 
The model encourages state-controlled publishing and would put national publishers in a situation of 
dependence towards such subsidies (very different from a policy of public purchases), while depriving 
them of other sources of revenues such as exports. A public policy based on such an approach certainly 
raises issues of unfair competition and possibly state aid, as well as a risk of nationalisation of scientific 
publishing, and therefore represents a threat to freedom to publish.

Already many Open Sciences publishing related projects are being launched through calls for tender 
by public authorities which tend to de facto favour state publishers. It is nonetheless more reasonable 
for public institutions, outside their primary areas of competence, to outsource rather than taking up 
on themselves all sorts of activities . Moreover, any move towards the state control of scientific 
publishing could only run counter to the freedom of researchers, the objective of quality content and 
the overall economic efficiency of the system. In the long run, such a measure will mainly benefit the 
internet giants who will be able to “sweep up”  this freely accessible content and offer their own 
products, without having invested in or ensured the quality of the original cont ent, to the detriment 
of cultural diversity and democratic principles. 
Finally, some of these calls for tender and more generally the requirements of the research institutes 
involved in Coalition S consist of non-exclusive rights transfers by researchers to publishers, as well as 
the use of the most liberal Creative Commons licence (CC -BY). Such conditions imply that publishers 
cannot expect a proper return on investment as they don’t get an exclusivity of the publication, and 
as other operators may re -use the article concerned even for commercial purposes. They seem to 
represent one more way to circumvent private publishers.  
Several of the conditions for healthy media markets identified by the Commission can also be 
translated into the educational publishi ng field. For example, scientific publications greatly benefit 
from being balanced and impartial – which is part of the publishers’ job to ensure. 
Appropriate resources should be allocated for scholarly communications 
Finally, the Commission advocates a fair allocation of state resources in the media markets. Once 
again, this fits very well the market for scientific publications. 
State budgets should e nsure that funding in the research publication system is adequate to protect 
quality, long-term sustainability, and integrity in the future , maintaining an open, competitive  
environment. This means allocating adequate resources, not aiming at cutting expenses nor  
circumventing the private enterprise. 
When the state acts replacing publishers, this simply ‘hides’ the cost of publishing without constituting 
a saving: public authorities have to increase their involvement in  the long-term in the publishing of 
materials (or outsource this task), leading to significant, long term  public investment/expenditure, 
increased by the necessary quality assurance, possibly reaching beyond what is normally spent on the 
procurement of professional content. This sort of unfair competition can drive private enterprise out 
of the market, thus resulting in publishers stopping their investments, leading in turn to worse quality 
and service, and the end of innovation, with negative consequences for the dissemination of research 
results and ultimately for the future of EU citizens.  
                 
 
National examples/cases 
Italy 
Starting from the humanities and social sciences means considering publishing markets where many 
players operate, both commercial and not-for-profit, in different languages, with low levels of market 
concentration. A study carried out in 2015 on the Italian market of scientific journals in the HSS 
surveyed as many as 3,272 scientific journals published by 865 professional publishers (publishing 65% 
of journals), 184 university departments and 808 learned societies8.  
This is a varied landscape, where articles in Italian coexist with articles in English and other languages, 
with a high level of interdisciplinarity (57% of journals host authors coming from different scientific 
areas), and where almost 500 journals were already published (in 2015) in open access. All these 
figures have further increased in the 7 years from the survey. 
The landscape of book publishing is even more complex. According to the Italian Institute of Statistics 
(ISTAT), in 2019, 34K new titles were published in the “specialist non-fiction” category in the HSS. SMEs 
published 80% of these titles.  
Nielsen BookScan data show that, in the same disciplines, 15 million books were purchased by Italian 
citizens in online and brick-and-mortar bookshops.  
The definition s behind these data do not coincide with the traditional definition of “scientific 
publication”. However, in the context of the open sciences we must look at the reality of non -fiction 
publications, often of very high quality, which already are a vehicle for a dialogue with society. 
These data highlight the need  to review the impact of policies to ensure they take into account 
markets and segments with completely different characteristics and avoid a one-size-fits-all approach. 
France 
While French public authorities promote the principle of immediate free access, most French research 
institutes do not provide budgets for the Gold model in Humanities and Social Sciences. The open 
science state fund uses criteria such as the requirement for an open -source platf orm or the 
participation of the scientific communities in the governance of the operators, which only state 
operators can fulfil. Such a restriction of the access to public funding to public actors seems again to 
go against unfair competition and possibly be equivalent to state aid.

real risk for the French scientific publishing landscape, mainly due to the fact that digital revenues do

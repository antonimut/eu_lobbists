The European Publishers Council (“EPC”), a high -level group of Chairmen and CEOs of 
Europe’s leading media and publishing groups representing companies with newspapers, 
magazines, online publishing, journals, databases, books, eLearning, online marketplaces, 
radio, TV broadcasting and audio-visual productions, has been working on the promotion of 
free, independent, and viable media sector since 1991. 
 
Process and legal basis 
The idea of a European Media Freedom Act (EMFA) was first presented publicly on 19 April 
2021 by Commissioner Thierry Breton, as part of the Action plan for media at the height of 
the Corona pandemic, published in December 2020.  Announcing the Commission’s 
intention to table a European Media Freedom Act on 15 September 2021 in the 2021 State 
of the Union address , President von der Leyen talked of the need to protect the safety of 
journalists (now a Recommendation) and stressed that media companies cannot be treated 
as any other businesses and that their independence must be protected ; but she specified 
this protection should be at EU level. However: 
 
Firstly, media is an exclusive competence of member states  or shared in some limited 
cases. The consultation goes on to say that with the EMFA, the Commission intends to take 
yet another step to ensure the proper functioning of the EU media market, in particular by 
eliminating barriers to the establishment and operation of media companies and  to 
safeguard a free and pluralistic media ecosystem. However there is no such thing, and never 
can be, an internal market for media given the differences in cultures, languages, and legal 
traditions.  
 
• Therefore, we do not agree with the Commission and its plan to use the Article 114 
TFEU as the legal basis for any future legal instrument in the field of media.

Secondly, we do not accept that the European Commission needs to establish new legal 
bases in order to challenge any Member State’s disregard for the rule of law . The 
consultation states that the Commission’s 2020 and 2021 Rule of Law Reports as well as the 
Media Plu ralism Monitor ( MPM) point to various instances of interference in the 
ownership, management operation or existence of media outlets across the EU . T he 
European Commission does not need a new European Fr eedom Act to take up these 
instances of interference with Member States because: 
 
• Already at its disposal are various Rule of Law instruments and we do no t accept 
that any additional regulation, however well intended, would make any difference 
to this matter; whereas a EMFA could impose new levels of regulation on legitimate 
media businesses throughout the EU for no good reason  and which could have 
unintended and unfortunate outcomes for the freedom of the media. 
 
• Media freedom, in particular the freedom of the press can only be guaranteed if the 
responsibilities and competences of the Member States are strictly adhered to.”1  
 
Thirdly, our members are troubled by the lack of process that has been followed on the 
EMFA. The European Commission has launched its public consultation, without previously 
publishing a Roadmap on the proposal, or provid ing evidence of any impact, on which  
stakeholders can already provide feedback. A study providing some evidence for the need to 
act is still under preparation, as it is the call for evidence, which has the same deadline as the 
public consultation. We caution against acting before  all the necessary inputs  have been 
collected and assessed before launching such a controversial issue as media regulation, which 
we do not believe is justified.  
 
Europe’s Digital Decade: targets for 2030 should embrace a free press is a financially robust 
and independent press 
When considering targets for the new “digital decade” for the media sector, one of the most 
important pre-conditions in support of a sustainable and well -functioning press, is to 
provide for the economic  and legal  framework to allow competition to flourish , and to 
ensure that dominant players in the online market do not overwhelm the capacity of the 
press to generate their own economic growth; or for example to undermine efforts such as 
to enforce the press publisher’s right under the copyright directive . This why the DMA and 
DSA are crucial and why they need some key amendments to fulfil their promise. 
 
• To put it simply, freedom of the press will flourish if publishers are financially 
independent. Therefore, the much -needed opening up of competition and  
preconditions for a level-playing field -  the objectives provided by the DMA and the 
DSA, are crucial to our economic future.

Scope of any proposal and economic impact of further regulation of the media 
Notwithstanding the recognition by the European Commission that the EU has no legal 
competence to legislate for pluralism, any discussion at European level on media pluralism 
and particularly media ownership  and media concentration , has traditionally always led to 
calls for stricter statutory controls, usually on the claimed grounds of safeguarding pluralism, 
rather than leading to any discussion on, or proposals for measures to stimulate innovation 
and competitiveness  in the media sector . Therefore, we see  no justification in terms of 
upholding media freedom to harmonise national ownership and concentration rules, which 
are adapted to national market situations and cultural mores.  
 
a)    Ownership and consolidation: 
Given the focus on questions around ownership, and the implications from others that 
consolidation is injurious to the competitiveness and plurality of the media, and thereby 
requiring harmonised rules on media concentration , we wish to make it clear that t he MFA 
should not become a harmonisation of rules on media concentration or an anti-consolidation 
tool. This would defy the realities of the market where consolidation is providing much 
needed investment into maintaining a diversity of titles and a means to compete with the 
dominance of gatekeeping platforms. 
• Consolidation does not mean an end to plurality ; on the contrary, in many cases 
titles and publishing companies that would otherwise have disappeared are 
maintained in the market through consolidation.  
• Consolidation does not mean an end to diverse editorial stances ; on the contrary, 
editorial independence is m aintained according to editorial codes and founding 
statutes.  
• Consolidation provides opportunities for companies to benefit from shared 
integration of commercial solutions, such as sharing technology, e.g. for advertising 
or subscription sales efforts, none of which interferes with the editorial processes. 
This provides the economic basis for media to compete better with gatekeeping 
platforms. 
 
DG Competition consulted last year on aspects of competition policy including definitions of 
relevant markets which in our view are in much need of reform under to remove outdated 
anti-consolidation rules for media. This would be an appropriate European intervention to 
redress the balance between media and digital platforms  which have been allowed to 
create comprehensive ecosystems and we regret the lack of reference to consolidation of 
online platforms in the consultation of the MFA.  
 
Media concentration rules and ownership restrictions would take no account, quite unfairly, 
not only of th e unfair competition from gatekeeping platforms, but also from the publicly 
funded media which compete head on with private media.  
 
The questionnaire covers several questions regarding various governance aspects of publicly 
funded media. However, it is essential for media pluralism that there should be fair play 
 
 
 4 
conditions to allow a free, independently funded European media to flourish alongside 
publicly funded media.  
 
As the publicly funded media work through their own digital transformation, they compete 
head on with publishers with multimedia news and general interest content online, through 
mobile apps, as well as with private TV broadcasters and audio. In many markets these public 
media have developed their own on-demand platforms. These no longer only include catch-
up from their TV and audio channels, but also exclusive content bought from third parties to 
enrich their offerings, funded by the taxpayer or from advertising . Furthermore, their 
multimedia offerings are promoted via the major social networks and content sharing 
platforms. Unchecked growth of publicly funded media endangers investment in wide areas 
of potential activity by the private sector. There should be much clearer limits on state 
broadcasters to prevent a crowding out effect through rigorous and independent scrutiny of 
their ever-expanding activities. 
 
b)    Advertising bans harm media revenues:  
• All publishers rely on advertising revenues, and in some cases, advertising is the only 
source of revenue  especially for smaller, local publications. Although many of our 
members have experienced success in growing subscriptions to paid -for content, 
these revenues are in no way compensating for the overall migration of advertising 
revenues to the gatekeeping platforms.   
• Furthermore, the content of advertising is under constant threat : either from new 
legislative moves with bans, content labelling and limitations, or ever more strict 
conditions around data processing for lawful media advertising, or because of unfair 
market conditions that benefit bi g online platforms and deprive publishers of fair 
revenues.  
 
c)   Editorial independence:  
• EPC members are also concerned with the worrying trend calling for legislation to 
promote editorial diversity and neutral views. Some questions in the consultation hint 
at content prominence or balance requirements, and there is no clarity as to how this 
would work or where this could lead. 
• We are extremely worried  therefore about new legislation that would lead to 
interference in edito rial freedom , in order to provide subjective, or politically-
motivated “balance”.  
• Freedom of the media should be non -negotiable and when it comes to the press in 
particular, consumers understand different titles reflect different editorial and 
political stances and make their choices accordingly. 
 
The importance of the DSA and the DMA in supporting a sustainable, viable free press and 
why we do not need a European Media Freedom Act 
As we have described above , we see no justification for introduc ing a new Media Freedom 
Act. We are of course aware of some specific problems in some Member States but this does 
not justify the introduct ion of a wide -ranging EU legal instrument on the grounds of 
 
 
 5 
supporting an internal market in media , and covering matters such as consolidation . More 
regulation, however well intentioned, always brings new restrictions, and additional costs. 
The best way to do something for media freedom is to provide for the best possible economic 
framework for media companies, especially regarding the gatekeeper platforms.  
  
➢ What is missing from the DMA to ensure media freedom? 
The EPC’s members use a variety of digital services in order to deliver their products and 
services to end users, meaning that they are dependent on gatekeeping platforms offering 
various “core platform services”. In particular, the media and publishing groups that the EPC 
represents are dependent on online intermediation services (including ad intermediation 
services, app stores, platforms incorporating voice assistant technologies and online 
marketplaces), online search engines, web browsers, online socia l networking services, 
operating systems, as well as video -sharing platform services provided by a few gatekeeper 
platforms.  
 
When it comes to the DMA, to future proof the regulation the fair and non -discriminatory 
general conditions of access for business users should apply to all designated gatekeepers 
and their core platform services (under article 6(1)(k)) not just App Stores. We are pleased to 
note that the latest agreement from the trialogues does indeed extend these provisions to 
include search engines and social networks, and that the list of core platform services now 
includes browsers and digital voice assistants. 
 
➢ What is missing from the DSA to ensure media freedom? 
The biggest threat to the fundamental rights enshrined in the Charter, including media 
freedom and freedom of speech, lies in the hands of the online platforms which today can 
decide to block access to or remove editorial content entirely on the basis of their terms and 
conditions. We are highly frustrated by the lack of understanding of our serious request for 
a) the maintenance of our constitutional protections, and b) by the conflation of this issue 
with the way in which disinformation is distributed and often amplified by platforms. It is 
essential that platforms take action to desist in the making available and amplification of 
disinformation alongside their obligations in the DSA to remove illegal content; but this must 
not be used as an excuse to justify inaction to safeguard the freedom of the media.  
 
EPC, together with all the media associations, are not asking for a ‘media exemption’ from 
the DSA; we are asking that the DSA recognises the proper protections of our fundamental 
rights, and establishes due process for how platforms deal with lawful content under the 
editorial control and legal liability of the publisher  (or broadcaster) under their content 
moderation policies and terms and conditions.  
 
Putting it another way, unless the DSA is amended, a Regulation will enshrine in law for the 
first-time that decisions about whether content under the editorial control of a publisher 
or broadcaster can be judged to be lawful or otherwise by gatekeeping platforms on the 
basis of their own, private content moderation policies with  no process in place to be 
informed of, or to challenge their decisions. This fundamental issue lies at the very heart of 
 
 
 6 
a free press, and it is therefore essential that this matter is dealt with satisfactorily by the co-
legislators. 
 
• Let us imagine a world where platforms can censor negative news about themselves, 
where they can make arbitrary adjustments to their terms and conditions to block or 
remove news stories for their own commercial purposes or curtail real debate 
between citizens online. This is what happens if platforms will not in future be obliged 
to respect European fundamental rights including the freedom of the press when 
both drawing up and enforcing their Ts and Cs and content moderation rules under 
the DSA. 
• The platforms’ algorithms are not remotely capable of making the very sophisticated 
judgements which editors make as part of their legal responsibilities, and which they 
defend in Court if necessary. 
• Furthermore, it is proven all too often that algorithms cannot understand context; for 
instance, an algorithm will be unable to understand the difference between a video 
of a terrorist incident used by a terrorist website to promote its aims, and the same 
piece of content used by a news publisher to illustrate a legitimate news report. 
 
Before continuing with plans for a European Media Freedom Act , we would respectfully ask 
the European Commission to re-consider what is at stake for the future of a free press. More 
regulation is not in our view justified. For further information, please find below our own 
charter for media freedom.

A charter for press and media freedom in Europe 
 
Freedom to report 
Our media perform a vital role in society in informing citizens about issues of importance and matters that 
they care about – everything from education and health to the economy and politics, wars and other world 
changing events, the environment and climate change, as well as life-style interests, travel, music and sport. 
 
Freedom to regulate ourselves 
The EPC is naturally opposed to any attempts to curtail editorial freedom and would regard any legislative 
interference with editorial policy as censorship.  
We are fully supportive of the independent media/press councils as they uphold editorial integrity and 
protect journalists against undue influence from both economic and political interests. We are supporting 
their development in all European Member States in order to foster coherent media self- regulation across 
the EU. 
The freedom to advertise is an integral element of the freedom of expression which is why the EPC, 
together with colleagues from throughout the media and advertising industry in the European Advertising 
Standards Alliance, is committed to high standards of advertising practice. We continue to update and 
develop best practice in line with market and technological changes and reject new laws to regulate the 
content of advertising which can be handled better through self-regulation.  
 
Freedom to flourish alongside gatekeeping platforms 
It is essential to ensure that fair and non-discriminatory general conditions of access for publishers apply 
to all gatekeepers  including online search engines, online social networking services  and video-sharing 
platforms and to their core platform services such as app s tores, web browsers, and voice assistant 
technologies. Furthermore, it is essential that platforms respect fundamental rights and media freedoms 
and do not block access to or remove lawful content under the editorial control of publishers. 
 
Freedom to earn advertising revenue 
Independent reporting costs money and our ability to earn advertising revenue has a direct impact on the 
number of pages in your ne wspaper, magazine, range of website content or on your choice of television 
or radio programmes. 
Advertising revenues in huge part are not going to the media, but to gatekeeping platforms. New bans or 
legal restrictions on advertising have an immediate detrimental economic impact on the economic viability 
of the media. 
 
Freedom to manage our intellectual property 
Copyright is not always respected online which means Europe’s cultural diversity and media pluralism are 
at stake. We distribute our content as widely as possible and our audience has never been greater thanks to 
website and mobile distribution of our content . Audience is not our problem  but paid for our content 
monetised by online platforms remains challenging, requiring in some cases the intervention of 
competition authorities to bring dominant gatekeeping platforms to the negotiating table as the 
enforcement of the new press publisher’s right becomes a reality.  
  
Freedom to compete with public service media 
It is essential for media pluralism and the maintenance of democracy that there should be fair play 
conditions to allow a free, independently funded European media to flourish alongside publicly funded 
media. But public media now compete head on with publishers online , via mobile apps as well as wit h 
private broadcasters for audience share and in some cases advertising revenue. In order to prevent a 
crowding out effect there  should be much clearer limits  set by Member States  on the ability of publicly 
funded state broadcasters to expand beyond their core public service remits through rigorous and 
independent scrutiny of their activities.

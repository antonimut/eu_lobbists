The Energy Performance of Buildings Directive Revision:     
Accelerate the decarbonisation of Europe’s diverse building stock 
 
COGEN Europe welcomes the revision of the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD) in the context of 
the Renovation Wave, to ensure that the buildings sector boost EU Green Deal ambitions and foster a green post-
pandemic recovery. This EPBD revision is an opportunity to accelerate building stock decarbonisation, by increasing 
energy efficiency, renewable energy, and empowering consumers to produce  and consume  their own clean 
distributed energy . This will create value at local level, reducing energy bills, addressing energy poverty and 
fostering green jobs for European communities.  We also believe that the EPBD recast should explore more 
opportunities resulting from the cost-effective use of high-efficiency cogeneration (CHP) in efficient district heating 
systems and micro-cogeneration (micro-CHP), including fuel cells. 
Below please find COGEN Europe’s key messages on the revision of the EPBD: 
 1. Take a district approach to energy efficiency and renewable energy in buildings 
• Enforce energy efficiency first principle across the entire energy value chain, in energy generation, transmission, 
distribution to and use in buildings   
• Create a level playing field for renewable energy produced on-site or delivered to buildings through all energy 
carriers – be it district heating, gas grids or electricity grids  
• Promote efficient cogeneration to increase renewable energy use and reduce fossil fuel consumption and GHG 
emissions 
 
2. Promote cost-effective, targeted and staged building renovations  
• Support both deep and staged  renovation to accelerate decarbonization of all building types 
• Foster a mix of cost-effective decarbonisation solutions to account for local circumstances and the diverse 
European building stock 
 
3. Put buildings at the centre of integrated energy systems 
• Promote faster decarbonisation of buildings through integrated planning and operation of heat, electricity and 
gas systems at local level 
• Consider the interaction of buildings with the wider energy system  
• Better recognise the value of hybrid solutions for heating of buildings both on-site and at district levels 
• Unlock the demand side flexibility potential of individual buildings, focusing on both short-term load shifting 
capabilities and long-term benefits of embedded capacity 
 
4. Empower consumers to produce their own clean heat and power 
• Promote a diverse mix of integrated, efficient and renewable buildings technologies 
• Customise and optimise renovation solutions at local, district and building levels 
 
5. Ensure consistency for buildings provisions across the EU Green Deal legislation 
• The EPBD revision must account for and be consistent with other provisions of the “Fit for 55” package and Green 
Gas Package 
• Energy efficiency, renewable energy and emissions objectives associated with  buildings must be aligned, to 
establish a level playing field for all relevant decarbonisation options

The cogeneration sector is committed to the creation of a resilient, decentralised and carbon neutral European 
energy system by 2050 with cogeneration as its backbone. This approach will accelerate emission reductions by 
2030, while empowering European cit izens and utilities to generate their own efficient, reliable, and affordable 
clean heat and power locally in and for buildings.  
The building sector is crucial for achieving the EU's energy and environmental goals, as it represents more than 
30% of EU’s emissions and energy consumption. According to the European Commission, in EU households, heating 
and hot water alone account for 79% of total final energy use (192.5Mtoe). Approximately 75% of heating and 
cooling is still generated from fossil fuels  while only 22% is generated from renewable energy. Moreover, only 
around 16.5% of heating and cooling is efficiently produced through high efficiency CHP . This means that more 
than 70% of heating and cooling in buildings is neither through efficient solutions no r is it based on renewable 
energy. While electrification is identified as a key solution to decarbonise buildings, inefficiencies in the power 
sector as well as the actual electricity mix used to electrify heating are often ignored. At the EU level, energy losses 
from power-only plants amount to 240Mtoe (out of 409Mtoe of total energy input) , wasted as heat dissipates 
into the atmosphere due to inefficient energy conversion through cooling towers . The se losses in electricity 
production exceed the total current heating and cooling demand of European buildings (193Mtoe)1. Moreover, 
many studies have documented the impact of heat electrification on significantly increasing peak power demand 
in winter at least twofold, which will for a long time require marginal coal and gas power plants to run longer as 
back-up to supply heat pumps with the needed extra electricity 2. These are  major challenge s that need to be 
immediately addressed, since the waste of yesterday should become a resource of today and tomorrow. 
 
As the worrying developments in  Ukraine unfold and given the ongoing energy price crisis , an ambitious and 
comprehensive approach on the  Renovation Wave can help reduce Europe’s dependence on gas imports from 
Russia, while diversifying our energy sources and further boosting our local economy. In this context, a “no regrets” 
approach for buildings must combine: 1) diversifying energy supply towards higher uptake  of renewable energy 
sources, including use of renewable gases and hydrogen; 2) promoting the efficient use of energy in buildings via 
heat pumps, cogeneration and district heating; 3) fostering system resiliency and flexibility by supporting the highly 
efficient and low-carbon dispatchable power solutions, including  on-site micro-CHP and fuel cells, which can run 
on increasingly renewable fuels. 
 
To further elaborate on our key messages regarding the EPBD and points mentioned above, serving as a broader 
perspective to consider when discussing different opportunities r esulting from high -efficiency cogeneration in 
district heating systems , please see below the reason for our position. We are convinced that high efficiency 
cogeneration can make a significant and vital contribution to delivering on the objectives of the Renovation Wave, 
particularly of the EPBD and REPowerEU. 
 
1. Take a district approach to energy efficiency and renewable energy in buildings 
Thus, to achieve the cost-effective and efficient decarbonisation of the building sector, the EPBD should not only 
focus on the lowest hanging fruit , namely accelerate renovation of buildings envelope , but also reinforce the 
role of the energy efficiency first (EE1st)principle in energy production, transmission and distribution, before it 
reaches the consumer, as well as energy systems integration . All this  while promoting solutions that enable 
significant energy savings will stabilise the energy system in a long-term perspective. The EE1st principle allows 
to create integrated planning for energy infrastructure and buildings by jointly considering demand - and supply-
side systems , which help s assess and coordinate long -term strategies for renovatio n of buildings based on 
individual planning tools (renovation passports) and heat and renovation roadmaps. By adopting such an approach, 
better and more energy efficient buildings can improve the quality of citizens' life while bringing additional benefits 
to the economy and the society, allowing to accommodate more sustainable energy sources.

As COGEN Europe, we actively support an approach where buildings are put at the centre of local integrated energy 
systems, unlocking synergies between different building solutions, including complementarity between 
cogeneration and end -use electrification of heat. A district approach  promotes faster decarbonisation and 
underscores the role of local integrated planning of heating and cooling, electricity and gas systems. Such a system 
focus requires new energy sources  in buildings to effectively interact with  the wider energy system , across all 
energy carriers, and its infrastructure parts to achieve EU 2030 and 2050 climate targets.  
Therefore, to foster integrated energy systems and enhance the energy efficiency first  principle, EPBD should 
prioritise high efficiency and flexible cogeneration for the use of all thermal energy sources needed for individual 
buildings or in efficient district heating, in cases where electrification is not feasible or not possible due to the 
high carbon intensity of the marginal power mix3. A higher focus on CHP will ensure the efficient switch to lower 
carbon and increasingly  renewable and connected energy sources. Flexible cogeneration installed close to the 
electricity and heat consumption allows to balance out renewable power generation  and ensures the rapid and 
cost-effective decarbonisation of buildings, further enhancing smart sector integration.

While electrification is expected to support some of the decarbonisation ambitions in buildings, we must ensure 
that this is done efficiently and predominantly from renewable electricity. To complement electrification and help 
manage the intermittency of r enewable energy sources, renewable and low carbon gases  used efficiently with 
cogeneration will be needed for hard to decarbonise buildings, where an existing gas network can be progressively 
decarbonised and alternatives such as electrification are not fe asible and/or cost-effective. This would allow to 
increase the capacity and resilience of the energy system  and electricity grids by applying different and the most 
adapted solutions for comprehensive renovation strategies.  
Moreover, the revised EPBD should foster a level-playing field for all decarbonising measures, given the diversity 
of the EU’s building stock  and its specific needs . This entails ensuring equal treatment between  various 
decarbonising solutions as long as they are co st-effective and match consumer needs and choices : 1) renewable 
heat produced on -site; 2) renewable heat delivered through efficient district heating; 3) renewable energy 
delivered through any energy carrier, including heat networks, gas grids or electricity networks; 4) improving supply 
side efficiency as well reducing buildings energy demand; 5) a mix of energy efficiency, renewable energy and smart 
system integration solutions. 
 
2. Promote cost-effective, tailored and staged building renovations  
 
The revised EPBD should ensure  and promote cost-effective and tailored approaches to building renovations  
depending on local circumstances and the characteristics of the building stock . The holistic renovation, which is 
necessary to decrease buildings’ total energy consumption, should support both deep and deep staged renovation.

The latter offers a good balance between taking early steps to decarbonise through cost-effective available 
solutions, while having a coherent long term deep renovation plan : 1) consumers can plan in advance and gather 
necessary financial resources to carry out deeper interventions; 2) less costly or disruptive measures can be carried 
out faster for immediate savings and emission reductions; 3) more buildings can undergo deep staged renovation 
compared to deep renovation at any point in time, which entails higher overall benefits being delivered sooner and 
more cost-effectively.   
 
It is also essential to s trengthen the role of local actors to ensure planning at local level to design customised 
solutions that offer the best value and comfort for the end -user, while maximising system level benefits in terms 
of carbon efficiency.  Therefore, the EPBD should incentivise Member States to create a framework to recognise 
hybrid solutions that combine complementary technologies to increase energy efficiency and maximise renewable 
energy either at district level or implemented by renewable or energy communities (e.g., combining CHP and heat 
pumps).  
 
3. Put buildings at the centre of integrated energy systems 
The revised EPBD should unlock the flexibility potential of buildings,  putting them at the centre of integrated 
energy systems. This requires reinforcing and broadening the scope of  smart readiness indicators in buildings to 
truly capture the ir system integration potentia l. The EPBD sh ould extend the definition of  smart readiness of 
buildings to incorporate: 1) the positive impact of high efficiency cogeneration used flexibly in district heating; 2) 
the important role of self -generation and self -consumption via micro - and small -scale CHP; 3) the benefits of 
demand-side dispatchable capacity in providing long term power system resiliency, reducing the need for costly 
grid reinforcements and new back-up generation deployment4.  
 
Figure 2: Net-zero emissions energy systems require CHP dispatchable electricity to match supply and demand where and when needed5. 
 
 
With increasing electrification of heat and transport, electricity demand in buildings will become even  more 
fluctuating, with high seasonal variation s between winter and summer , as well as more pronounced daily and 
weekly peaks. Therefore, energy system reliability and cost can be considered an important driver of energy-related 
decisions for consumers as well as local operators of energy grids. To ensure the flexibility of the energy system, 
the following aspects should be taken into account: 1) load reducing and shifting strategies for buildings should be 
considered in an integrated framework  in ord er to shift loads to off peak periods using energy storage where 
feasible or sector coupling ; 2) smart controls and storage; 3) promotion of on-site firm capacity which supplies 
dispatchable electricity, including high efficiency solutions as micro-CHP and fuel cells; and 4) integration of local 
dispatchable electricity via utility-scale cogeneration connected to district heating and cooling, increasingly using 
renewable gases and hydrogen produced from local excess intermittent renewable energy.

4. Empower consumers to produce their own clean heat and power 
 
The EU’s building stock very diverse and local in nature. A mix of solutions is available to meet the needs and 
preferences of consumers . The EPBD should provide enough flexibility to ensure an ambitious uptake of 
decarbonisation solutions that can be tailored to a range of different buildings, districts and communities, taking 
into account local and national circumstances, as well as the existing infrastructure. 
 
Cogeneration is a versatile technology, with applications available for individual buildings, district heating a s well 
as micro-grids/energy communities. It can supply most of the heat and power demand in a region or be used to 
complement other sources of heat and power.

5. Ensure consistency for buildings provisions across EU Green Deal legislation 
  
Finally, given that the EU Green Deal includes several important files that directly affect the building sector, it is of 
the utmost importance for the EPBD to be designed in coherence with other buildings-related measures under 
the “Fit for 55” package as well as the Green Gas Package , including the Energy Efficiency Directive (EED), the 
Renewable Energy Directive (RED), Ecodesign/Energy Labelling and the EU Emission Trading System  (EU ETS) and 
its extension to the building sector.  
 
Policymakers must take a wholistic approach to EU policy and address any inconsistencies between the EED, RED 
III, EU ETS I and II, Green Gas Package and the EPBD, with the aim of enabling an ambitious, but workable framework 
for buildings renovation. Targets, measures and definitions that require more coherence approach include: 
  
• EPBD roadmaps/strategies for buildings must be synchronised with the Heating and cooling assessments 
developed under the EED/RED III; 
• Nearly zero-emission and zero -emission buildings definition s must be coherent with the definitions of 
efficient district heating and high efficiency cogeneration in the EED, to avoid scenarios where the positive 
contribution of these solutions cannot be taken into account; 
• The RED, which sets new targets for renewables in heating and cooling, buildings, as well as the overall 
target for the EU could be translated into the EPBD through the  possibility to deploy of green gases, 
including hydrogen, and their promotion in the building sector; 
• Take a technology neutral approach to buildings’ decarbonisation by differentiating between the energy 
sources used (a region’s fuels mix can include bot h fossil fuels and renewables), the energy vectors 
(electricity, heat or gas grids, which can transport either renewable or fossil-based sources or a mix of the 
two) and the energy technologies used in buildings (using either electricity, gas or heat to meet the various 
energy needs of consumers); 
• Provide a level playing field between renewable energy produced on-site, nearby or delivered through grids 
to buildings; 
• The EPBD should reflect relevant provisions in the Green Gas Package, including the notion o f the energy 
efficiency with regard to  network planning and investments  enabling the most energy and cost-efficient 
mix of solutions for buildings in a specific district from a system perspective. 
 
 
Cogeneration for higher ambition in buildings  
 
 
Energy and resource efficiency: Cogeneration makes better use of energy resources available 
locally to consumers across a range of increasingly low carbon and renewable energy sources  
Cogeneration plants will ensure the immediate switch to lower carbon and renewable fuels when 
they become available. Savings of lower carbon and renewable fuels due to cogeneration will 
accelerate the phase out of fossil fuels. 
 
• Today, CHP saves 31Mtoe of primary energy. [1] 
• By 2030, realising CHP potential could reduce 74Mtoe of energy. [2] 
• In 2050, optimising the efficient and flexible operation of CHP will save 20Mtoe. [3] 
  
 
Emission reductions: Cogeneration is an enabler of a net-zero emissions economy by securing the 
efficient switch to lower carbon, decarbonised and increasingly renewable energy sources.  
• Already today, CHP reduces at least 140Mt of CO2 by consuming less energy compared to less 
efficient power-only and heat only alternatives. [4] 
• By 2030, realising the potential of CHP would ensure 350Mt of CO2 cuts, accounting for both 
the energy saved and the switch to cleaner fuels. [2] 
• By 2050, CHP would primarily contribute towards system efficiency and flexibility, while 
reducing 5.5Mt of remaining CO2 at EU level. [3]

Reliable and flexible energy: Cogeneration solutions are ready to respond to the future energy 
system challenges, complementing electrification and intermittent renewables  in an ideal way. 
• Cogeneration is a key local systems integration solution, smartly and efficiently linking 
electricity, heat and gas systems. 
• CHP can generate on-demand electricity at times of peak demand and insufficient intermittent 
renewable energy production. 
• Embedded CHP in industry and buildings are key to provide demand-side capacity and are a 
key contributor to reliable supply. 
• CHP is future proof and fuel flexible, having the capability to easily switch to renewable 
sources including biomethane, bio-LPG, hydrogen. 
  
 
Cost-effective: Cogeneration used on-site or nearby reduces consumer bills. Moreover, it 
significantly lowers overall energy system costs, by minimising operating costs of power grids and 
helping avoid grid reinforcements. 
• By 2050, optimising CHP across energy systems and sectors will deliver net savings of up to 
EUR 8.4M per year at EU level. [3] 
• Energy bills can be reduced significantly by adding CHP to mix of solutions to heat and power 
buildings, districts and industries. [3] 
  
 
Empowered consumers: Cogeneration accompanies the decarbonisation pathways of industry, 
buildings, and districts across Europe. 
Today CHP provides: 
• 12% of EU's power and 14% its heat. 
• 65% of DHC heat. [6] 
•  60GW of CHP installed on-site, embedded across EU industries and strongly contributing to 
the security of supply. 
• >100,000 active energy consumers like homes, hospitals and SMEs . 
By 2030 on the path to 2050: 
• By 2030, CHP could deliver 20% of electricity and 25% of heat. [2] 
• By 2050, CHP could cover important shares of non-electrified heat, up to 52% in buildings, 81% 
in industry and 50-91% in DHC. [3] & [5]

About COGEN Europe 
COGEN Europe, the European Association for the Promotion of Cogeneration, is the cross -sectoral voice of the cogeneration 
industry. We have over 60 members: 13 national associations and 50 organisations spanning the entire value chain from 
technology manufacturers and users to consultancies. The cogeneration sector is committed to the creation of a resilient, 
decentralised and carbon neutral European energy system by 2050 with cogeneration as its backbone, empowering European 
citizens and industry to generate their own efficient, reliable and affordable clean heat and power locally.

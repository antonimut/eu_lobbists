Revision of the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD) 
 
As one of the corner stones of the European Green Deal, the European Commission has 
launched a revision of the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive. Currently, buildings are 
responsible for 40 percent of the energy used in the EU as well as 36 percent of the energy -
related greenhouse gas emissions. The greening of the European building stock is thus one of 
the main challenges of the European green transition. 
 
The revision of the EPBD is intrinsically linked to several other main initiatives under the 
European Green Deal and is an integral part of the Fit for 55 agenda. The revision of the Energy 
Efficiency directive will create many areas of synergy with the EPBD, and the Renovation Wave 
will naturally play a major part in the realization of the EPBD.  
 
EFBWW therefore welcomes the revision of the EPBD as a necessary and important step to 
achieve the climate targets enshrined in the European Climate Law. 
 
The EPBD also presents a lot of opportunities to solve other problems related to the quality of 
the European building stock which should not be overlooked in the revision. These points, as 
well as some points of criticism will be further explained in this document: 
 
 
System integration 
 
One of the main keys to unlocking a successful green transition is to create synergy between 
all sectors of the energy infrastructure. In practical terms, this can be achieved in different 
ways: excess heat from burning waste can be used to fuel district heating, recharging electrical 
vehicles can be used as a power backup in the energy grid, periods of optimal solar or wind 
excess energy production can be transformed into e-fuels, etc. The European Commission has 
on many occasions highlighted system integr ation as one of the key solutions to ensure a 
successful green transition – for instance in their EU Strategy for Energy System Integration 
(COM/2020/299).  
 
Unfortunately, the EPBD proposal lacks this focus on the importance of system integration. In 
its proposal, the EC states that a “ ‘zero-emission building’ means a building with a very high 
energy performance […] where the very low amount of energy still required is fully covered by 
energy from renewable sources generated on-site, from a renewable energy community […] or 
from a district heating and cooling system”.

Whereas EFBWW appreciates and acknowledges the first part of the definition, where the 
Commission wholeheartedly commit to the principle of energy efficiency first, the latter part 
of the sentence creates a lot of unintended consequences.  
 
Many buildings would not be able to generate sufficient amounts of renewable energy on site. 
For instance, solutions such as photovoltaic power generation are often inefficient in larger 
cities, as buildings will often cast shade onto one another. For the foreseeable future, the vast 
majority of buildings will be part of a larger energy system, and their energy needs will be 
dependent on energy generated elsewhere.  
 
Furthermore, buildings will often have periods where energy usage will be significantly higher 
compared to its average energy needs. By sticking to the Commission’s definition, this would 
either mean that a building would need to have an energy production capacity that will 
significantly outweigh its need most of the time, or the building would need to be part of a 
larger environment of integrated energy systems capable of delivering renewable energy 
during e.g., hot summers or cold winters. By keeping the approach taken by the Commission 
it would force building owners to implement solutions that would not be cost optimal – and 
in many cases not even feasible – to reach the definition of a zero -emission building. 
Furthermore, this approach would also fundamentally distort the expansion of renewable  
energy in most EU Member States, as Member States would be forced to prioritize renewable 
energy solutions that were neither cost optimal, nor based on the actual energy needs. This 
would significantly increase the cost of the green transition in the EU. 
 
 
Embedded emissions 
 
New buildings become more and more energy efficient up to such a degree that the majority 
of a building’s greenhouse gas emissions today will often not stem from the emissions caused 
by the building’s energy usage, but rather – seen from a lifetime perspective -  be embedded 
in the building. In other words, the emissions stemming from the production of the building 
materials, the construction of the building itself, renovations throughout its lifetime and its 
eventual demolition will often surpass the emissions from the heating, cooling, or lighting of 
the building.  
 
As such, the EPBD should create a framework that takes these embedded emissions into 
consideration. Such a framework should put forward targets for the reduction of embed ded 
emissions similar to those already put forward by the Commission considering energy 
efficiency. To this end, the definition of a climate neutral building or a zero -emission building 
should also take embedded emissions into account. Embedded emissions s hould not only be 
considered when constructi ng a new building, but also when it comes to renovation, 
maintenance and demolition of existing buildings. All of this could be supported by having 
common, European databases with updated data on emissions from b uilding materials, new 
renovation methods etc.

Increase of the rate of energy efficiency measures 
 
The EPBD puts forward minimum standards for the least energy efficient buildings. The 
directive puts forward an obligation on the Member States to increas e the energy efficiency 
of these buildings by 2033 at the latest, with public buildings and commercial buildings having 
to do so earlier. Whereas this on its own is a positive step towards increasing the energy 
efficiency of the European building stock, it is not nearly ambitious enough. 
 
Firstly, the current proposal would only encompass roughly 15 % of the existing building stock. 
The scope of the proposal should be expanded to encompass all buildings with different 
targets based on the needs and potential of the buildings in question. One way to do so would 
be to put an obligation on the Member States to classify the remainder of the building stock 
into different classes based on their age and energy performance. A minimum standard could 
be put in place f or each of these classes based on cost efficiency. This would direct funding 
and renovations towards the projects that would create the largest energy savings. To this 
end, minimum standards should also be put into place  that each building class should rea ch 
by 2030, 2040 and 2050. 
 
 
Renovation passports 
 
EFBWW has been a long -time supporter for the inclusion and harmonization of renovation 
passports and supports the Commission’s willingness to include these in the directive. 
However, such passports should be mandatory and not voluntary, as proposed by the 
Commission, to have the maximum effect. This could be introduced into the directive via a 
delegated act allowing the Commission to introduce guidelines on renovation passports, 
timelines for their introduction, evaluation of Member States’ implementation etc. 
 
 
Financing 
 
In order to reach the ambitious targets suggested by the EPBD as well as the additional 
measures suggested in this paper, the European Commission as well as the Member States 
should provide additional and sufficient funding. This funding should not only be  targeted 
towards energy efficiency renovations themselves , but should also be made available for 
house and building owners in order to get individual, independent advice on how their home 
or buildings could benefit the most from renovation. This would include a mapping of existing 
energy usage and performance, advice on different solutions tailored to the individual building 
etc. In this way, home and building owners could direct their investments towards the 
solutions that would result in the greatest energy savings. Furthermore, financing should also 
be made available for other aims of the directive such as the removal of asbestos and the 
improvement of indoor climate (see below).

Long-term renovation strategy 
 
In order to live up to the ambitious goals of the EPBD as well as the Renovation Wave, millions 
of buildings will need to be renovated over the next decades. This means that many buildings 
will undergo major renovation. While this work is being carried out many other objectives can 
be reached at the same time. This opportunity must not be wasted. 
 
Firstly, asbestos is a silent killer , still very much present in the European built environment. 
Thousands of construction workers all over Europe have to handle asbestos daily. The EPBD 
should be used a s an opportunity to put forward demands for asbestos screenings of the 
buildings being renovated as well as demands for the safe removal of asbestos in these 
buildings. To put an end to asbestos in European buildings once and for  all should be a 
mandatory objective, and the Renovation Wave and the EPBD presents an opportunity to do 
so and should not be wasted. To achieve this, the European Commission should create a 
European framework for national strategies for the safe removal of all asbestos in the EU. 
These strategies should be based on a comprehensive analysis and registration of 
contaminated buildings, a clear and realistic timeline for each Member State for asbestos 
removal, and safe, documented, and controlled disposal of asbestos waste. 
 
Secondly, the revision fails to take into account why we use energy in buildings. Mostly, we 
do so in order to create a better indoor climate through e.g. heating, cooling, ventilation etc. 
Even though article 1 of the directive states that it should take “indoor climate requirements” 
into account, there is a lack of focus on indoor climate throughout the entire revision. By 
having a single-minded focus on energy savings the revision will as a best case scenario miss 
a golden opportunity to achieve a lot of added bene fits. Improving indoor climate can bring 
about many added benefits such as better performing students in schools, improved living 
quality in residential housing, increased productivity in office spaces etc. As such, Member 
States should introduce targets c oncerning the improvement in indoor climate in their long -
term renovation strategies.

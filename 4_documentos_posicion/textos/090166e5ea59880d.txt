The Proposed Revision of the EU Energy Performance of Buildings Directive 
The Views of the Enel Group 
 
 
Enel welcomes the EU Energy Performance of Buildings  recast proposal, as part of the Fit for 55  
package. Energy efficiency is one of the most cost -effective ways of addressing the EU’s strategic policy 
objectives of carbon neutrality by 2050, ensuring security of energy supply and enhancing competitiveness.  
Energy efficiency has become a structural trend, accelerated by ambitious policy targets, which place the 
"energy efficiency first” principle in the center of the energy policy in Europe.  Energy efficiency acquires even 
more importance within the context of the current energy crisis and the REPowe rEU communication, as it is 
one of the key tools to reach independency from foreign gas importation. The building sector is amongst the 
principal energy consumers , responsible for  40% of the energy use and 36 % of GHG  related emissions . 
Decarbonization of the building sector is therefore crucial to reach the EU climate ambition. Simultaneously 
the building sector plays a key role in the reduction of emissions in the transport sector by providing the 
necessary charging infrastructure for e-vehicles and fostering e-mobility. 
The actual renovation rate and depth of the EU buildings stock is not sufficient to meet the energy 
consumption and GHG emissions reduction s that are required to achieve the EU climate goals.  To 
reach the 55% of GHG emissions redu ction by 2030 and climate neutrality by 2050,  by 2030, the building 
sector should reduce its overall GHG emissions by approximately 60% , its final energy consumption by 14% 
and energy consumption for heating and cooling by 18% , compared to 2015 levels1. At the actual pace of 
renovation, there remains a significant gap and those targets will not be met. The annual renovation rate of 
the building stock should more than double and target deep renovations that significantly improve energy 
performance. This should go paired with an increased use of renewable energy and the smartening of 
buildings, which ultimately contribute to the efficient production and consumption of renewable energy , and 
the decarbonization of the transport sector. 
To scale up energy renovations in the EU building stock, it is crucial to remove the barriers that 
withhold building owners to invest  and implement measures that trigger deep renovation . Energy 
renovations bring multiple social, environmental, and economic benefits, beyond energy performance 
improvement. Renovating the EU building stock at a 3,5% weighted energy renovation rate could yearly cost 
over 300 billion euros from 2035 onwards, but it would create significant short- term impacts on GDP growth 
and employment, resulting in a net beneficial outcome for the EU economy2. This is particularly the case when 
electrification of heating  is combined with large scale renovations that improve energy efficiency.  Moreover, 
decarbonization of the building sector through electrification of the heat supply reduces energy import 
dependence and favors domestic production of heating equipment. Deep renovation also has a direct impact 
on energy poverty, which affects the most vulne rable, since better performing buildings reduce heat demand 
and significantly lower the electricity bills. Energy efficiency projects in buildings therefore are a cornerstone 
for a just transition towards a climate -resilient economy. From an environmental and health perspective, 
deeper renovations are needed to improve air quality as particularly in urban areas air pollution – for a large 
part coming from buildings – still is critical. Nevertheless, deep renovations are being hampered by economic, 
technical, administrative, behavioral and information barriers, such as upfront costs, insufficient information on 
the benefits of energy renovation, lack of skilled work force and split incentives, that have not been sufficiently

addressed by the existing regulatory framework. The Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD), being 
the cornerstone for energy renovation, doesn’t provide the needed measures to trigger the desired renovation 
rate or depth. 
Enel therefore welcomes the proposed recast of the EPBD, and particularly: 
• The shift from Long Term Renovation Strategies towards operational N ational Building 
Renovation Plans (NBRPs), which will ensure concrete targets, measurement and integration 
with other EU regulation, aiming at the decarbonization of building stocks.  The requirement to 
define a roadmap with nationally established  targets, measurable progress indicators , and the 
implementation of a common template with mandatory information will improve monitoring and align 
Member States ’ renovation plans with the EU 55% GHG reduction goal by 2030. Moreover, the 
integration of NBRPs within National Energy and Climate Plans will provide for a proper governance 
procedure which guaranties a more leveled playing field between Member States. 
 
• The requirement for Member States to implement a national common scheme for  building 
renovation passports which will be  an effective tool to incentivize and monitor staged deep 
renovation, by providing the required information on the renovation roadmap of a building.  
Building renovation passports help to bridge the information gap for owners and investors by outlining 
a long-term path for staged energy renovations. Staged renovations might overcome the upfront cost 
barrier of a single deep renovation. Provided by a qualified expert, they ensure quality renovations 
which aim at the long-term decarbonization of a building. They also are a valuable tool for targeting 
financial support towards deep renovations. 
 
• The increase in required EV charging structure as well as the withdrawal of existing barriers to 
the installation of private charging points which will be critical to respond to the projected EV 
charging demand by 2030. As charging is mostly done at home or at work, a significant increase in 
private charging infrastructure is needed. The EPBD foresees pre -cabling and/or charging points for 
most of new buildings and buildings undergoing renovation. Nevertheless, a clear definition of pre -
cabling is needed to effectively guaranty the possibility to install a charging point when needed. On 
the other hand, though the withdrawal of existing barriers such as complex permitting procedures and 
the need to obtain the consent from the landlord or co-owners for the installation of a private charging 
point are welcomed, additional measures should be implemented by Member States to ensure t he 
right to plug in existing residential buildings. Those are not covered by the EPBD proposal, which is 
critical, particularly for multi dwelling units. Furthermore, the Directive should include provisions for the 
charging of heavy-duty vehicles. Those ar e most conveniently charged with minimal interferences to 
their schedule, when stationary during their normal operating patterns – i.e. during loading and 
unloading at logistics hubs and distribution centers, or while parked at private truck depots at nigh t. 
Therefore, the proposal should cover new or renovated private truck depots, as well as logistic hubs 
and distribution centers, requiring them to be ready for battery electric truck charging. This should 
include both pre -equipment and proper grid connect ion. Finally, the definition of “zero -emission 
buildings” currently does not allow for these buildings to cover their low residual energy requirements 
from distributed sources of flexibility such as electric vehicles. This possibility should be explicitly 
included. 
 
• The mandatory implementation of smart readiness indicators for large non -residential 
buildings with a high energy demand as an initial step towards the smartening of all buildings. 
Smart technologies in buildings are a cost -effective means to create healthier and more comfortable 
buildings with a lower energy use and carbon impact and can also facilitate the integration of 
renewable energy sources in future energy systems. Therefore,  Enel supports the implementation of

• Mandatory Minimum Energy Performance Standards (MEP Ss) which are considered essential 
to step up the energy renovat ion rate of national building stocks - especially for the worst 
performing buildings- and to reach the decarbonization of the EU building stock . The upgrade 
of the 15% worst performing buildings will significantly impact on the overall decarbonization of 
Member States’ building stocks as  well as on the alleviation of energy poverty . Worst performing 
buildings commonly consume more, and less carbon neutral fuels for heating and cooling, being thus 
mayor GHG emitters. Additionally, low performing buildings are generally occupied by more vulnerable 
households, who spend a significant part of their expenditure to energy consumptio n. Upgrading the 
worst performing buildings therefore should be at the basis of the buildings’ decarbonization pathway 
and a just transition. The requirement for all buildings to be zero emission by 2050 and the mandatory 
set up of national intermediate en ergy performance standards is aligned with the overall 
decarbonization target for the building sector. The combined implementation of these three measures 
should lower energy consumption with 12% and GHG emissions with 23% by 2030, compared to the 
baseline scenario (EU Reference Scenario 2020). Such outcomes will largely rely on a significant and 
progressive reduction of the use of fossil fuels for heating and cooling and the implementation of clean 
technologies such as heat pumps. At its turn, the shift fr om fossil to clean energy consumption will 
bring significant socio-economic and environmental benefits  by 2030, notably by lowering household 
expenditure on electricity and heating by almost 12%, creating 1 .8 million additional jobs and 
significantly improving air quality. 
 
• New provisions regarding the content, quality and issuance of energy performance certificates 
(EPCs) and the creation of national databases that will improve monitoring and stock taking of 
the buildings’ energy performance and decarbonization. The requirement to use a common 
template, including the energy performance class of a building is essential to create a level pl aying 
field amongst Member States and monitor progress towards national targets. Further digitalization and 
broader coverage of information regarding GHG emissions reductions, smart readiness and energy 
savings should provide owners and investors with comparable, complete and reliable information that 
might guide renovation decisions.  This way EPCs should become a key instrument to reduce 
information and behavioral barriers to deep renovation. The digitalization of EPCs and creation of EPC 
databases are essential to improve the knowledge of the national building stocks and might guide 
policy decisions as well as investors, financing institutions or any other market players.

 Feedback to call for evidence March 2022 
 
EBC feedback to the call for evidence on protecting workers 
from asbestos exposure 
About EBC 
Established in 1990, the European Builders Confederation (EBC) is a European professional organisation 
representing national construction employer associations of micro, small and medium -sized enterprises. 
EBC is a member and partner of SMEunited, the Euro pean association of SMEs, and Small Business 
Standards (SBS), the European association representing SMEs in standardisation. 
The construction sector is of vital importance to the European economy. With 3.4 million enterprises and 
a total direct workforce of 18 million, the construction sector contributes at around 9% to the GDP of the 
European Union. 99.9% of the European construction sector is composed of micro, small and medium -
sized enterprises. 
 
General remarks 
In its Renovation Wave Strategy, the European Commission aims to renovate 35 million buildings by 2030, 
also ambitioning to upkeep a set of quality standards, such as high health and environmental standards. 
This includes “the removal of and protection against harmful substances such as asbestos,” which is to be 
achieved while paying “particular attention to protecting workers renovating old buildings from exposure 
to asbestos, also through appropriate training .” The Commission therefore aims to improve the existing 
framework, notably by considering revising Directive 2009/148/EC on the protection of workers from the 
risks related to exposure to asbestos at work, which will be determined following the present “Call for 
evidence”. 
 
Previously, last October, the European Parliament adopted an own initiative report with recommendations 
to the Commission on protecting workers from asbestos. The report calls, inter alia, for the Commission 
to propose a radically lower EU -wide binding occupational exposure limit (OEL) value for asbestos, 
specifically at 0.001 fibres/cm3 as an 8 -hour time-weighted average, instead of the current EU threshold 
of is 0.1 fibres/cm3. 
 
In light of the above, the European Builders Confederation (EBC) reiterates its firm belief that workers’ 
health is a top priority for the  construction sector, as construction SMEs take the utmost care to ensure 
that training and equipment of the highest standards is always provided to workers across the EU. At the 
same time, we are obliged to stress that the new occupational exposure limit proposed by the European 
Parliament may be too low, not practicably enforceable by professionals —and even counterproductive 
based on the reality of the market— thus leading to potential perpetuation of the presence of asbestos in 
existing buildings, which will undermine the implementation of the Renovation Wave. Instead, priority 
should be given to strong preventive and accompanying measures thereof, together with training schemes

Specific remarks 
• Realistic and applicable OEL for construction SMEs 
EBC stress that any revision of occupational exposure limit values should be realistic, as their application 
on the ground greatly depends on whether it is feasible for employers to measure and implement them. 
If companies are not able to apply limit values, they will not be  able to protect workers as intended . 
Such a development will effectively result in penalising the companies that already strictly abide by the 
rules and do their best to protect workers from exposure. 
Bearing in mind that many small and micro construction enterprises will be affected by such measures, it 
is important to ensure that the legislation can be implemented by construction SMEs in all Member 
States, thus making sure that there is a level playing field for workers’ protection across the European 
Union.  
EBC considers that the existing OEL of 0.1 fibres/cm3 is sufficient and should not be modified. The fact 
that the current OEL is functioning well can also be understood through the results of  the independent 
study commissioned by DG EMPL and undertaken by RPA and COWI in 20211. According to those findings, 
approximately between 4.1 - 7.3 million workers are exposed to asbestos, and if no further action is taken, 
approximately 22 cases of cancer will occur each year over the next 40 years due to expos ure during this 
period. Given the analogy of workers that come in contact with the substance and the expected cancer 
cases, it is clear that the current limit is functional. Further lowering the OEL would do little to restrict 
exposure, while on the other hand a better application of existing rules accompanied with improved 
guidance could be key.  
• Better harmonization of measurement methods and tools 
EBC calls on the European Commission to base its future actions on sound scientific evidence, a thorough 
assessment of technical and economic feasibility and an analysis of the consequent socio -economic 
impact, for which the role of the Advisory Committee on Safety and Health (ACSH) is central. These future 
actions must also depend on the availability of common measurement methods, to ensure that there is a 
level playing field across the EU. 
It should be noted that the measurement methods and tools chosen across different countries are not 
the same, do not focus on same indicators and test environments, and do not have the same 
performances, which constitutes a major difficulty to compare the OELs and their impact across Member 
States. For example, the analyses conducted using optical microscopy (recommended in a 199 7 WHO

The measurements made with these two methods therefore underestimate the number of fibres counted 
in the laboratory. Only the ATEM (Analytical Transmission Electron Microscopy) method allows this. 
It is thus perhaps premature to show as an example the practices of the Netherlands, which put forward 
an OEL of 2 fibres/litre of air, since the analytical method used in this country i s not the ATEM one, but 
ABEM. Even in the Dutch case, should the limit be lowered further (1  fibres/litre of air ) and/or the 
measurement method be harmonised opting for ATEM, the equipment of Dutch construction SMEs and 
laboratories would need to undergo broad and expensive changes to respond to the demands of the new 
framework. Should a limit this low be applied, Belgian and Italian SMEs would face similar difficulties; the 
situation for construction SMEs could turn  worse depending on the financial risks caused by too heavy 
investments. Such costs would then unfortunately burden, at least partly, the costs of construc tion and 
renovation works, thus financially impacting citizens who want to remove asbestos from their buildings. 
In view of the expected increase in activity in the next decade  given the Renovation Wave , we consider 
that the specialised asbestos removal co mpanies cannot tackle the challenge alone . Traditional 
construction SMEs need to be on board as well, benefitting from concrete financial support and guidelines. 
• The difficulty of measuring extremely low concentrations 
An example from French laboratories c ould be useful to understand the significant practical difficulty of 
applying a limit as low as suggested by the European Parliament. The Analytical Sensitivity (AS) is an 
indicator used by laboratories on the accuracy of the measurement3: the lower the AS, the more accurate 
the measurement. For instance, the existing French regulations require a minimum AS of 1 fibre/litre, i.e., 
1/10th of the OEL/8h  (currently set at 10 fibres/litre in France) , for low dust levels and for short 
interventions. This measurement accuracy parameter is already difficult to obtain for low dust levels. If 
the OEL were lowered to 1 fibre/litre, it would not be possible to maintain this accuracy  as it would also 
be necessary to lower this SA to 0.1 fibre/litre. However,  today accredited laboratories already have 
difficulties in reaching an AS of less than or equal to 1 fibre/litre. Therefore, lowering the SA will only be 
possible under the following scenarios: either changing the environment for the testing phase (increasing 
the volume of air sampled  for example), developing new methods and tools for extremely low OEL, or 
considerably multiplying the observation time in the laboratory . All th ese scenarios  would result in 
significant additional costs for all relevant actors, an increase in the time taken to deliver the results of 
dust measurements, and additional training to be provided to construction professionals and inspectors.

• Focus more on preventive and accompanying measures 
Before setting a new OEL the European Commission’s action should focus more on preventive and 
accompanying measures to eliminate or minimise risks. Strengthening the technical and financial 
assistance support for homeowners  to assess the presence of asbestos in their dwellings in order to 
reduce the risks upfront is key. 
Increasing technical and financial support for training, awareness-raising, and exchange of best practices 
on asbestos removal for both employers and workers  is a central element of this process.  In France for 
instance, only 1500 enterprises are certified for the removal of asbestos. More will need to be done in this 
respect, not only to increase the number of certified companies allowed to remove asbestos, but also to 
train all professionals that are involved in renovation processes that could potentially include the handling 
of asbestos (e.g., plumbers, electricians, floor covering craftsmen etc.). In France, a worker currently needs 
to be trained between 2 to 5 days, depending on their function on the construction site, to be allowed and 
able to handle asbestos. Similar requirements apply to Germany and many other Member States. This time 
can be a considerable burden, especially for construction micro and small companies . It is therefore 
important not only to support SMEs in training workers, but also to keep the amount of training time 
required at reasonable levels (ideally not more than 2 working days), to encourage the training of more 
workers in light of the Renovation Wave. 
At the same time, the development of specific treatment protocols on asbestos for disposal/removal 
operators of construction/demolition waste would facilitate this complex process, especially considering 
that asbestos is not recyclable.  Such protocols are already in place i n certain Member States and should 
be used for an exchange of practices . Information and prevention campaigns as well as guidance 
documents developed by specialised bodies (e.g., OPPBTP in France, INAIL in Italy) already in place at 
national level should be further supported in this sense. 
Finally, it is essential to ensure the access of  construction enterprises to an appropriate number of 
adapted waste facilities for asbestos  at the regional/local level . These facilities need to be  easily 
accessible within an acceptable distance, including cross-border. Also, the problem of the cost of disposal 
in the waste facilities is a major obstacle for our construction SMEs, especially for those companies whose 
core business is not asbestos re moval. Indeed, these elements are  pushing up tariffs and prices, 
jeopardising renovation projects and putting the workers involved at risk.

INTRODUCTION 
 
The European Commission published the revised Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD) 
on 15 December as part of the "Fit for 55". The revision is driven by the need to align the European 
Union's building stock with the goals of energy and climate -related policies aimed at 
decarbonising the European Union. 
Energy storage solutions are paramount for achieving the goals set by the European Commission 
in the revised directive, as key enablers of energy efficiency and grid flexibility. Furthermore, the 
ambitious targets for the deployment of a private charging infrastructure will increasingly require 
the deployment of energy storage solutions. EASE welcomes the increased ambition for improving 
the energy performance of the European Union's building stock . However, despite the benefits 
they provide, the focus on energy storage technologies remains limited.

1. Energy storage roll-out coupled with high standards and renovation targets 
The proposal overlooks the opportunity to capture the positive contribution of energy storage 
technologies to decarbonisation of the European Union building stock, via flexibility services, RES 
curtailment minimisation, grid investment postponement, energy optimisation, and sector system 
integration. To maximise the benefits, the Directive should allow the possibility of energy storage 
solutions insta lled together with renewable energy generation  in the Minimum Energy 
Performance Standards in Article 9 in order to facilitate access to a low carbon and cost-efficient 
energy system.  Furthermore, in Article 2a (3) the revised Directive does not take fully into account 
the potential to carry out multiple renovation processes in parallel to reduce the burden on 
occupants and to achieve greater energy savings.   
 
2. Energy storage in performance methodologies  
The methodologies presented in the proposal to asses energy performance, technology readiness, 
and Member States’ strategies to decarbonise building stock neglect the role energy storage plays 
for the optimisation of energy use. The absence of energy stora ge policies and measures in the 
National Buildings Renovation Plans  in Article 3 (c)  and Annex II (c)  is a barrier hindering the 
benefits energy storage provide.  
EASE welcomes the provision enhancing the scope of application of the Smart Readiness Indicator 
(SRI); however, it is critical in Article 13 (2) and (4) to apply SRI to new and existing buildings that 
will undergo major renovations. This will have a broader and more positive impact on measuring 
a building's ability to provide flexibility services to the grid. Furthermore, the capability of energy 
storage to provide energy to both the building or the grid at a later time is currently neglected in 
Annex IV 2 (c) which should assess energy shifting and system integration in the Smart Readiness 
of Buildings Rating.

3. Buildings as active participants in the energy system 
The extensive building renovation process  called for by  the revised Directive offers the 
opportunity to integrate buildings into the energy system, by providing flexibility to the system   
s and empowering consumers. However, the definition of zero-emission buildings in Article 2 (2) 
of the Directive proposal makes no mention of the electrical grid. Given the potential for supplying 
zero-emission energy to these buildings, this omission is both restrictive and counterproductive. 
Furthermore, the current definition limits the contribution of buildings to the grid and overlooks 
the opportunities offered by the installation of stand -alone and RES -coupled energy storage 
solutions in buildings. 
4. Electric vehicle charging infrastructure 
EASE welcomes the improved ambition for the installation of charging points  in Article 12  
represented by the reduction in the number of parking spaces required for the installation of 
charging points and pre-cabling infrastructure in buildings. However, the ambition for charging 
points installed in public buildings is still limits the important leading role that the public sector 
has in the energy transition, where public procurement of charging points should be encouraged. 
In 2030, private recharging will continue to account for between 60% and 85% of all recharging : 
the stress on the electrical grid must be taken into account in order to mitigate the potential risks 
of an unmanaged EV charging points roll-out. The possibility to include the co-located of energy 
storage solutions in areas with a high penetration of EV char ging points should be investigated 
by National Regulatory Authorities.

About EASE 
The European Association for Storage of Energy (EASE) is the voice of the energy storage community, 
actively promoting the use of energy storage in Europe and worldwide. It supports the deployment of 
energy storage as an indispensable instrument within the framework of the European energy and climate 
policy to deliver services to, and improve the flexibility of, the European energy system. EASE seeks to 
build a European platform for sharing and disseminating energy storage-related information and supports 
the transition towards a sustainable, flexible and stable energy system in Europe.  
For more information please visit www.ease-storage.eu

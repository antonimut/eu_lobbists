 
 
Saint-Gobain feedback to the EPBD revision proposals – March 2022 
Saint-Gobain welcomes the 2021 EPBD revision proposal as a much needed cornerstone to articulate a solid decarbonisation 
path by 2030 towards a highly efficient, climate neutral building stock by 2050.  
A number of elements however need to be substantially enhanced to secure a tangible progress in the energy performance 
of both new and existing buildings by 2030, in line with the 2050 EU ambition. Importantly, the role of the building sector in 
the overall energy and climate transition needs to be better considered when designing this new EPBD  and related files of 
the Fit for 55 package, in terms of GHG emissions, citizens’ buy-in, and energy system costs. Not least, it is vital to place the 
energy efficiency first (EE1) principle at th e heart of the EPBD, and to factor into the directive’s tools the full costs and 
benefits of energy efficiency for climate, health, jobs and energy security.  
1. Minimum Energy Performance Requirements (MEPS) should deliver much more  
We welcome the introduction of MEPS in the EPBD . They are the missing piece in the energy renovation  puzzle, which 
requires 3 dimensions, namely: 1) financing; 2) support scheme s; and, to enable the first two dimensions, 3) a clear 
regulatory framework. MEPS are powerful demand drivers: they can signal a clear calendar of improvements to all actors, 
incl. owners, finance institutions , industries and professionals, hence trigger market preparedness: this is key to secure a 
continuous increase in renovation rates , supported by an adequate financing, solutions offer, innovation and skilled 
workforce. MEPS simplicity and early warning are important to help financing schemes prepare the market .  
This being said, t he 2020’s need to be the “make or break” decade for building renovation. To drive demand, t he EU level 
MEPS framework should kick-off much earlier than 2030 /2033, as otherwise we risk seeing no visible progress from the 
average flat 1% EU energy renovation rate in this decade. In addition , the targeted ambition level should be much higher 
than Class F by 2030 and Class E by 2033, and rather aim for Class C by 2030.  Indeed, as such, the current MEPS proposals 
will incentivize renovations consisting of one or two minor measures, with two major drawbacks. First, this will jeopardize 
the chance to make the renovated buildings climate neutral ready (cf . IEA, 2021) as there won’t be any practical time left 
until 2050 to renovate these buildings again. And , second, this does not answer t he energy poverty issue. Indeed, going 
from Class G to F or E only creates little or no tangible improvements , neither in running costs, nor in terms of thermal 
comfort. With continuous rise in energy costs – to be also anticipated in case of massive electrification of heat supply - these 
households risk being unable to quit energy poverty, or will turn back into such conditions it in the early 2030’s.  
Further, the requirement to introduce national MEPS within National Building Renovation Plans (NBRP) should not be left 
voluntary: it should be an essential, mandatory requirement of the pl an. All buildings, not only the F &G Class ones, shall 
be integrated in a renovation trajectory towards nZEB / ZEB levels , enabling owners to make the right choice s as early as 
possible, to bundle energy improvement investment with other works, and to bene fit from lower bills , enhanced living 
conditions and healthier indoor environments  as soon as possible. A clear MEPS calendar in all segments is essential to 
trigger innovation and acceleration of new renovation business models, such as industrialised and off-site solutions.  
2. On the road to ZEB: Securing low energy needs in new and renovated buildings (deep renovation) 
The proposal triggers too much focus on switching fuel supply at the expense of a  truly integrated approach enabling win-
win benefits between energy efficiency and renewables, in both new and renovated buildings.  
What gets measured, get improved. For both new and existing buildings, Energy Performance Certificate (EPCs) are key to 
secure ambition. They should be developed as part of a clear and stable framework, and require to calculate primary and 
final energy, in order to secure final end use savings on top of reducing primary energy consumption. EPCs should encourage 
a focus on the reduction of “energy needs”, which can be achieved via a good thermal envelope performance. The quality 
of the building envelope  should be made visible and understandable, using relevant indicators such as the UBat, which 
characterizes the overall heat transmission performance (French RE 2020). Passive design measures , e.g. building 
orientation, insulation, air-tightness, or passive solar gains, which help secure long term reduced energy needs without any 
further energy consumption, as well as health & comfort, should be better recognized in achieving nZEB/ZEB. To improve 
the quality of EPCs, certifiers should perform on-site visits, and the quality of renovation should be checked in situ, once 
measures are installed. Not least, in order to boost trust in EPCs ahead of MEPS rolled out, national liability schemes for 
EPC certifiers should be developed. 
The Zero Emissions Buildings (Z EB) definition rightly suggests a fully decarbonized energy supply , but falls short of 
encouraging enough focus on further reducing energy demand. For new buildings, indeed, energy consumption thresholds 
of Annex III are not ambitious enough and should be adjusted for several climate zones, beyond the current nZEB levels, as 
otherwise they risk encouraging stagnation in building codes, this way missing the foundations of truly ZEB buildings.  
In existing buildings, accelerating deep renovation  is a prerequisite to facilitate a large scale uptake of renewables and 
achieve climate neutral buildings. Deep and holistic renovation is also the solution to energy poverty. From a marginal role,

deep renovation needs to become “standard practice” (BPIE 2021). Defining deep renovation is a positive first step , but 
the EPBD proposals needs to go further in at least two respects.  
First, it needs to catalyz e a process whereby , next to defining quantified energy performance levels  (e.g. NZEB/ZEB) , all 
components of a buildings are improved where needed (cf. définition of rénovation performante, French 2021 Climate and 
Resilience Law). The order of measures should prioritise the reduction of energy needs, which will facilitate the integration 
of renewables and mitigate the energy peak demand that is responsible for costly additional grid capacity. Deep renovation 
should be a holistic approach, integrating improvements in health & and comfort alongside energy efficiency gains.   
Second, the linkages between the concept of  deep renovation and the wider renovation ecosystem need to be improved , 
so that it is becoming the default approach. Not only should MEPS targeting F & G Class buildings drive deep renovation  of 
these buildings. But the National Building Renovation Plans shall also establish a very clear trajectory , from 2025 onwards, 
towards Class A/B for all buildings in each segment. Signaling the end goal to building owners enables them to select relevant 
packages of renovation measures; and it will stimulate the developmen t of support schemes, one stop shops  and project 
aggregators (ensembliers), and relevant financing schemes . To facilitate staged deep renovation s, building renovation 
passports should be deployed earlier than 2024 , in a much wider scale than suggested . They should be integrated in  
financing tools such as green mortgages. Finally, all public and private financing tools should incentivise deep renovation 
(e.g. zero rate loans or positive rate loans, like in Flanders, BE). Accessing public subsidies for staged deep renovations should 
be made conditional on realizing a renovation passport. The confusing reference to the EU taxonomy criteria (30% energy 
savings) should be deleted, and these criteria themselves should be updated, in line with the Fit for 55 ambition.    
3. National Buildings Renovations Plans should become more operational   
The evolution towards more strategic, impactful and operational building renovation plans, is welcome. In particular, the 
monitoring of the energy poverty situation in each country makes sense, as well as fully integrating NBRPs in National Energy 
and Climate Plans (NECPs). However, the NBRPs proposals are unfortunately still unlikely to deliver the kind of comparable 
and consolidated information needed to mobilise the entire value chain for the objectives of the EPBD and the Renovation 
Wave. The more ambitious option from the EPBD Impact Assessment should be considered in this regard.   
Common template s with a larger set of mandatory key indicat ors should be integrated in the  NBRPs to facilitate the 
monitoring of progress and comparability. This is key to leverage the role of building renovation in delivering against EU 
climate goals, the Effort Sharing Regulation and the Energy Efficiency Directive. Such monitoring, backed up by transparent 
indicators, will also be key to monitor progress on newest strategic concerns, such as gas dependency. NPRPs should provide 
a quantified planning of renovations to come in the coming years, in each segment, coupled with an assessment of benefits, 
costs, needed workforce and skills. This will enable proper planning and support by educational institutions and companies.  
The ramping up of local project bundling capacity (aggregating entities, “ensembliers”, or one stop shops) should get central 
attention. The mapping of potentials associated with renovation in each segment should systematically  integrate digital 
technologies. Existing tools already exist to identify priority buildings in need of renovation (e.g. IMOPE URBS), and help to 
roll out of renovation programmes in a faster, more impactful manner . Additionally, the multiple benefits should be duly 
quantified and monetized in order to facilitate decision -making on renovation schemes at national, regional or local level, 
so as to translate the EE1 principle in a more concrete, granular reality. The governance of NBRPs should be further enhanced 
to secure continuous feedback and inputs from interested stakeholders. Work on the replicability of successful renovation 
tools, such as the BuildUpon² renovation impact framework should be a priority.  
4. A more solid framework towards environmental performance 
We welcome the evolution towards whole life carbon (WLC) assessment of new and existing buildings, which will trigger 
additional improvements at building level, in addition to decarbonisation and circularity driven by other regulatory 
instruments. This evolution should be introduced earlier than 2030, but in good synergy with the main objective of the 
Renovation Wave – to tackle operational emissions of existing buildings. Member States should help pre pare and create 
capacity for assessing and reporting whole life carbon and other environmental impacts in buildings.  
A key point of attention is the need for a truly European approach on whole life carbon and other environmental impacts. 
The EPBD proposals rightly refers to indicators developed in Level(s) and to EN15978 to account for such impacts, but these 
references should be made stronger and binding, as not only the reporting but also the design of specific WLC thresholds 
should be fully aligned with the pan -European framework. Securing alignment in the way WLC is introduced in all Membe r 
States is an essential factor to secure success and drive innovation.  
Finally, building level WLC assessment should work hand in hand with product level requirements that will be discussed as 
part of the revision of the Construction Product Regulation. Existing methodologies to assess environmental performance 
of products and buildings (EN15804+A2 and EN15978) should be used, in a fully harmonized way.

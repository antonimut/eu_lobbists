Eurosmart's feedback on: Amendments to the Regulation (EU) No 1025/2012 European standardisation  The European Commission presented on February 2nd a much-awaited Standardisation Strategy. The Digital security industry represented by Eurosmart welcomes such an initiative; Europe's standardisation approach is of utmost importance for the efficiency of the digital single market. Europe standardisation is clearly challenged by the increasing evolution and innovation pace of ICT products, services, and processes. Digitalisation is increasing in innovation towards connected devices and is no longer limited to information and computer technology platforms, e.g. connected cars, digitalised infrastructure, automated factories, etc. In the field of digital technologies, as it is the case of digital identities, digital payments and other digital services, all part of a service-oriented economy, standards are faced with the challenge of the rapid evolution of the technology itself. By nature, digital technology grows exponentially12. This has deep implications in the field of cyber security standardization: To develop standards applicable to cyber security, as well its maintenance, requires specific knowhow that goes beyond the individual expertise. It requires a dialog and contribution from different stakeholders, especially those closer to the technologies and applications in need of standards. Such a construct is difficult, when not impossible at NSBs when taken in consideration the shortage of resources on the field of cyber security worldwide, and the importance of an state-of-the-art knowhow required for this task.  Eurosmart expects this renewed standardisation approach will complement the current initiatives to place cyber resilience at the heart of the market access and market surveillance principles.  Eurosmart and its members are committed to supporting European Standards development to enable solutions' interoperability, increase Europe's competitiveness technological sovereignty, and rely on European values of digital privacy. For this purpose, our organisation and its members are very active in different standardisation fora and consortia, European Commission expert groups, European Standardisation Organisations and national bodies. The digital security industry acknowledges that standards in Europe deserve a fresh impetus to become more agile on the global stage. However, the proposed amendment to the regulation (EU) No 1025/2012 in parallel with some provisions of the European Commission communication COM/2022/31 would profoundly modify the current standardisation governance in Europe and on the role of private stakeholders.

Necessary involvement of private organisations and balanced representativeness The European Commission is concerned about ESO's internal governance; over the years, their initial structure has been evolving to consider the representativeness of the whole value chain. It naturally mirrors the current state of the European Digital Market, which is made of EU and non-EU producers and organisations3. The involvement of such stakeholders from Europe and third countries impacts the edition and adoption of technical specifications, standards, and harmonised standards. Decisions are based on art. 10(1) of the Standardisation regulation, which mainly concerns the acceptance, and the edition of harmonised standards, could be influenced by private stakeholders. In other words, non-EU private organisations may weigh on standardisation decisions whose primary objectives are to support EU legislation and policies. Eurosmart agrees that safeguards should be established to protect what could be associated with the European principles (e.g. respect of human rights, right to privacy, ...) and regulatory perspectives. However, EU harmonised standard development deserves to convene a balanced representation of stakeholders' interests in line with policy priorities but as well as with the reality of the actors involved in the EU digital single market. The development of standards and, in particular, harmonised standards should reflect a ground truth. Besides official representatives from the Member States and the Commission, the involvement of a proper value chain is necessary at some stage to define and adopt standards that really fit the consumers' and market players' needs.  Clarify the impact of EN standards development on harmonised standards adoption The option proposed by the Commission is to restrict some activities to the national standardisation (NSBs) bodies only when it comes to harmonised standards adoption and development. The proposed amendments specifically tackled the harmonised standards decision-making process. However, the impact on the edition of such standards and how private stakeholders would be associated are not efficiently assessed. The current proposal does not prohibit legal entities belonging to the non-EU organisation from participating in standardisation activities through the NSBs (provided the required conditions for joining are met). This proposal still leaves room for private stakeholders and non-EU organisations to weigh on harmonised standards. When considering the current functioning of  CEN/CENELEC/ISO/IEC work: stakeholders have always contributed through their NSBs. From Eurosmart's perspective, the proposed amendment should be more specific and decorrelate the decision-making process – that would be restricted to the national standardisation bodies - from the standard editing work and its technical work, which various stakeholders could support. In opposition, Article 5 of the Regulation (EU) No 1025/2012 encourages and facilitates the "appropriate" representation of the different stakeholders at different stages of European standards edition and adoption. Here the legislator recognises the added value of the involvement of private stakeholders in the standard editing work. However, the new proposal does not consider the influence of the European standards developed by various stakeholders on the development of harmonised standards. In most cases, the development of new work items, edition of EU standards and technical specifications pave the way for the future acceptance of harmonised standards. On the maintenance of adopted harmonised European standards. Finally, EU standards and harmonised EU standards face a constantly changing environment. This is especially true for the ICT domain, whose today's safety requirements will become outdated due to the technology evolution. Hence, the need for maintenance of adopted harmonised standards should be a priority. The recent trend of the EU legislator is to get the safety world even closer to some cybersecurity aspects, links with EU certifications as alternate methods to demonstrate compliance with particular regulation requirements should be developed. In this respect, EU standards maintenance is expected to accelerate. This task cannot be accomplished without the involvement and expertise of private stakeholders. Private stakeholders massively support such activities. This field knowledge is essential to create harmonised EU standards that fit regulatory and market expectations.  Conclusion The Amendment to the Regulation (EU) No 1025/2012 European standardisation, its accompanying communication and the report "Standardisation package” address some of the issues regarding the balanced representativity of the private stakeholders. However, due to the indirect involvement of interested stakeholders, the current proposal would slow down the standard editing process. Clear guidance to ensure at the same time private organisations' involvement, a balanced representativity and a clear view of the standardisation development and priorities would deserve a more detailed approach.  Industry and other key players need incentives and transparent rules to support the EU standard work. In this respect, further concrete proposals should be developed with the support of the different European Commission's expert groups and the ESOs. The proposed “High-Level Forum” is an interesting instrument to anticipate the standardisation needs; the digital security industry encourages the Commission to provide more concrete tools to support the edition and adoption of harmonised standards within a transparent process. Without clear and predictable links between EU standardisation activities, harmonised standardisation requests and development of technical specifications or minimum requirements by the legislator, the situation may introduce disinterest in EU standardisation activities. It could encourage stakeholders to rely on alternative methods. Even when using harmonised standards, a producer always remains fully responsible for assessing all the risks of his product to determine which essential requirements are applicable.

This paper provides UNIZO’s view with regard to the impact assessment conducted by the European 
Commission on several policy options  aiming at promoting repair and reuse with a view to 
enhancing the sustainable consumption of goods. UNIZO is an organization defending the interests 
of self-employed persons and SME’s in Belgium. 
The European Commission considers the following policy options:  
 
In summary, UNIZO asks: 
➢ A European right of redress  for the final seller , acknowledging the existing fear factor 
requiring su fficient ex officio inquiries  by th e national enforcement authorities of the 
Member States. 
➢ No extension of the duration of the legal guarantee. 
➢ A greater  focus on sensitising consumers and on repair and reuse  by: (i) organising 
awareness campaigns to consumers to buy "better" and not "more"  and to buy with local 
retailers, (ii) giving preference to repair over replacement when repair is less expensive or 
at least as expensive as replacement and (iii) leaving the possibility to the  final seller to 
replace a product by a refurbished or second-hand product.  
➢ The direct liability of the manufacturer only if the final seller remains the prior point of 
contact (direct liability of the manufacturer in “second order”).

S T R E N G T H E N I N G  T H E  F I N A L  S E L L E R’ S  R I G H T  OF  R E D R E S S  A S  A  P R I O R I T Y 
 
As a preliminary remark, UNIZO w ishes to stress that whatever policy option will be chosen, the 
result will be that the stricter obligations and hence, increasing costs will be carried by the final 
seller and not by the manufacturer who is often ultimately responsible for defective goods. 
 
The consumer sales directiv es (both Directives (EU) 2019/771 and 2019/77 0 as well as the (old) 
Directive 1999/44/EC) provide for a right to  obtain redress for the final seller  from the person or 
persons liable in the chain of transactions when consumers invoke their legal guarantee towards 
the final seller .  The directives leave it up to the Member States to define the modalities of this 
right.  
 
However, this right is in fact an empty shell as this can hardly be exercised effectively: 
 
- On the one hand, this is due to the fact that the chain of transactions is often 
international. As a result, compliance with the Belgian legal provisions cannot be 
guaranteed to the final sellers.  
- The final seller is very often in a subordinate position to the much more powerful 
manufacturers. 
As a result, in practice, the final se ller is often fully  responsible for the costs incurred  following 
consumers’ claims under the legal guarantee. In contrast, defects in goods are usually caused by an 
error in the manufacturing proces s and hence, the responsible company is usually the 
manufacturer.  
 
Since the chain of transactions is often international and in order to have a strong and effective 
right of redress, UNIZO asks to regulate this on a European level and to create a uniform European 
right of redress for the final seller who is often an SME. 
 
UNIZO recommends to provide at least the following remedies to the final seller:  
- Imposing a legal obligation on the supplier/manufacturer to provide a replacement appliance to 
the final seller so that the latter can provide it to the consumer when the consumer asks for a 
replacement; 
- Imposing a legal obligation on the supplier/manufacturer to make the necessary spare parts 
available to the final seller free of charge and within a reasonable period of time, enabling the final 
seller to comply with his obligation to repair; 
- Introducing a legal possibility for the final seller to claim from the supplier/manufacturer the travel 
and working hours necessary to repair or replace goods, even if the supplier/manufacturer has 
already provided parts and spare parts to the final seller; 
 
The consumer sales directives also provide for a legal presumption (for at least one year) in favour 
of the consumer that the non -conformity is present at the moment of the delivery of the goods, 
during a certain time (so-called “reversal of the burden of proof”).This condition should be met in 
 3 
order to successfully invoke the legal guarantee. This presumption should also be  introduced in 
favour of the final seller towards its supplier/manufacturer. 
 
Furthermore, measures should also be taken in order to protect final sellers when their 
supplier/manufacturer is bankrupt or ceased to exist. Final sellers also often encounter t his 
problem if they claim redress. 
 
Although these rights will have a preventive and informative use, we wish to underline that  this 
fear factor for final sellers to confront their suppliers remains a very important obstacle. Therefore, 
it is crucial that the MS can guarantee  sufficient enquiries ex officio by  their public enforcement 
bodies e.g. competition authority or economic inspection.  
 
Note that UNIZO does not plea to introduce the direct liability of the supplier/manufacturer for 
claims under the legal guarantee. For self-employed persons and SME’s, being the direct point of 
contact and providing help to consumers is an asset.  From the point of view from consumers, an 
obligation to directly contact the supplier/manufacturer in order to obtain redress under the legal 
guarantee is not convenient eithe r (e.g. if the manufacturer is located in another (EU) country). 
UNIZO can only agree with such direct liability on the condition that the final sellers remain  the 
prior point of contact.1 
 
Hence, imposing stricter obligations on final sellers should come hand-in-hand with a strong right 
of redress for such sellers. If not, the ultimate liability will be on the final seller although he is not 
involved in the production process  (generally lying on the basis of  a defect). Stricter obligations 
under the legal guarantee will thus have a significant (negative) impact on SME’s, rather than that 
they will affect manufacturers. 
S P E C I F I C  R E M A R K S O N  T H E  P R O P O S E D  P O L I C Y  O P T I O N S 
 
(i) Preliminary remarks 
UNIZO observes that the responsibility for creating more sustainable consumption is entirely placed 
on companies. However, creating awareness with consumers  is absolutely necessary in order to 
reach this goal. Products are often replaced by others and become waste way before the end of 
their potential useful life. Consumers should also be made more aware to buy better and not more 
and to buy with local retailers. 
 
When new obligations are imposed on final sellers, a level playing field (within the EU) should be 
guaranteed. The transposition of the consumers sales directives in the different Member States 
significantly differs (e.g. regarding the duration of the guarantee period and the reversal of the 
burden of proof). The legal divergence became even more significant after the transposition of the 
new consumer sales directives (2019/771). 
 
A level playing field with companies outside the EU should be guaranteed either. In order to create 
a level playing field, E U guarantee rules  should also apply to goods from China or America.  As

(ii) Direct liability for manufacturers: only in “second order” 
It is , however, true that  the proposed policy options  do not only contain amendments to the 
consumer sales directives and hence, adding additional obligations to final sellers. Two options also 
provide for additional obligations towards manufacturers (or sellers). 2 However, as stated above, 
the direct liability of manufacturers (as the direct point of contact for consumers  for obligations 
under the legal guarantee) “limits” the asset for final sellers to provide repair services to their 
customers. 
 
Therefore, UNIZO can only agree with direct liability for manufacturers for repair and replacement 
on the condition that the final seller remains the prior point of contact. Only when the final seller 
is not able to offer repair and replacement to the consumer, the consumer is allowed to call directly 
on the manufacturer. 
 
 
(iii) No extended guarantee period for all goods/second-hand goods 
 
Extending the guarantee period should not be used as a “solution” for premature depreciation of 
goods. Pre mature depreciation is  an exception and an extended guarantee period would be 
applicable to all goods. Premature depreciation is legally prohibited, and hence, measures should 
be taken in order to specifically ban this practice rather than targeting all goods. 
 
The imposition of an extended guarantee period3 that must be observed by the final seller has the 
consequence that its duration would generally not coincide with the guarantee provided  to the 
final seller by the manufacturer. This is not acceptable. For example, a retailer selling cars (or other 
vehicles such as vans and motorbikes) can often only rely on a commercial guarantee issued by the 
manufacturer which is limited to two years. Consequently, the final seller would have to assume 
liability claims under the legal guarantee for an additional period, for which he could not turn to 
the manufacturer under the commercial guarantee.  To compensate for this, end sellers could take 
out "extended warranty" insurance, which would also be reflected in the price at which the car is 
sold. This, in turn, would be to the consumer's disadvantage.  
 
The same is true for second-hand goods.4 
 
- Second-hand goods are goods that can be clearly and objectively distinguished from new goods. 
Consumers generally turn to this type of goods for financial reasons. Moreover, consumers'

Imposing extended legal guarantees/more stringent obligations under the legal guarantee for more 
only for sustainable goods is not a solution either. It is impossible to establish objective categories 
of sustainable and less sustainable goods. It is therefore more appropriate to allow companies the 
choice of offering an additional commercial guarantee for their products.  
 
Furthermore, the longer the consumer uses a good, the higher the chance that the good  is 
defective due to a wrong use of the good by the consumer. It is impossible for the final seller to 
prove that the defect is caused due to a wrong use of the good or due to external circumstances 
(e.g. humidity of the room where the good is placed). 
 
Finally and again, an extended guarantee period will ultimately only impact the final seller as the 
right of redress of the final seller towards the supplier/manufacturer is an empty shell.  
Hence, UNIZO cannot agree with sub-options 2 A) and 3 B). 
 
(iv) Focus on awareness, repair and reuse 
For the reasons set o ut above, UNIZO is not in favour of  an extended legal guarantee period . 
However, UNIZO is in favour of: 
 
➢ Option 1: voluntary commitments.  
➢ Sub-option 2B):  
o Make repair the preferred remedy when repair is less expensive than or as expensive as 
replacement. This (sub)option strikes a good balance between the  interests of 
consumers/sellers.5

“reasonable” price? Note that when final sellers conduct repair services this can exceed the 
cost of the product itself  (e.g. in case of cheaper products or products incorporated in 
immovable goods), without the price  of repair being “unreasonable” (certainly given the

Other measures:  
 
- Introducing a lower VAT-rate for consumers buying second-hand goods. 
- Awareness campaigns on the importance of sustainability towards companies and consumers.6 
- Ban on free returns  when buying goods through e -commerce. Foreign Internet giants cause 
masses of free return shipments, of which the environmental and mobility costs are very hig h. 
UNIZO therefore argues for a European ban on free returns. This will ensure that the CO2 impact 
of return shipments decreases and that there is a level playing field for local businesses. 
- An approximation of rules between Member States and between physical and online shops, e.g.

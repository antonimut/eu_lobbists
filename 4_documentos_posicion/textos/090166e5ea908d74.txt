 
 
 
Brussels April 4th, 2022 
 
Equinor welcomes the European Commission’s proposal submitted 15 th Dec 2021  
for a regulation on methane emission reduction in the energy sector . 
 
Equinor is the largest gas producer on the Norwegian Continental Shelf (NCS), and  the second-largest gas supplier in 
Europe. The combined gas volumes from Equinor and the SDFI (the Norwegian state’s gas volumes) constitute more 
than 20% of the gas market in Europe. Equinor’s total methane intensity for all our operated activities is 0.02%1, one 
tenth of the OGCI average of 0.2%2. The methane intensity of the Norwegian gas delivered to Europe, from production 
to where the gas enters the EU is less than 0.02%3. The International Methane Emission Observatory (IMEO) concluded 
in its 2021 re port4 that Equinor’s methane emission reporting  for assets in Norway is at gold standard. Independent 
research of oil and gas installation on the NCS demonstrated excellent alignment between site-level measurements and 
the operator reported emissions , deri ved from predominantly source -based quantificat ion methods.  The aircraft 
measurement campaign was executed as part of the Climate and Clean Air Coalition (CCAC) Methane Science Studies 
Programme and the results were published 5 November 2021. The existing g ood regulatory and industry practise in 
Norway was also confirmed in the latest IEA Methane tracker report (February 2022) 6. 
 
It is our ambition to maintain this near-zero methane intensity. We will continue to develop and implement technologies 
and procedures to detect, quantify, avoid, and minimise methane emissions. Equinor participates in industry efforts to 
reduce methane emissions across the oil and gas value chain, as well as increase the quality and transparency of 
reported data. We support the European Commission’s effort to (i) advance the av ailability, quality, and reliability of 
methane emission data; (ii) ensure that operational routines are in place to minimise emissions ; and (iii) reduce 
imported methane emission from international operators. 
 
Our comments to the proposed regulation are submitted under the premise that the regulation will be implemented in 
Norwegian law and also will be given effect on the Norwegian Continental Shelf (NCS).  
 
Although we support the overall purpose of the regulation, we believe the proposal needs to be improved and clarified 
in several respects. The proposed rules are often too prescriptive such that they get in the way of implementing Best 
Available Technologies (BAT) which offer a better balance between effort and environmental outcome. Our main  
concerns are as follows: 
 
1. Article 2(24) definition of inactive wells lacks accuracy and needs to be improved such that permanently 
plugged wells are excluded from the definition. The integrity of permanently plugged wells is ensured by 
installing barriers that extend across the full cross section of a well, such that no fluid or flow is possible 
through the wellbore and that there is no communication from any permeable formation to the seabed via

The provisions in Article 18 to remediate leaking inactive wells, appear to be written with onshore wells in 
mind, without explicitly excluding offshore wells. The inclusion of offshore wells and the requirement on 
Member States to install measuring equipment in this article would challenge the EU / EEA legal principle of 
proportionality7. The additional complexity of offshore logistics, combined with the need to develop new 
technology (as it currently does not exist) pushes the required effort far beyond the potential environmental 
benefit. There is no need to install measuring equipment on all potentially leaking inactive wells as 
alternative quantification technologies are available to determine if a leak is present . Any requirement to 
address leakages from inactive wells should be based on risk, i.e a combination of consequence (materiality) 
and likelihood that leakages occur. On this basis, we suggest that offshore wells are explicitly excluded from 
the definition of inactive wells. 
2. Article 14 introduces Leak Detection And Repair (LDAR) programmes with a fixed three months frequency 
for all installations. Installations differ in terms of materiality (e.g. number of potential leak sources), the 
likelihood that leaks occur, and not least the presence of existing routines and technologies that will quickly 
identify significant leaks should they occur. Experience shows that when initial LDAR is conducted properly, a 
high level of control can be maintained with an annual frequency since typically not many new leaks are 
discovered in subsequent LDAR campaigns. A targeted approach based upon emission risk will be more 
impactful to reduce emissions.  
The introduction of a 500 ppm concentration threshold offers little guidance without technology specification 
as methane concentration in the air is dependent on many variables, among others: the distance from the 
emission source, meteorological conditions such as windspeed and direction. In the US, this threshold was 
used in combination with a specific technology (method 21), which is already outdated by current standards. 
3. Article 15 instils a ban on routine venting and flaring, a practise that was put in place on the NCS in 1971.The 
definitions and concepts should be better aligned with those of the World Bank8. The prescriptive 
requirements should be brought in check with technical possibilities and specific technology choices should 
be avoided. The inspection and reporting frequencies can be reduced without there being a loss of r eporting 
accuracy or environmental performance.  
4. The Monitoring, Reporting and Verification standard poses some challenges in a global context. The 
proposed methane regulation references but also redefines key concepts and definitions of the OGMP2.0 
framework, which risks undermining its very purpose. The legislation frames the OGMP2.0 reporting 
framework9 as an industry led initiative, while the main strength of the OGMP is the fact that it is a voluntary 
multi-stakeholder initiative, with governments, civil society organizations and industry amongst its 
membership. The European Commission currently co-chairs the OGMP steering group, with the aim to 
facilitate transparent and comparable global methane emission data. Deviations in the definitions of Article 2 
from agreed OGMP2.0 concepts should be fixed. The focus on 'direct measurement' at the source level is 
counterproductive. In our experience, the best available source-level quantification methodologies depend 
on the source type, configuration, and operation. In many cases, quantification using simulation tools, 
engineering calculations supported with process and equipment data will give more accurate emissions data  
over the reporting year than 'direct measurement'.  Article 12 should be adjusted to not hinder the use of the 
best available tools and technology so that high quality methane emission reporting can be safeguarded over 
time. It is however useful to complement source-based quantification methods with site-level measurements

Equinor highly appreciates the inclusive approach by the European Commission. The proposed methane regulation will

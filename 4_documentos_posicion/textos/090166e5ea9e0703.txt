EEB response to the public consultation: Sustainable consumption of 
goods - promoting repair and reuse 
4th April 2022 
General comments  
The European Environmental Bureau welcomes the initiative “sustainable consumption of goods - 
promoting repair and reuse”. We see that establishing a genuine Right to Repair for consumer 
products will not be achieved through a single piece of legislation but will rather depend on a policy 
mix of different instruments covering product, consumer, competition, standardisation etc. 
 
Until the recent launch of the “Empowering the Consumer initiative” legislative work on Right to 
Repair was exclusively found in ecodesign requirements for energy using products and some criteria 
in voluntary initiatives (such as the EU ecolabel and GPP criteria). Taking a product-by-product 
approach can deliver a necessarily high level of precision for specific product groups but can take 
time and resources to develop and implement. Horizontal measures applied to manufacturers or 
vendors have an advantage in that they can be applied to any product on the EU market. This said, 
existing consumer legislation does not effectively maximise the potential of repair to reduce resource 
use and GHG emissions, or save consumer expenditure, as product longevity has not previously been 
explored in this area of law in a comprehensive manner. Consumer rights are not yet aligned with the 
expected lifetime of products. Consumer legislation also tends to prioritise replacement over repair 
during the warranty period, and market actors are under limited obligations to support repair or 
product life extension. Therefore, it is worthwhile strengthening EU Consumer law to enable potential 
savings from product lifetime extension.  
 
The major concern which we have with the policy options presented in this consultation is that they 
will most likely encourage manufacturers to “close” the repair market to independent actors and user 
repair. We believe strongly that in order for product life extension to remain effective, timely and 
affordable the repair market should be open and competitive. This means maximising access to a 
range of repair actors and options - i.e., ensuring open access to professional (affiliated and 
independent) repairers; social economy repair actors; repair cafes and community repairers; and user 
repairers. 
 
Looking beyond the measures tabled here, we also support the introduction of a general prohibition 
on practices which deliberately limit repairability by professionals and end users. The Commission 
should also consider how consumer legislation will apply to “product as a service” business models, 
which do not necessarily prioritise repair over replacement. Lastly, we encourage the Commission to 
ensure that online market places and fulfilment service providers are under the same obligations as 
vendors and manufacturers. 
 
Specific comments on the proposed measures: 
 
Option 1) Low intervention – voluntary commitments 
- Existing voluntary agreements in ecodesign have demonstrated low effectiveness compared to 
minimum requirements, therefore we do not support the voluntary agreements as a legitimate 
substitute for legal requirements. Voluntary initiatives may be supported where they encourage 
“best in class” performance which go beyond minimum requirements.

Sub-options 2A) Extend the legal guarantee period: (i) for new goods that consumers choose to repair 
instead of replacing them; and/or (ii) for second-hand and/or refurbished goods (amendments to the 
Directive). 
- We support an extension of the legal guarantee period for new products - this should be associated 
with an obligation to repair rather than replace where possible 
- We encourage the Commission to explore options to link the legal guarantee period to the expected 
product lifetime (e.g. as defined in preparatory Ecodesign studies) 
- We encourage the Commission to explore how remedies can also be provided by independent 
repair actors or social economy actors 
- At this stage we do not think there should be an obligation to include a legal guarantee for second 
hand goods or refurbished goods. Though, for some high value products it could be considered e.g. 
as half the legal guarantee period. 
 
Sub-options 2B) Make repair the preferred remedy when repair is less expensive than or as expensive 
as replacement (amendment to the Directive); oblige producers or sellers to repair goods beyond the 
legal guarantee period for a reasonable price (new right to repair within the Directive or a separate 
instrument).  
- Generally, repair should be the first remedy compared to replacement unless repair results in a 
higher environmental footprint. Repair should be offered as the main remedy unless repair is not 
possible or it results in a higher environmental footprint. 
- This measure should encourage manufacturers/vendors to sell longer lasting products. If we accept 
the cost of repair as a reason not to repair, this measure will have limited impact on the most 
“problematic” or poorly designed products. 
 
Option 3) High intervention:  
Sub-option 3A) Limit consumers’ choice of remedies by prioritising repair over replacement 
(amendment to the Directive); Oblige producers or sellers to repair goods beyond the legal guarantee 
period, in some cases for free (new right to repair within the Directive or a separate instrument).  
- Generally, repair should be the first remedy compared to replacement unless repair results in a 
higher environmental footprint. Repair should be offered as the main remedy unless repair is not 
possible or it results in a higher environmental footprint. 
- Repair should be encouraged beyond the legal guarantee period.  
- The cost of repair outside of the legal guarantee period should be minimised by fostering an open 
repair market involving a wide range of repair actors (independent actors, repair cafes, self repair) 
- Overall this policy option needs to be more detailed to understand its implications. 
 
Sub-option 3B) Extend the legal guarantee period beyond the current minimum period of 2 years 
(amendment to the Directive).  
- We support an extension of the legal guarantee period for new products - this should be associated 
with an obligation to repair rather than replace where possible 
- We encourage the Commission to explore options to link the legal guarantee period to the expected 
product lifetime (e.g. as defined in preparatory Ecodesign studies) 
- We encourage the Commission to explore how remedies can also be provided by independent 
repair actors  
 
Sub-option 3C) Enable the seller to replace defective products with refurbished goods and not new 
ones (amendment to the Directive). In addition, the Commission welcomes views on the need for, and

Question 1 YES 
Correct and complete company information from reliable business registers is essential 
for doing business with companies. Business partners and other third parties use the 
information disclosed in business registers on a day -to-day basis as a tool to make 
important decisions in transactions where often large sums and other important interests 
are at stake. Only if correct and complete company information is easily accessible 
through reliable business registers, third parties can safely enter into agreements with 
companies and subsequently enforce claims against the company itself or individual 
shareholders. This is essential for the functioning of a modern economy. It is also 
particularly essential for the increasing number of cross -border transactions and 
procedures and thus the functioning of the single market. 
 
Therefore, we strongly believe that it is important “ to have more harmonised company 
information available at EU level”. We would like to emphasise that harmonising the 
means for disclosure of information through BRIS, does not necessarily harmonise the 
information itself. In our view , more harmoni sed structured information  is crucial for  
increasing the value and adoption of BRIS.

Question 6   
YES, with the EU interconnection of beneficial ownership registers  
 
We believe that adding links to the registers of beneficial owners in the E -Justice portal 
would be good from a practical point of view as this would be in line with the once-only-
principle. That way, certain data to be published in both registers can be automatically 
imported from a business register to a beneficial ownership register. The relevant 
information would be filed with the business register where the information can be 
reliably ver ified by register courts and/or notaries and would then be automatically 
imported to the beneficial owner registers. This would reduce the administrative burden 
for companies and obliged entities , increase reliability of the disclosed information  and 
prevent conflicting data entries.  Consequently, simple access to company related 
information should be provided by linking BRIS with the EU interconnection of 
beneficial ownership registers.  
 
No, with the EU interconnection of land registers 
 
Land registers disclose information on immovable property and the related rights and 
encumbrances. Business registers disclose information on a legal entity. The subject 
matter of both registers and the information disclosed in them differs fundamentally. 
Given these differences, there should not be an interconnection between them. Mixing 
the information available in different registers may undermine their specific functions. 
We also note that in some Member States  access to registers is rightly restricted for 
privacy reasons and access is only granted on the basis of a justified interest. BRIS must 
not be a way to circumvent this.

Question 18  
Lack of common rules for the verification of company data before it is entered in a 
business register; different content, format or presentation of company documents or 
information from a register in another Member State 
 
On the lack of common rules for the verification of company data: 
It is important to remove unjustified barriers and difficulties to the use of company 
information in cross -border transactions and procedures. Unnecessary administrative 
burden and costs for citizens and b usinesses must be avoided. Business registers, 
however, can only fulfil their essential function of providing transparency and legal 
certainty for business transactions if the company information entered in reliable business 
registers is complete, correct and easily accessible. Only then can businesses and other 
third parties safely and cost -effectively rely on that information, thereby reducing 
administrative burden, costs and time spent, also in a cross-border context. 
 
The information in business registers is only correct and reliable if register courts and/or 
notaries as public officials check the legal validity of corporate transactions and decisions, 
verify the relevant company data and securely identify the parties and applicants. This 
system o f preventive control is the prerequisite for the public’s reliance on business 
registers and their publicity effects (public faith). This must particularly apply in a cross-
border context when company data from one Member State shall be used in another 
Member State. If business registers in a Member State lacked such preventive control and  
 
 
 
the information in such deficient business register were required to be used by public 
registers in another Member State, the deficiencies of that Member State’s business  
 
register could then spread to other public registers of other Member States. There would 
thus be the risk that only one Member State’s deficient business register threatens the 
reliability of other Member States’ public registers. In such case, Member States would  
 
lose control over the data in their own public registers. This also has a constitutional 
dimension: because of incorrect company data in business registers and their publicity 
effect (public faith), legitimate property rights could be lost becaus e of the actions of 
unauthorized third parties. In view of the state's duty of care to protect the fundamental 
right to property, such a restriction on ownership based on the legal effects of public 
registers is justified only if the state provides for spe cial precautions to ensure the 
accuracy of the registers. This is the only way that the state can fulfill its duty of care to 
protect property in accordance with national constitutional law and Article 17 of the EU 
Charter of Fundamental Rights. 
 
For these reasons, company information from one Member State’s business register could 
only be used in another Member State if that Member State provides for a high standard 
of preventive control of company data as described above.  
 
It should be up to the Member S tates to decide whether the standard of input control in 
another Member State satisfies its verification standards, i.e. whether it can be deemed 
equivalent to an own standard of input control. This is absolutely necessary to enable the 
Member States to pr otect the reliability of their public registers. On the one hand, 
common minimum standards for input control would threaten the reliability of business 
registers. On the other hand, very high common verification standards would threaten the 
legal systems of Member States that traditionally have lower standards of input control. 
However, the different legal traditions and systems of the Member States must be 
respected. Member States should have discretion as to whether and how to design a 
system of preventive control that is in line with their legal traditions. 
 
We note that unreliable public registers with incorrect and incomplete company 
information facilitate illegal activities such as money laundering or illegal employment 
practices. Therefore, unreliable public registers may endanger the business location of a 
country as a whole. 
 
On the different content etc. of company information: 
As outlined in the answer to Question 3, practical hindrances to the cross -border use are 
the different content, format and presentation of company documents and information in 
business registers. The effectiveness of BRIS would increase if company extracts showed  
 
 
the same relevant information in a common format. The information mandatorily to be 
disclosed should include, inte r alia, the power of representation of the person acting on 
behalf of the company. We refer to the answer to Question 3.

Question 20 NO 
Given that t he rules on the verifications on the company data before it is entered in 
business register s are not harmonised,  we believe that the information contained in  
business registers can hardly be used in dealing with authorities and court proceedings in 
another Member State . Obviously, Member State s may still consider the verifications 
carried out abroad somewhat equivalent to th ose existing in their own jurisdictions. 
However, at least in some instances it already is the case that notaries in one Member 
State can use company information from another Member State’s busin ess register 
thereby offering an efficient and legally secure way of facilitating the cross-border use of 
company information. 
 
If, for instance, a company from one Member State wants to form a company or acquire 
real estate in another Member State, the notary will use the company data from the 
incorporating/acquiring company’s business register in order to ascertain that the 
incorporating/acquiring company validly exists. However, basic company data from 
business registers in other Member States should only be usable if the other Member State 
has a high standard of preventive input control that the notary deems equivalent to the 
standard in the own Member State (cf. answer to Question 18).  
 
This example shows that , at least for basic information,  if the Member States’ business 
registers are reliable and the company information therein correct, complete and 
accessible, the involveme nt of notaries ensures a quick and cost -effective tool to use 
company information from another Member State. At the same time, the notary using the 
company information vis -à-vis the public registers performs the required strict input 
control, which is the prerequisite for the important comprehensive publicity effects of 
public registers (public faith).

Question 22 b. Providing for recognition of electronic certified copies. 
 
 
With regard to our answer ‘providing for recognition of electronic certified copies’, we 
would like to stress that these copies should be digitally certified by public authorities, 
and that the simple use of digital tools, like electronic signatures or the use of the BRIS,  
 
will not be sufficient. Otherwise, the reliability of public registers and the correctness of 
the company information is at risk. 
 
In order to protect the reliability of public registers and the correctness of the company 
information, a race to the bottom because of “common minimum” rules for the  
 
verification of the correctness of company information must be avoided at all cost. As 
explained in more detail in the answer to Question 18, a high standard of preventive 
control must ensure the correctness of company information. Otherwise, the reliability of 
business registers is at risk, particularly in connection with the cross -border use of 
company data. It must be within the discretion of each Member State’s national law to 
decide on whether  the standard of input control for company information in another 
Member State’s business register is equivalent to the own Member State’s public 
registers.

Question 24 NO 
 
We believe that the policies implied by questions 23-24 seriously undermine (and do not 
simply upgrade) the acquis on digital company law. In fact, the procedures envisaged for 
creating branches and companies would be incompatible with directive 2017/1132 , as 
modified by directive 2019/1151. In any case, we note that for both (i) subsidiaries in the 
form of a limited liability company and (ii) branches directive 2019/1151 contains 
comprehensive provisions on their online formation/registration and the online filing of 
documents and information. There is no objective need for further specific provisions on 
the cross-border use of company data for expanding to markets in other Member States 
by way of setting up subsidiaries or branches. 
 
The solutions adopted in directive 2019/1151, which was enacted after lengthy debates, 
still represent the likely outcome of the democratic political process within the European 
Union. In contrast, the cited ‘Declaration on the EU Startup Nations Standard of 
Excellence’ is not relevant in this regard because it  does not make any reference to 
branches and, with respect to subsidiaries, is too abstract to be of any relevant use.  
 
 
 
Question 25 NO  
 
At this point in time, many Member States are still in the process of implementing the 
Digitalisation Directive – both legally and technically. In some Member States, the new 
online procedures will not be offered to the public before 1 August 2022. This means that 
the new online procedures have not yet been comprehensively tested in practice in all 
Member States. It is also unclear how the public will accept these new procedures, how  
 
well-suited they are for important legal transactions and procedures and there thus is no 
experience on whether these procedures should be expanded to other areas of company 
law. The Digitalisation Directive itself expressly acknowledges this by providing for an 
evaluation period until 1 August 2024 and 1 August 2025, respectively. Before the 
conclusion of a comprehensive evaluation of the new digital tools, the EU legislator 
should not mandatorily introduce new online procedures or procedural steps in company  
 
law. Of course, this does not mean, however, that the national legislator may not decide 
on further digitalization steps in company law. 
 
We would like to emphasize that new online procedu res should only be introduced if 
effective preventive legal control by intermediaries such as notaries can ensure the 
following:  
 
- the legal validity of company law transactions and procedures; 
- the provision of correct, comprehensive and individual legal advice to the parties; 
and 
- the reliable identification of the parties pursuant to the highest identification 
standards.  
 
This is the only way to guarantee the reliability of business registers and the correctness 
and completeness of the company information  entered therein. The involvement of 
intermediaries such as notaries is also particularly important to guarantee legal certainty 
and prevent the misuse of company law for illegal purposes. This has been acknowledged 
by the EU legislator in the Digitalisation Directive. 
 
We would like to further emphasize the absolute necessity that any online procedures 
must provide electronic identification standards of the highest security. From the point of 
view of the state and society, it would be unacceptable that illegal activities such as money 
laundering and terrorist financing could be facilitated again by insecure online 
identification procedures, particularly after considerable efforts are currently being made 
and have been made in recent years to prevent these i llegal activities. Companies must 
not be formed in online procedures with insufficient identification standards, especially 
not from parties residing in foreign countries. Otherwise, these companies would be 
entered in business registers, thereby opening the door to money laundering activities and  
 
 
also jeopardizing the publicity of the business registers with severe effects on third parties 
and the public in general.   
 
 
Question 26 NO 
CNUE believes that the decision on whether or not to open the online procedure to other 
types of companies should remain strictly a matter for the Member States at national level. 
If, after more than 50 years, the Commission intends to change this approach, we believe 
that there should be other aspects of the rules applicable to companies other than limited  
 
liability companies, that should deserve to be harmonised before  those on the online 
formation, in particular the power to represent companies and the rules on disclosure. In 
fact, for limited liability companies, t hese rules were introduced only recently in EU 
company law , and this happened only after more relevant aspects of the legislation 
applicable to these companies were harmonised. 
 
 
 
Question 27 
The concept of virtual registered office is a novelty in company law. For example, it could 
be a certified email address to which documents can be served, a connecting element for 
private international law purposes, or a virtual place of business. 
What appears important is the coordination of this concept with other strictly related ideas 
like, e.g. the registered office, the place of management, the principal establishment, or 
the statutory seat. It is therefore difficult to provide any feedback on this part of the 
consultation, in lack of knowing what a virtual office could be in practice.  
 
However, when assessing the need for legislative action on virtual registered offices, the 
legislators should in any case ensure that a “virtual registered office” must not be a tool 
for businesses to engage in forum shopping as regards the legal form of companies. It 
thus must not enable businesses to circumvent Union and national laws particularly on (i) 
employees’ rights and rights of participation; (ii) social legislation and tax laws; (iii) anti- 
money laundering rules (iv) creditor protection laws and insolvency regimes; as well as 
(v) rights of minority shareholders. In line with the discussion on letterbox companies, it 
is clear that a virtual registered office must particularly not lea d to the recognition of 
corporations governed by non -EU laws. This in turn would lead to a complete loss of 
control by the EU legislator and both EU and national agencies: Compliance with EU 
laws would be at the discretion of shareholders. This would open the door to increased 
risks of illegal activities, money laundering or other dubious business practices. Even 
within the EU, company law standards diverge substantially in important areas such as 
the ones listed above from (i) to (iv). The concept of a “virtual registered office” must not 
permit the circumvention of these important laws. Member States should therefore not be 
required to recognize purely virtual companies with no real economic link to that Member 
State. The EU legislator has wide leeway to prevent such practices.

Question 33  
We appreciate the work the Commission is doing on the possible upgrading of digital 
Company Law and in these final comments , we wish to provide more detailed 
explanations of the answers we gave during the consultation. Except for the issues 
included in the fourth part of the survey, we the questions asked suggest that the scope of 
the exchange of information via the BRIS should be expanded. Instead, the primary goal 
of any reforms should be making BRIS a useful instrument for companies, public 
authorities, professionals, and, more generally, the public, before expanding the ambit of 
its activities.  
 
As it is now, the BRIS still  has room for improvement when it comes to accessing 
information on businesses across borders. Moreover, the directives and regulations 
governing the BRIS are not fully implemented by the Member States (even those 
countries that have already implemented di rective 2019/1151). For example, although 
directive 2019/1151 requires Member States to make available some information for free, 
it only takes a search on the e -Justice Portal ‘Find a company’ to see that the BRIS still 
has gaps in delivering such data.  
 
The majority of Member States attach legal value to the information available in business 
registers, and the legal value of this information is essential to carry out transactions, 
which might have counter-values of millions of euros, without asking legal opinions and 
costly advice. Having efficient rules on company disclosure is fundamental to ensure the 
smooth running of the internal market. Any new legislation must not negatively affect  
public faith in the information stored in business registers.  
 
 
As the First Council Directive 68/151/EEC said  – and directive 2017/1132 still echoes 
today – ‘ the basic documents of the company should be disclosed in order that third 
parties may be able to ascertain their contents and other information concerning the 
company, especially particulars of the persons who are authorised to bind the company’. 
We believe that it should be the essence of any legislation governing the disclosure of 
company information, and therefore of any legislation aimed at strengthening the 
interconnection of business registers across Member States.

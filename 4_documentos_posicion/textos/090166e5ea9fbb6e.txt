The Sporting goods industry welcomes the opportunity to comment on the ‘Right To Repair initiative’. Our 
sector is building momentum to support the objectives of the EU Green Deal and has developed ambitious 
circular economy programs by improving new busin ess models or investing in existing models including 
rental, resell and repairing.1 
FESI’s members have been front runners when it comes to developing or trialing new ways of adding value 
to their products and services, and many have put forward innovativ e commitments and programmes to 
deliver safe and quality products and services in a sustainable way. 
FESI’s members empower consumers to take part in the circular economy through numerous strategies, 
including providing information on the sustainability of  their products and services throughout their entire

Executive summary : 
• The sporting goods industry is a front runner when it comes to sustainability and innovation.  
• The sporting goods industry covers a broad range of very diverse products and not all these 
products can be made easily repairable.  
• The Right to Repair should provide specific criteria for various product categories.  
• A thorough life cycle assessment should be made to better understand if reparability is the 
best option for every product category.  
• Right to repair should address issues around responsibility when it comes to product 
functionality, safety, quality, performance, liability, and intellectual property rights  
• There should be more incentives and support  for repair and refurbishment operations and 
business models.  
• Spare parts, repair instructions s hould be made available only if this does not compromise 
safety or quality, nor intellectual property rights. 
• Right to Repair requirements should be harmonized across the entire European Union and 
products should be able to be sent to designated repair fac ilities anywhere in the EU if the 
repairs are done by the manufacturer or licensed representative.

products. However, keeping in mind a “Life Cycle Approach”, it is also necessary to calculate what are the 
environmental tradeoffs for collecting and transporting various type of products back into the system and 
what would be the most appropriate and sustainable way of organizing such programs. In some cases, based 
on the durability and longevity of a product, it could be more sustainable to provide products a second life 
or to put them into a secondhand program. Therefore, FESI believes there is not a one-size-fits all approach 
to a “right to repair”. Product specific criteria should be developed for products depending on product value 
and life expectancy. For example, an outdoor jacket or a ski boot will not be comparable to a mono material 
t-shirt and vice versa.  
In our opinion it is important to protect consumers by defining the ‘Right to Repai r’ as consumer access to 
high quality, high performance, safe and secure repair options for specific product categories, recognizing 
the importance of scalable, sustainable, and cost-efficient repair schemes and supporting the development 
of new business models especially by supporting and facilitating repair and refurbishment professionals and 
networks.  
The sporting goods and outdoor industry cover a broad range of product categories such as hardgoods 
(accessories, equipment, PPE) and soft goods (clothing and footwear). Some of those are easier to repair than 
others. And some of them have longer durability and therefore, there is a higher value in repairing them. 
While soft goods such as clothing and footwear might be more inclined to be collected for re-use or recycling 
depending on their state, other products such as hardgoods are more inclined to be repaired, leased, or 
rented such as skis, ski boots, ski bindings, high end outdoor products and Personal Protective Equipment 2. 
However, it needs to be observed that such products are under strict safety certification rules which must be 
enforced and cannot be compromised.  While we do not think that sporting goods are a high priority sector 
for mandated reparability, some of our members have already put in place ambitious programs on extending 
the durability of their products, promoting repair where possible or valuable, supporting refurbishment and 
remanufacturing activities as part of their services.3  
Consumers should have access to high quality , safe and secure repair options when the product category 
criteria allow it. However, this does not mean that repairs can be carried out safely and successfully by 
consumers themselves or independent repairers. Especially for high -complexity products, or Personal 
Protective Equipment consumer-led repairs could impact the safety integrity of the repaired products, and 
such unintended outcomes should be reflected in any future regulation. 
In addition to this, when designing ‘Right to Repair’,  consideration s hould be given that repairs that 
jeopardise the quality or safety of a product not only endanger persons and property but may have legal 
liability and brand implications for manufacturers. Manufacturers should not be held liable for mistakes 
consumers make when repairing their products. Similarly, products repaired by unlicensed individuals should 
not fall under an original manufacturer warrantee if resold, as the original manufacturer can no longer 
guarantee the product quality. The Product Liability Directive’s recent revision (2018/246) questions who will 
be the manufacturer in case of repair and refurbishment. Other considerations include confidential 
proprietary knowledge and intellectual property rights. The obligation should also be designed in a way as

not to create unnecessary burdens on the manufacturer. The existing ‘Right to Repair’ provision in the Waste 
Framework Directive  for example details a reasonable approach, as it lays out that spare parts, repair 
instructions, etc. should be made available only if this does not compromise safety or quality, nor intellectual 
property rights (IPRs). These three boundary conditions of accepted European law must be reflected in the 
future’ Right to Repair’ or reparability mandate proposals. 
Furthermore, for environmental reasons and accessibility we believer Right to Repair requirements should 
be harmonized across the entire European Union and products should be able to be sent to designated repair 
facilities anywhere in the EU if the repairs are done by the manufacturer or licensed representative. 
For many products, manufacturers have supported a balance between a design for consumer -replaceable, 
or recycler-removable (replaceability by professional, trained repair operators) components. These business 
models should be incentivized and supported.  
For all the reasons above we support the “Right to Repair” provided specific criteria are developed for various 
product categories. A thorough life cycle assessment should be made to better understand if reparability is 
the best option for every product. In addition, liability and warranty implications should be defined and 
looked at in case third party repairers intervene.

About FESI 
Founded in 1960 FESI - the Federation of the European Sporting Goods Industry 
represents the interests of approximately 1.800 sporting goods manufacturers (85% of 
the European market) through its National Sporting Goods Industry Federations and its 
directly affiliated member companies. 70-75% of FESI's membership is made up of Small 
and Medium Sized Enterprises. In total, the European Sporting Goods Industry employs 
over 700.000 EU citizens and has an annual turnover of some 81 billion euro.

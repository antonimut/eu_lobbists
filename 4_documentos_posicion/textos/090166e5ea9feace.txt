 Swappie’s feedback on the Right to Repair 
 At  Swappie  we  are  on  a  mission  to  make  refurbished  electronics  mainstream  by  ensuring 
 consumers  buy  smart,  not  new.  However  for  consumer  devices  to  last  longer,  they  need  to  be 
 more repairable. 
 Swappie  welcomes  the  European  Commission’s  ambition  to  directly  address  the  right  to  repair 
 beyond  the  existing  legislative  framework.  Repair  and  refurbishment  are  essential  contributors 
 to  the  circular  economy;  however,  the  rules  as  they  stand  today  are  far  from  being  enough.  We 
 need  to  have  a  more  comprehensive  and  systematic  approach  by  targeting  all  stages  of  the 
 product lifetime: from design and manufacturing to its use phase and end-of-life. 
 Repair  and  refurbishment  play  a  critical  role  in  the  reduction  of  the  EU’s  environmental  and 
 carbon  footprint.  They  enable  the  reuse  of  products,  consequently  prolonging  their  useful 
 lifetime,  as  well  as  increasing  resource  efficiency  and  savings.  The  refurbishment  of  products 
 enables  the  creation  of  secondary  loops  for  devices  that  would  otherwise  become  waste  (and 
 most  likely  be  destined  for  the  landfill).  It  further  allows  for  the  recovery  of  critical  parts  and 
 materials,  such  as  batteries  and  cameras,  that  are  still  functional  and  can  be  reused, 
 preventing  unnecessary  mining,  manufacturing  and  the  generation  of  waste.  Ultimately, 
 ensuring  that  materials  are  recovered  and  reused  contributes  to  the  reduction  of  the  pressure 
 on production. 
 In  order  to  achieve  an  effective  EU  framework  for  the  Right  to  Repair,  we  call  on  the  European 
 Commission to: 
 ●  Have  a  holistic  approach  to  avoid  loopholes  and  ensure  consistency  across  all  EU 
 initiatives  being  adopted  that  play  a  role  in  the  achievement  of  the  EU  Right  to  Repair.  We 
 believe  that  in  order  to  achieve  an  ambitious  Right  to  Repair,  the  legislator  needs  to 
 consider  all  stages  of  a  device:  from  product  design  requirements  to  the  product  use  phase 
 until its end-of-life phase. 
 ●  Ensure  full  harmonisation  of  the  framework  on  the  right  to  repair  ,  to  ensure  legal 
 certainty  for  businesses  active  in  multiple  markets,  and  for  consumers  to  benefit  from  the 
 same level of protection and rights across the EU Member States. 
 Comments on legislative options in the Call for Evidence 
 We  welcome  the  opportunity  to  provide  feedback  on  some  of  the  legislative  options  raised  by 
 the  European  Commission’s  call  of  evidence.  We  understand  this  initiative  focuses  specifically 
 on consumer rights and the after-sales of products. 
 ●  Option  1:  Voluntary  agreements  -  Based  on  our  assessment  of  the  existing  agreements 
 for  certain  product  categories  (in  particular  printers)  we  do  not  believe  that  voluntary 
 agreements  should  continue  to  be  the  EU’s  main  line  of  action.  They  can  work  at  a  very 
 early  stage  when  there  is  no  legislative  framework  in  place,  however  in  this  particular 
 instance,  we  strongly  believe  that  voluntary  agreements  have  shown  their  limitations. 
 The  EU  needs  to  be  far  more  ambitious  and  mandatory  regulatory  requirements  would 
 be  in  our  opinion  the  only  path  to  promote  repair  and  reuse  across  all  stages  of  a 
 device’s lifetime. The European Commission should discard this policy option entirely.

 ●  Options  2A:  Extending  warranty  for  repaired  and  refurbished  goods  -  The  European 
 Commission  has  proposed  to  amend  the  Sales  of  Goods  Directive  (SGD)  and  extend 
 legal  guarantee  periods  for  a)  new  goods  that  are  repaired  instead  of  replaced  and/or 
 b) for second-hand and/or refurbished goods. 
 ○  First,  we  strongly  believe  that  the  SGD  should  be  amended  in  order  to  promote 
 repair  over  replacement,  as  a  matter  of  principle,  regardless  of  the  costs  of 
 repair.  By  limiting  consumers’  ability  to  request  automatic  replacement  we  will 
 promote  more  sustainable  consumption  patterns  and  ensure  devices  are  used 
 longer.  The  price  of  repairs  will  very  rarely  exceed  the  price  of  a  new  device  and 
 in  those  fringe  cases,  when  they  might,  an  amended  hierarchy  of  remedies 
 would probably incentivise the markets to bring the prices down. 
 ○  We  also  believe  that  different  legal  regimes  should  apply  to  new  and  used/ 
 refurbished  devices.  It  is  very  important  to  clarify  here  the  definition  of 
 refurbishment.  Refurbishment  is  a  standalone  process,  very  different  from 
 manufacturing  and  remanufacturing,  which  is  limited  to  testing  the 
 functionalities  of  a  used  good  to  ensure  it  still  performs  its  main  functionalities. 
 Repairs  are  not  automatic  and  contrary  to  other  industrial  processes  such  as 
 manufacturing,  refurbishing  will  never  modify  a  good  or  affect  its  functionalities 
 or  purpose  as  originally  intended  by  the  original  manufacturer.  The  devices  can 
 carry  some  physical  defects  and  are  not  intended  to  perform  to  an  equivalent 
 standard  as  a  new  device  would.  For  example,  smartphone  batteries  will  not 
 automatically  be  changed;  as  long  as  the  battery  is  in  a  sufficient  health 
 condition  to  sustain  a  functional  charge  (80%  or  above)  it  will  be  considered 
 good  for  resale.  This  is  why  refurbishers  rely  on  a  system  of  grading  to  provide 
 accurate  information  to  consumers  on  the  expected  performance  and  esthetics 
 of  a  device.  In  this  respect,  refurbishers  act  as  resellers  extending  the  lifetime  of 
 used  devices  and  it  would  be  very  difficult  for  us  to  provide  a  warranty 
 equivalent  in  length  to  the  one  used  for  new  devices.  In  addition,  the  quality  of 
 refurbishing  varies  highly  across  Europe  and  imposing  too  long  legal  warranties 
 on  second-hand  devices  could  be  economically  disproportionate  on  small 
 operators, preventing them from reaching economic viability. 
 ●  Option  2B:  Requiring  manufacturers  and  sellers  to  repair  goods  at  reasonable  price 
 beyond  warranty  period  -  This  option  would  only  work  if  the  hierarchy  of  remedies 
 (available  in  the  SGD)  is  amended  in  order  to  prioritise  repair  over  replacement.  This 
 should  be  the  de  facto  standard  even  beyond  the  expiry  of  the  legal  warranty  period  but 
 also at a fair price for the manufacturer or the (re)seller. 
 ●  Option  3B  -  Extending  the  legal  warranty  period  beyond  2  years  -  We  do  not  believe 
 this  particular  option  will  yield  any  particular  benefit  for  consumers.  To  have  any  impact, 
 legal  warranties  should  first  be  harmonised  across  all  Member  States.  Legal  warranties 
 should  also  differ  by  product  categories:  small  and  larger  consumer  appliances  should 
 not be subject to the same warranty periods. 
 ●  Option  3C  -  Enabling  sellers  to  replace  defective  devices  by  refurbished  ones  -  As 
 refurbishers  ourselves  we  are  obviously  very  sensitive  to  ideas  that  would  promote 
 refurbished  products  in  the  eyes  of  consumers.  For  consumption  patterns  to  change  in

 the  future,  we  need  to  find  ways  to  promote  the  value  of  buying  and  using  refurbished 
 goods.  However  we  need  to  clarify  that  refurbished  devices  are  in  no  way  equivalent  to 
 new.  As  a  matter  of  fact,  some  Member  States  such  as  France  have  recently  banned 
 the  use  of  “like  new”  or  “equivalent  to  new”  when  referring  to  refurbished.  We  could 
 support  this  policy  option  if  the  legislator  would  make  it  optional,  i.e.  leaving  it  up  to 
 consumers  to  make  an  active  choice  and  choose  a  refurb  over  replacement,  but  never 
 should  it  be  mandatory.  Forcing  a  replacement  with  a  refurbished  device  might  have 
 the  opposite  effect  on  consumer  trust,  especially  if  the  quality  of  refurb  is  not 
 harmonised across the board. 
 Additional comments and suggestions 
 In  order  to  achieve  an  ambitious  “right  to  repair”  we  believe  the  European  Commission  should 
 also  consider  the  following  ideas  to  ensure  that  products  can  be  repairable  throughout  their 
 useful lifetime, beyond first and second ownership: 
 ●  An  ambitious  and  detailed  overarching  product  design  and  disassembly  requirements, 
 with  a  product  by  product  approach.  We  strongly  support  the  recent  proposal  on 
 ecodesign  for  sustainable  products  and  look  forward  to  the  Commission  issuing 
 ambitious regulatory requirements applicable to smartphones in the coming months. 
 ●  Ensure  access  to  spare  parts,  repair  information  and  maintenance  manuals  at  a 
 reasonable  price  for  all  interested  parties,  for  a  minimum  of  10  years  from  the  moment 
 a good was first made available on the market. 
 ●  Harmonise  legal  warranty  periods  across  Europe  in  order  to  promote  business 
 predictability,  especially  for  smaller  operators.  Should  the  legislator  consider  extending 
 the  warranty  period  when  repairs  have  been  made,  we  recommend  that  the  extension 
 apply  to  the  device  in  its  entirety  and  be  limited  to  an  additional  6  months  maximum. 
 Attaching  the  warranty  period  to  each  part  would  prove  to  be  very  complicated  from  an 
 enforcement  and  traceability  point  of  view,  and  open  up  the  door  to  consumer  abuse. 
 This  is  why  we  support  the  idea  of  a  digital  product  passport  in  order  to  keep  a  record 
 of  all  repairs  and  other  types  of  servicing  to  a  device.  This  would  be  most  useful  should 
 the  device  change  ownership  along  the  way,  and  improve  traceability  of  components 
 and improve safety for users. 
 ●  Prohibit  techniques  that  limit  third  party’s  ability  to  perform  repairs  outside  of  authorised 
 networks  most  notably  software  pairing  and  component  serialisation  that  have  the 
 effect  of  uniquely  linking  parts  to  a  device.  This  is  already  happening  for  batteries  and 
 cameras  and  has  the  de  facto  effect  of  preventing  third  party  independent  repairs  and 
 promoting OEM’s monopoly over a subset of specific repairs. 
 ●  Ensure  that  if  software  is  required  to  make  repairs  -  for  safety  reasons  for  example  -  it 
 should  be  made  available  like  any  other  tool  or  instructions,  at  minimum  against  fair 
 market price. 
 ●  Allow  the  use  of  compatible  generic  parts,  and  more  generally  promote  reuse  of  pulled 
 originals at minimum.

 ○  Legislation  should  be  harmonised  across  all  Member  States  in  order  to  explicitly 
 allow  the  use  of  pulled  originals  or  refurbished  original  parts  (from  donor 
 devices)  for  repairs.  This  however  can  only  be  achieved  by  preventing  OEM 
 from using software to serialise parts (see point above about software locks). 
 ○  The  EU  could  even  go  a  step  further  by  opening  up  access  to  component 
 specifications  to  allow  third  parties  to  manufacture  generic  compatible  safe 
 parts. 
 ●  Incentivize  consumers  to  adopt  a  more  circular  consumption  lifestyle  and  EU 
 businesses  to  provide  repair  services.  To  this  end,  the  European  Commission  should 
 consider  encouraging  consumers  to  repair  through  the  use  of  buy-back  /  trade  in 
 bonuses,  as  well  as  develop  together  with  the  Member  States  financial  incentives  for 
 repair services to make repair convenient and attractive to consumers.

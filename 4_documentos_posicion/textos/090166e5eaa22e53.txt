Response of ACT | The App Association to the European Commission’s call for evidence for an impact assessment on a legislative initiative ‘Sustainable consumption of goods – promoting repair and reuse’  I/ Introduction ACT | The App Association is a not-for-profit trade association that represents over 5,000 small and medium-sized application developers and connected device companies located across the European Union and around the globe. Alongside the rapid adoption of mobile technologies, our members develop innovative applications and products that improve workplace productivity, accelerate academic achievement, monitor health, and support the transition towards a digital and sustainable global economy. Today, the global app ecosystem is worth more than €1.5 trillion, is responsible for over one million European jobs and serves as a key driver of the €7 trillion internet of things (IoT) revolution.   While fully supportive of an ambitious approach to increasing the sustainability of goods and promoting repair and reuse, we have concerns regarding options two and three the Commission proposes in its call for evidence. Especially in the context of electronic devices, extending the legal guarantee period for new goods and/or second-hand/refurbished goods or obliging producers/sellers to repair goods beyond the legal guarantee period for a reasonable price goes beyond the issue of reducing the environmental footprint of goods. In fact, such actions may have far-ranging negative consequences on the software ecosystem and the app economy, which the App Association represents. Policymakers need to address the digital and green transitions jointly to consider their respective challenges holistically. We encourage European decision-makers to future-proof legislation by considering the two transitions as inseparable twins.  II/ Political context and problems the initiative aims to tackle The App Association fully agrees with the European Commission that the unsustainable consumption of goods and growing amount of waste cause negative impacts on the global environment. As such, both consumers and businesses should be incentivised and able to easily make sustainable choices. While we agree with the Council that the initiative on the ‘right to repair’ could be a tool to promote more systematic repairs beyond the liability period and at a reasonable cost, we believe it is essential to establish several safeguards to ensure this initiative doesn’t cause more harm than good.   Giving businesses more resources, information, and other incentives to produce more sustainably as well as informing consumers on how to make environmentally-friendly choices is an important problem to address within this initiative. Nonetheless, how long to use a purchased good isn’t always fully up to the consumer as it may sometimes be reasonable to replace a product rather than prolong its lifecycle. Particularly concerning high tech products, devices and standards evolve, and software needs to be updated and maintained because the environment changes. At some point there aren’t enough users to justify these updates and a device becomes obsolete. In those cases, continuing to use a product or refurbishing it may have negative impacts on the consumer’s security, in terms of physical safety or data privacy, especially in the context of mobile devices. Often, when consumers choose between having the seller repair or replace products, replacing the good is the rational choice for them for reasons other than dissuasive repair costs or expired liability period. They simply prefer having a newer product that includes the newest technology.

The App Association applauds the initiative’s goal to tackle these problems through consumer-related/relevant measures to extend the useful life of goods, but we emphasise the notion of ‘useful’ and urge the Commission to pay attention to this when it drafts its amendment to the Sale of Goods Directive. As always, we welcome the ambition to avoid diverging national mandatory consumer rights to ease and harmonise compliance requirements for all EU businesses. We agree that coordinated intervention at the EU level is the best tool to contribute effectively to the continued functioning of the internal market and create legal certainty for businesses that operate across Member State borders.  III/ Evaluating the policy options The App Association fully supports the Commission’s ambitious goal to encourage consumers to use goods for a longer time by repairing defective goods and by purchasing more second-hand and refurbished goods. However, when it comes to extending the life of mobile phones, cordless phones, and tablets, and promoting energy efficiency, we have strong concerns regarding the policy options the call for evidence document includes.   Besides the baseline of ‘no policy change’, all other policy options include some version of encouraging/mandating businesses to repair goods and promote the purchase of second-hand and refurbished goods. Option 1 is most likely the most favourable policy choice as it is the lowest level of intervention and only relies on a voluntary commitment from businesses. Option 2 would extend the legal guarantee period for new goods and mandate repair as the preferred remedy when a repair is less expensive. It would oblige producers or sellers to repair goods beyond the legal guarantee period for a reasonable price. Option 3 goes even further, limiting consumers’ choice of remedies by prioritising repair over replacement, obliging producers or sellers to repair goods beyond the legal guarantee period, in some cases for free and extending the legal guarantee period beyond the current minimum period of two years. Option 3 would also enable the seller to replace defective products with refurbished goods and not new ones.   While repairing or refurbishing hardware for mobile devices may be desirable and even useful, we strongly urge the Commission to clarify that the right to repair does not include any software features of a device. It is important to understand the difference between an upgrade and an update of the operating system. The main difference between an update and an upgrade is that an update keeps something current or makes it a better version of the older one. Updates often solve glitches or fix bugs, whereas an upgrade raises something to a higher standard by adding or replacing a few components. The Commission should specify that ‘repair’ or ‘refurbishment’ does not include the option for users to uninstall and/or downgrade to an older version of an operating system (OS) on their devices. Such ‘roll back’ would have far-ranging consequences on the software ecosystem and the broader app economy. We have concerns that consumers may misuse the right to repair for instances in which, for example, older devices upgrade to a newer OS and may lose some processing speed and as a result, consumers claim that a device manufacturer must undo the upgrade. We thus encourage the European Commission to clearly define which aspects of devices the ‘right to repair’ covers, i.e. whether it concerns exclusively hardware or whether it includes software. members in the context of security, cost, and fragmentation for developers, and innovation, as we explain in the remainder of this document. A. Security For a small and medium-sized enterprise (SME), a cyberattack can cause irreparable damage and the loss of reputation, constituting an existential threat to most of them. Therefore, SMEs must implement stringent cybersecurity to increase their resilience and preserve their existence. The continued ability of our SME members to mitigate serious and dynamic cyber threats is crucial to their livelihood.   As App Association members are mostly app developers, they rely on safe operating systems (OS) because the OS manages all the functionality on a smartphone or other electronic device. OS can, therefore, be vulnerable targets for hackers. Luckily, OS have many built-in functions that mitigate cybersecurity risks and enhance the security of end-users’ devices and thus help our members’ apps gain consumer trust easily.1 Malicious actors, however, use extreme social engineering techniques to reach their goals, lucrative or political. In its 2021 Mobile Threat Report, McAfee indicated that cybercriminals consolidated their efforts to focus on more dangerous (and profitable) targets, such as banking, billing fraud, and COVID-19 vaccines. Recent mobile malware has become more technically complex, featuring new ways to steal users’ banking credentials, as well as other personal data and circumventing security screening of application stores.2 Even the most informed users cannot always see when malware or a sideloaded application carries out other functions than it has indicated. Malicious apps, for example, can mine cryptocurrency, activate microphones and cameras, track keyboard strokes, or function as an entry point to other larger organisations’ IT networks, such as hospitals, often the target of ransomware. Operating system developers continue to track down these attacks and restrict the use and functionality of these apps to decrease opportunities for abuse.   In this changing environment where malware is on the rise, operating system updates and upgrades are essential to keep up and prevent constantly changing cyber threats. For instance, the infamous ‘Wannacry’ malware attack affected hundreds of thousands of devices in more than 150 countries in 2017. If users had updated their OS, they could have avoided this attack. 3  If the user has the option to downgrade to an older version of an OS as part of the ‘right to repair’ initiative, their device will become more vulnerable to cyber threats, and significantly increase the risks of being hacked. Any European legislation promoting the ‘retrograding’ or installation of older OS endangers the cybersecurity of electronic devices and puts consumers at risk. B. Cost and fragmentation A rule of thumb for developers is to always support the latest OS release and the one before that. The reason behind this is simple: it’s difficult to support too many OS upgrades. When comparing the adoption rates of the two most popular OS, the adoption rate of iOS updates is noticeably higher than that of Android updates, although upgrades on both OS are free. The number of malware instances is lower on devices with up-to-date OS.4 As soon as an iOS update is released, 20 per cent of users download it immediately, and 80-90 per cent do so within a year. This represents an advantage for small app developers in terms of testing since they have to decide which version to test their app on before releasing it. For developers, it’s vital to have well-functioning apps, especially if they are smaller companies with lesser-known brands that rely on the trust that the app ecosystem provides. One malfunctioning app update will likely lead to consumer disappointment, one-star ratings, and lower uptakes and may mean the end of operations for a small business.  Releasing an app or an update for an existing app is much harder to do well if developers have to test it on all prior versions of the OS and make it functional on all those previous versions. Testing on multiple prior versions, and, therefore, on multiple devices (previous OS, plus all the .dot revisions) adds up to hundreds of devices running different versions. For small app developers it would become difficult or nearly impossible to successfully test and release an app, for several reasons:  • The cost is too high: Providing technical support instructions to consumers for all the different OS versions, tailored instructions and the need to be up to date for all previous versions would likely be impossible for small developers and confusing to consumers. • The requirement in terms of human resources is disproportionate for small businesses. • It fragments the app market space. • SMEs face the difficult choice of either: o Refraining from providing the app on previous versions, or o Providing the app by diminishing the quality of their software.   C. Innovation Instead of using precious time to adapt the app to numerous previous versions, a developer could instead use that time to create new features and innovate considering the limited staff these SMEs have. New devices introduce new OS upgrades and new functionalities on which developers innovate. For example, developers may choose to compete on privacy by making the most of the enhanced privacy features in the latest OS updates. Others may wish to include these new functionalities in their apps using a subscription-based model. However, it is not always possible to provide those new capabilities on older devices or with previous OS versions. If a consumer can use the ‘right to repair’ to downgrade back to a previous OS version, it could create business development uncertainties, reduce the uptake of innovative apps, and generate new obstacles for developers to innovate to the detriment of consumers. Consumers change devices as they want to have a better user experience and take advantage of new technological features. Including software in the right to repair may hinder app developers from innovating on top of software and device upgrades. Further, as a device’s battery gets older, processing speeds naturally decrease, and users may want to remedy that by getting a newer device.      4 https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-installing-software-updates-makes-us-wannacry/

IV/ Conclusion The App Association urges the European Commission to exclude software from the scope of the ‘right to repair’ initiative. While we understand the need to maintain products in the market as long as possible and use natural resources efficiently, a requirement to repair software or undo OS upgrades would raise cybersecurity risks, harm innovation, and increase costs to potentially prohibitive levels for small developers. Smartphones, like other electronic devices, become less efficient after a certain period. As the battery works with different software updates over time, its power may decrease but that should not become a reason for consumers to misuse the ‘right to repair’ initiative.  We thank the European Commission for considering our comments and remain at your disposal for further discussion on this issue.  Sincerely,

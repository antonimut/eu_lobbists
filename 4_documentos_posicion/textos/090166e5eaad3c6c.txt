Confederation of Danish Industry (DI) on ’Right to repair’ 
 
DI supports the transition towards a circular economy in Europe, and we strongly feel that making products 
more circular – as in lasting longer – is the right way to go for both industry and environment. We also find 
that circularity of products is high on the agenda of the majority of our member companies, whether it be by 
focusing on design for reuse or recycling, use of recycled materials or prolonging the lifetime of products. 
This is definitely a process already underway, underpinned by current market incentives that we predict will 
only strengthen in the coming years. 
A ‘right to repair’ – depending on how such a right is designed - may further push producers to prolong the 
lifetime of products. However, the design of such a right is far from simple, and in our opinion requires careful 
consideration of the scope of the initiative as well as other considerations in the legislation  such as 
proportionality, fairness and avoiding perverse incentives. Notably, the best solution from an environmental 
point of view may not be desirable from a consumer point of view.  
Product-by-product approach, using the Ecodesign methodology  
As with other legislative instruments used to promote sustainability of products, we believe that any ‘right 
to repair’ must be considered in a product-by-product manner. DI represents many different industries, and 
in our estimation, one-size does not fit all. For some product there will be significant environmental gains to 
be had by repairing rather than recycling or remanufacturing products and buying new  ones. For other 
product categories, though, repair will make less sense for consumers, businesses, or the environment.  
 
Some products have - by choice - a design that is not conducive to repair . For example, when a product is 
made by casting in one piece rather than a more modular design, as one piece ensures a longer lifetime for 
the product and extending the lifetime may be the better choice from an environmental point of view, even 
if it makes repair more difficult. Both durability and repairability are desirable characteristics in products, but 
sometimes one must take precedence over the other. In yet other cases, when a product is of negligible 
value, it may be so costly to repair that recycling will be the better s olution for that reason . Different 
products, different circumstances. 
 
Consequently, our recommendation is that legislation must leave room to evaluate when repair is the best 
choice. W e recommend integrating these evaluations in the lifecycle-assessments done following the  
Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation. 
 
Yes, to incentives to repair rather than buying new – but keep the balance 
Repair can only be conducted if the product is 1) designed to be repaired, 2) if the right parts are available 
and 3) if either the consumer himself or a professional is capable of and available to repair the product. The 
first two conditions should – and to some extents are or will be – regulated in the Ecodesign 
Directive/Regulation.

As for the 3 rd point, several studies have pointed to the potential for job growth in the repair sector of the 
circular economy. We expect that with more products repairabl e, more will see a business case in doing 
repair work. Added to that, DI supports incentives to consumers, such as tax deductions for repair as we have 
suggested in Denmark.  
 
For producers and retail sellers, consumers’ rights in some instances stand in the way of choosing repair over 
replacing a defect product. It could be left to the retail sellers and producers to decide if repair is feasible in 
the specific situation , not giving the consumer a choice. However, that would affect the balance  between 
consumer rights and seller’s/producers’ responsibilities. We find that tackling the challenges of design for 
repair and availability of spare parts is a more proportionate way of promoting repair, without reducing 
consumers’ rights. Within the framework of this proposal, however, we do support establishing same legal 
guarantee period for new and second -hand goods and/or refurbished goods as well as a right to replace 
defective products with refurbished goods. 
 
The consumer must pay for repairs  
Price is another important part of this issue – for producers and consumers alike. According to a 2018 
European Commission study on behavior, price (especially the ratio between the cost of repair and the price 
of replacement) was the most important driver for consumers' decisions on repair, followed by convenience1.  
 
Undoubtably, repairs paid for by producers or sellers would induce consumers to have more products 
repaired. However, the repairs would not be free, they would be paid for via general price increases. 
Furthermore, there would be a high risk of influencing behavior, encouraging carelessness in the handling, 
maintenance and use of products  thereby creating perverse incentives . Therefore, we strongly caution 
against a “right to repair” free of charge for consumers. 
 
Business models should not be determined by law 
DI consider circular business models as the business models of the future, and as such  the market will 
promote them. That is also the case for how to handle the repairing of products. As referred to above: there 
is a business case for repair, and in an increasingly circular economy there is a growing demand for repair 
service. 
 
Some companies will wish to integrate this service as part of their value chain, perhaps collecting information 
about the use of products as a valuable by -product of accommodating costumers requiring repair. Others 
will establish cooperation with specific partners regarding repair service, yet others want to refer to the open 
market. These different solutions have in common that the organisational choice is up to the company.  
 
DI believe the choice is best kept there, in the companies, and we caution against legislati on detailing 
business models by requiring specific ways to organise the repairing of products. That type of requirements 
risks inefficient solutions as well as hampering innovation. Rather, we believe that the Ecodesign Directive 
has been an immense success setting minimum requirements that let companies come up with the best and

Focus on consumer products 
When it comes to repairing products, there is a big difference between B2B and B2C. B2B relation s often 
include service contracts. In case of the breakdown of machinery it would often be necessary to immediately 
repair or replace as the cost of shutting down would be high. Thus, in our opinion a right to repair in those 
relations would be superfluous. In general, we recommend starting with consumer products and start small.  
 
Safety first 
Finally, we would like to stress that to DI’s members, safety is a high priority with any product. It is important 
that repairability does not compromise the safety of the product . Access to make a repair should only be 
allowed when there are no safety concerns, and in some cases only by qualified or authorized personnel.  
Questionnaire biases 
We note that the online questionnaire contains several biased questions or answering options, and that some 
of the questions are mandatory to answer even though the respondent does not have an opinion or does not 
agree with the limited answering options. For example, DI does not have a consolidated opinion on Q8 , Q9 
and Q10 but we are forced to choose an opinion we don’t have, in order to submit answers to other questions 
where we do have an opinion.

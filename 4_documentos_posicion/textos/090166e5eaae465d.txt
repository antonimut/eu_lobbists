The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise ’s position on the 
Right to repair initiative 
 
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise brings together 50 industry and employer 
organisations and 60,000 companies and work on issues that are important to all 
companies, irrespective of sector and size. We support the European Commission’s 
right to repair initiative and its objective of extending the useful life of goods. 
Swedish Enterprise welcomes that the initiatives and regulations for prolonged life of 
goods are set at an EU-level. Harmonized rules are essential for a well-functioning 
internal market, with more sustainable production and consumption patterns.  
 
In the circular economy follows greater collaboration between actors along the value 
chain, and the emergence of new actors to, amongst other things, offer repair 
services. It is important that the right to repair initiative creates the right market 
conditions for repair services and second hand/refurbished goods, where new 
business models have the potential to be profitable, and where all actors compete on 
a level playing field. This includes that design protection for spare parts should be 
possible in all Member States, as an incentive for design innovation to facilitate 
repairs.  
 
Before implementation, an impact assessment should be made to make sure that the 
implementation of the initiative is workable, proportionate, enforceable and 
ultimately will contribute to a circular economy. The environmental benefits must 
exceed the costs for the industry.  
 
Swedish Enterprise welcomes the possibility to contribute to the process of deve-
loping the right to repair initiative, and would like to highlight the following key 
messages: 
 
Policy measures for increased repair and reuse must always be set with a 
product-by-product approach.  
The prerequisites for products vary and thus the possibility to repair, refurbish and 
reuse. All actions within the right to repair initiative must further consider both 
circularity, safety and quality perspectives when implemented.

The requirements must adhere to the New Legislative Framework, NLF. 
Product requirements must be based on relevant internal market regulations to ensure 
a level playing field and to adjust relevant provisions to specifically solve uncertain-
ties related to definitions, requirements and responsibilities of the different actors. 
 
The initiative should be limited to B2C relations.  
The initiative should be limited to business-to-consumer transactions, as business-to-
business transactions generally are covered by contractual agreements. 
 
Safety must be ensured for repaired, refurbished and second-hand goods. 
Swedish Enterprise would like to specifically highlight the importance to include the 
safety aspect in connection to the right to repair. Some product groups require 
authorized repairers as well as testing after a repair is performed. Not least electrical 
and electronic products that fall under the Low Voltage Directive (2014/35/EU) and 
the EMC Directive (2014/30/EU).  
 
Compliance must be ensured for all products on the EU internal market.  
To ensure a level playing field rules which apply to European companies must also 
apply to imported products from third countries including products sold directly to 
European consumers, for example via platforms. Corresponding to new product 
requirements it is important that measures for increased Market surveillance follows 
accordingly.  
 
The right to repair must be aligned with other ongoing initiatives.  
Ecodesign initiatives and the initiative to empower the consumers in the green 
transition are closely linked to the right-to-repair. Product specific regulations under 
the Sustainable Products Initiative and other ongoing actions under the Circular 
Economy Action Plan must be aligned to ensure clarity and avoid double regulation. 
It is further important that the definitions of concepts, e.g., refurbishment, repair, 
second hand etc., are consistent across the initiatives. Swedish Enterprise welcomes 
that the EU-Commission has highlighted synergies to other initiatives in their impact 
assessment.  
 
Swedish Enterprise supports repair as the primary remedy.  
A study conducted by The Swedish Retail Research Institution (HUI) show that a 
majority of consumers chooses replacement (receiving a new product) rather than 
repair when offered a choice between the two. Thus, Swedish Enterprise consider the 
implementation of repair as the primary remedy to be an efficient measure to 
extending the use period of goods, provided that a repair is possible from a safety 
perspective. However, such measure would contradict the OMNIBUS directive, 
which allows the possibility for consumers to choose between repair and replace-
ment, and which is now being implemented across Member States. Swedish

Enterprise emphasizes that the Right to repair initiative must coincide with other 
legislation on consumer rights, the Sales of Goods Directive (2019/771), and the 
discrepancy between the directives must be addressed before right to repair is 
implemented. 
 
The liable part for re-pair or replacement should have mandate to decide the 
final remedy.  
The liable part should only be obliged to prioritize repair over replacement if it is 
reasonable in terms of the environmental impact, safety and cost. When other options 
are more suitable (refurbishment, remanufacturing, replacement etc.) the manufac-
turer should also be entitled to choose this remedy.  
 
It must be clear who is responsible for ensuring the repair and where the 
liability lies.  
Increased collaborations between actors such as manufacturers, retailers, consumers, 
and professional repair services, are important to enable prolonged life of goods. To 
increase the credibility for- and willingness to consume refurbished and repaired, it is 
however important that the repairs are done in a safe way and that it is clearly stated 
where the liability lies. Swedish Enterprise believes that the manufacturer is most 
suitable to manage repair on a general level, as the right skills and expertise are often 
needed for repairs and the manufacturer has the most knowledge of the product. The 
product prerequires and the feasibility (such as distance to manufacturer) should 
nonetheless be considered.  
 
The free right to repair must be limited to cases regarding original faults, a 
manufacturing defect.  
The quality and design of a product have a large impact on the longevity of its life. 
However, a manufacturer cannot control of how the product is used and whether the 
user follows the service advice provided. The area of use is decisive for a product's 
function and free repair should not be mandated the actor responsible for repairs if 
the defects are due to improper use. Free repairs can further not be offered after the 
legal guarantee period has expired.  
 
It should be easier for companies to repair products and then sell them again as 
refurbished.  
Swedish Enterprise consider the measure to encourage businesses to voluntarily 
commit to repairing goods and promoting second-hand/refurbished goods very 
effective. However, several legislative files at EU-level must be updated to fully 
enable these business models, for example, the Waste Framework Directive 
(2008/98/EC). Faulty goods should not mandatory be classified as waste, and 
consequently be owned by the local government/municipalities (Swedish case).

Protection of industrial design is an important incentive for facilitating repairs. 
The protection of industrial design could grant protection for spare parts, which is 
done in some Member States. In the current review of both the Design Directive 
(98/71/EC) and the Community Design Regulation (6/2002) it is possible to create a 
harmonised design protection for spare parts as an incentive for designing products 
that are easy to repair.  
 
Swedish Enterprise do not support the option to restart the legal guarantee 
period after every repair.  
This option does not consider that other parts rather than the repaired might be 
subject to reduced performance due to prior usage. The usage of a restarted guarantee 
period could lead to suboptimization, in addition to an unnecessary cost increase, 
contradicting the overall sustainability. Innovation might lead to better performance 
and the development of new design in newer products, that enable for example less 
water and energy usage. Commercial guarantees are a preferred option.

REPSOL’S RESPONSE TO PUBLIC CONSULTATION – EU METHANE REGULATION  (The Regulation)1 
 
Repsol supports the development of a cost-efficient EU-wide regulation addressing methane emissions.  
Repsol supports the use of natural gas to bring about a structured transition to a low -emissions future, 
particularly due to its short -term potential to substitute coal in electricity generation and consequently 
an substantially contribution to emissions reduction , which  is why we have taken on the objective of 
achieving a methane emissions intensity of our operated assets of 0.2% by 2025, a value recognized as 
near-zero by relevant shareholders for the O&G sector and which also coincides with the new target 
announced by the OGCI (Oil and Gas Climate Initiative) of which Repsol is a member.  
As an OGMP signatory, Repsol agrees with the European Commission’s objective to improve the accuracy 
of information about sources of methane emissions and to seek  to reduce emissions , control fugitive 
emissions and to seek to minimize flaring and venting.  
However, we are generally concerned that the Regulation requires operators to conduct overall 
overwhelming measurements, verification, leak detection and reporting activities with corresponding cost 
to the industry and consumers, that do not have any detectable environmental benefit, thereby not being 
in line with the Better Regulation principles. Whilst we acknowledge there should be some cost related to 
methane emission reduction in general, such activities should be based on an assessment  of a source 
emission potential and possibility for occurrence (i.e. risk-based).  
 
Inactive wells: 
Repsol suggests a more clear definition of inactive wells. As they are described  in the Regulation, MRV 
(Monitoring, Reporting and Verification) may be required  on thousands of inactive offshore and onshore 
wells whilst not contributing to the Regulation’s objectives, but incurring significant cost to the industry 
and consumers.  
A revised definition of ‘inactive wells’ is required to avoid an overly broad scope of application. As written, 
the requirements in the Commission’s proposal could possibly apply to plugged and abandoned wells . 
Therefore, the Regulation should apply then only to inactive wells included in the scope of this definition 
and exclude plugged and abandoned wells. 
Emissions from inactive wells typically do not exist  or are negligible and would not justify installing 
measurement equipment on them.  Permanent measurement of methane emiss ions at all inactive wells  
seems disproportionate. Moreover, the required technology does not exist for such measuring of subsea 
wells. However, sufficiently accurate alternative quantification methods are available.  Emission 
quantification requirements for inactive wells should be consistent with quantification requirements from 
other sources, sites, and assets, and be based on the assessment of a methane emission risk.  
 
Monitoring, Reporting and Verification (MRV):  
The proposed Regulation supports the idea of the establishment of comparable global methane emissions 
data in line with OGMP 2.0 reporting framework , but sometimes in fact undermines its objective. It is 
important to emphasize that the Regulation presents OGMP 2.0 as an energy sector led initiative when in 
fact is a is a multi -stakeholder initiative launched by UNEP  and the CCAC , and its membership includes 
industry, governments and civil society.  
The terms ‘quantification’ and ‘measurement’ are used interchangeably and should better reflect the 
flexibilities of the OGMP 2.0 framework, including materiality provisions, which consider technology, 
safety, security, and other challenges in meeting framework expectations.  
It is also important that the legislative proposal exceeds the OGMP 2.0 requirements with regards 
reconciliation and timeframes to achieve a specific level of reporting . These requirements exceeds the 
OGMP materiality provisions. Nevertheless, in Repsol we believe that the steps towards reconciliation of

LDAR: 
The proposed Regulation may even have adverse effects on the detection of methane emissions because 
of its very prescriptive approach to Leak Detection and Repair (LDAR) requirements with regard to 
minimum survey frequencies, the detection devices to be us ed, and components to be assessed. We 
believe that the LDAR provision should be opened for more outcome-focused and risk-based approaches 
and allow for the use of a wide range of technologies.  
The Regulation does not take into account the current conditions of installations, operations, 
maintenance and leak tightness of systems. For example, in the upstream context, annual frequencies 
have proven to be sufficiently robust to detect and repair potential leaks. Extensive and high -frequency 
monitoring of all relevant components seems not the most effective way to reduce methane emissions 
quickly and further. The Regulation should focus on addressing larger potential leak sources first, and 
therefore, LDAR survey frequencies should be proportionate and risk -based. The 500ppm concentration-
based threshold is only achievable with some specific technology, which should be avoided.  
Last, but not least, we would suggest a longer threshold than 5 days where immediate repair  or 
replacement is not possible. This tight deadline could lead to incorrect decisions because insufficient time 
to assess requirements or alternatives. 
 
Venting and flaring:  
Flaring should be the preference over venting, where possible. An overall GHG (CO2e) emissions and cost-
benefit approach needs to be considered for small venting sources e.g., where there is a lack of sufficient 
gas pressure to allow it to enter the flare system. In such cases the GHG emissions from the use of 
equipment to recover the gas needs be considered in the benefit assessment. 
The definitions and use of the terms ‘venting’ and ‘flaring’ can be better aligned with those used by the 
World Bank's Global Gas Flaring Reduction Partnership (GGFR). The proposed flaring standards are not 
feasible, particularly for lo w volume onshore facilities. The flaring considerations  may be addressed for 
new facilities, exclu ding the existing ones  in order to keep the spirit of the GGFR definitions on 
“economically feasible” approach.  
The proposed requirements for complete destruction removal efficiency (DRE) are not technically possible 
and should be further clarified.  
 
Finally, the text of the general mitigation obligation (article 13) provides uncertainty as to the extent of 
the obligation and could result in numerous complaints raised in circumstances that might be immaterial.  
We recommend applying a standard based on reasonableness.

EQUINOR COMMENTS TO THE EUROPEAN COMMISSION ON THE GAS DECARBONISATION 
PACKAGE COMPRISING THE PROPOSAL FOR THE REVISION OF THE GAS DIRECTIVE AND THE 
GAS REGULATION 
 
 
Extensive work has been carried out by the European Commission in developing the gas decarbonisation 
package, preparing scenarios and impact assessments, and drafting the revised Gas Regulation and Gas 
Directive. Drafting the appropriate legislation to initiate, direct and accelerate a just energy transition is an 
enterprise of long-term strategic significance. Success in mastering its challenges and in harvesting the 
opportunities it enables requires close collaboration between all energy industries and public authorities. 
The war in Ukraine and the resulting extraordinary market conditions risk jeopardising the  successful long-
term liberalisation of the European gas market and the Fit for 55 objectives. While extraordinary times 
require extraordinary temporary measures to address the emergency, care should be taken by legislators 
and market actors not to derail the necessary investments and operations resulting from a reliable and stable 
long-term framework.  
Equinor welcomes the European Commission’s efforts to propose such a framework in the Gas 
Decarbonisation Package and supports many of the principles included therein. Considering this, we want to 
emphasise that: 
• without a just transition there is a risk that the energy transition stalls, and that climate, emissions and 
environmental pledges fail to be met, 
• gases, of which natural gas will remain a key component, will be indispensable throughout the energy 
transition to a net-zero society, 
• climate neutrality requires carbon management solutions. 
We are in crunch time for taking climate action at the same time as security of energy supplies and 
affordability for consumers are more critical than ever. The Commission’s proposed directive and regulation 
show the necessity of leveraging the well-functioning European gas market to deliver needed GHG emission 
reductions at scale and in a cost-efficient manner by means of both renewable and low-carbon gases.  
The speed at which investments in renewable and low-carbon solutions are enabled and incentivised is 
critical for the EU to reach its climate ambitions. An ambitious 2030 and 2050 decarbonisation agenda 
implies that the EU has little time to realise effective decarbonisation under a limited set of technological 
solutions. To develop a well-functioning market for hydrogen that helps drive decarbonisation we will need 
many forms of hydrogen with zero to low-carbon footprint as well as new sources of natural gas. The volume 
needs are massive. This is what makes policies supporting both renewables and various forms of low -carbon 
gases so important and for investors to have a line of sight to an appropriate legislative framework already 
today and well into the 2030s.  
Today renewable gases are supported under the Renewable Energy Directive. EU ETS pricing will 
increasingly contribute to drive technology choices. However, given the timeframe in which we seek deep 
decarbonisation, it makes economic sense to make efficient use of infrastructure in place and existing 
technologies for production of zero to low-carbon hydrogen. Cross border trade, information transparency 
and access to infrastructure will be as important for hydrogen as it is for natural gas.

We see a maturing of the understanding of the many ways the gas system can be decarbonised, as well as 
acceptance that the pace of deployment of the various technologies might differ across the European 
system. In some countries and sectors natural gas will retain a central role, in others, biogases will play a 
more important role, and in others still, various forms of hydrogen and synthetic gases will be essential.  
For hydrogen to become a vector of change for the European energy transition we will need all its sou rces 
and means of production. In the proposals we see a growing recognition of the importance of natural gas-
based hydrogen combined with carbon capture and storage (CCS). This is welcome and we encourage 
European law makers to be even bolder, to prioritise GHG emissions reduction and establish clean energy 
targets and quotas open to both renewable and low-carbon solutions. Equinor’s large-scale decarbonisation 
projects in the UK, the Netherlands, Belgium, and Germany have strengthened our belief in the importance 
of combining offshore wind, CCS, natural gas reforming and renewable hydrogen in smart new ways for an 
effective and affordable pathway to net zero.  
While we see signs of a more technology open approach and acceptance of the crucial role of natura l gas 
and low carbon (LC) gases to decarbonise the European society, we believe the proposal could be even 
bolder in providing space for various technologies to compete in the market.  
We therefore believe that the proposal for the Decarbonisation Package needs to:  
• Clarify the definition of low carbon hydrogen by specifying, before this package is adopted, the 
methodology to account for related GHG emissions and maintain coherence across legislations.  
• Establish clean energy targets and quotas open to both renewable and low-carbon solutions with primary 
focus on GHG emissions reduction. 
• Clarify what thresholds are meant by “high grade of purity” of hydrogen by establishing the chemical 
compounds and blending attributes for a gas to qualify as “hydrogen”. 
• Consider how upstream natural gas pipeline networks can be used to import renewable and low-carbon 
hydrogen to Europe, with hydrogen blends potentially reaching high levels of purity over time.  
o Such utilised upstream natural gas pipelines should maintain their upstream nature with regards to 
entry into the European system. 
o The same principles applied to the cap at interconnection points between Union Member States 
transmission networks should be applied at import entry points from upstream natural gas pipeline s. 
o The 5% blending cap should be revised upwards to reflect growing hydrogen blends.  
• Establish unbundling principles that facilitate and enable, rather than inhibit, hydrogen network 
investments and their optimal operations and maintenance as applied in the Third Energy Package. The 
application of strict unbundling requirements on hydrogen network operators may constitute an obstacle 
to the repurposing of gas networks, and to the materialization of the potential operational and cost 
synergies between gas and hydrogen networks. The positive experience from the natural gas market 
should be used to guide legislation where full ownership unbundling is not mandatory  and different 
unbundling models (such as ISO and ITO) should remain options. 
• Resolve the remaining lack of clarity on, and ensure ease of use of, a certification system for low carb on 
gases, notably in enabling commodity and certificates to be traded separately  and in establishing user 
friendly ways of ensuring compliance and transparency. For instance, the establishment of a Union 
Database may introduce processes that are incompatible with the operation of the internal energy market 
as the implied rules for tracking of molecules and certificates and their registration in the Union Database 
may not match how gas flows are optimized within a networked system. 
• Ensure simple tariff and access rules that rely on few exemptions. For an energy transition to be 
perceived as fair and just, costs need to closely reflect use. Tariffs should not be utilised to favour a 
technology over another as there are other effective policy instruments that legislators s hould use to

facilitate and incentivise the deployment of renewable and low carbon solutions (ETS/CO2 price, 
taxonomy, GHG reduction targets, sectorial penetration targets). The proposed scheme to offer tariff 
discounts for RES and LC gases does not reflect use of the physical system, is discriminatory, constitutes 
a form of cross-subsidization, and may be unenforceable at interconnection points. 
• Enforce the principle of separate regulatory accounts for hydrogen and natural gas infrastructure by 
avoiding cross-subsidisation between energy carriers. It is also crucial that the value of assets transferred 
between regulated asset bases is transparent, fully auditable and controlled.  
• Consider that removing cross-border tariffication for certain gases would remove investments signals and 
create risks of inappropriate investment levels. This merits careful assessment by the co -legislators. As 
highlighted in the Energy System Integration and Hydrogen Strategies, the nascent and still -yet-to be 
created hydrogen market is expected to first develop around pockets of industrial clusters, and local 
TSOs will most likely develop hydrogen backbones. Interconnection Point tariffs provide important 
investment signals for the gradual retrofitting of the existing natural gas grid into a dedicated hydrogen 
backbone where the system is under-utilised based on natural gas transportation alone (e.g. the Dutch 
Lo-Cal gas pipelines). 
• Maintain the means for commercial parties to mitigate risks, underpin investments and enhance security 
of supply through the contractual freedom to enter long-term natural gas contracts provided these are 
compatible with competition law and consistent with EU policies and decarbonisation objectives . 
• Use this process to lay the foundations of a more active approach to carbon management by proposing a 
European Carbon Reduction and Removal Strategy and by establishing clear rules for the capture and 
transportation of CO2 and the establishment of a regional CO2 network as an integral part of the EU 
energy system along with power and gas networks. 
Encouraged by signs of a more technology open approach, Equinor will seek to engage with co -legislators to 
further incentivise and accelerate the large-scale deployment of low-carbon hydrogen and carbon 
management solutions as well as the development of a renewable hydrogen value chain  while continuing to 
develop the most sustainable natural gas supplies to Europe.

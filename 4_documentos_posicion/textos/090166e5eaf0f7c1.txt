MOL Group assessment of the Commission proposal for a revised Gas directive and regulation 
– COM (2021) 803 final and COM(2021) 804 final  
MOL Group has a strong ambition to improve its operations and gradually transition to a low-carbon, 
sustainable business model. We welcome the Gas Package aiming to establish rules for the transport, 
supply and storage of natural gas system and its transition to the system based on renewable and low-
carbon gases.  
Such a green transition can only take place if the required changes do not impose disproportionate 
burden on society and provide appropriate incentive s for investment in the energy systems. In 
addition, during the transition, security of energy supply must also be ensured. 
Based on these principles MOL Group proposes some comments to be considered. 
Definition of low-carbon hydrogen  
Directive Art. 2. (1), (2), (10), (11), (12) 
We welcome that the proposals facilitate the penetration of low -carbon gases including low -carbon 
hydrogen by adding clear definitions. Along with renewable hydrogen, low-carbon hydrogen production 
can contribute to significant emissions savings in line with the Green Deal targets.  
It is reasonable to set the GHG-emission threshold for low-carbon hydrogen at 70%, as we would like to 
emphasise that the GHG saving target and the certification system need to be fully consistent with the 
Fit for 55 package (i.e. mirror the thresholds under the REDIII). 
We are concerned, however, that the methodology to calculate GHG savings of low -carbon hydrogen 
will only be developed in 2024, as clarity on this is needed urgently to ensure investor certainty. We call 
on the Commission to clarify the methodology of certification of low -carbon gases and low -carbon 
hydrogen in parallel with the adoption of the recast Gas Dire ctive. We consider a certification scheme 
based on mass balance a workable solution, as it is in line with the REDII , however, we are of the view 
that a book and claim model would provide more flexible solution for all involved parties, leading to 
cost-efficiency, lower hydrogen production costs, larger scale projects, and therefore more 
decarbonisation potential. 
Finally, to ensure investor certainty, we believe that the revision of the 70% GHG-threshold due to 
technological advancements should only apply to new projects after any possible revision, and existing 
projects should be able to keep the 70% threshold. 
5% blended hydrogen in natural gas system 
Regulation Art. 20. 
We are supportive of the provisions enabling up to 5% blended hydrogen from 2025 and their transfer 
across gas interconnectors, as we generally believe that blending is needed to scale up hydrogen 
markets. In our view, some further analysis is needed to clarify how much investment is necessary for 
pipelines to accept such blends, as the presence of H 2, H 2S and water content together are likely to 
accelerate pipeline deterioration.  
Moreover, with gradually increasing hydrogen volumes in the system, and with lower quality of locally 
produced gas, required for ensuring adequate gas supply , we are concerned that managing a precise 
gas quality may become a more demanding task.  Therefore, in case of higher (such as 5%) H 2 blend 
values, we propose to adjust the ranges of gas quality parameters to accommodate the maximum H 2 
blend (as opposed to the actual levels of H2 in the system), thereby providing maximum flexibility, also 
for the inclusion of lower quality gases, enhancing security of supply as much as possible.

Separation of regulated asset bases (RAB) 
Regulation Art. 4. (1), (2), (3), (4) 
Financial transfer between RABs is an essential prerequisite in supporting investments (both 
repurposing and new builds) in hydrogen assets. This should create transparent development and 
operation of separated networks with a possibility of allowing cross-financing measures needed to be 
introduced to enhance early-stage development of pure hydrogen networks. The most effective way to 
finance a new hydrogen infrastructure, that will enable decarbonisation of the gas system, is through 
building on the financing capacity of the existing robust and mature natural gas system. 
The current energy crisis greatly emphasizes the need to accelerate the development of a hydrogen 
market, and its enabling infrastructure. The public interest of security of supply justifies more than ever 
the application of effective cost -allocation and mutualization of infrastructure development costs, to 
ensure that much needed investments happen as fast as possible. Legislation, therefore, should  
• be clearly formulated , especially in cas e of restrictive provisions such as separation of RAB s, 
thus there is a need for a precise definition of “financial  transfer” as provided for in the Draft 
Regulation; and  
•  widen the possibility for financial transfers between the separate regulated asset bases. 
Moreover because of the possibility of blending  (using the natural gas system for hydrogen as well ), 
there is a need for clarification that the separation requirement of RABs is only applicable between pure 
hydrogen systems and the natural gas system , and not applicable within the system which provides 
regulated service for both gas and hydrogen blended , because in this case there will be no separate 
RABs for gas and hydrogen. 
Tariff discounts system 
Regulation Art. 16. 
Renewable and low-carbon gases entry tariff discount (75%) 
We support renewable and low-carbon gases entry tariff discounts which are necessary to promote the 
development of hydrogen production. The revenue equivalence of TSO’s investments can and must be 
ensured through the integrated tariff system. 
Cross-border interconnection tariff discount (100%) 
We believe that the 100% cross -border interconnection tariff discount system is unworkable and will 
become a hindrance to investments in cross -border interconnectors. Zero effective tariffs at cross -
border IPs assume highly complex Inter-TSO Compensation Mechanisms, that have never been able to 
be put in place (despite attempts) on the gas market. The requirement of ITCs would mean in practice 
lengthy, inconclusive debate between TSOs and national regulatory authorities (NRAs). Building a fair 
regulatory framework could also entail setting up an EU -wide Capacity Weighted Distance (CWD) 
Reference Price Model, which also constitutes an immense (and in our opinion unachievable)  task for 
all involved parties. 
In order to facilitate investment, we call to amend the proposal in respect of cross-border tariffication, 
in a way that it allows tariff collection on cross-border interconnection points, and builds on the proven 
tariff models of the natural gas system. 
Financing cross-border hydrogen infrastructure 
Directive Art. 53. 
The proposal lacks clarity on the financing of cross-border infrastructure. 
It is unclear which parties are exactly concerned (only TSOs or regulators?) under Article 53. (5). We 
would also like to seek an explanation why PCI projects are excluded from the process of financing cross-

border hydrogen infrastructure under Article 53. (1). Furthermore, shall the process under Article 53.

The ambiguous system proposed in the draft package does not sufficiently help in the quick

Geographically confined hydrogen networks 
Directive Art. 48. 
For geographically confined hydrogen networks, NRAs may only provide derogation from unbundling 
rules until 31 December 2030. According to Article 48 of the recast Directive, beginning from 1 January 
2031, ‘the derogation shall expire when one of the conditions below are fulfilled: 
(a) where a competing renewable hydrogen producer wants to get access to the network; 
(b)  where the exempted hydrogen network becomes connected to another hydrogen network.’ 
For example, s hould such pipelines become subject to mandatory third -party access and unbundling 
rules, companies may not be willing to invest in hydrogen assets at all, if with their investment they risk 
mandatory sharing and/or divestment of such assets. 
Regarding geographically confined hydrogen networks, we propose that changing nTPA regimes to rTPA 
should be based on the NRA decision within 15 years following commissio ning of the given 
geographically confined hydrogen networks. 
This solution would allow for the fastest and most cost-effective development of the hydrogen market 
in its initial phase with full respect to local specificities under the sovereignty of the Member State and 
the NRA supervision. 
Closed hydrogen networks 
Closed hydrogen systems , where for specific technical or safety reasons, the operations  or the 
production process of the users of that hydrogen system are integrated; or that are built for the internal 
purposes of companies or groups of companies – (as defined in the previous version of the draft 
regulation) should be regulated under differe nt conditions than geographically confined hydrogen 
networks. We call on the legislator to include in the legislation a derogation – subject to NRA approval 
– for closed, within-site hydrogen networks, that is not limited in time. 
Discrimination against TSOs operating under the ITO unbundling model (including FGSZ) 
Regulation. 
The regulation aims at creating a legislative framework for the functioning of gas markets and 
infrastructure, that enables: 
• the creation of a hydrogen and low-carbon gas market, 
• the integration of these renewable and low-carbon gases into the gas system, 
• the development of supply capacity for renewable and low carbon gases (including hydrogen) 
• and an infrastructure that is capable of handling the new gases (especially hydrogen with 
characteristics and requirements very different to those of natural gas). 
To create supply capacity and infrastructure, a huge amount of investment will be needed: 
• in production capacity: renewable power, renewable hydrogen (electroly sers) and other low -
carbon gases (biogas and low-carbon hydrogen) 
• in pipeline infrastructure to transmit the gases from their producers to their consumers.

The proposal’s main challenge – especially with regard to hydrogen – is to regulate a market that is not 
yet existent or embryonic, and at the same time to foster the creation of the necessary infrastructure 
for this market to start. Detailed (and as such inflexible and restrictive) market rules risk inhibiting the 
flow of investment and thereby the cost-effective creation of capacities and infrastructure. 
In such a situation, the regulator cannot solely rely on the financing capacity of the (non -existent or 
embryonic) market, and thus needs to take into account existing operators with the needed assets, 
expertise, and financing  capacity. Even so, this is only realistic if the most cost -efficient solutions are 
pursued. In infrastructure development this means that member states should be allowed to choose 
their own system development path, in accordance with their country -specific characteristics and 
possibilities. Generally in Central-Eastern Europe, the most opportune path towards the creation of the 
first hydrogen transmission pipelines is likely to take place on the basis of the existing gas network – 
mainly through repurposing of existing natural gas (or any other suitable, e.g. oil or oil product) 
pipelines. For this to happen, the operators of natural gas networks need to b e 1) allowed and 2) 
incentivised to invest in repurposing their infrastructure. As the natural gas transmission networks are 
well-developed and robust systems with a wide coverage of network users, natural gas transmission 
tariffs could partially be used t o finance and incentivize repurposing investments of gas TSOs and the 
build-up of the new hydrogen network. 
In member states with gas TSOs unbundled under the ITO model,  the proposal severely limits the 
participation of the natural gas transmission system operator  (gas-ITOs), or a subsidiarity thereof  in 
participating in the development of the hydrogen network. 
The gas market rules in effect allow for three, equally market-conform unbundling models for the 
operation of natural gas networks: Ownership Unbundled Operator (OU), Independent System Operator 
(ISO) and Independent Transmission Operator (ITO).  
The new proposal, however, in respect of allowing the operation o f hydrogen networks, differentiates 
between natural gas transmission system (TSOs) operators according to their unbundling model. Only 
gas-OUs and gas-ISOs are allowed (assuming they create a legally separated H-TSO within their group) 
to operate hydrogen networks. Gas-ITOs are excluded from the development and long-term operation 
of hydrogen networks.  
The proposal discriminates, and in effect prohibits ITO natural gas transmission system operators (even 
assuming legal separation within their groups) to invest in hydrogen assets. It prevents the use of the 
network of such operators from being repurposed to hydrogen transmission, and therefore even inhibit 
entire regions and countries to properly participate in the hydrogen economy. In this sense, the proposal 
is discriminatory and self-defeating. 
While limiting the use of the ITO model, the proposal does not establish why this limitation is necessary, 
let alone why this limitation is the least restrictive measure to enable the efficient build-up of a hydrogen 
network. As such, the proposal violates the proportionality principle . Regulatory assessments of ITO 
models did not reveal deficiencies in the independence of ITOs, thus there are no objective reasons why 
ITO models could not be kept by member stat es. There are clear reasons why member states would 
wish to choose the ITO model for their hydrogen network operators, and that the unavailability of this 
model would bring clear disadvantages to them. Given the above, member states are best placed to 
make their choice as regards the unbundling model,  this way, the proposal violates the subsidiarity 
principle, by regulating areas that can be perfectly achieved at Member State level – and at the same 
time depriving these member states from the benefits of relying on their gas transmission system 
operator in the development of their hydrogen infrastructure. 
Through the negative effects of such a provision, the Decarbonised Gas package risks the development 
of an EU -wide integrated hydrogen network, ultimately to the detriment of security of supply. In an 
emergency, when hydrogen is one of the key alternatives to break dependence on Russian natural gas 
supplies, all players who are willing to invest in hydrogen infrastructure should not only be allowed to 
do so but should even be supported. ITO gas TSOs – as much as any other TSO – are eager to offer their

knowledge, technology, assets and financing-capacity for investments in hydrogen networks and their 
long-term operation, for not only the benefit of climate, but also security of supply. 
Insufficient derogations for pure hydrogen systems 
Directive Art. 47. 
This is a concern if the discrimination of transmission system operators in ITO structures remain in the 
proposal. 
Subject to member state decision, t he proposal  allows an exemption from third party access and 
unbundling rules only for existing hydrogen networks and not for existi ng networks that are yet to be 
repurposed for hydrogen. While the intention of the proposal is to foster the development of hydrogen 
infrastructure, this limitation practically prohibits TSOs that are operating in a gas -ITO regime (even 
assuming legal separation within their groups) to invest in the repurposing of their pipelines, even within 
the transition period. As a minimum, t he derogation should cover all existing networks, regardless 
whether they are already repurposed for hydrogen or not.

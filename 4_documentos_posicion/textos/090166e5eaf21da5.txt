CONFINDUSTRIA 
April 12th, 2022 
Ref. Ares(2022)2981486 - 12/04/2022
 
 
 
1 
The dynamics of the gas market in recent months and the current geopolitical situation 
require an important reflection at European level on the evolution of the energy system with 
a view to defining a framework that guarantees, in addition to environmental sustainability, 
the certainty of supplies and the stability of the markets. 
The Hydrogen and Gas Decarbonisation Package will have to define paths of development 
of a gas and hydrogen market consistent with the measures adopted and being adopted by 
the European institutions to manage the current emergency, on the one hand, and 
implement a structural revision of the rules, on the other hand. 
The green transition remains at the heart of European energy policy but at the same time it 
must be oriented not only towards the achievement of environmental objectives: the 
decarbonisation process becomes a fundamental element to achieve energy independence 
and implement a diversification of supply sources and routes.  
In this context, renewable and low carbon gases represent an important path of development 
to value new productions and imports , and from this perspective it is imp ortant that the 
European Gas and Hydrogen Package creates the conditions for a rapid development of 
the market, in line with the new prospects for the production and penetration of these gases 
in the European energy system.   
The current legislative initia tive is an opportunity to rethink the existing gas regulatory 
framework to support the qualitative leap  necessary for the decarbonization of “hard to 
abate” sectors. In this regard, a coordinated planning approach for the electricity and 
gas/hydrogen sectors is instrumental in fully unlocking the electricity carrier's contribution to 
decarbonisation by hig hlighting the potential synergies between the electricity and 
gas/hydrogen sectors. 
We therefore believe that the proposal to revise the Gas Package should be evaluated with 
this approach, considering a radically changed European and international context. 
 
Renewable and low carbon gas development targets 
First of all, it should be noted that the proposal for the Gas Directive introduces a definition 
of renewable gas that does not consider gaseous fuels produced from biogenic fraction of 
waste. This definit ion therefore excludes hydrogen obtained from (Bio)WTE from green 
gases. Conversely, the RED II Directive considers gaseous fuels from biomass (including 
 
 
 
2 
the biogenic fraction of waste) to be renewable.  A legislative paradox is therefore created 
according to which hydrogen produced by (Bio)WTE is a renewable fuel (within the meaning 
of RED II) but is not considered renewable gas within the meaning of the Gas Directive. 
To complete the definition framework, it should be noted that the proposal for the Gas 
Directive introduces the concept of “low-carbon hydrogen ” obtained from non -renewable 
sources that are able to reduce GHG emissions by 70% (compared to the use of fossil 
fuels). However, a similar emission parameter for molecules obtained from renewable 
sources is placed in the REDII Directive creating further interpretative doubts about the 
typing of renewable gas with particular reference to molecules other than the RFNBO type.  
In addition, Member States should be free to set their individual renewable an d low carbon 
gas targets in their national energy and climate plan, being free to set different sectoral 
targets and obligations, favouring in any case solutions for achieving those targets based 
on forms of direct support for the development of renewable and low-carbon sources.  
 
Hydrogen RAB financing, gas/H2 cross -subsidies, Transport tariff discount – 
Certification and GO 
In principle, hydrogen pipelines should be financed by hydrogen network users, not by gas 
users, without generating cross-subsidies. However, the cost could be anticipated by those 
gas customers who will switch to hydrogen in the near future. This should not be configured 
as a cross-subsidy, but as a choice of risk/cost allocation.  Gas-intensive companies must 
be protected from bearing the costs of new hydrogen pipelines so as not to displace them 
competitively. However, it is considered preferable to  define explicit measures to 
compensate for the higher costs of hydrogen production, notably an explicit incentive policy 
should be promoted both on the demand  and supply side, in order to pursue energy policy 
objectives for hard to abate  sectors. Specifically, it is possible to assess forms of support 
such as: the provision of an immediate and direct subsidy to producers – e.g., through 
contracts for difference or capital grants for the part of the investment on the installation – 
and cap ital subsidies to end -users to promote the fuel switch, which would avoid cross-
subsidisation from gas to hydrogen.  
 
 
 
3 
The provisions of the recast proposal on possible cross -subsidisation between hydrogen 
and gas revenues should be subject to appropriate consultations, with reference to specific 
measures declined at the level of individual countries.  
Instead of possible cross -subsidies between carriers and discounts on transport tariffs for 
renewable and low-carbon gases, explicit incentive mechanisms would be desirable, as they 
would be more effective for the development of a market for  renewable and low-carbon 
gases, and  with clear repercussions in terms of higher costs for the system, avoiding 
opaque mechanisms (the discounts would be appl ied to transport capacity and not to 
volumes/molecules), applied ex post and implying compensation between adjacent TSOs 
for the lost revenues related to these discounts. This is also in favor of creating a level 
playing field between producers of the different MSs. 
In addition to what has already been highlighted regarding the opportunity to provide explicit 
incentives, we stress that the  blending tariff discount provisions should be clarified ,  
especially with re gard to the way in which the blending discount is to be implemented in 
relation to the share of transported volumes , since this also depends on the ways of the 
GOS system is developed.  
A possible measure to be investigated could be to provide for the establishment of a fund 
intended to cover incentives / discounts for the promo tion of the start of the renewable gas 
market. 
 
Blending 5% at cross-border points 
The measure, which appears to be aimed at harmonising the evolution of the hydrogen 
market throughout the supply chain, is welcomed.  Blending, i ndeed, could play a 
significant initial role as it could favor the development of the hydrogen market and  
contribute to the decarbonization of the gas system. However, it is necessary to address 
the blending hypothesis in a more structured way, both at TSO and DSO network level, 
and to define the methods of treatment of investments functional to blending / de blending 
or to the possible future transport of hydrogen in gas networks, given the prospect of future 
mixing of hydrogen in existing networks without prejudice to the need to make economically 
efficient investment choices, thus avoiding the risk of generating stranded assets. 
 
 
 
 
4 
There are still several issues to be addressed: 
- definition of criteria for the classification of gas/hydrogen networks; 
- difficulty in identifying functional investments in hydrogen, distinguishing them fro m 
those related to natural gas (e.g. digitalization, which concerns natural gas and 
biomethane and is at the same time functional to the input of hydrogen); 
- the separation of RAB gas and hydrogen generates management complexities in 
view of the different situations that could arise at local level, with different % blending. 
 
Low carbon gas certification 
The methodology for assessing greenhouse gas emission savings related to low carbon 
gases is postponed to 2024 and will be the subject of a delegated act. Th is timing is not 
acceptable as it would have the effect of  slowing down the development of the hydrogen 
market and the development of low carbon gases as a whole. The Commission's proposal 
should include a clear classification of low carbon gases and the methodology for calculating 
their carbon footprint based on the entire life cycle. This is to avoid carrying out projects in 
the coming years without a clear regulatory framework, which risk being blocked or excluded 
from more stringent emission reduction requirements known a posteriori.  
It should be noted that the certification system of renewable and low carbon gases is urgent 
to enable investment choices in this direction, as well as to encourage the rapid development 
of these gases. To this end, it is necessary to: 
o develop a  certification scheme  for low carbon  gases, complementary to that 
provided for in the RED II Directive for renewable gases; 
o give priority to the European framework of guarantees of origin, over the adoption 
of alternative certification schemes developed at national level and therefore difficult 
to use in other Member States; 
o accept, where compatible,  rules and mechanisms for issuing and validating GOs 
(for green and low carbon gas) adopted by Third Countries. 
In addition, it is envisaged the definition of an entry -exit system that includes the 
transmission and distribution system, which should flow into a single national balancing area 
to ensure a level playing field between renewable and low carbon gases connected to the 
transmission network and those to the distribution network.  This measure would also have

significant impacts in terms of setting transport tariffs, and therefore should be evaluated 
taking into account all its “systemic” implications. 
 
Governance 
The prediction that gas DSOs will join the existing electricity DSO does not seem to be 
acceptable. In fact, hoping for - in the long term - the progressive consolidation of a common 
and coordinated vision of the development and management of energy infrastructures in 
their entirety, as in the case of transport, it is considered that, at this stage, an ad hoc entity 
representing the gas DSOs should be envisaged. It should also be structured and organised 
separately from the same entity of the electricity sector  (so as to ensure a more complete 
focus on the specificities - however existing - in the two distinct sectors and on the 
corresponding opportunities that they can offer for the evolution of the energ y system as a 
whole, hoping for the provision of structured forms of cooperation and coordination with the 
DSO entity of the electricity sector, as also occurred for ENTSO-G and ENTSO-E, which are 
created separately but whose progressive cooperation and in creasing coordination is 
envisaged) and should be involved in the definition of energy scenarios of supply and 
demand, as well as infrastructure planning and the definition of network codes. We also 
agree with what has been envisaged regarding the participation in the DSO entity of 
associations of distribution operators - whether European or national - as well as companies. 
In this regard, it is considered appropriate that the procedural aspects that will govern the 
constitutive phase of the association and the role of the associations of gas distribution 
companies, which are believed to be involved and must have proactive and decision-making 
power from the early stages of development / consolidation of the organization of the DSO 
entity, are more detailed. 
 
Infrastructure planning 
The proposal provides for the definition of a single network development plan per Member 
State, based on joint scenarios, aligned with the relevant National Energy and Climate Plan 
and with the EU ten -year plans, defined involving all gas, hydrogen  and electricity 
infrastructure operators. The plan will have to include information on the infrastructure that 
 
 
 
6 
can be or will be decommissioned , and on those that could be reused for the transport of 
hydrogen (repurposed). 
The Commission’s proposal would seem proportionate and balanced on this point, but there 
is no adequate recognition and involvement of the DSO: the new legislation should consider 
the decentralised production of renewable and low-carbon gas, thus considering the role of 
gas DSOs as enablers of a local virtuous circular economy, helping final customers to adopt 
more effective consumption and energy efficiency models.  
Similarly, the Commission's proposal lacks an explicit reference to the role of the electric 
TSO. It is noted that given the substantial amount of electricity required to power the capacity 
of electrolysers under the EU Hydrogen Strategy, the location of electrolysers is a strategic 
structural issue for electricity TSOs even more than for gas TSOs. We therefore consider it 
necessary to clarify that electric TSOs must have a primary role, together with gas TSOs, in 
the process of defining the location and suitable dimensions of power-to-X (P2X) plants. 
P2Xs are an interface between electricity and gas/H2 carriers and the involvement of electric 
TSOs in their planning process is particularly relevant considering that such installations 
affect both the efficiency and safety of the electricity system, being the latter bound by 
thermal limits in case of congestion. P2X can also h ave positive effects on the electricity 
system by providing flexibility and contributing to the integration of renewables. 
Finally, in the initial phase (until 2030) there could be a greater mix between networks 
dedicated to gas  and networks dedicated to hydrogen . The reuse of the infrastructure 
currently dedicated to gas for the transport of hydrogen can have advantages for end users 
of gas and hydrogen . It is essential to integrate and enhance the role of the national 
development plans of gas network operators to also identify hydrogen convertible assets. 
With a view to cost -efficient investments, it is proposed to first optimise the use of existing 
networks by making the necessary adjustments to accommodate renewable and low-carbon 
gases, including hydrogen, and to postpone to 2030, following appropriate assessments and 
necessary technological developments, the construction of any ad hoc infrastructure that 
should in any case be interconnected with the nearest gas transport/distribution networks.

Vertical and horizontal unbundling – Role of infrastructure operators 
The transitional arrangements allowing investors to benefit from the exemption from 
unbundling should have a reasonable duration, but not less than 10 years.  
In the early stages of the development of the hydrogen market, regulation could give 
operators the opportunity to test innovative solutions for the production and transport of 
hydrogen, possibly also pro viding for a “regulatory sandbox” regime in which a wider role 
can also be recognized for network operators. 
It is considered preferable that the package includes the three unbundling models already 
foreseen for gas, leaving to the Member States the possibility to dec ide the one to be 
adopted. This is especially true because the Commission ’s proposal for hydrogen differs 
from what is envisaged for the gas sector, with a more rigorous approach. Where the 
Member State provides for derogations from the applic ation of the unbundling framework 
with regard to production activity, national regulatory authorities should monitor the way in 
which network operators operate Power to Gas installations and prevent conflicts of interest 
in the use of such infrastructures as balancing/flexibility tools in the electricity market.  
A level playing field must be guaranteed in Europe for the H2 sector, irrespective of models 
already adopted in the gas and electricity sectors, otherwise the European hydrogen market 
will develop at different speeds and bottlenecks will occur.  
In add ition, the application of other provisions, such as horizontal unbundling, can be 
evaluated, taking due account of the management complexities deriving from the separation 
of the gas and hydrogen RABs, but also of the difficulty of providing in practice fo r a 
differentiated management, at the level of local distribution networks, of gas and hydrogen, 
which instead will share facilities, operating structures and general costs. It also would 
appear advisable to clarify in advance how blending is to be managed (see above - definition 
of criteria for the classification of gas/hydrogen networks and for the separate identification 
of investments for hydrogen and natural gas). 
The separation obligations, without prejudice to the need to ensure an adequate level of 
independence of the infrastructure manager and transparent and non-discriminatory access 
conditions, shall be proportionate to the need to promote and not prevent the start of the 
development of the hydrogen market by allowing all possible synergies with the gas sector 
to be exploited for the benefit of the whole system.

Security of Supply 
The strengthening of the regulation of storage for the purposes of SoS must take place using 
market mechanisms. 
Positive examples to refer to are France and Italy, where the allocation of storage capacity 
is based on regulated auctions that allow operators  to purchase capacity by expressing 
prices in line with market value. In the storage year that has just ended, these two countries 
have presented a better situation (in terms of capacity conferred and filling of storage) than 
other countries such as Germany, Austria and the Netherlands, where such mechanisms 
are not in place. In a virtuous mechanism such as the Italian one  - already market-based - 
further changes to the regulatory framework appear useful, in order to guarantee more 
flexible methods of using storage and a greater filling of storage , despite the contingent 
market price situations.  
Specific measures are introduced to improve transpa rency, cooperation and resilience, in 
particular the conditions allowing for the implementation of voluntary joint supply of strategic 
gas stocks to be used in the event of an emergency;  future common cybersecurity rules to 
address cybersecurity risks are  extended to renewable and low carbon gases; ‘automatic 
cross-border solidarity’ is introduced through new pre-defined agreements between Member 
States, clarifications and ex-post controls of compensation costs.  
To be clarified: 
o any obligations placed on the market players who use the storage, which we do not 
support. In general it should be guaranteed to all users an adequate level playing 
field. The measures taken should not distort competition; 
o arrangements for the remuneration of voluntary measures envisaged  for Member 
States and arrangements for the transfer of such costs; 
o the planned introduction of strategic storage and possible alternative market 
measures. 
In view of the current situation, it is considered urgent to introduce an ad hoc measures at 
European level to protect both citizens and businesses by acting on gas prices and ensuring 
their supply.  
In this sense, there are measures/instruments that the system can already develop to 
guarantee volumes and price. We refer, for example, to mechanisms on all European 
 
 
 
9 
wholesale markets that ensure that market operators do not sell/buy at exc essively high 
prices. In addition to these, it could be considered to flank the spot price mechanisms with 
new market mechanisms that stabilise the dynamics of price volatility both in the short and 
in the medium and long term, guarant eeing investments and therefore the coverage of the 
needs of the system. These mechanisms, aimed at guaranteeing the adequacy of the gas 
volumes available, will have to be appropriately designed and calibrated, so as to manage 
the tensions between supply and demand in the short term and to make volumes available 
on the market in a structural manner, providing long -term price signals to stabilise the 
dynamics and support the investments of the operators. These mechanisms should not 
replace the market but rei nforce it, being defined by each Member State under European 
direction and coordination.  
 
Prohibition of LT contracts relating to " unabated fossil gas " with a duration longer 
than 2049 
The economic situation of the energy markets confirms on the one hand the need to continue 
even more quickly along the path of energy transition by maximizing the production and use 
of renewable sources, on the other hand the key role that  the gas industry can and must 
play in the short, medium, and even long term to support this transition, ensuring the security 
of energy supply for European markets at reasonable prices.  In this perspective, it is 
important to decline this measure taking into account the related aspects of  Security of 
Supply. To fully highlight its implications, it  also highlights the need for clarification, with 
reference to:  
o what are the scopes of the measure (scope 1, 2 or 3)? 
o methods of application along the supply ch ain (must the abatement measures be 
explained in each contract)? 
o what exactly is meant by " unabated fossil gas" (the use of credits for off -set CO2 is 
allowed? Is abatement with CCS allowed)?

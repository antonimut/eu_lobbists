Born Free feedback: Environmental crime – improving EU 
rules on environmental protection through criminal law  
 
12 April 2022 
 
Born Free welcomes the proposed revision of the Environmental Crime Directive and the 
opportunity to provide feedback. Environmental crime is one the most profitable and growing 
crimes. Given its transnational nature and scale, environmental crime must be addressed at 
EU level, to ensure cross-border cooperation as well as a robust and harmonised approach 
to the detection, prosecution and punishment of environmental crimes across EU Member 
States, which have been identified as shortcomings of the Directive to date. 
It is also vital that the revised Directive addresses the incomplete and inconsistent 
implementation of the current Directive by Member States. This will be key to the 
effectiveness of the revised Action Plan against Wildlife Trafficking, and ultimately to the 
success of the European Green Deal and EU Biodiversity Strategy. 
Born Free is broadly in agreement with the Commission’s gap analysis of the current 
Directive and the objectives established for its revision, and we support the main elements 
of the initiative. 
We particularly welcome proposals to increase penalties and sanctions and to reinforce 
their equivalency and consistency across Member States. Environmental crimes are often 
seen as low risk/high reward activities by perpetrators due to a lack of deterrent sanctions 
and the limited likelihood of being apprehended or prosecuted. Penalties and sanctions 
must therefore be significantly reinforced to be truly dissuasive and effective. Their 
consistency and harmonisation across Member States is also essential to prevent 
criminals from continuing to take advantage of those Member States with weaker rules.    
We also support proposals to include clearer terminology and to strengthen the 
implementation of the Directive, in particular through the inclusion of national strategies, 
reporting requirements, and minimum requirements for resources, training, investigative 
tools and cooperation and coordination between competent authorities. These measures 
will also greatly contribute to the objective of harmonisation. 
Born Free also strongly supports the inclusion of provisions on the involvement and 
protection of civil society organisations and individual citizens who are essential actors in 
the detection and reporting of environmental crimes. 
We would also recommend the following additional elements for consideration: 
Scope and futureproofing  
• There is no general definition of what constitutes an environmental crime. Instead, the 
proposal relies on a list of secondary legislation. This means the Directive will not be 
future proof and will require amending each time new environmental laws are adopted. 
Recital 10 states that the Directive will “cover any updated or amended Union

legislation” but that it will be amended “when new legal instruments prohibit new 
conduct harmful to the environment.” However, it is unclear how these provisions will 
be effectively and systematically introduced in practice, how long it will be before new 
offences would be placed under the scope of the Directive, or how long Member States 
will have to ensure any such changes are effectively reflected in national law. The 
proposal also lacks clarity on the timescale in which the Directive and national 
legislation must be amended following the adoption of new legal instruments.  
 
• It is also crucial to specify that any amendment of the Directive to include new crimes 
under new legal instruments must be limited to this sole purpose and should not 
constitute an opportunity to change the substance of the Directive. 
 
• Greater legal clarity is needed around the definition of certain terms such as 
‘substantial damage’ or ‘negligible quantity’. We welcome the reference to ecocide in 
the recitals but a clear definition of what it refers to is lacking and should be added, and 
this crime should be included in the operational part of the Directive. 
 
• The Directive’s scope should be extended to cover illegal, unreported and unregulated 
fishing, and should include, as a criminal offence, the placing or making available on the 
Union market of wild species that have been taken in violation of national laws in their 
country of origin. 
 
• Online trade and cybercrime, which represent an increasing proportion of 
environmental criminal activities, should be explicitly mentioned in the directive to avoid 
loopholes and inconsistencies with other EU legislation. 
Sanctions and aggravating circumstances 
• The minimum standards for maximum limits for penalties and sanctions is still too low 
and should be increased for the legislation to be truly effective and dissuasive. Among 
others, the new legal basis of article 83(2) TFEU should be utilised to its fullest 
potential and a minimum standard for a maximum sanction of 15% of annual turnover 
for legal persons should be adopted.  
 
• The development and adoption of sentencing guidelines should be included to assist 
Member States in defining and applying appropriate (dissuasive, proportionate and 
aligned with the Directive’s provisions) and harmonised penalties and sanctions.  
 
• It is also essential that the Directive includes provisions ensuring that prosecuted offenders 
do not profit from their crimes, and provisions on the obligation to repair the damages 
caused, including covering the cost of reinstatement of the environment, compensating 
victims and financing measures aimed at combatting similar crimes, where feasible 
through the use of confiscated proceeds of crime.  
 
• Article 10 fails to provide details on the management of frozen and confiscated assets. 
The Directive should clarify how seized and confiscated live wild animals and wildlife 
products should be managed in a humane and appropriate way.

• Aggravating circumstances should include offences which cause destruction, 
irreversible or long-lasting substantial damage to the conservation of populations of 
wild animal species or cause the unnecessary suffering of animals (that are recognised 
as sentient beings under article 13 TFEU). 
Enforcement 
• Provisions for the establishment of specialised coordination bodies in every MS should 
be included, and jurisdictions should be extended as far as possible to increase 
environmental protection.  
 
• Statistical data as well as any quantitative or qualitative data gathered at Member State 
level, alongside any other information used in the preparation of evaluation reports, 
should be made public in its raw form rather than via consolidated reviews. All reports 
produced both by the Commission and Member States should also be made publicly 
available in full to ensure transparency for stakeholders.  
Monitoring, reporting and evaluation  
• Provisions on reporting and evaluation should be strengthened to facilitate the 
implementation and enforcement of the Directive and enhance its effectiveness. 
Information that will be required for the preparation of evaluation reports should be 
communicated to Member States alongside reporting deadlines to ensure Member 
States collate and transmit the required information in the appropriate format and in a 
timely manner. Standard formats should be developed and used to ensure information 
is relevant and objective, and to facilitate comparative analyses between Member 
States. In addition to Member States’ implementation reports, the Commission, which 
is responsible for the effective application, implementation and enforcement of the 
Directive as per Article 17(1) TFEU, should also produce its own reports evaluating the 
implementation and enforcement of the Directive, accompanied by practical and 
individualised recommendations. 
 
• A reason for the incomplete and inconsistent implementation of EU environmental law 
and guidance is that Member States are not provided with clear indicators that can assist 
them in meeting their objectives. As it stands, statistical data referred to under article 21 
of the proposal will not enable adequate evaluation of implementation. Quantitative and 
qualitative indicators should be developed specifically for this purpose.  
 
• The Commission should be empowered to conduct effective monitoring of the Directive 
and its implementation, including by developing a series of tools, processes and systems 
facilitating the effective and timely reporting of information and data by Member States. 
The establishment of a dedicated Unit within the Commission should also be considered 
to facilitate monitoring and evaluation, and to provide guidance to Member States. 
Cross-border cooperation  
• Given the transnational nature of environmental crimes, cross-border cooperation is 
essential to combatting them. Coordination and cooperation must be facilitated among 
competent authorities both within and between Member States.

• The mandate of the European Public Prosecutor’s Office (EPPO) should be extended 
to cover serious environmental crimes with a cross-border dimension for circumstances 
where reinforcement of the criminal law response is unlikely to be achieved through 
traditional channels. 
 
• In addition, given the global aspect of environmental crimes, cooperation with third 
countries should be addressed within the Directive.   
Civil society  
• In addition to people (e.g. whistle-blowers), the Directive should make explicit reference 
to organisations, such as civil society organisations, that play a key role in exposing, 
reporting and preventing breaches of environmental law. These organisations should 
also be protected from repercussions when reporting environmental crimes or assisting 
investigations.   
 
 
We thank you again for this opportunity to share our feedback and we are 
available to provide further information. 
 
 
About the Born Free Foundation  
Born Free is a UK-based international wildlife protection charity. We promote 
compassionate conservation to enhance the survival of threatened species in the wild and 
protect natural habitats while respecting the needs and safeguarding the welfare of 
individual animals. As a leading wildlife charity, we oppose the exploitation of wild animals 
in captivity and campaign to keep them where they belong – in the wild.

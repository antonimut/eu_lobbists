Yara position – Gas networks - revision of EU rules on 
market access 
Yara shares the EU’s ambition of a climate neutral future and is actively working to decarbonize our 
industry in collaboration with companies and governments. Renewable energy, renewable hydrogen 
and low-carbon hydrogen will play a key role in achieving the objective of a climate neutral society. To 
achieve climate neutrality, all players along the value chain need to work together as this will require 
massive investments and an unprecedented transformation of all sectors of the economy. The EU 
policy framework must create the right conditions to ensure that we can all succeed. In that context, 
Yara welcomes and in many aspects supports the European Commission’s proposal for a Hydrogen 
and Gas Decarbonization Package. The efficient and sustainable development of renewable and low-
carbon hydrogen can only be achieved through well-functioning gas markets and a reliable, dedicated 
as well as cost-effective infrastructure.  
 
Yara is on an ambitious path to decarbonize the production of mineral fertilizers. We have already 
reduced our greenhouse gas emissions1 globally by about 45 per cent since 2005 and in Europe by 
about 55 per cent. Our goal is to reduce our global emissions by a total of around 60 per cent by 2030 
and become climate neutral by 2050. Decarbonizing ammonia production, today predominantly based 
on the use of natural gas and steam methane reforming, is key to this process. Indeed, Yara is one of 
the historical pioneers of clean hydrogen technology, specifically for ammonia production, and strongly 
supports the roll out of green & blue hydrogen economy.  
 
Yara is pursuing projects that can allow a rapid replacement of fossil fuels and cut the carbon footprint 
of nitrate-based fertilizer products up to 80-90%. However, recognition must be given to the fact that 
achieving carbon neutrality is a marathon and not a sprint. For Yara to deliver a transition that is as 
rapid and cost-effective as possible, step wise decarbonization is necessary. The definitions and 
framework laid down in the EU Hydrogen and Gas Decarbonization package as well as other relevant 
legislation, lay the premises for the continuation of the decarbonization process we have embarked on. 
The overarching objective must be to reduce emissions as fast and cost-effectively as possible. This 
requires  the legal framework under discussion being clear, taking into account the specificities of 
industrial feedstock users and making sure it does not unduly hamper the step wise decarbonization 
that needs to take place. 
 
In this context, Yara is happy to take the opportunity given to contribute some relevant aspects which 
we believe will be helpful to accelerate the transition, facilitate the uptake of such gases, while at the 
same time safeguarding the competitiveness of European industry.

We welcome the consideration of low-carbon hydrogen, low-carbon gas and low-carbon fuels as 
technology neutral alternatives to renewable hydrogen, gases and fuels. However, a clear, 
legally certain and stable definition of low carbon hydrogen, gas and fuels is key 
 
➢ The definitions of the low-carbon alternatives are incomplete as to the methodology to be 
employed and furthermore lack a clear reference point, i.e. a benchmark to which the  
greenhouse gas emissions reduction should be applied. Yara calls for a timely and clear 
definition of this reference point to be included in the text of the Directive rather than in a 
subsequent delegated act. This is essential information, without which necessary investment 
decisions on decarbonization projects might prove difficult.  
 
➢ The European certification scheme CertifHy2 offers a technology neutral, intuitive and clear 
methodology that in our opinion could be used as template. This would entail that greenhouse 
gas emission reductions are compared to the fossil alternative, i.e. production of hydrogen 
through steam reforming of natural gas. The associated  benchmark should however be 
thoroughly assessed.  
 
➢ Regulatory predictability is essential for a healthy investment environment. Decarbonization 
projects demand, in many cases, very large investments and have long lifetime and payback 
horizons. The risk related to having a definition of low-carbon hydrogen that might change over 
time could hamper investments and the decarbonization process of industry. The definition and 
related framework should therefore be stable over time (e.g. for a defined period of 15 years). 
 
Yara appreciates that the Commission wish to introduce an ambitious, clear-cut and technology 
neutral threshold  to drive the decarbonization process. We, however, find it is important to 
point out the pitfalls that might result from such a binary approach.  
 
➢ When transposed into national regulation, the definitions are likely to be tied to incentive 
schemes, funding and targets. One could very easily end up in a situation where the best 
becomes the enemy of the good: 
 
o with the risk of cost effective decarbonization projects landing on the “wrong side” of the 
proposed 70% GHG-emission reduction threshold not being realized (for lack of funding 
and low incentives as the end-product is not “low-carbon” or “Gas Directive compliant”),  
o while those landing on the “right side” might not be competitive and achievable from an 
internal investment perspective and fail to secure funding from authorities or investors 
because the marginal abatement cost is higher than decarbonization projects in other 
sectors. 
 
The end-result for European decarbonization efforts would in this case risk being worse than 
without the legislative framework. 
 
➢ Therefore, if the aim is – as it should be – to decarbonize European gas consumption and 
industrial processes as quickly and cost-effectively as possible, one could consider a less binary 
approach appreciating that every unit of CO2 abated is a good and valuable one, contributing 
towards our common objective of a carbon-neutral future. Having several thresholds/categories 
would be one alternative we think could be particularly helpful.

Yara recognizes the effort to kick-start the development of the hydrogen economy by 
incentivizing blending of hydrogen into the gas stream. Due notice must however be taken of 
the fact that hydrogen blending in an industrial setting is a suboptimal solution that leads to 
inefficiency from both an energy, climate and a cost perspective. 
 
➢ Ammonia production is the most energy intensive part of fertilizer production. When natural gas 
mixed with hydrogen is used in ammonia production, energy is lost compared to use of pure gas 
streams - natural gas or hydrogen.  
 
−  Natural gas has about 3 times the volumetric energy density of hydrogen. A blend of 
natural gas and hydrogen therefore will lead to lower steam production in the SMR 
process. As a result, for an equal amount of ammonia produced, one may - depending 
on the blending rate and technology - need to compensate this by burning additional 
natural gas mix with the effect of cancelling out some or all of the CO2 saving from 
feeding hydrogen instead of natural gas into the SMR.  
− Furthermore, feeding hydrogen into the SMR, in order to produce hydrogen, is clearly not 
as efficient as using that hydrogen directly as input in the subsequent ammonia 
production process. This is why Yara strongly prefers a pure hydrogen backbone.  
 
➢ Beyond the unwanted effects on energy use and efficient decarbonization, there are a number of 
technical challenges associated with such mixture of hydrogen in the gas stream, both in terms 
of existing infrastructure and production facilities. Hydrogen blending has consequences for all 
stages of ammonia production (front-end, process and installations) and these consequences 
increase with the amount of hydrogen in the gas mix. For higher blending rates, significant 
investments will be necessary, raising the societal costs of the transition and undercutting the 
competitiveness of the fertilizer industry. 
 
➢ Hydrogen is a valuable molecule, and optimal use must be considered. To put in place a 
dedicated, independent and well-functioning hydrogen market and infrastructure is indeed 
essential to support a cost- and energy-efficient decarbonization of the fertilizer industry. This 
must be the clear and ultimate goal. If blending is nevertheless necessary for a limited transition 
period, Yara can technically live with levels up to 5% like suggested. This, however, with a 
significant associated operational cost.  
 
➢ It is furthermore of utmost importance that the final goal – the speedy development of a 
dedicated hydrogen backbone – is kept clearly in mind, and that the blending rate does not 
increase over time.  
 
Yara appreciates that the decarbonization of European gas consumption requires a portfolio of 
measures and is a complicated task. We are however of the firm opinion that technological 
neutrality, non-discrimination and sound market signals are the most efficient way to ensure a 
speedy and cost-effective transition. 
 
➢ There is a lack of clarity in the provision imposing a ban on long-term contracts for unabated gas 
beyond 2049. It should be explicitly clarified whether the abatement needs to take place 
upstream, or whether downstream abatement would still be permissible (i.e. natural gas could 
still be purchased but needs to be abated when consumed in one's installations, e.g. blue 
hydrogen production). If the intention of the provision is indeed a general ban on all natural gas 
contracts regardless of intended use, this explicitly goes against a technology neutral approach, 
with the unfortunate effects that might have on technological progress, investment and 
necessary step wise decarbonization processes. Furthermore, with a comprehensive, sound

European legal framework setting the stage for the decarbonization process, such bans should

➢ The EU has always been, and rightly so, proponents for network tariffs that are transparent, 
cost-reflective and applied in non-discriminatory manner. Yara firmly supports the uptake of 
renewable and low-carbon gases (including through re-purposing of assets where appropriate). 
However, tariff discounts on infrastructure are an inappropriate financing measure as they are 
not in line with principles of non-discrimination, transparency and cost-efficiency and unduly 
distort investment signals. This includes tariffs discounts for entry, exit and interconnections 
points as well as financial transfers between asset bases.

About Yara  
 
Yara grows knowledge to responsibly feed the world and protect the planet. Supporting our vision of a world without hunger 
and a planet respected, we pursue a strategy of sustainable value growth, promoting climate-friendly crop nutrition and zero-
emission energy solutions. Yara’s ambition is focused on growing a climate positive food future that creates value for our 
customers, shareholders and society at large and delivers a more sustainable food value chain.  
 
To achieve our ambition, we have taken the lead in developing digital farming tools for precision farming, and work closely with 
partners throughout the food value chain to improve the efficiency and sustainability of food production. Through our focus on 
clean ammonia production, we aim to enable the hydrogen economy, driving a green transition of shipping, fertilizer production 
and other energy intensive industries.  
 
Founded in 1905 to solve the emerging famine in Europe, Yara has established a unique position as the industry’s only global 
crop nutrition company. We operate an integrated business model with around 17,000 employees and operations in over 60 
countries, with a proven track record of strong returns. In 2021, Yara reported revenues of USD 16.6 billion.

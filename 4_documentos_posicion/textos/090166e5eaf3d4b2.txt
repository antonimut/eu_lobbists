VNG is a group of companies active across Europe with more than 20 companies, a broad, sustainable 
portfolio of gas and infrastructure services and over 60 years of experience in the energy market. The Group, 
headquartered in Leipzig, employs around 1, 400 people and achieved invoiced sales of around € 18,5 billion 
in the 2021 financial year. 
Along the gas value chain, VNG focuses on the four business areas of trading & sales, transport, storage and 
biogas. Based on this core competence in gas, VNG is focusing on new business areas with its "VNG 2030+" 
strategy. This includes in particular hydrogen and digital infrastructure. 
Making gas infrastructures fit for climate neutrality  
VNG shares the vision of a climate-neutral energy system and fully supports the goal of climate neutrality by 
2050. Gas as an energy source can make a significant contribution to achieving the climate targets. In the 
medium and long term, the energy carrie r natural gas will be continuously decarbonised through the 
increased use of renewable and decarbonised gases, especially hydrogen and biomethane.  
In view of the Ukraine war, however, it is now necessary - with a holistic focus on the economy, politics and 
society - to succeed not only in acute crisis management in shaping the future, but also in bringing together 
security of supply and climate protection to a much greater extent, while not losing sight of the economic 
viability and affordability of energy supply for industry and consumers. A more rapid transformation towards 
green gases and hydrogen as well as a diversification of natural gas supplies is therefore indispensable.  
In this context, it is particularly important to quickly create stable and reliable rules for a market ramp -up 
of renewable and decarbonised  gases. VNG would like to contribute its market experience to the politi cal 
discourse in order to find an efficient solution. It is positive that large parts of the proposal of the Internal 
Gas Market Directive and Regulation for the establishment of a hydrogen market correspond to the model 
of the existing and functioning gas market. 
Nevertheless, VNG has identified a number of points in the directive as obstacles to the universally desired 
rapid switch to renewable and decarbonised gases. These can be divided into three major fields:  
Definition and certification of gases, in  particular renewable and decarbonised 
gases 
The comprehensive definition and the largely associated integration of hydrogen into the existing internal 
gas market rules is a good approach by the EU Commission. This fundamentally leads to planning security 
and reliability. The uniform definition and certification of renewable and decarbonised gases throughout 
Europe should be supported. According to the definition, "low-carbon gas" and "low-carbon hydrogen" must 
fulfil a GHG reduction quota of 70 %.  
 
Certification of renewable and decarbonised fuels should be practicable (Directive Art. 8) 
The introduction of a uniform Europe -wide mass balancing system in accordance with RED II is in principle 
a positive signal for trade in renewable and decarbonised gases. However, the proposed use of a Union 
database appears to VNG to be excessively complex and entails too much administrative effort. A uniform 
Europe-wide database would make trading much easier, as no transfers between different mass bal ance 
systems would be necessary and uniform criteria would be used as a basis.  
An alternative to achieving the goal is a cross -border trading system based on guarantees of origin ( GOs) 
according to the "book & claim" principle, i.e. separate trading betwee n energy carrier and guarantee of 
origin. The certificates do not follow the entire supply chain of the gas but can be traded independently 
between the first-time injector and the consumer. This reduces the effort of having to provide proof of the 
green properties of the gas at every point in the transport chain. In addition to the introduction of the "book 
& claim" principle, it is necessary for the entire European gas network to be regarded as a mass balancing

Delegated act to define only by the end of 2024 is too late (Directive Art. 8, 5) 
It is also critical that the EU Commission reserves the right to define the calculation methodology for the 
above-mentioned renewable and decarbonised gases by means of a delegated act only by 31 December 2024. 
From VNG's point of view, this calculation methodology is a fundamental part of the definition. This brings 
uncertainty in the medium term. 
Major investment decisions for production plants are already pending, which of co urse also depend on the 
recognition of the gases produced as low-carbon. In the negative case, these decisions could be postponed 
by 3 years.  
▶ For this reason, VNG pleads for a faster definition of the calculation methodology, in the best case 
as part of the directive. 
Strict unbundling regulations from 2030 onwards may become a threat to the 
financing of the H2 infrastructure and create redundant structures 
The directive provides for justified exemptions from certain unbundling rules for a rapid ma rket ramp-up, 
but in VNG's view overlooks the fact that even announced regulations from 2030 onwards are already having 
an impact on the investment behaviour of market participants. The regulations on vertical and horizontal 
unbundling in the Commission's proposal represent overregulation.   
Particularly in view of the current geopolitical situation and the resulting pressure for rapid diversification 
of gas supplies, clear signals should be given for a rapid conversion of the infrastructure to hydrogen. In  
VNG's view, no obstacles should be placed in the way of existing network operators.  
 
Vertical unbundling (Directive Art. 62) 
The current rules for electricity and gas networks provide a good and functioning framework for vertical 
unbundling. The models a re common practice and do not currently suggest any competitive disadvantage. 
VNG sees no reason to tighten these rules for the development of a hydrogen infrastructure in the way 
proposed. The planned ownership unbundling makes it more difficult for existing gas network operators and 
integrated energy supply companies to enter the hydrogen economy or to build up the necessary hydrogen 
infrastructure. The limitation of the ITO model for hydrogen network operators would have the consequence 
that numerous Eur opean transmission system operators would be de facto excluded from the hydrogen 
transport business. This would thus jeopardise the very existence of a European backbone network for 
hydrogen. The ITO model is widespread in Europe in both the gas and electr icity sectors and is currently 
used by about 20 transmission system operators - 11 of which are transmission system operators in Germany. 
 
It should be noted that none of these ITOs has been found to be in breach of the unbundling rules by the 
EU or national regulators since the Third Internal Market Package came into existence. In a report on the ITO 
model, the EU Commission itself stated in  2014 that the model "seems to work in practice and is generally 
sufficient and appropriate to ensure effective separation of the transport business from the day -to-day 
generation and marketing activities“1.

An unchanged Art. 62 bears the risk of a "de-facto" expropriation for these companies after 2030, as the ISO 
model is not a viable alternative under the current conditions. This is also shown by experience from the 
electricity and natural gas sectors.  
The ISO model option for hydrogen network operators, which only applies under the condition that network 
operators already own hydrogen networks at the time of entry into force of the directive, is not an alternative 
in practice. The ISO model requires that a network operator leases the future hydrogen n etwork - including 
all converted lines - to a company that meets the requirements of ownership unbundling. It would be 
required to finance (or allow third-party financing) expansions that the independent company and regulator 
deem necessary and to assume operational risk that it could not limit or control. Existing gas ITOs own their 
infrastructure and have the necessary technical staff and know -how to operate it safely. If the ISO model 
were to be implemented, these companies would be obliged to transfer the operational business to another 
company, which would make integrated operation of both infrastructures with corresponding synergies 
impossible.  
 
Furthermore, it should be noted that the few cases of application in Europe are exclusively state -owned 
companies that have preferred this model to ownership unbundling for structural reasons, or small 
individual lines that do not justify their own operational business. It can thus by no means be assumed that 
the ISO model would represent a real alternative for VIUs. 
▶ VNG therefore pleads for the deletion of the time limit for the ITO model in Article 62 of the Gas 
Directive. 
 
Horizontal unbundling (Directive Art. 63) 
From VNG's point of view, unbundling under company law between hydrogen network operators on the  one 
hand and network operators for natural gas/biomethane or electricity is not advisable. On the one hand, 
such an approach leads to the establishment of inefficient and cost -intensive duplicate structures and 
prevents the exploitation of synergies between the operation of the gas network and the hydrogen network. 
For example, separate teams would have to be kept on hand for line conversion and maintenance. In 
addition, a separation under company law does not create any added value in terms of (cost) tran sparency. 
With regard to the distribution grids, a tightening of the existing unbundling rules would also have a 
considerable impact on the municipal utilities and municipal companies.  
The objective of controlling possible cross -subsidies and separating th e respective relevant costs for 
charging can be ensured without the described negative effects by an accounting separation with a 
corresponding supervisory structure.  
▶ For the reasons mentioned above, VNG advocates the abolition of the unbundling provision s under 
company law (deletion of Art. 63 of the Gas Directive). 
 
The role of storage in the future system must not be underestimated, but 
supported 
The discussion about storage levels in the current heating season has made it clear how important storage 
facilities are in our energy system and how the importance is expected to increase in the coming years. In 
the short term to secure gas demand for the  next winter and in the medium term for seasonal storage of 
renewable and decarbonised  gases and beyond as a short -term buffer of renewable generation in the 
electricity market.

Access to hydrogen storage (Article 33) 
VNG welcomes the Commission's proposal in that it gives Member States flexibility in the design of access to 
storage facilities. However, VNG also recognises the high importance of investments in hydrogen storage 
facilities for the market ramp-up. The obligation of regulated access to storage facilities via tariffs previously 
approved by regulatory authorities does not seem to create the necessary incentives for this. Meaningful 
market-based incentives for storage management are needed.  
▶ VNG advocates also allowing the possibility of free access to hydrogen storage facilities on a 
contract basis and only providing for state intervention where this is not possible.

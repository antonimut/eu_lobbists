DVGW welcomes the fact that the European Commission recognises the importance of 
renewable hydrogen for achieving climate neutrality in the European Union and that it has 
presented a regulatory framework for the development of a European hydrogen 
infrastructure with the proposals for a recast of the Gas Market Directive and Regulation.  
The German gas industry is not only ready for this transformation, but is already 
actively preparing the transition of the gas infrastructure to wards hydrogen, both 
technically and organisationally.  
As the situation in Ukraine illustrates once again, the European Union must now push 
ahead with the switch to climate-neutral gases at full speed. At the same time, rapid action 
is needed anyway to achieve  the ambitious climate targets of the “Fitfor55” package. It is 
therefore imperative that the European regulatory framework ensures that a rapid ramp -up 
of hydrogen can succeed. Since the rapid development of a hydrogen infrastructure is 
primarily a techni cal challenge, the regulatory framework should not create any 
additional hurdles.  Since a nationwide hydrogen infrastructure can only be developed 
from the existing gas infrastructure given the time requirements, we urge the 
Commission to implement an integrated regulatory framework for gas and hydrogen 
infrastructures. Only in this way , the common goal of the European member states of a 
resilient and crisis-proof energy supply can be achieved.  
 
Unbundling of hydrogen infrastructures 
A regulatory framework for the development of a European hydrogen infrastructure is 
urgently needed. Such a regulatory framework should not only enable the climate goals to 
be achieved quickly, but also enable resource - and cost-efficient planning. It is regrettable 
that the Commission's proposals for the unbundling of hydrogen networks do not meet 
these requi rements and even obstruct the development of a European hydrogen 
infrastructure.  
According to the unbundling requirements in Art. 65 and 66  (Directive), most existing gas 
network operators would be forced to either sell their gas networks for further use with 
hydrogen to another hydrogen network operator or to transfer all the tasks of a network 
operator while retaining all the financial risks. This would prevent the necessary rapid 
development of a hydrogen infrastructure from the existing infrastructure both in Germ any 
and Europe, as there would be no incentive for gas network operators to invest in 
hydrogen networks or to repurpose existing gas pipelines.  
However, a fast conversion of gas pipelines into hydrogen pipelines is the only way 
forward: As numerous current  studies from Europe and Germany show, the supply of 
hydrogen to the various application sectors can only take place via pipelines, given the 
enormous volume requirements and the ambitious climate targets. It is also clear that a 
rapid and cost -efficient t ransformation towards climate neutrality can only be made 
possible from an economic and business perspective by using and converting existing gas 
pipelines. This was recently confirmed by the European study project “Ready4H2”. The 
construction of a new hyd rogen infrastructure based on a greenfield approach would take 
decades and would hardly be accepted by industry and the population.  
 
2 
 
In particular, the timely transformation of industry towards climate neutrality can only 
succeed via a hydrogen infrastruct ure developed from the existing gas infrastructure. A 
look at Germany shows that such a supply task requires a conversion of both gas 
transmission and distribution networks – the German distribution network alone currently 
supplies 1.8 million industrial and commercial customers with gas and is thus a main pillar 
of the German industrial system. The supply of heavy goods transport with hydrogen and 
the injection of decentrally produced hydrogen into the gas  network are also not feasible 
without the 500,000 km long gas distribution network. As DVGW was able to show in the 
“H2vorOrt” project, the complete conversion of the distribution network to hydrogen is 
possible by 2040. The  gas network operators in “H2vorOrt” and the European projects 
“European Hydrogen Backbone ” and “Ready4H2” are already preparing for the efficient 
transition towards climate -neutral gases while also ensuring the supply of existing natural 
gas consumers. It  can be assumed that in practice this process will be similar to the 
conversion from L -gas to H -gas and that end users and networks will be successively 
converted to hydrogen. Integrated technical and organisational planning is an important 
prerequisite for this transition.  
Instead of effectively preventing the development of a hydrogen infrastructure from the 
existing gas infrastructure with impracticable unbundling requirements, the European 
regulatory framework should provide regulatory support for this transition in order to create 
the basis for a successful market ramp -up of hydrogen. In our view, the following 
recommendations must be taken into consideration: 
 The European regulatory framework for hydrogen should be open to all 
technologies and enable member states to use hydrogen in all sectors. This 
explicitly includes the use of hydrogen in the heat and transport markets. 
 As with electricity and gas networks, a differentiation should be made between 
“transmission” and “distribution” systems for hydrogen, so that hydrogen network 
operators do not have to fulfil unnecessarily excessive legal and organisational 
requirements at the local and regional level. 
 For an integrated technical and organisational planning of the hydrogen 
infrastructure from the existing gas infrastructure, the unbundling regulations for gas 
distribution system operators should also be applicable to hydrogen distribution 
networks. The same applies to the ITO model at the transmission level, which is 
widely used and generally recognised for its effectiveness, even after 2030. The 
proposed requirements for a horizontal unbundling of gas and hydrogen networks 
should also be adapted to meet the technical-organisational requirements of a 
conversion process. This means that prohibitions on the exchange of information 
and the transfer of commercially sensitive information between gas and hydrogen 
network operators should be removed. 
 Since achieving climate neutrality is a task for society as a whole, incentives should 
be created for a rapid conversion, for example by allowing revenues from the 
operation of gas networks to also be used to convert gas pipelines into hydrogen 
pipelines.

Blending of hydrogen 
We welcome that the European Commission, in addition to the development of a hydrogen 
infrastructure, also enables the blending of hydrogen into the existing European gas 
system as a technical possibility.  
In principle, the obligation to accept 5 percent hydrogen by volume at the cross -border 
connection points of the trans -European gas ne twork, as provided for in Article 20  
(Regulation), is technically possible. A large number of network customers have even 
requested higher blending levels.  
Blending is an important transitional solution on the way to a dedicated  hydrogen 
infrastructure via a conversio n of gas pipelines. While there are still some technical 
limitations in the existing gas infrastructure, these can be solved by replacing or upgrading 
some components.  
There are also industrial processes in which methane is used as a raw material, or 
sensitive melting processes such as in ceramic or glass production which require a stable 
gas quality. The technical implementation of blending specific hydrogen proportions  into 
the gas system may depend on various parameters (e.g. fluctuation of blend ed hydrogen 
proportions, network characteristics, regulation of blending in pendulum zones, gas 
applications) and requires further technical investigations.  
However, solutions are already available for the most important technical challenges:  
• Within the gas infrastructure, gas turbines are used in most cases to drive the 
compressors, which are supplied with the pipeline's operating gas as fuel. 
Depending on the manufacturer and model, these turbines vary greatly in their 
sensitivity to hydrogen. New turbine series of some manufacturers are, however, 
relatively insensitive towards hydrogen and tolerate hydrogen levels between 15 
and 100 percent by volume. Accordin g to the manufacturers, existing turbines have 
a hydrogen compatibility of between 0.5 and 5 percent by volume. A switch to H2 -
ready components is technically possible, but the necessary investment costs for 
the conversion process must be recognised by the NRAs. 
• The process gas chromatographs (PGC) used for gas quality determination have 
only been able to measure hydrogen since around 2015, which is required for 
correct calibration accounting for proportions > 0.5 percent hydrogen by volume. 
The replaceme nt of the devices is currently taking place in Germany but  will still 
take some time. There are currently around 900 natural gas filling stations in 
Germany that supply gas to vehicles. If natural gas vehicles are equipped with steel 
tanks, there is a limit of 2 percent hydrogen by volume due to a technical regulation 
(UN ECE R 110). If these vehicles are, however, equipped with composite tanks 
(pressure tanks made of fibre composite material), a higher blending of hydrogen is 
possible.

• Underground storages (pore storages, aquifers) also require individual examination 
with regard to formation behaviour and the activity of the underground biology.  
In general, the obligation to accept 5 percent hydrogen by volume at cross-border 
connection points is technically uncritical, provided that some components are renewed or 
replaced, which in turn requires a certain lead time. However, from our point of view, the 
following aspects must be taken into account: 
 The costs for network operator s must be recognised by the NRAs. Likewise, the 
costs for the necessary conversion processes for industrial and commercial 
customers must be regulated. In addition, any liability risks must also be cle arly 
regulated.  
 A Europe-wide regulation at cross -border connection points must always be in line 
with the European regulations on gas quality (here: EN 16726), as these are part of 
bilateral contracts and are legally binding for all market partners in gas supply. 
 
Network Planning 
As mentioned above, a fast, climate -friendly and economically efficient development of a 
hydrogen transport infrastructure is only possible by converti ng existing natural gas 
pipelines into hydrogen pipelines. This requires an integrated network development 
planning for gas and hydrogen networks. This can be ensured by expanding the NDP Gas 
to include hydrogen. Any o ther approach would lead to con siderable additional work and 
thus slow down the process. With respect to converting existing natural gas pipelines into 
hydrogen pipelines, the positive experience of the conversion from L -gas to H -gas shows 
that such an integrated process can be managed t ransparently and efficiently within the 
NDP Gas.  
 
Smart Metering 
From today's perspective, European harmonisation regarding the interoperability of smart 
metering systems is no longer possible. For this, detailed, graduated technical 
requirements would have to be defined in advance at the European level and anchored in 
the corresponding national laws and regulations in the member states. At the metrological 
level – both in measurement and in communication with smart metering systems – there is 
no interoperability either nationally or at  the European level. The high investments already 
made and long -term plans for further developments in the member states reduce the 
prospect of implementing interoperability of smart metering systems in the short term.  
The requirement made in Chapter 5, Annex 1 of the Directive to determine the share of 
renewable or low-carbon gas supply for the final consumer is in most cases very complex 
or not possible, as the gas quality at the individual entry points can be measured or 
evaluated, but not the origin of the gas.

  Consultation response  
 
Recast Gas Directive 
Overall, E.ON supports the Commission proposal to revise the current gas markets rules and establish 
a framework that aims to boost the hydrogen and decarbonized gas markets. We are fully committed 
to support the energy transition which is at the heart of Europe’s climate ambitions.  By integrating 
hydrogen into the regulatory framework and applying many rules and principles set out for gas to 
hydrogen, the Commission has taken an important step in the right direction. However, there is also a 
variety of provisions that seem to hinder more than to spur  the transition, especially with regards to 
the unbundling rules. Instead of imposing strict and centrally defined rules in the initial phase of the H2 
market ramp -up, a stepwise introduction of rules , which initially allows for enough flexibility  for 
national circumstances would be suitable. In order to successfully and cost-effectively transition from 
natural gas to renewable and decarbonized gases , the current proposal need s to be adjusted, 
particularly with regards the following points: 
 
1. The separation between transmission and distribution levels for H2 should be kept and the role of 
DSOs must be recognized: Whilst the distribution level plays a key role in the energy transition, the 
proposal focus es mainly on transmission networks:  In Germany around 99% of industrial, 
commercial, and other non-household customers receive their natural gas via the gas distribution 
networks.1 Besides, no “one-size-fits-all principle” may apply to hydrogen scaling-up across Europe 
and depending on various local parameters, some Member -States may opt for a decentralized 
hydrogen production set -up (close to consump tion clusters).  While building of 
new hydrogen networks will be needed, using existing gas infrastructures (i.e.  repurposing) will 
save time, costs and resources (both human and material resources are already limited today) and 
will therefore also be much more environmentally sustainable.  
2. The proposed vertical unbundling of hydrogen grids will hinder a rapid and efficient hydrogen 
market ramp-up and is to be rejected: The current regulatory framework applied to natural gas and 
power distribution networks has proven successful in operating, maintaining, and developing a 
secure, reliable, and efficient system while ensuring non-discriminatory access to systems users at 
fair market prices . These experiences should be taken into consideration. A strict er vertical 
unbundling requirement would create obstacles and would (financially) disincentivize the gradual 
and resource-saving conversion of existing natural gas pipelines to hydrogen. This would also delay 
the ramp-up of the hydrogen economy and would ultimately hinder distribution networks to play 
their crucial part in the timely transition from natural gas to H2 supply . Therefore, the current 
unbundling framework should also be applied to hydrogen networks.  
3. DSO should be allowed to operate H2 grids and gas grids in one company as this enables a step-by-
step gas infrastructure transition on the DSO level, transforming existing natural gas distribution 
grids into hydrogen grids. The requirement of the proposed horizontal unbundling  would bind 
significant costs for legal and organisational issues within the energy companies as well as hinder 
cross-fertilization in terms of knowledge, skills, and capabilities  instead of  driving forward the  
conversion of the grids. The inefficient establishment of duplicate structures must be avoided.  
4. DSO should only be obliged to connect a new production facility of renewable and low carbon gases 
if it is economically reasonable and technically feasible: Any connection obligations must be 
thoroughly assessed and, if necessary, adapted so that gas network operato rs can avoid 
unnecessary costs and stranded investments. Network operators must be enabled to make 
appropriate decisions as early as possible. The cost and associated benefits of a production facility 
connecting to the network at any point varies on the size of the plant, its location in relation to gas 
network entry point, whether it requires high or low pressure and the demand level. The quality of 
the gas to be injected is also relevant.  
5. Definition of low carbon gases: E.ON welcomes the inclusion of low carbon gases and low -carbon 
hydrogen in the proposed directive . However, the proposed definitions need some clarification, 
especially with regards to the methodology for calculating the 70% GHG savings threshold which

definitely must allow CCS. As the intended Delegated Act to determine the 70% GHG emissions 
threshold will only be published by the end of 2024 , an essential definition will initially remain 
unclear, which will create uncertainty for investors and will ultimately delay the hydrogen market 
ramp-up. In particular, experiences from the RED II Delegated Act demonstrate that despite aiming 
to provide more clarity, the pending publication in fact causes huge uncertainties in definitions and 
thus also in the market. We therefore believe a clear definition should be implemented already 
within the framework of this directive.  
6. Certification of renewable and low -carbon fuels: Leaving the certification up to Member States 
seems like a practicable approach . However, it must be ensured that these certifications are 
standardized so that they can be accepted in each Member State. The proposed use of a Union 
database would be complex and costly, and it must be ensured that the information provided are 
reliable and of high quality. As low-carbon gases should be easily tradeable across borders to fully 
realize the decarbonization potential, the use of guarantees of origin is the more suitable approach. 
The European Commission should be empowered to adopt a delegated act to introduce such a 
uniform system for guarantees of origin. In order to enable a single European market for a uniform 
system, it is in particular necessary that the entire European gas network is considered as a mass 
balancing system. In this way, the guarantee of origin for gas fed into the European gas grid can be 
traded within the EU, irrespective of the physical flow of the gas, and the properties contained can 
thus be transferred to each molecule of gas fed out of the European gas grid and consumed. 
7. Setting a minimum 70% GHG emission reduction threshold permits Member States to impose an 
even higher threshold. From a market perspective, it seems too early to offer such an additional 
threshold option for Member States in order to establish a single European market. The market is 
currently just forming, and its final shape remains unclear. So additional restriction s that may 
separate the market should be avoided.

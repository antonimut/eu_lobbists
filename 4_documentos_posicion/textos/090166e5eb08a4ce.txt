As part of the European Green Deal (EGD) - specifically with the “Fit for 55” package 
presented in the summer 2021 - the EU's energy and climate legislation is being 
comprehensively revised. The EU climate law provides the political and legal framework to 
achieve the overarching goal of climate neutrality in 2050 or CO2 reductions of at least -
55% by 2030. The "Fit for 55" package shows the way of implementation. The Austrian 
Federal Economic Chamber (WKÖ) wants to help ensure that the end result is a package 
that fulfils the climate protection purpose and is practicable, compatible and as 
stimulating as possible for the economy. 
 
I. General position about the “Fit for 55“ package 
 
The dossiers presented in the "Fit for 55" package aim to reduce CO2 emissions by 55% by 
2030 and thereafter to follow the path towards achieving the goals of the Paris Agreement, 
in particular towards climate neutrality from 2050. The Austrian Federal Economic 
Chamber stands behind these objectives. The goal of climate neutrality has been set; it is 
a matter of shaping the transition in such a way that growth is generated and losses are 
avoided.  
 
Given the dimension of the challenge, all technologies under consideration will have to 
make their contributions. For example, the conversion of CO2 into a raw material, more 
rail capacity for freight transport, hydropower as well as wind power and photovoltaics not 
only on roofs are needed. The approval procedures for the necessary energy and transport 
infrastructure projects must become much faster. These building blocks are missing from 
the “Fit for 55” package. 
 
The question of the availability of non-fossil energy sources in sufficient quantities would 
have to be addressed quickly by the EC. This requires a long-lasting huge investment push. 
The EU Commission is avoiding the problem of the long approval periods. Obviously, the 
long procedural durations also have to do with Union law, as the public interest in the 
energy transition is not always sufficiently weighted in approval decisions, even for high-
value and urgent projects. 
 
In the sense of the "climate protection through growth" orientation set by the highest 
bodies of the EU, the goal of international competitiveness of EU industry must be given a 
high, equal priority. The EU's climate and energy policy must not be a stumbling block for 
Europe as a business location and for its companies but must always be formulated with a 
view to maintaining international competitiveness. Economic success is the best way to 
ensure that Europe can actually fulfil the envisaged role of a pacesetter in global climate 
protection and that other countries and economic areas follow this path. 
 
II. General position about the proposal 
 
The proposal aims to establish a new EU regulatory framework to ensure the highest 
standard for measuring, reporting and verifying methane emissions. Companies in the 
energy sector would be required to measure and quantify their methane emissions at the 
  3/6 
source at the facility level and conduct comprehensive surveys to detect and repair 
methane leaks in their operations. In addition, the proposal prohibits venting and flaring 
practices ("venting" and "flaring") that release methane into the atmosphere, except in 
narrowly defined circumstances. Member states are also to develop plans to reduce 
methane emissions that include measurements of methane from abandoned mines and 
inactive wells. 
 
Regarding methane emissions from the EU's energy imports, the Commission proposes a 
two-step approach. First, fossil fuel importers will have to provide information on how 
their suppliers measure, report and verify their emissions and how they reduce them. The 
Commission will establish two transparency tools to show the performance and reduction 
efforts of countries and energy companies around the world in curbing their methane 
emissions: a transparency data bank, where data reported by importers and EU operators 
will be made available to the public, and a global monitoring tool that will show methane 
emissions hotspots inside and outside the EU, leveraging the EU's global leadership in 
environmental monitoring via satellites. 
 
To effectively address emissions of imported fossil fuels along the supply chain to Europe, 
the EC will, as a second step, engage in diplomatic dialogue with inter-national partners 
and review the Methane Regulation by 2025 with the aim of introducing stricter measures 
for fossil fuel imports when all data is available. 
 
The energy sector in the EU is responsible for about one-fifth of anthropogenic methane 
emissions. Agriculture (53%) and waste management (26%), which are not covered by the 
current legislative proposal, account for larger shares. However, the share of the energy 
sector is significantly higher if methane emissions from imported gas, oil and coal are also 
taken into account. 
 
In Austria, methane is the second most important greenhouse gas in Austria with a share of 
8.0% in 2017. Methane emissions decreased by 36.3% between 1990 and 2017. In Austria, 
72% of methane emissions in 2018 were caused by agriculture and 18% by waste 
management. The energy supply sector accounted for only 9% of methane emissions. 
Fugitive emissions from energy production and distribution (from fossil fuel extraction, 
processing, and transport) contributed 0.5% or 0.4 million metric tons of CO2 equivalent to 
total national emissions in 2017. Fugitive GHG emissions from energy production and 
distribution decreased by 39% over the 1990-2017 period. 
 
Methane emissions are continuously monitored and systematically controlled through leak 
detection and repair programs. Unlike agriculture, which has the highest methane 
emissions, losses from natural gas extraction, transportation, storage, and use are 
comparatively low. It is therefore important to implement reporting requirements and 
create transparency about emissions and possible measures.

In Austria, as mentioned above, 72 percent of methane emissions are caused by agriculture 
(in 2018). By far the greatest savings potential is therefore to be found in the agricultural 
sector, and this could be achieved relatively easily from a technical point of view. 
 
With an already small share of the total methane emissions in Austria of just 9 percent of 
the "emissions from energy supply and energy distribution", there are only very small 
reduction possibilities. This circumstance should also be taken into account insofar as the 
associated bureaucratic burdens (measurement, testing and reporting obligations, etc.) are 
kept as low as possible and attention is paid to practicality. In addition, an exception 
should be made for the import of small or very small quantities, especially in the case of 
the very complex presentation of supply chains. 
 
The Austrian gas industry has been making a significant contribution to reducing methane 
emissions for decades. This is also reflected in the Federal Environment Agency's figures on 
methane emissions: according to the Climate Protection Report 2020, in 2018 a total of 
72% of methane emissions in Austria were caused by agriculture, 18% by waste 
management and only 9% by the energy sector. According to the Austrian Greenhouse Gas 
Inventory, methane emissions from gas extraction, storage and distribution were 0.22 Mt 
CO2-eq in 2019. This is 0.28% of total Austrian greenhouse gas emissions. 
 
However, even this very limited focus of the draft regulation on very specific actors paints 
a distorted picture of reality for less informed circles because the impression could arise 
that the European gas industry is mainly responsible for methane emissions, which is not 
the case. 
 
The Commission itself states in the October 2020 Methane Strategy: 
"In the EU, 53% of anthropogenic methane emissions come from agriculture, 26% from 
waste and 19% from energy." 
 
The focus of the strategy should be a focus on the main emitters to reduce as much 
emissions as possible. However, there are no concrete legislative proposals for the other 
areas yet. The Austrian gas industry is also committed to gradually replacing fossil gas with 
green gas (biogas and hydrogen). The production of biogas represents a "win-win" situation, 
since on the one hand methane emissions from the waste and agricultural sector are 
minimized and on the other hand fossil gas can be replaced by renewable gas. 
 
In principle, we welcome the fact that the EU wants to create improved framework 
conditions for the further reduction of methane emissions and thus supports the efforts of 
the Austrian gas industry. However, the monitoring requirements demanded in its 
legislative proposal are clearly out of step with current practice and do not offer any 
added value for accelerated emission reduction: for example, they impose on plant 
operators a far too tight deadline for measuring, reporting and verifying the data. 
Furthermore, although the proposals are based on the established OGMP 2.0 initiative, 
they are not congruent with it or go far beyond it. This would unnecessarily increase 
bureaucracy, which would not provide an incentive to accelerate emission reductions. In

this context, we would like to point out the high additional costs that can be expected, 
which one transmission system operator alone estimates at a total of around € 16 million. 
 
Concerning the scope of application, a clarification is necessary that the applicability is 
limited to "public networks" (in the sense of gas market design TSOs, DSOs, ...). Therefore, 
the regulation is not applicable to private "industrial networks", such as "non-public" gas 
networks at industrial sites, even if third party consumers are connected through individual 
supply contracts. 
 
The European oil and gas industry has been working successfully for many years to reduce 
methane emissions through mandatory and voluntary programs (e.g., recompression, smart 
plug, replacement of gas-fired valves). We recommend that the responsible authorities 
carefully evaluate what activities have already taken place and are planned by companies 
on a mandatory or voluntary basis before defining new measures. We therefore call for a 
careful evaluation and recognition of the measures already implemented and the 
reductions in methane emissions achieved by the respective companies. 
 
In view of the in Austria quantitatively subordinate importance of the abandoned coal 
mining industry with regard to methane leakage, the related provisions of the draft 
regulation can be dispensed with completely for Austria without failing to achieve the 
purpose of the directive.  
 
Alternatively, measurements already carried out in the coal industry on the basis of legal 
obligations to avert danger should suffice in order to avoid a financial burden on the 
companies that is ineffective in terms of results. 
 
If the demand to recognise the previous monitoring measures in the coal industry as being 
in line with the Regulation is not successful, we recommend at least a discussion of 
threshold values. In order to identify significant methane emission sources, a threshold 
value should be developed above which monitoring measures have to be carried out. 
Essential aspects regarding spatial distribution and concentration are to be discussed here. 
For example, consecutive measurements over several years without emissions should also 
be included as a yardstick for the completion of monitoring. 
 
The existing differences between the member states and the companies must be taken 
into account. Efforts must be made to identify the major emitters and support them while 
requiring them to minimize their methane emissions. Companies with experience should 
share their knowledge with those that have less experience. 
 
A very critical issue is the management of and access to very sensitive information about 
the energy sector, which is the backbone of each member state's energy security. We see 
no benefit in the proposal to give third parties access to this information. An education 
and information campaign about methane emissions in all relevant sectors and the need to 
change our daily habits could be more effective.

We see the establishment of multiple competent authorities in the member states as 
imposing a huge administrative burden. Furthermore, it is questionable how the flow of 
information on emission levels between authorities, operators, auditors, the European 
Commission and IMEO will take place.

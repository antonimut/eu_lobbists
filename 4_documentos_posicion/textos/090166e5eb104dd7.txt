Regulation on methane emissions reduction in the energy sector 
Methane is the second greatest contributor to climate change and over 80 times more 
potent than CO 2 for global warming over a period of 20 years . Methane mitigation is 
one of the most cost-efficient methods to slow down climate change and avoid 
irreversible tipping points.  
In 2021, momentum built to address the catastrophic impacts of methane emissions 
and led to the Global Methane Pledge, initiated by the EU and the US and officially 
launched at the COP26, with more than 1 10 countries committing to reduce their 
methane emissions by 30% by 2030. To achieve this goal the EU will need to 
maximise its reductions in the oil, gas and coal sectors far beyond 30%, since sectors 
such as agriculture are more challenging to address.  Only through strong standards 
to aggressively cut methane emissions both inside the Union and  from the EU’s 
consumption of imported oil, gas and coal, will the EU be able to strengthen its global 
leadership on this issue. 
Clean Air Task Force (CATF) welcomes the legislative proposal on methane 
emissions reduction in the energy  sector as a very positive step to address one this 
critical climate pollutant. However, based on its twenty years of experience in methane 
mitigation, CATF has suggestions on how to improve the proposal, with state-of-the-
art requirements and ambitious measures, to ensure the effectiveness of the domestic 
measures and develop far stronger ambitions on imports.  
KEY RECOMMENDATIONS  
Imports

IMPORTANCE OF METHANE EMISSIONS MITIGATION 
Methane only stays in the atmosphere for about 12 years but, on a 20 year period, is over 
80 times more potent than CO 2 for global warming 1. Methane emissions have highly 
damaging consequences for both the European citizens’ health and the climate. 
On health, m ethane is a precursor gas for harmful ground -level ozone (O 3).  Ozone 
produced from anthropogenic methane emissions are responsible for tens of thousands 
of premature deaths globally per year , including thousands within Europe 2. CATF 
CutMethaneEU campaign has recorded large methane leaks across Europe.  
On climate, methane is the second greatest contributor to global warming. It is responsible 
for about half a degree of warming so far, and its levels are rising fast . The atmospheric 
growth rate in 2021 was the fastest in recorded history3. Moreover, climate change risks 
triggering the natural release of more methane due to rising t emperatures, ice melting,  
and the emergence of new wetlands.

The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6)  stressed that deep reductions in methane 
emissions will be needed by 2030 to stay below an increase of 1.5°C4. Reducing methane 
emissions will be indispensable to have a chance to reach  the Paris Agreement’s goals. 
Furthermore, reducing methane emissions will be crucial to prevent some of the near-term 
and irreversible impacts  of climate change we are currently headi ng towards. Due to its 
short-lived nature, cutting methane emissions  provides a unique opportunity to quickly 
slow the rate of global warming. As CO 2 remains longer in the atmosphere, measures 
addressing carbon dioxide will first lead to CO2 accumulating slower. Methane and carbon 
dioxide are therefore complementary measures that work together, with the methane 
reductions slowing down the pace of global warming as deep decarboni sation efforts 
accelerate to deliver significant CO2 reductions. 
Fortunately, affordable  (and often profitable)  methane reduction technologies  available 
today could prevent 0.3°C of global warming by the 2040s 5.  The Global Methane 
Assessment found that currently available measures could reduce methane emissions 
across all sectors by 45% by 2030, with nearly half coming from the energy sector6. The 
oil, gas, and coal sectors can significantly reduce their emissions at very little or negative 
cost with existing technology. 
The Methane Regulation, as the first of its kind  in the EU , needs to set ambitious 
requirements reflecting the best practices and technologies being utilized globally in order 
to protect the health of its citizens, slow down global warming, and set standards that 
could be used as example by other countries developing methane legislations. 
METHANE MITIGATION MEASURES ON IMPORTS  
What is the issue? 
The EU imports 70% of its hard coal, 97% of its oil , and 90% of its fossil ga s7.  
According to the Commission, 75 to 90% of the methane emissions associated with 
the EU energy consumption are emitted outside of the EU borders8. Only cleaning up 
EU domestic emissions of methane would consequently ignore most of the methane 
emissions related to EU consumption. 
Adopting monitoring and mitigation measures on imports would match calls from civil 
society, the European Parliament, and major energy compan ies. In its methane INI 
report, the ENVI Committee called on the Commission to “make all fossil fuel imports 
into the Union conditional on their compliance with EU regulations on MRV and LDAR 
and the rules on venting and flaring, applicable to the entire fossil fuels supply chain, 
up to and including production.”9 Energy companies such as Shell, BP, and Total also 
supported the introduction of EU rules to all oil and gas consumed in the EU10.

The Commission did not include monitoring and mitigation measures in its import 
section because the enforcement and verification of such measures w ould be 
“challenging” and because of energy security considerations 11. However, on the 
implementation challenges, the Commission adopted mandatory measures on 
imports with enforcement and verification frameworks on other issues such as illegally 
harvested timber or goods produced with slavery and forced labour. More recently, 
the CBAM and the due diligence proposals involve measures on the parts of the value 
chains outside the EU. Adding mandatory requirements on imports in the Methane 
Regulation would cons equently be far from unprecedented and could build on  the 
lessons from these legislations.  
On the Commission’s second argument of energy security, the measures on imports 
could be drafted to ensure compliance without endangering security of supply by 
implementing penalties on importers in case of violation rather than a ban of the non-
compliant goods. Moreover, any measures on imports added to the Regulation would 
not be implemented before 2025, which would leave time for importers to clean up 
their value chains.  
In addition, a s the largest importer of fossil gas and oil in the world, the EU is in a  
unique position to lead internationally and to support the global implementation of 
strong MRV , LDAR, and venting and flaring measures . The EU should not 
underestimate its market power and should use it to influence global standards.  
Furthermore, adding measures on imports to enforce methane mitigation would allow 
companies to save fossil gas, as the measures keep gas in the system inste ad of 
allowing the gas to be emitted.  Therefore, these measures have little or no impact on 
energy prices. This is especially true during this time of higher prices for natural gas 
and oil, where nearly everything in the draft legislation is either zero cost or profitable. 
Finally, as methane is a short -lived super-pollutant and cutting methane emissions 
aims to slow-down climate change, any postponement of measures on imports to a 
second legislative process starting at best in 2025 would defeat its goal and put at risk 
the chances to reach the Paris Agreement objective. 
How can the EU solve it? 
Expanding the regulatory framework on MRV, LDAR and venting and flaring to 
imports 
The upcoming EU standards on MRV, LDAR, and venting and flaring should apply to the 
full supply chain, up to and including the point of production . This framework would be 
based on a reporting and certification system to ensure and verify compliance , similar to 
other third-party certification systems used for other EU  laws effecting production or 
practices outside the EU borders , for example on deforestation or child -labour. Non -
compliance would not lead to a ban on the placing of good in the internal market, but to 
penalties. 
To ensure the respect of WTO rules, the Regulation should establish a process allowing 
importers to rely on alternative measures when deemed comparable in effectiveness 
and a system of equivalence  via an approval process administered by the 
Commission.

MEASUREMENT, REPORTING, AND VERIFICATION (MRV) 
What is the issue? 
Methane emissions inventories have so far been the object of demonstrated low bias: 
global anthropogenic fossil methane emissions are up to 70% higher than reported12. 
A robust and scientifically rigorous Measurement, Reporting, and Verification (MRV) 
system is necessary to identify issues and efficient measures, and to assess the 
progress achieved.  
How can the EU solve it? 
CATF welcomes the decision of the Commission to build on the Oil and Gas Methane 
Partnership (OGMP). The gradual two -steps approach, going from measurement of 
source-level methane emissions first and then adding site-level measurement follows 
the OGMP framework and is an efficient approach to progressively develop more 
accurate monitoring of methane emissions. 
However, the deadlines in Article 12 of the Regulation are not ambitious enough. The 
proposal includes a deadline of 36 months for operated assets and 48 months for non-
operated assets to add site -level measurements. The companies in the OGMP 
planned to reach that level earlier (2023 for operated assets), demonstrating that this 
requirement could be realistically advanced since it would not require new 
technologies or methodologies. Implementing site-level measurement together with 
the source-level ones could be implemented within two years. Moreover, it is unclear 
which assets would be included in the non-operated assets category since every asset 
is operated by a company or entity. This distinction should be deleted. 
Besides the timeline, th e site-level measurements should also be  unannounced and 
take place at least once a year  on every site to ensure that some operations are not 
postponed to non-measurement days and that every site is properly taken into 
account.  
The Regulation should also specify in Annex or by Delegated Act requirements for 
sampling strategies to ensure that samples are representative  and of adequate size. 
At the moment, companies are not required to prove that site measurements represent 
a representative and statistically significant sample of their overall facility footprint. 
LEAK DETECTION AND REPAIR (LDAR)  
What is the issue? 
Methane leaks refer to the escape of gas from static components (connectors, valves, 
regulators, and hatches) throughout the oil and gas sector, as well as super-emitters 
– infrequent but very large emissions events that are responsible for up to 50% of the 
leaks. Leaks are widespread in the oil and gas industr y and are due to many causes 
like thermal or mechanica l stresses, human error, or w orn-out equipment. Super -

emitters are caused by some improper condition at an oil and gas site such as valves 
and hatches that stuck open or flares that are unlit.  
Leaks are heterogeneously distributed, they are unpredictable, and they are not well 
correlated with specific characteristics allowing for predictive models. The recent 
campaign CutMethaneEU recorded methane emissions across Europe and 
highlighted the widespread issue of leaks in the EU.  
As leaks cannot be predicted, frequent inspections of all facilities, as part of an LDAR 
program, are necessary to mitigate methane emissions from leaks, and can be 
implemented with  mature technology. LDAR programs can be extreme ly cost -
effective, as in most cases the value of saved gas exceeds the cost of repairing the 
leak13.  The higher the gas price the more cost effective the program will be, but even 
in the very low-price environment of the United States in recent years, LDAR programs 
remained cost-effective. 
How can the EU solve it? 
The Commission proposal is a good starting point and includes the key elements of 
an efficient LDAR programme: regular mandatory instrument -based LDAR, with 
deadlines for repair and resurvey. Some points however need to be improved. 
First, LDAR should be monthly instead of quarterly. Based on US EPA data, emissions 
from leaks can be cut by about 80% with quarterly inspections and 90% with monthly 
ones14. Companies should also move to continuous emissions monitoring as soon as 
the technology becomes commercially available. 
Second, the legislation should require companies to repair all leaks found during the 
LDAR survey, rather than setting a threshold for determining when repair is warranted. 
The standard instruments used for LDAR (optical gas imaging cameras) are able to 
detect leaks, but are not able to quantify methane concentrations of leak rate —
quantifying leaks is therefore more time consuming and more expensive.  Once 
identified, fixing leaks is very cost effective. Therefore, in keeping with precedent from 
other jurisdiction, it is more efficient to require operators to simply repair all identified 
leaks, rather than quantifying and evaluating each leak compared to a threshold. 
Third, requirements for LDAR programmes should be detailed, in an Annex or a 
Delegated Act. Requirements are needed on the distance between the equipment 
checked and the inspection instrument, the weather during the leak screening (since 
leaks are very difficult to find during inclement weather) , inspector qualifications, 
calibration techniques, etc. At the same time, it is critical that the requirements allow 
flexibility to allow the use of new technologies as they become commercially available. 
Fourth, the Regulation should establish an EU -wide certification and accreditation 
framework to avoid discrepancy between Member States.  
LIMITS ON VENTING AND FLARING (LVF)  
What is the issue? 
Venting and flaring have devastating environmental impact as they represent large 
emissions of methane. They also represent an economic loss as gas is lost while it 
could have been available for productive use otherwise. In 2020, flaring reached levels 
that haven’t been reached since 2009, highlighting the need to address the issue to 
efficiently mitigate methane emissions.

CATF welcomes the inclusion of a ban on routine venting and flaring in the Regulation. 
We also support the approach of a general exclusion with a list of specific exceptions. 
However, significant amounts of equipment venting are  not covered by the 
regulations.  Since equipment venting is a very large portion of total methane 
emissions from oil and gas operations, this omissions will endanger the goals of the 
ban on venting and flaring. 
How can the EU solve it? 
In the proposal, venting is prohibited but for a few situations, one of which is “during 
normal operations of certain components, provided that the equipment meets all the 
specified equipment standards”.  This is something that is also known as venting-by-
design: oil and gas equipment that  during normal operations vents gas to the 
atmosphere. While this has been accepted pr actice in the industry for many years, 
technologies and practices have been developed and implemented in other 
jurisdictions that greatly reduce or eliminate emissions from all types of equipment that 
are the source of significant methane emissions.   
Yet, without clear equipment standard requiring the use of lower - or non -emitting 
equipment, operators will be free to continue to use out -dated, high -emitting 
equipment.  This is a major problem and can only be solved by establishing specific 
equipment standards for  equipment such as storage tanks, compressor seals, 
pneumatic equipment, and dehydrators . The proposed regulations allude to 
“equipment standards” but nowhere are the y included in the draft.  A Delegated Act 
specifying these standards would be required to prevent the ban on routing venting 
and flaring to be negate d by the abuse of the venting -by-design exception.   The 
specified standards should be developed based on the best practices and precedents 
from other jurisdictions.   
Additionally, the Delegated Act should also specifically address venting-by-design 
from equipment handling LNG (see below). 
ABANDONED AND UNUSED OIL AND GAS WELLS  
What is the issue? 
Europe counts many closed or abandoned oil and gas wells still emitting methane. 
The issue of abandoned and unused wells is complicated by the difficulty in identifying 
which company owns them and is therefore responsibl e for the ir clean-up. In some 
cases, due to the way these wells were decommissioned, no owner can be identified 
and held accountable for the emissions and the measures to tackle them , meaning 
that public authorities must address those wells.  
How can the EU solve it? 
The Commission’s proposal sets out measures for the inventory, installation of 
measurement equipment, and submission of reports but does not include a date by 
which Member States shall develop and implement a mitigation plan to remediate, 
reclaim or permanently plug inactive wells nor a date when such impl ementation 
should be completed. The plan should describe, at a minimum, the schedule of 
addressing each inactive well, including the actions to be performed; what person or 
entity, if any, owns such well; and the projected end date of all remediations, 
reclamations, or pluggings of inventoried inactive wells.  A plan could realistically be 
developed once the inventory is completed and implementation of mitigation plans 
should begin no later than 12 months after the first measurements are reported.  
Specific measures should also  be added on the wells where no owner can be 
identified.

PUBLIC PARTICIPATION IN IMPLEMENTATION AND COMPLIANCE   
What is the issue? 
With the development of satellite, aerial, and ground-based technologies to detect and 
record methane l eaks, non -profit organisations are now contributing to identifying 
leaks and venting at oil and gas infrastructure and coal mines. Some NGOs are either 
conducting OGI camera checks themselves or investing in satellite technologies. 
However, this is no form al mechanism for incorporating the results of these 
observations into a formal complaint that regulators or companies must act upon. 
How can the EU solve it? 
The participation of non-governmental civil society organisation should be encouraged 
by allowing them to lodge substantiated complaints o n a breach of the EU Methane 
Regulation. The current proposal requires the persons to have suffered injury due to 
the breach in order to be able to lodge a substantiated complaint. Methane should 
follow the model of the Timber Regulation and delete the “injury” requirement15. 
LNG 
What is the issue? 
As the EU is redefining its energy mix, special attention should be paid to the methane 
emissions from facilities that handle LNG as well as coherence on this issue among 
the different Fit for 55 legislation. LNG, by its nature as a cryogenic liquid, presents a 
number of unique risks for emissions (including large emissions) from: equipment and 
seal failures associated with cryogenic temperatures; working losses from transfers of 
LNG between vessels and boil-off while cooling vessels; losses from purging residual 
LNG or gas-phase methane from transfer lines and storage vessels; management of 
boil-off gas during storage (in static vessels or tankers).    
How can the EU solve it? 
Special attention shou ld be paid to methane emissions from LNG in the Methane 
Regulation, to ensure that methane leaks in LNG sites are well -covered by the 
legislation and that the methane emissions from the full LNG value -chain are 
addressed. Additionally, the Delegated Act specifying emissions standards for 
equipment venting -by-design described above should specify standards for 
equipment handling LNG , to ensure that best practices are used to minimize 
emissions from these potentially high-emitting facilities.  Moreover, companies should 
use best practices for reducing methane from LNG16.  
COAL 
CATF welcomes the ambitious requirements in the coal chapter of the legislative 
proposal and calls on the Parliament and the Council to prevent any watering -down 
of ambition. To strengthen the proposal, the Regulation should clarify the specific 
criteria companies are required to demonstrate to Member States to opt for venting 
instead of flaring, or for venting or flaring instead of utilisation or mitigation.

INTERNATIONAL ACTION  
Global methane emissions from human sources increased faster in 2020 than at any 
time since at least the 1980s17 and, in the absence of ambitious policies, are projected 
to continue rising. Methane emissions are a global issue, and their mitigation will 
consequently require global efforts and commitments. 
In November at COP26, the Global Methane Pledge, which was initiated by the EU 
and the US , was launched with over 100 signatories that committed to reduce their 
methane emissions globally by 30% by 2030 compared to 2020 level.  
To support the signatories in reaching their goal, the EU should foster global 
collaboration and coordination on methane mitigation. We recently saw  a growing 
interest in tackling methane emissions, not only in the EU but also in other parts of 
the world. The US, France, and Argentina all recently talked about the need to 
decrease methane emissions. Besides declarations, actions are also being taken 
around the world. Canada and Mexico have both issued strong regulations for 
methane. The US is in the process of restoring  and strengthening  its regulations. 
Colombia just recently finalized regulations and Ecuador and Nigeria are currently in 
the process o f developing methane mitigation policies, with Vietnam and China 
planning to do the same soon. It would therefore be a great opportunity for the EU to 
take the lead, propose closer coordination on methane mitigation, and offer technical 
and/or financial su pport, as well to call on the Member States to develop methane 
strategies and share best practices.

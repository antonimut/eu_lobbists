   
 
ENI POSITION PAPER | REGULATION ON METHANE EMISSIONS REDUCTION IN THE ENERGY 
SECTOR 
The increased ambition in emissions reduction proposed in the EU Green Deal, which Eni fully 
subscribes to, requires additional commitment and the addressing of all GHGs and not just CO2. As a 
signatory of the Methane Guiding Principles, OGCI and OGMP 2.0 , Eni is keen to provide valuable 
contributions to this process. Indeed, we support the proposal of a European -wide law to combat 
methane emissions, with the goal of achieving climate neutrality by 2050. In this regard, we endorse 
the European Commission's goal of improving the accuracy of information about methane emission 
sources and reducing emissions. Fully aware of the importance of reducing methane emissions as 
much as possible, Eni has indeed been participating in numerous international partnerships  and 
initiatives for several years  and has already adopted a broad strategy for mitigat ing the emissions 
associated with its operations. 
It seems however that certain of the requirements included in the Regulation relating to leakage 
measurement, verification, and mitigation activities, can and should be improved in order to avoid 
generating costs for industry and consumers without any noticeable environmental benefit. 
Consideration should also be given to divergent costs and challenges faced by all operations in fulfilling 
the obligations. 
In particular, we notice:  
- misaligned requirements in terms of scope, boundaries and frequency, of some key points of 
Monitoring, Reporting and Verification (MRV) activities with respect to the OGMP 2.0 
standard; 
- excessively prescriptive approach to Leak Detection and Repair requirements  (e.g. LDAR 
provisions should be opened for more outcome-focused and risk-based approaches and allow 
the use of a wide range of technologies); 
- overly broad definition of inactive wells that would significantly increase MRV by including 
thousands of permanently plugged and aband oned offshore and onshore wells whilst not  
contributing to the Regulation’s objectives and impacting with significant cost to the industry 
and consumers; 
- challenging flaring/venting standards. We agree with the regulation principle of eliminating 
venting a nd flaring  practices, but there should be some thresholds for the materiality of 
venting and a criterion of cost efficiency of mitigation measures versus benefits; 
- with regards to the reporting requirements related to gas imports from third countries , an 
incomplete regulatory framework  (specifically in respect to the proposed definition of 
“importer” and to the proper addressing of  LNG), as well as controversial liability profiles 
envisaged for importers. 
We therefore suggest to improve the proposed regula tion, taking into account that r equirements 
should balance the emission rate and the associated probability of occurrence against the costs for 
operators of complying with obligations. In particular, the principle of proportionality should be 
considered, avoiding obligating high -cost measures for end -users and Society with little or no 
mitigation effect.  
We therefore recommend the following: 
1. With regard s to the requirements on Monitoring Reporting and Verification (MRV) a 
greater alignment with the OGMP framework (which the Commission deliberately draws 
inspiration) should be encouraged, e.g. in terms of independent reporting and verification 
obligations and also in relation to the expected timelines for their implementation. Terms 
like ‘quantification’ and ‘measurement’ are at times used interchangeably and should 
better reflect the flexibilities of the OGMP 2.0 framework, including materiality provisions.

Moreover, reporting requirements should ensure consistency and alignment with current 
frameworks (e.g. OGMP 2.0) to be more effective. 
2. With regards to LDAR requirements, the proposal for a regulation can be improved by 
moving from a prescriptive approach to a regulatory framework more oriented towards a 
risk-based approach, in order to prioritize actions and maximize the benefit in terms of 
environmental protection. Although setting a minimum frequency helps with establishing 
a level-playing field and promoting industry consistency, strict fixed frequencies seem not 
appropriate in cases where no emissions have been identified in multiple surveys, 
especially in cases of low emission potential. We also believe it is appropriate to guarantee 
the opportunity to use different measurement methodologies and technologies, 
recognized and validated, according to the specific characteristics of the assets and the 
various segments of the supply chain. 
3. Regarding the requirements for mitigating emissions fro m venting and flaring, the 
definition of terms ‘venting’ and ‘flaring’ can be better aligned with those used by the 
World Bank's Global Gas Flaring Reduction Partnership (GGFR). Moreover, the proposed 
flaring standards is particularly challenging for low emission potential facilities, and more 
flexibility is needed to prioritise actions and ensure a cost-effective approach. For 
example, it is important to warrant a lead time for implementing the venting and flaring 
provisions and address the issue of materiality, also in line with what is provided for in the 
OGMP framework , for venting/flaring leading to non -material emissions. Immediate 
repairs shall be carried out whenever possible, but the Regulation shall allow for adequate 
repair times that respect the technical, safety, environmental and administrative 
constraints. It is also necessary to consider, the technological constraints regarding the 
methodologies for detecting the efficiency of combustion in torch. The criteria for 
requirements of weekly inspection must also be better detailed.  
4. With regard to the monitoring requirements on abandoned wells, also in this case the 
issue of materiality must be addressed, and the detailed criteria not defined to date must 
be detailed (e .g. duration of inactivi ty of the wells, duration and methodology of the 
measurements, etc.) 
Furthermore, in relation to the reporting requirements for importers: 
1. the definition of “importer” is unclear, in particular the current wording defining the importer 
as the one who is “placing fossil energy from a third country on the Union market” is too vague, 
and it should be better framed in the context of the EU customs law 
2. the regulatory framework should properly consider LNG imports: for example, amongst the  
information to be prov ided by importers (ref. Annex VIII), it is requested to identify the 
countries through which the energy was transported until it was placed on the Union market. 
While this is possible for imports by pipelines, in case of LNG imports, carried by sea, this 
information is not relevant, and it might be not available 
3. the regulatory framework shall consider the information that is actually available to importers 
under current supply contracts; importers may not have any contractual or legal leverage over 
exporters or produces to request the information required and/or control the quality of data 
provided. It may also be impossible to clearly identify an exporter or producer. In those cases, 
or where such entity refuses to provide the requested information or provides incomplete or 
inaccurate information, the importer cannot be held liable where it is outside of his legal or 
contractual responsibility, beyond all reasonable endeavours that have been undertaken to 
acquire the information requested. In particular, it should be avoided that importers could be 
exposed to sanctions if they fail to report information, held by their counterparties, that they 
do not have access to. 
   
 
4. In relation to possible additional obligations, including methane emissions standards or 
targets (see Article 27, par. 3 letter d), multiple issues need to be considered, including the 
design of appropriate implementation framework.  It is extremely important that such 
potential obligations on imports target those players in the value chain who have control over 
the methane emissions of the product being exported/imported. The imposition of such 
obligations on the European importers would not help reducing methane emissions, as gas 
importers under existing supply contracts do not have any contractual leverage to reduce the 
methane emissions along the value chain and would be unduly exposed to the risk of non -
compliance.

EU Rules for Certifying Carbon Removals  
Background 
The lack of a common EU standard for carbon removals is currently a major barrier to upscaling.  
This initiative will aim to reduce that barrier by proposing EU rules for monitoring, reporting and 
verifying the authenticity of carbon removals and thereby enabling them to be certified.  
These rules should take into account existing certifications under standards such as Verra, Gold 
Standard, etc., but formulate separate certification for EU purposes. 
The credibility of certification under EU rules will enable a project attract customers and therefore 
finance. 
The aim is to expand the scale of sustainable carbon removals by encouraging farmers, foresters and 
industries to develop and implement innovative solutions for capturing, recycling and storing carbon 
dioxide.  Rules for certification will also enable the EU to integrate carbon removals into its climate 
policies. 
Approach 
The definition of carbon removal and a body of evidence for solutions that deliver it form the basis 
of the approach to formulating the rules that will enable the EU to appraise proposed projects and 
identify the ones eligible for certification.  A project will have to maintain its certification by 
monitoring its carbon uptake and impacts on indigenous populations, biodiversity and resources 
such as water. 
Defining Carbon Removal 
A Carbon Removal Project is one that removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere permanently.   
Permanent removal requires the carbon dioxide removed to be stored and not used in subsequent 
processes that release it to the atmosphere, even if it is subsequently removed from the atmosphere 
once more. 
Removal can be dynamic; i.e. a Project such as a mature forest might release carbon to the 
atmosphere as the trees in it die, but capture carbon as new ones grow to take their place.  Carbon 
removal is the net effect of these two processes. 
A Carbon Avoidance Project is one that merely avoids the release of further carbon dioxide into the 
atmosphere.    
Specifying Carbon Removal Solutions 
A cross-sector EU project team reviews carbon capture and storage solutions under development 
and implementation and classifies them as Carbon Removal or Carbon Avoidance Projects. 
In doing so it identifies the features of solutions that determine whether they remove or avoid 
carbon, and so enables the systematic identification of Carbon Removal Projects.

Registering a Potential Carbon Removal Project 
An organisation registers a potential Carbon Removal Project for certification.  Registration not only 
identifies a project but also specifies its nature, location, projected carbon storage capacity over 
time (its “trajectory”) and the basis of that projection.  
Preliminary Assessment and Appraisal of a Registered Potential 
Carbon Removal Project 
An EU body: 
1. Assesses the basic eligibility and feasibility of a registered potential Carbon Removal Project;  
e.g. for a proposal to afforest savannah or agricultural land, it would consider whether similar 
land supported forests, whether the rainfall or water supply, soil fertility, etc. of the proposed 
Project were sufficient. 
2. Reviews the projected carbon storage capacity and trajectory of the project, and validates them 
and the methods used to calculate them using experience from similar projects 
The output of this stage of the process would be acceptance or rejection of a proposed project for 
subsequent feasibility assessment. 
The EU body would ask the body submitting an accepted proposal for further information about  
1. ownership or rights to the assets and resources that the project required,  
2. potential threats to its implementation, such as those from infrastructure projects 
3. the impacts that it would have on indigenous peoples, biodiversity and resources such as water, 
and 
4. proposals for management and maintenance, and monitoring of carbon uptake 
The eligibility of a project for certification depends strongly on the means of monitoring carbon 
removal; for forest projects, satellite imaging and ground-based data-gathering systems will 
probably be the cheapest and most effective means of initial assessment and ongoing monitoring of 
carbon storage. 
Feasibility Assessment and Approval of a Registered Carbon Removal 
Project 
On receiving further information from the body proposing the registered Carbon Removal Project, 
the EU body would 
1. Check legal and contractual requirements, i.e. ownership, rights 
2. Check potential prospective development affecting the Project, e.g.  roads, dams, pipelines  
3. Check potential impacts of the Project, e.g. on indigenous peoples, biodiversity, water demand 
4. Assess the means and cost of monitoring carbon removal and potential impacts

The output from this stage would be rejection or approval in principle for registration as a certified

Implementation of Certification 
The implementation of the certification of a Carbon Removal Project would take the following steps 
1. Implement monitoring processes 
 Human and environmental impact as well as carbon storage 
2. Implement storage certification publication and sales processes 
 Digitalised registry, recording over time 
 Carbon storage 
 Ownership of credits 
 Carbon storage shortfall warning process 
 In case climate change alters trajectory 
3. Issue baseline carbon storage certificate  
 Specifying the starting point with expected trajectory 
The EU should consider developing a risk rating system for carbon removal projects that would 
enable stakeholders – including the project owners, subscribers, lenders and investors - to judge 
their exposure to the risk of shortfall and hence the insurance that they might need. 
Maintenance of Certification 
The following processes would maintain the certification of a Carbon Removal Project: 
1. Operate monitoring process 
Each Project provides an independent report of the carbon removed from a certified auditor to a 
standard and at a frequency acceptable for its type of project. 
2. Operate sales support process 
 Secure registry 
 Record ownership and sales of carbon credits over time (especially in advance) 
 Issue shortfall warnings and sales credits  
3. Have integrity of the registry independently audited 
The EU Body would not itself be responsible for sales of carbon credits, but would aim to maintain 
the integrity of certification by recording sales of such credits against the capacity of the project for 
carbon removal.

Rules for Certification of Carbon Removal: a matter of context 
A Carbon Removal Project is one that removes carbon dioxide (CO₂) from the atmosphere 
permanently.    
In principle, permanent removal requires the CO₂ removed to be stored and not used in subsequent 
processes that release it to the atmosphere, even if it is subsequently removed from the atmosphere 
once more. 
In practice, the removal of CO₂ can be dynamic; i.e. a Project such as a mature forest might release 
carbon to the atmosphere as the trees in it die, but capture carbon as new ones grow to take their 
place.  Carbon removal is the net effect of these two processes. 
Man-made processes might also be able to remove CO₂ from the atmosphere.  (For example, a 
company, Verdox, in Massachusetts, USA has developed technology for doing just that.)   
If the captured CO₂ were stored and so kept permanently out of the atmosphere, such processes 
would perform carbon removal.  If the captured CO₂ were used as a raw material and eventually 
released into the atmosphere once more, these processes would remove a net amount of carbon 
equivalent to the amount in the raw material.  They could remove more carbon only by producing 
more raw material. 
These effects are like those at work in a forest; the manufacture of raw materials corresponds to the 
growth of new tree; the use of raw materials corresponds to the death of old trees; the net carbon 
removed in the stock of living trees corresponds to the net carbon removed in the stock of raw 
material. 
The economics of such processes are likely to combine the value of such raw materials against the 
prevailing value of carbon removal. 
Note that the use of wood from a forest does not necessarily mean that its embedded carbon is 
released; wood used in furniture, for example, is effectively stored while the furniture is in use; 
wood used in recycled paper would also remain stored. 
Some kinds of projects would enable carbon removal.  For example, manufacturing starch instead of 
extracting it from plants could free agricultural land for afforestation and hence carbon removal.  
Projects in behavioural change that reduced demand for meat could have the same effect. 
Carbon capture and storage (CCS) projects that extract CO₂ from flue gases, for example, and store it 
underground avoid the release of that carbon dioxide to the atmosphere and so perform carbon 
avoidance if the source of the carbon in that CO₂ is fossil.  (An example of such a project is Project 
Longship; it will capture CO₂ from cement and waste-to-energy plants in the Oslo-fjord region and 
inject it into the bed of the North Sea.) 
However, such projects that captured CO₂ from the combustion of wood and not fossil fuels would 
prolong indefinitely the storage of carbon in that wood and so extend its carbon removal. 
So, in summary, such CCS projects remove carbon from organic sources and avoid carbon from fossil 
sources. 
The examples of projects that enable carbon removal and those that prolong it show that the 
certification of one for carbon removal will depend on its context; in the case of enablement the

The Basis of Monitoring Carbon Removal 
The process of monitoring carbon removals depends on measuring carbon stores in a defined 
project at a time x and measuring them again at later time y.  The project has removed carbon if the 
amount stored at the later time y is greater than the amount stored at time x, with the following 
proviso: 
The amount removed and stored by natural (e.g. tree- and plant-based) projects might depend on 
season; e.g. there might be greater absorption of carbon in summer than in winter, and so times of 
measurement should be in the same season. 
Suitable means of measuring and monitoring carbon storage will depend on the nature of the 
carbon removal project 
 For natural (e.g. tree- and plant-based) storage, satellite imagery analysis 
 For man-made storage (e.g. subterranean gas injection); certify injection measurement 
process and instruments 
The human and environmental impact of a project as well as the amount of carbon it has stored will 
need to be monitored and perhaps certified as neutral or positive. 
Acceptable Standards 
Some existing standards (e.g. Verra, Gold, Lifecycle Assessment (LCA) or Environmental Product 
Declaration (EPD)) attest whether a project or solution has absorbed more CO₂ than it has emitted. 
New technologies and solutions might require new standards if they fall outside the scopes of 
existing ones.

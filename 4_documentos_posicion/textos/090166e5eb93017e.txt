Introduction 
Carbon Direct Inc. combines scientific expertise and technology to scale carbon removal and 
utilisation into a major global industry. Our advisory business helps clients fulfil their carbon 
commitments, from carbon footprinting through to carbon removal portfolio procurement, and our 
software technology facilitates the purchasing of high-quality removals with proprietary APIs. Our 
team includes over 40 of the world’s foremost carbon removal and utilisation scientists with broad 
expertise. We have contributed to keystone publications such as the Carbon Dioxide Removal 
(CDR) Primer, and we leverage cross-functional knowledge to understand the true opportunities, 
risks, limitations, and applications of various carbon removal and utilisation technologies.  
 
We have first-hand experience with nature-based solutions, including “Carbon Farming,” and 
industrial solutions like Bioenergy Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS), Direct Air Capture (DAC) 
and carbon mineralisation. We synthesise our expertise to identify and help scale technologies and 
find the best mix of solutions. An example of this includes Requests for Carbon Removal Proposals 
for Microsoft, where our broadly applicable learnings from this work have been shared publicly in 
Nature. Additionally, as part of the partnership with Microsoft, Carbon Direct developed the Criteria 
for High-Quality Carbon Dioxide Removal, which emphasised that certification is essential to the 
credibility of carbon removals in carbon markets, on the basis of defined and detailed criteria.  
 
Carbon Direct applauds the EU for their recognition of the role of carbon dioxide removal in 
achieving EU climate neutrality by 2050. The recent IPCC WGIII report further underscores the 
importance of carbon dioxide removal in achieving climate goals. 
 
Carbon Direct supports the EU Sustainable Carbon Cycles (SCC) initiative and the development of 
the EU ‘CRC-M’ certification mechanism. Certifications based on unambiguous standards can 
establish a “quality floor” in the carbon market and are a precondition for effective market operation 
(both voluntary and regulated). We also support the SCC initiative’s goal to scale up “Carbon 
Farming” as a business model, incentivising practices that increase carbon sequestration, and 
creating new industrial value chains for the sustainable capture, recycling, transport, and storage of 
atmospheric carbon, as well as recognizing the role for this carbon as a feedstock.  
 
Carbon Direct is happy to share our experiences with high-quality carbon removal solutions via this 
submission as well as further engagements and contributions.

Key Recommendations for the EU CRC-M  
1. Establish minimum quality principles and criteria for EU carbon removal standardisation 
and certification 
 
Carbon Direct defines high-quality carbon removals as certified carbon removal credits which 
are durable, additional, accountable, measurable and verifiable. They must also avoid 
economic and physical  leakage, not be “double-counted”, and finally avoid harm while 
ensuring a just transition to a decarbonised global economy.  
 
Carbon Direct regularly assesses, for our clients, the quality of proposals for carbon removal 
projects. In our experience, there are large variations between different ex-ante and proposed 
projects, with a substantial number not meeting basic quality criteria. This experience led to 
the publication, in partnership with Microsoft Corporation, of a white paper describing criteria 
for high-quality CDR.1 This document defines essential principles and criteria for all CDR 
solutions and additional criteria for specific CDR solutions.2 
 
We propose the EU consider developing their own science-based quality principles and 
framework of required criteria for minimum standards, with tailored criteria for specific CDR 
solutions. This recommendation is in line with the EU aspiration to develop “scientifically robust 
requirements for transparent measurement, monitoring, reporting and verification of the carbon 
removed from the atmosphere, ensuring a high level of environmental integrity and biodiversity 
protection.” The standards which will be the basis for EU CRC-M certification should in turn be 
informed by these EU criteria for quality carbon removal.  
Because a broad range of CDR solutions are feasible, but are at different stages of experience 
and market maturity, it will be difficult to unambiguously classify all CDR projects using the 
same principles and criteria. As such, we recommend the EU begin by identifying a limited set 
of broadly applicable best practices to be expanded upon with further maturation of CDR 
markets (see the next recommendation). This approach would also foster a mindset of 
continuously improving and raising the floor on carbon credit quality.

2. Develop the EU CRC-M in a phased approach in order to include learnings 
 
A broad range of CDR solutions are in development globally. Each is at a different stage of 
market and technological maturity.  Each has inherently different characteristics and risk 
profiles, which creates a need for the development of specific requirements for certification.  
 
A phased approach to maturing the CRC-M will enable the EU to sequentially improve and 
apply learnings in these new and evolving CDR sectors. To do so, Carbon Direct recommends 
beginning with issuing certification for those CDR solutions that have sufficient technological 
and deployment maturity against minimum criteria. Examples include ecosystem restoration–
such as reforestation and peatland restoration– BECCS, and direct air capture with storage 
(also referred to as DACCS). Other pathways, notably most soil carbon restoration pathways, 
will require more time, experience, and technological advancement before CRC-M can 
confidently issue quality certificates.  
 
Additionally, a pragmatic approach would be to define paths to standardisation and verification 
of less mature CDR solutions, allowing standards underpinning that certification to draw on the 
learnings from (EU supported) pilot/ demonstration projects. A structured learning cycle to 
improve standardisation methodologies and develop underlying practices, specifically with 
regards to LCA and measuring, reporting and verification (MRV) methodologies, will improve 
the quality of the EU certification system over time.

3. The EU CRC-M should certify Carbon Dioxide Removals only 
 
As of 2021, Carbon Direct has undertaken an annual review of the Voluntary Registry Offsets 
Database.3 Current voluntary carbon markets (VCMs) trade carbon credits (also referred to as 
“offsets”) for avoidance, reduction or removals.4 Removal credits are currently scarce and 
ambiguous in VCMs. Some “certified” projects, particularly in forestry, mix both avoidance and 
removal credits.  Carbon Direct supports the recommendation of The Oxford Principles for Net 
Zero Aligned Carbon Offsetting principles for a better separation between the use of removal 
credits towards climate neutrality, and avoidance and reduction credits towards 
decarbonisation of the global economy.  
 
On this basis we recommend that the CRC-M should exclusively develop standards which 
certify genuine carbon removals, with a framework that distinguishes between short and 
long lived storage permanence (also referred to as ‘durability,’ see recommendation 4). 
 
Furthermore Carbon Direct recommends that “double-counting” carbon credits (for example 
credits being counted towards both voluntary and regional commitments) should be prevented 
through a EU CRC-M retirement registry. The climate benefit of a credit of CO2 removal is only 
achieved at retirement. The registry could be distributed (i.e., via blockchain technology) or not, 
and could be updated as scientific advances recalibrate global understanding of carbon fluxes 
and durability (much as natural resource reserves are updated). This should not undermine the 
necessary and critical role of avoidances and reductions,  some of which are catered for in 
alternative reporting and regulatory mechanisms like the EU Emissions Trading Scheme (ETS). 
 
Accountability for a reversal of a carbon removal credit (if the CO2 is prematurely re-released 
into the atmosphere) should be with the carbon removal supplier during a certificate duration 
term.

4. Develop separate standards and certifications for distinct CDR solutions with defined 
durabilities, clustered for (future) interchangeability 
 
CDR solutions are inherently distinct and embedded in different value chains, which results in 
carbon removal products with differentiated characteristics or attributes – particularly with 
regards to permanence, or duration, of carbon dioxide storage.5 As such, when accounted for 
as a ton of carbon stored, different CDR solutions are not currently fungible. Rather, they 
are differentiated commodities with distinct properties (like different metals, e.g. gold, nickel, or 
zinc) reflecting their varying characteristics (e.g. risk of reversal, ease of verification,durability, 
etc.) and prices. Linking them at this stage of development would invite false comparisons, and 
diminish the validity, transparency, value, and purpose of the carbon markets.  
 
Conversely, the range of properties and prices reveals an essential need for diverse portfolios 
of CDR solutions in climate neutrality strategies. This includes the need to blend together 
projects with higher risk of reversal or leakage but lower cost (e.g. forests, soil) with those of 
lower risk of reversal but small volume and high cost (e.g. DACCS). Carbon Direct therefore 
recommends that the EU progresses with developing separate standards and 
certifications for distinct CDR solutions with defined durabilities. This will give rise to 
diverse certified carbon removal “products” which present a range of durabilities and co-
benefits.  
 
To implement this approach, carbon removal “product” certifications could be clustered by 
sector to limit fragmentation and enable (future) convergence for interchangeability of certified 
credits for specific sectoral decarbonisation objectives. One potential sectoral clustering6 could 
be:  
● Forestry: Afforestation/ agroforestry/ forest management 
● Farming: Soil carbon/ carbon farming/ biochar 
● Industrial: BECCS / DACCS / mineralisation 
● Materials: Bio/ DAC-derived CO2 used for long lived products 
 
Time-limited credits will ensure further accountability, placing the onus on the credit holder to 
renew any expired credit, either with one of permanent duration or a time-limited one in 
perpetuity. Registries and corporate reporting should cite these expiration dates. An example 
of nomenclature which could be utilised is: 10-30 yCRC, 30-100 yCRC, 100-1000yCRC.

5. Cradle-to-grave life cycle analysis (LCA) of carbon removal solutions should be included 
in the development of standards for CDR certification 
All CDR projects result in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions to some extent. Examples include 
production and transportation of feedstocks, energy use, materials and other greenhouse 
gases emitted during agricultural production, BECCS or DACCS. Carbon Direct therefore 
recommends including robust cradle-to-grave life cycle accounting (LCA) into the specific 
standards for CDR solutions to substantiate quality carbon accounting.8  
 
In general, the use of attributional LCA is often sufficient. However, given the limited maturity of 
CDR value chains, there are ancillary issues that should be addressed alongside cradle-to-
grave attributional LCA: 1) LCA methodologies for CDR should provide clear guidance 
regarding allocation of carbon emissions and removals to CDR processes which generate 
multiple products; and 2) Many CDR value chains will entail systemic effects. For example, 
feedstocks may be diverted from alternative uses or co-products such as power or heat may 
displace or be displaced by alternative sources such that (economic) carbon leakage occurs. 
Demand for some feedstocks may induce land use change and subsequent emissions. These 
phenomena will not be captured by attributional life cycle assessment. Carbon Direct therefore 
supports the application of consequential life cycle assessment (CLCA) where appropriate to 
mitigate risk of indirect emissions and economic carbon leakage. 
 
Data access and communication of data quality is essential to the integrity of LCA. Carbon 
Direct recommends that the standard require assessment of data quality in accordance with 
the ten data quality indicators recommended by the ISO 14040 standard. 
 
Specific to feedstocks, the standard should provide clear criteria to define “wastes.” These 
materials receive special treatment in the practice of LCA for good reason. With growing 
emphasis on the recovery of underutilised resources, there is an increasingly vague boundary 
between wastes, typically understood as outputs with no economic value representing a 
treatment cost to a producer, and low or moderate value “co-products,” which represent a 
revenue stream. In the former case, it is typically assumed that all environmental burdens 
associated with the waste generating process are borne by the primary product. In the latter 
case, environmental or emissions burdens may be shared by the feedstock.  
 
It is not our goal to determine the threshold at which wastes become co-products, but Carbon 
Direct recommends the CRC-M develop clear definitions. That would help ensure all emissions 
are accounted for and allocated for at some stage of the value chain and would help mitigate

The EU has an objective: that by 2050, after ambitiously scaling decarbonisation efforts to

There exist already well-intentioned methodologies to address the higher risk of reversal for 
certain CDR solutions, particularly forest-based solutions. To substantiate their permanence it 
is commonplace for registries to require buffer pools, tons-year accounting and vertical 
stacking of multiple high-risk credits to represent one assured “permanent” credit. It is the 
position of Carbon Direct that these approaches are currently immature and that the 
industry will benefit from and will be more credible with transparency on the time limits of 
specific certificates. In that same context, we believe the obligation lies with the credit owner to 
either replace a retired certificate with a permanent one upon expiry or end of the contract, or 
replace the time-bounded certifications sequentially in perpetuity. 
 
In cases where CO2 is re-released or reversed before the end of contract, the credit issuer is 
responsible, while after that expiry point it is the credit owner's responsibility to replace, 
against the baseline of CO2 returned to the atmosphere.

into a credit to permanently offset atmospheric emissions strengthens the responsibility of 
credit holders of short term credits to be prudent and factual in their claims. This responsibility 
also applies to being transparent about the commitment to credit renewal, and the financial 
reservations taken to do so. 
 
Carbon Direct strongly supports further academic and scientific work to improve these 
methodologies, which potentially could converge removals of short- and long-term duration 
(and their certificates) towards a more harmonised “CDR commodity”.9

6. Develop a taxonomy for the CRC-M which is aligned with international discourse 
 
Different CDR solutions follow different paths from atmospheric removal of carbon, via 
processing and transport (in some cases), to the final storage medium. To ensure consistency 
Carbon Direct recommends the CRC-M establish a new taxonomy to coordinate terminologies 
and agree upon quality standards for feedstocks.  
 
Different taxonomies have been proposed to date: the CDR Primer with its building blocks, and 
more recently the IPCC AR6 WGIII report, which focuses on two dimensions; a) removal 
process and; b) timescale of storage (on the basis of Minx et al., 2018). Understandably, with 
the parallel development of the CRC-M, the IPCC and CDR Primer taxonomies differ from the 
nature-based /technology-based structure of the CRC-M research project. 
 
Carbon Direct recommends developing a CRC-M taxonomy that reflects the most recent 
scientific understanding and to do so in collaboration with international CDR discourse in order 
to create further convergence. This will also be an opportunity to share the EU’s learnings on 
consolidation of this mechanism.

7. Develop the EU CRC-M to be applicable for other carbon markets, including voluntary 
 
Regulations like the CRC-M certification can create a quality floor for all carbon markets and 
support market confidence required for the research, deployment, and commercial scale-up of 
further CDR innovation. Carbon Direct therefore recommends that the EU Commission 
consider developing the EU CRC-M such that the certification is able to be utilised in 
developing voluntary markets and future EU carbon regulated markets too. 
 
One specific use case could be utilising CRC-M as a direct basis for corresponding 
adjustment”, where EU member countries could authorise the use of certified credits for 
international mitigation purposes, with a corresponding adjustment of nationally-determined 
contributions (NDCs) to prevent double-counting.

8. Collaborate with ongoing initiatives for convergence of CDR standards and certification 
 
The outcomes of the EU Commission research project CLIMA/2020/OP/0006, “Support on 
devising a carbon removal certification mechanism,”  described in its review of carbon removal 
certification mechanisms and methodologies the development of multiple initiatives for 
standardisation and certification of carbon removals from similar solutions. This proliferation 
was further visualised in the evolving map of the carbon sequestration certification market 
ecosystem from Arcusa et. al., (2022). 
 
Carbon Direct posits that this proliferation can undermine the quality floor of CDR carbon 
credits and inhibit transparency of the voluntary and regulated carbon removal markets. 
Carbon Direct urges the EU to collaborate with ongoing standardisation and certification 
initiatives across different countries and voluntary markets to foster greater understanding and 
collaboration. Examples include registries such as Verra, Gold Standard and Puro.Earth, as 
well initiatives with broader mandates such as the Integrity Council for Voluntary Carbon 
Markets (IC-VCM), and the Voluntary Carbon Markets Integrity Initiative (VCMI). Ideally, the EU 
CRC-M development will act as a catalyst  toward global convergence on a limited number of 
CDR standards and certification channels for specific CDR solutions. Carbon Direct recognises 
that this process will take time, and will benefit from experimentation and learning with different 
approaches. 
 
 
9. Support for setting up an EU agency for CRC-M certification 
 
Carbon Direct supports the creation of an EU agency responsible for operationalising, 
reviewing, and monitoring application of the CRC-M. The scope of this agency could extend 
from the certification process to maintaining a CRC registry with expiry dates and a registry of 
retirements of the credits for which the certificates have been used to ensure singular use.10 
Furthermore the agency could be the EU authority for the corresponding adjustments use of 
CDR credits for international mitigation outcomes. 
 
Within this agency we recommend installing a scientific advisory board for the integrity of the 
standards for the certification process and a market advisory board for the use of certificates in 
markets to assess conflicts of interests. Another role for the agency could be maintaining an 
inventory of the unresolved areas of scientific uncertainty for specific CDR solutions against the 
stated minimum criteria.

Specific Solutions 
In addition to the Criteria for High-Quality CDR, here we provide some recommendations 
specific to typical CDR solutions (as referenced in recommendation 1). 
 
Use of innovative technologies for carbon accounting 
Remote-sensing technologies using both aircraft and satellites are increasingly able to support 
measurement and accounting for carbon removal and storage across several sectors. The 
most important of these are for landscape-level carbon monitoring in soils and forests, and can 
significantly reduce costs for on-going high-quality monitoring. 
 
● When properly calibrated by in-situ physical soil sampling, hyperspectral imagery such 
as data provided by the ESA Sentinel-2 mission can help measure soil organic carbon 
content (Castaldi2019), and can be further refined by radar data such as that provided 
by the ESA Sentinel-1 mission (Urbina-Salazar2021). While these techniques have 
enormous potential to reduce the cost of ongoing monitoring, reporting and verification 
(MRV) for soil carbon, they require additional research and development to become fully 
mature. 
● Multiple solutions are under development for monitoring forest aboveground carbon 
content, leveraging optical and radar data from missions including ESA Sentinel-1 and -
2 and USGS Landsat. These solutions are increasingly being supplemented by lidar 
data from airborne platforms and the NASA GEDI mission (Puletti2020).  
● Multiple solutions are under development for monitoring blue carbon, including carbon 
content in mangroves, seagrass meadows, and salt marshes. To date these have 
included optical, radar, and lidar data similar to that noted above (Pham2019). 
● Commercial satellite missions are increasingly able to provide high-resolution and in 
some cases high-revisit optical imagery to support these carbon monitoring 
applications. While these imagery sources offer an expanded capability they also incur 
direct costs in terms of imagery purchases, which must be considered carefully when 
contemplating including them in carbon measurement protocols. 
● Carbon removals from wood harvesting and burial offer a form of hybrid, long-duration 
carbon removal (Zeng2022). A key concern is monitoring any potential methane 
emissions from burial sites, which could be addressed by a growing number of 
methane-detection remote sensing missions such as GHGSat and Carbon Mapper.

Forestry 
Forestry represents significant potential for carbon sequestration, through both forestation and 
improved forest management. Partially for this reason, many existing carbon protocols have 
focused on forestry. For example, the California compliance offsets program is composed of 
over 80% improved forest management credits. However, these programs have been faced 
with significant challenges in establishing additionality, robust carbon accounting, and 
mitigating disturbance risks. 
 
New programs should make the following key considerations: 
● Remote sensing, paired with forest inventories and machine learning approaches, can 
make carbon accounting in forest projects cheaper and more accurate. New EU forest 
carbon protocols should make use of these technologies in ways that simultaneously 
reduce barriers to entry and reduce opportunities for inaccurate carbon accounting. 
Specific recommendations are mentioned above. 
● Modelling counterfactual scenarios, or baselines, is one of the greatest challenges to 
robust carbon accounting in forest protocols. New EU forest protocols should work to 
set conservative baselines that are based on robust historical harvest data, either 
reported or remote-sensed. In general, baseline setting should represent the most likely 
counterfactual scenario, not simply a counterfactual scenario that is technically 
possible, but unlikely. 
● In working forests, harvested wood products can constitute a large carbon flow that 
should be appropriately accounted for. Mischaracterising the role of carbon stored in 
wood within forest protocols can create perverse incentives that create sub-optimal 
outcomes. For example, assuming that wood products (including bioenergy) are always 
carbon neutral can incentivise increased, unsustainable harvesting and reduce carbon 
storage overall. In all cases, conservative accounting of harvested wood use is critical. 
● Finally, forest protocols should consider the risk of natural disturbances that may lead 
to carbon storage reversal and how those risks are likely to change due to climate 
change. New forest protocols should incentivise practices that create forests that are 
resilient to disturbance and changing climate.

Mangrove reforestation 
Mangrove reforestation is one form of coastal blue carbon where CDR can be achieved 
through accumulation and storage of carbon in forest biomass and soils. An EU certification 
framework should, at a minimum, ensure robust application of methodologies for monitoring, 
reporting and verification, to constrain estimates and variability of annual changes in coastal 
extent, forest biomass and soil carbon accumulation. Similar consideration should be given to 
mangrove soil carbon, and field-validated models, remote-sensing products, machine learning 
and advanced modelling, which can ensure credible, accurate and affordable monitoring and 
verification platforms. The framework should also include determination of baseline carbon 
stocks and fluxes, assessment of indirect climate impacts, and life cycle assessment. 
Permanence risk should be clarified and include consideration of extreme events.  
 
 
 
BECCS 
Bioenergy with carbon capture and sequestration (BECCS) processes use biomass growth to 
remove CO2 from the atmosphere and sequester that embodied carbon - typically in geological 
storage underground as CO2. As such it has a  distinctly different climate mitigation 
contribution compared to biomass-fuelled energy generation which ultimately releases the CO2 
captured during biomass growth. Biomass feedstocks for BECCS should adhere to the same 
standards and sustainability criteria as laid out in the European Taxonomy for pure bio-energy 
generation. However, for clarity it would be valuable to categorise activities whose primary 
activity is CDR, such as BECCS, in a separate taxonomy sector to Energy (and Hydrogen).  
 
For geological storage, BECCS can build on the EU CCS directive and CCS industry 
experiences, amongst other monitoring and verification learnings. These are likely to suffice for 
most proposed projects and could be readily amended.  
 
Certification of net-negativity of a BECCS process should be confirmed via a credible cradle-
to-grave LCA.  Specifically for BECCS the sustainability of feedstock supply needs to be 
appropriately addressed, including implications for land-use change,  energy use, fertiliser use, 
waste definition and carbon allocation (see the LCA recommendation above).

DACCS 
An EU certification framework for DAC could set common minimum standards for the 
certification methodologies, including for monitoring, reporting and verification, or provide for 
comprehensive rules on the certification. The different functions, including the prior validation 
of projects and the subsequent verification of carbon removals achieved, could be carried out 
by public authorities, or executed for them by private operators, as part of a centralised EU 
system. 
 
For geological storage DAC can build on the EU CCS directive and CCS industry experiences, 
amongst other monitoring, reporting and verification learnings. These are likely to suffice for 
most proposed projects and could be readily amended.  
 
The key point for DACCS certification should be rigorous LCA. This is chiefly due to the 
substantial energy inputs needed to perform direct air capture and geological storage. The 
methodology to assess the life-cycle carbon footprint for electric power and heat generation 
are relatively straightforward, including upstream emissions from methane and embodied CO2 
footprints from renewable, fossil, or nuclear power. Attention areas around the footprint of 
biomass-based energy supplies are discussed under BECCS (see above). 
 
 
 
Mineralisation  
Despite the large potential and interest in enhanced weathering (EW) technologies, EW faces 
difficult complexities in developing methodologies for measuring, monitoring, and verifying 
(MRV) of carbon removals. Terrestrial EW in Europe has limited potential due to limited 
cropland space relative to other countries, and possible LCA issues related to transport and 
processing of mineral feedstocks. With the right policy and standardisation protocols, 
terrestrial EW could be a viable method of CDR due to the potential co-benefits to the 
agricultural industry. The EU could be particularly stringent about baseline measurements as a 
key component of monitoring and verification protocols for enhanced weathering. Minimal 
requirements for reporting should include geochemical measurements of elevated soil pH or 
cropland runoff alkalinity that is meaningfully above baseline for each project location, and we 
recommend third-party verification. A key challenge here is the timescale of mineral 
weathering, and projects should be monitored for several years to ensure accurate monitoring 
and verification.  
 
Alternatively, mineralisation monitoring and verification is much more straightforward, but 
brings up other challenges in the EU, such as limitations on industrial waste volumes and the 
potential for incentivising further mining operations just for mineral feedstocks. For operations 
involving utilising the carbonated mineral, considering the end-of-life of the mineral in the LCA

Biochar 
The EU has undertaken much previous work on biochar certification, which can form the basis 
of effective certification schemes for biochar-based carbon removal. Specifically, the European 
Biochar Certificate (EBC) sets minimum standards for biochar production and composition, 
while the EBC ‘C-Sink’ quantifies the magnitude and duration of carbon storage through 
biochar use. We recommend that these protocols form the basis of EU Certification efforts. 
 
Nevertheless, there are several potential enhancements to the EBC C-Sink worthy of 
consideration: 
(1) Inclusion of non-waste biomass sources 
(2) Prohibition on low-tech biochar production technologies; 
(3) Inclusion of minimum durability requirements 
(4) Inclusion of variable durability characteristics based on biochar properties 
(5) Chain of custody certification 
 
These revisions would expand the system boundary for life cycle assessment, reduce the 
potential for negative air quality and other health impacts from biochar production, enhance 
additionality, clarify durability, and encourage innovation amongst biochar producers. 
 
 
 
Soil carbon  
The main barrier to implementing CDR through soil carbon sequestration is related to 
accurately monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV) of carbon removals in the soil, for a 
number of reasons (Smith et al., 2020). Firstly, changes in soil organic carbon occur slowly and 
are small relative to the large background of carbon in the soil. The second challenge is the 
spatial heterogeneity of soils – many samples are required to get a representative estimate of 
soil organic carbon content, which in heterogenous soils can be dozens of samples per field

Demonstrating a change in soil carbon using direct measurement could cost more than the 
value of the carbon sequestered (Smith, 2004b). Paustian et al. (2019) and Smith et al. (2020) 
propose similar MRV frameworks for soil organic carbon sequestration that use a combination 
for modelling, benchmark long-term experiments, measurement networks, activity data, soil 
resampling networks, earth observation, and artificial intelligence / machine learning, which 
could complement direct measurement and provide a framework for soil MRV.  
 
As described by Paustian et al. (2019) and Smith et al. (2020), even the most difficult MRV 
challenges (e.g. for soil organic carbon) are not insurmountable. By combining existing 
datasets and models, in combination with earth observation products, machine learning and 
advanced modelling, credible, accurate and affordable MRV platforms can be developed; this 
would remove a significant barrier preventing the upscaling of natural climate solutions, and 
could help natural climate solutions to come to market.

TUI Group position (April 2022) on the review of the EU Package Travel Directive 
 
 
TUI recognises and highly values the importance of strong consumer rights in the travel sector , and 
supports the EU in its efforts to continue to improve its framework including the Package Travel 
Directive, so that it works well for both consumers and travel operators. 
 
While the PTD overall functions well and was reformed just relatively recently, improvements are still 
needed. TUI would in particular welcome further reforms of the definitions and concepts, to ensure 
that consumers buying identical or similar combinations of travel services enjoy the same level of 
protection, regardless of the selling model  used (I). Next to this, the pandemic has shown a need to 
support the provision of customer refunds for exceptional crises triggering mass cancellations  (II). 
Any changes to achieve this goal should however not negatively impact the travel ecosystem during 
normal business times (III). 
 
In this paper TUI highlights its current views on these issues and the potential solutions on which the 
Commission is consulting. 
 
 
I. Click-through packages and Linked Travel Arrangements 
 
It is imperative that consumers have clarity on their rights when something goes wrong  regarding 
their holiday. Consumers who buy the same, or similar, combinations of holiday products, ought to 
be entitled to the same levels of protection , irrespective of the selling model . This directly benefits 
the consumer and also ensures a level competitive landscape. 
 
The Directive was last revised in light of changes to how consumers purchase combinations of travel 
services and the rise in the number of those consumers booking combinations of services through 
non-traditional packages and therefore without appropriate protection. 
 
 
(a) Click-through packages 
 
One original intention of the Directive was to capture so -called “click -through sales” where a 
consumer buys one travel service and is then invited to purchase another travel service via 
redirection to another trader’s website. 
 
When the legislative proposals were initially presented, TUI proposed that as a minimum, all click -
through arrangements where details of the customer’s destination or dates of travel are 
communicated from one service provider to another, and the customer t hen makes a booking with 
that second service provider, should be defined as a package. This is because the transfer of such

The current review is an opportunity to consider the effectiveness of the Directive in this respect. 
 
While a consumer buying a holiday in this way may be lieve they are purchasing a protected 
combination of products, the current drafting of the definition 1 means the consumer is only 
protected if the consumer’s details, including payment card details, are transferred following 
conclusion of the agreement for the first service (i.e. when the customer accepts to pay). In practice, 
this rarely happens, and business models can easily be tailored to avoid such a construction.  
 
The way the definition is drafted cause consumer detriment, as significant numbers of consumers 
travel without the protections afforded by the Directive . Also  competitors operating in the same 
market and selling effectively the same or similar products and services are not therefore regulated 
in the same way and do not have the same economic or restricting burden. 
 
Under the definition set out in the Directive , a consumer who purchases a holiday via one set of 
seemingly connected booking processes, but by visiting more than one website, is not offered the 
level of protection commensurate with booking a holiday considered a package. In contrast, another 
consumer buys an identical set of services via a single point of sale and is afforded the high est level 
of protection afforded by a package under the Directive.  
 
Ensuring that the consumer is aware of the distinction goes some way but it is not sufficient to 
address the detriment. Consumers become emotionally attached to arrangements well before they 
consider protection – the latter largely only becoming even relevant to a consumer when something 
goes wrong, by which time it is too late. 
 
The definition contained within Article 3(2)(b)(v) should be redrafted to properly cover click -
through sales , without the requirement that the consumer’s payment  or other  details should be 
transferred. Providing the date of travel, or the destination should be sufficient. The time limit (24h) 
should also be reconsidered (removed or extended).

(b) Linked Travel Arrangements 
 
Similarly as is the case with click -through packages as currently defined, consumers buying a 
combination of travel services qualified as  a Linked Travel Arrangement (LTA) may not be receiving 
the protection they expect. 
 
Professional traders who are currently offering LTAs, albeit physical travel shops, online travel agents 
(OTAs) or providers of single services such as airlines, in many cases also offer package holidays as 
part of their services portfolio. Consumers who, following the purchase of a first travel service, are 
invited through facilitation by the same trader to buy further travel services, may therefore quite 
easily believe that they are purchasing a fully protected combination of travel services. 
 
This holds true in the context of a physical travel agency but especially in the case of online sales, 
where market players make use of marketing activities which give them the appeal of a tour operator, 
suggesting and promising everything customers loo k f or when arranging  their individual holiday 
experience. 
 
While the required standard notices were intended and do come at least some way in preventing  
customers from being misled, these are often not read or fully understood by customers, as they may 
be hidden in terms and conditions which are generally ignored by customers. 
 
Often only when an issue occurs during the holiday, consumers will uncover that the facilitating 
trader acts as a mere intermediary and does not take any responsibility for the proper p erformance 
of the holiday as a whole or even for the individual services included. 
 
Therefore the combinations of travel services currently considered as an LTA should be integrated 
into the package definition . Only this way it can be prevented that consumers are left without 
appropriate protection and market players can continue to attract  customers by acting like a tour 
operator but without incurring the responsibilities and liabilities attached to it.  
 
With regard to the second category of LTAs ( “targeted facilitation” as defined in Article 3(5)b) 2, as 
mentioned these should also be captured under the definition of a package. From our view this 
should apply if the offer of a second travel service is tailored to the first travel service decided by the 
customer. This is for instance the case where, upon booking a flight, the customer is targeted with 
an offer for car rental or accommodation at the relevant destination booked by the customer. Equally 
as is the case for click-through packages, the time limit for the second service to be agreed should be 
removed or extended, to avoid circumvention.

II. Cancellations in times of exceptional crisis 
 
The organised travel sector is used to flexibly provide solutions to its customers when  unforeseen 
situations occur. Even when large numbers of customers were affected in the past, for instance when 
holiday destinations were closed even mid-season due to political instability or other force majeure 
reasons, alternative holidays were arranged and where this could not be agreed customers received 
a full refund. 
 
The exceptional situation which presented itself in the early stages of the pandemic was of a different 
nature and proved to be very challenging for the entire travel sector. Due to the complete stalling of 
global travel and great levels of uncertainty, there was no opportunity to redirect travel in time and 
place, and travel operators had to refund a great number of customers simultaneously while  their 
fixed financial commitments continued and hardly any new revenue was coming in.  
 
While it cannot be ruled out that another pandemic  (wave) or similar situation could again  trigger 
mass cancellations in the future, such a situation is however likely to remain exceptional. Therefore, 
a smart solution should be found to better protect both consumers and travel operators without 
hampering the sector’s competitiveness  during normal times  through added costs or  unneeded 
regulation of business conditions (see further III). 
 
A possible solution may be found in a mechanism to temporarily take over the cash refund obligation 
from travel providers in a defined situation of prolonged and widespread standstill of travel only. 
Denmark has introduced such a system covering cancellati ons of package holidays during the early 
stages of the pandemic through a loan by the Government to the travel guarantee fund which will be 
reimbursed by the travel sector over an extended period of time. Other ways of setting this up may 
be explored but what is important is that this system has allowed the sector to safeguard its liquidity 
while at the same time fulfilling the promise of customer refunds , at no unnecessary cost or burden 
to anyone and without having to change functioning arrangements.  
 
Preferably any such measure  to temporarily take over the cash refund obligation from travel 
providers should be harmonized at EU level and be available both to the organised travel sector and 
to providers of standalone travel services  (particularly airlines)  in order to provide a coherent 
protection to consumers. 
 
Alongside this, travel operators should also continue to be able to issue vouchers in mutual 
agreement with affected customers.

III. Need to retain existing rules for normal business situation 
 
 
(a) Possible regulation of customer advance payments 
 
Under the current rules of the PTD, tour operators and intermediaries are free to set customer 
payment terms as is the case in other economic sectors.  
 
Usually an advance of around 25-30% is charged at the time of booking and payment of the balance 
is due a few weeks ahead of the holiday taking place.  
 
It has been suggested to abolish advance payments and that travellers should only pay as they receive 
the service. While this may appear to be an easy solution to protect consumers, this would be highly 
detrimental as pre -payments play an essential role in the travel value chain, to the benefit of all 
parties involved, including consumers themselves. Package holiday prices are lower compared with 
separate reservations 3 because travel operators purchase large allotments of hotel and transport 
capacity in advance that ensure optimal capacity utilisation for the suppliers. Notably for hotels, 
these agreements often require fixed commitments and pre-payments by the tour operator to the 
hotelier, as the hotelier demands booking security in exchange for low prices and securing the best 
rooms. Additionally, pre-payments are often used by hoteliers to improve their service for instance 
by renovating the hotel ahead of the holiday season.  
 
Prohibition or restrictive regulation of pre-payments would thus negatively affect existing, valid and 
beneficial business models that make use of pre -payments, to the detriment of consumers. This 
would also distort the level playing field in the sector with some of the platform models that do not 
make use of pre-payments. 
 
Consumers today  already have the choice to book and thus pay shortly before their holiday. 
Nevertheless many consumers choose to make an early booking and pay an advance as this way they 
can benefit from the greatest possible choice, booking security (especially important for travel in the 
high season) and early booking promotions. 
 
In the interest of consumers and travel operators, advance payments should not be abolished or 
limited in the PTD.

service provider in case the holiday needs to be cancelled and a refund must be provided to the

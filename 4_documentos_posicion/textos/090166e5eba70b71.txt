Certification of Carbon Removals 
 
 
 
Preliminary remarks 
Under the European Climate Law, the EU is to achieve a balance between greenhouse gas 
emissions (GHG) and removals by 2050 and to achieve negative emissions thereafter. These 
goals are very ambitious and can only be achieved if all economic actors contribute to reducing 
their emissions, particularly those who can contribute the most to phasing out fossil fuels. The 
AFOLU sector is regarded as a promising enabler of this green transition and a crucial player in 
achieving climate neutrality in the EU, mainly through carbon farming. Nonetheless, this 
requires scaling up the deployment of sustainable carbon removals and of a new business model 
for land managers and foresters.   
Carbon farming practices (such as afforestation, agroforestry, enhancing soil organic matter, 
land and forest management, and animal feed techniques to reduce emissions, to name but a 
few) are key to delivering on environmental goals beyond the removal of GHG emissions, such 
as enhancing biodiversity or maintaining ecosystems.   
Over the last decade, the AFOLU sector has been implementing carbon farming practices on a 
small scale across the EU. Some notable examples include the German project MoorFutures and 
CarboHedge, the Danish Wetland restoration RDP measure (DKW), the French CarboCage and 
LIFE Beef Carbon (also present in Italy and Spain), the Portuguese project Montado, and the 
Austrian project Zukunft Erde. While the sector has reached a generally mature status, upscaling 
the implementation of carbon farming practices requires enabling policies at local, national and 
EU-level capable of generating political and legal security, enabling financial tools, and 
facilitating knowledge dissemination among a large community of farmers and foresters mainly 
operating on a small and medium scale and, thus, with limited resources.  
Call for Evidence on the Certification of Carbon Removals: In a nutshell  
The Commission’s Call for Evidence identifies the lack of a common EU standard for the 
transparent identification of activities that remove carbon from the atmosphere in a sustainable 
way as a major barrier to upscaling carbon farming. It indicates that current public and private 
schemes differ in their approach to quantifying the climate benefits they deliver. Central to this 
is the certification of carbon removals, which raises questions about the quantification and 
verification of removals.  
The call for evidence also indicates that the certification framework should identify types of 
carbon removals and set robust requirements for quality of measurement, monitoring, reporting 
and verification, ensuring environmental integrity. In addition, it states that the initiative will 
assess whether an EU certification framework should set common minimum standards for the 
certification methodologies used for each type of carbon removal. It will also assess whether it

Our reflection  
Copa and Cogeca agree with the Commission’s assessment that current public and private 
schemes differ in their approach to carbon credits. To this end, we welcome the establishment of 
a set of common minimum standards for the certification of carbon removals, provided that 
current public and private schemes can be validated and that the efforts and removals achieved 
through them are recognised and accounted for. With regards to the types of carbon removals, 
we envisage a classification system based on environmental contribution, namely: carbon credits 
that reduce CO2-eq emissions, carbon credits that sequester CO2-eq emissions, and carbon 
credits that store CO2-eq emissions.  
Baseline and additionality 
Regarding the uncertainties relating to measurability, verifiability and additionality, most 
existing mechanisms issue carbon credits at project-level following a baseline (based on a 
“screenshot” of the farm’s performance on Day 1) and credit-based method whereby only 
additional new removals or reductions are rewarded (those that would not have occurred in the 
absence of the carbon farming practice being implemented). While this attempts to safeguard 
the principle of environmental integrity by demonstrating an implicit causality between the 
carbon farming practice and the amount of carbon being removed, it is unfair to those farmers 
and foresters that have been implementing sustainable practices for years now. A potential way 
to countervail this unfairness would be to focus on “carbon farming practices” rather than on 
“carbon credits”. Through this system, the farmer should be able to generate a carbon credit 
based on an evaluation of the estimated environmental gains generated through the 
implementation of a specific carbon farming practice. This could be calculated based on a 
comparison with the average practices in the department or, if not possible, in the region (i.e. 
Label Bas Carbone method). 
Permanency 
Another major challenge carbon credits face is “permanency” since emissions can be released 
back into the atmosphere either through natural disasters or due to an intentional disruption of 
the given carbon farming practice’s implementation. A potential solution would be to establish 
buffer accounts or insurance systems, which could take up to 20% of the value of the carbon 
credit being generated, to potentially compensate for “future released emissions”. These buffer 
accounts or insurance systems should gather and retain this “economic input/percentage of the 
value of the credit” prior to the final economic transaction to the farmer/forester. This would 
avoid creating any future debt and other legal or administrative burdens for farmers/foresters. If 
the stored carbon turns out to be permanent, the remaining 20% is also paid out to the farmer 
afterwards. This will also encourage the farmer to make maximum use of permanence. At the 
same time, an accountability mechanism should be established to ensure that those carbon units 
that have been released are properly reflected in the emissions balance sheet in question.  
Monitoring, verifying, and reporting  
Finally, with regards to monitoring, verifying, and reporting on carbon credits, the system 
should not create an unnecessary administrative and economic burden for farmers and 
foresters. Thus, we encourage the creation of a centre at EU-level for the verification, monitoring 
and accountability of carbon credits, with professionals (i.e., agronomists, environmentalists, 
scientists, etc.) operating in units in every EU Member State who are capable of handling on-
the-ground aspects and accountable for keeping records of their respective Member State’s GHG

Final remarks 
Given the undeniable need to maintain agricultural production levels (or even increase them to 
cope with risks to food security arising from the current humanitarian crisis), carbon farming 
and the generating of carbon credits must work in tandem with, rather than hinder, food 
production.

 
 
Position Paper on the Proposal for a  
Directive on Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence and amending 
Directive (EU) 2019/1937  
Transparency Register: No 10405322962-08 
 
15.4.2022 
 
 
 
The Austrian Federal Economic Chamber is the legal representative of the entire 
Austrian business community and represents all Austrian businesses drawn from the areas 
of Crafts and Trade, Industry, Commerce, Banking and Insurance, Information and 
Consultancy, Tourism and Leisure, Transportation and Communication. 99,6% of our 
members are SME.  
 
The Austrian business community is committed to sustainable, responsible, and future-
oriented business. In this sense, it supports the intentions of the initiative to improve 
international human rights and environmental protection through a coherent legal 
framework. However, neither the timing nor the content of the EU proposal seems likely to 
actually result in improvements. Global supply chains are subject to massive strains 
already as a result of the current crises. 
 
I. General Remarks 
 
Any legislative action should be undertaken with a sense of proportion and in accordance 
with the principle of proportionality. In our opinion, however, this draft directive is an 
unsuitable instrument and cannot be implemented by companies in practice. Moreover, in 
these difficult times, companies must not be put at a competitive disadvantage by 
disproportionate additional administrative burdens. 
 
Responsibility for compliance with and enforcement of environmental and social standards 
as well as human rights is an essential task of the states. The transfer of state 
responsibilities to companies must be viewed critically in light of legal tradition and the 
fundamental principle of the rule of law. Only in addition to the central responsibility of 
states can companies provide support within their own sphere of influence. 
 
Our objective is to arrive at a solution that is feasible for companies. The directive should 
not make companies responsible for factors over which they have no control. In addition, 
for the initiative to be successful, the requirements must be clear, manageable, and 
proportionate. However, with the text presented, there are doubts about effectiveness 
and implementability, as well as considerable legal uncertainty due to vague and 
undefined formulations. As subjects of the law, companies are currently unable to 
determine which specific obligations they will face, nor which measures they should take 
in individual cases to fulfil their due diligence obligations.

II. Key Points 
 
The WKÖ therefore advocates a practical and realistic approach. The following key points 
should be taken into due consideration by the legislator in this complex matter: 
 
• Rule of law considerations (principle of legality and better regulation) 
The principle of legality also requires laws to be sufficiently specific and thus predictable 
for the subjects of the law. The proposal for a directive is completely vague in essential 
points. Moreover, the legal act refers to international agreements that contain very broad 
obligations under international law and whose addressees are states. The "imposition" of 
these indeterminate regulations on companies is called into question. 
 
Furthermore, the proposed directive and the envisaged procedure are problematic with 
regard to legal protection. On the one hand, vague provisions would ultimately have to be 
specified by judicial interpretation. This shifts important decisions from the democratically 
legitimized legislature to the courts. Such an approach should be questioned, particularly 
regarding predictability for subjects of the law and unequal competitive situations. On the 
other hand, the proposal provides for the application of several guidelines, model contract 
clauses and delegated acts that have yet to be developed. These are intended to provide a 
more concrete definition of the indeterminate provisions of the law. Although their 
application is not mandatory, in practice they will create decisive requirements outside 
the regulated legislative process. This results in a considerable legal protection deficit. 
 
Considering that the Regulatory Scrutiny Board (RSB) has issued two negative opinions, 
largely based on the corporate governance part of this proposal, there is cause for concern 
regarding compliance with the Better Regulation Principles. Compliance with these 
principles is a crucial safeguard to ensure that EU proposals are evidence-based and 
sufficiently justified in terms of the need for the proposed EU intervention, its 
consequences, its proportionality and, not least, compliance with the principle of 
subsidiarity. 
 
• Creating a level playing field  
A Europe-wide regulatory framework is preferable to national regulations in the sense of a 
level playing field. The leeway that the EU states will have in implementing the directive 
according to the draft (minimum harmonization) could lead to a patchwork of different 
requirements, which is supposed to be prevented by a uniform legal act as such. Full 
harmonization would better support a level playing field in the area of due diligence. It 
must also be ensured that the provisions do not lead to competitive disadvantages for the 
European/Austrian economy compared to competing markets.  
 
It is likely that EU companies will (have to) withdraw from developing and emerging 
countries due to legal pressure and that the market will be left to aggressively operating 
companies. This may lead to job losses and a decline in economic growth in these 
countries and even lower environmental or human rights standards. 
 
• No excessive administrative and financial burdens 
Efforts to comply with the unclear requirements of the directive give reason to anticipate 
a very considerable additional bureaucratic burden, without any likelihood of achieving the 
 
intended goals. The addressees of the directive's obligations are not only larger companies, 
because they will pass their obligations on to their suppliers by contract. SMEs will be 
strongly affected by the "trickle-down" effect, as many of them are in the supply chain of 
large companies. Accordingly, there will be additional burdens for the entire business 
location. The administratively burdensome and cost-intensive administrative provisions 
place a disproportionately greater burden on small companies. 
 
• Proportionality 
Mandatory due diligence can realistically only be carried out in the company's sphere and 
with its direct suppliers (tier 1) with whom a business relationship exists. It is important to 
clarify that a due diligence obligation does not aim to establish strict liability. The focus 
must be on process requirements (obligations of means) and continuous improvement. The 
complexity of companies' supply chains and the need to prioritize must be taken into 
account accordingly. 
 
• Exclusion of unforeseeable liability risks 
Due diligence obligations can and should be discussed independently of damages.  
The existing liability regimes of the Member States already provide sufficient and 
appropriate rules for the civil liability of companies. Companies should not be held liable 
for damage in their supply chain if they did not directly cause it, could not reasonably have 
known about it, or if they took the appropriate due diligence measures to prevent it. Legal 
requirements should not inadvertently lead to situations where companies are held liable 
precisely because they have taken reasonable due diligence measures. 
 
The provisions on the enforcement of claims must be consistent with the international 
legal framework (including on jurisdiction and applicable law). The rules on the burden of 
proof must not be reversed to the detriment of companies. 
 
• Coherence of different initiatives 
Europe has long been a leader in responsible business. Mandatory due diligence 
requirements must be harmonized with international frameworks (e.g. OECD Guidelines, 
UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights). This also applies to numerous 
industry initiatives that have proven effective in practice. Instruments such as codes of 
conduct or model contractual clauses should be carefully examined in terms of legal 
certainty. Companies must be able to rely on the legal sufficiency of compliance with 
certain instruments under this directive. 
 
III. In detail 
 
Article 1 Subject matter  
The word value chain should be replaced by supply chain in order to maintain 
proportionality and practicability (cf. regulations in Germany and France). 
 
Due diligence should only apply to activities carried out by companies from third countries 
with which the company has a business relationship. EU-based companies should be 
presumed to act in accordance with national and Union law. Supply chains within the EU 
should be exempt from the directive. In addition, the European Commission should issue a 
 
"white list" listing states or regions where the legality of the trade can be presumed, which 
could greatly reduce scrutiny. 
 
A periodic reassessment of the business relationships can be carried out on a regular basis. 
The specification of a twelve-month minimum interval is excessive. 
 
Article 2 Scope  
Since the German Supply Chain Act and the French Loi de vigilance only apply to 
companies that employ more than 1000 employees, the personal scope of application 
envisaged in the Commission proposal seems too broad. Following the French and German 
legislation, the EU due diligence obligation should also only apply to companies with more 
than 1000 employees. An ex-ante differentiation according to risk level must be rejected. 
 
While the general exemption of SMEs is a step in the right direction, all SMEs will be 
severely affected by the trickle-down effect, as many of them are in the supply chain of 
large companies. SMEs need to be effectively supported, whether or not they fall within 
the scope of a mandatory framework. 
 
The application of the EU framework to companies from third countries operating in the EU 
is welcomed in principle in terms of a level playing field, but the enforceability is 
questioned. 
 
Article 3 Definitions  
• b) und c) adverse impacts Annex I  
The scope of application should be limited to specific human rights violations or 
environmentally harmful practices. A broad and vague scope of due diligence obligations 
requiring companies to avoid any adverse human rights or environmental impact is 
practically impossible for companies to manage.  
 
The legal acts listed in the annex impose obligations on states. Such obligations cannot 
easily be passed on directly to private entrepreneurs because they do not have the 
instruments of law enforcement available to states. The listed legal acts have by no means 
been ratified by all states. According to recital 25, the annex is to be understood as a 
demonstrative list. In order to create legal certainty, the rights in question must be listed 
in a taxative manner. 
 
• f) established business relationship 
The term "established business relationship" is absolutely unclear. Companies are therefore 
faced with considerable difficulties in categorizing their suppliers or deciding where 
corresponding due diligence obligations are to be observed. As a more concrete definition, 
only direct partners should be included. 
 
• g) value chain 
The value chain includes not only the original producer, but also all other suppliers, 
refiners, etc., so that the value chain is defined not only in a straight line, but also with all 
the cross-connections that complete the product (suppliers, service providers, refiners, 
etc.). The value chain is too broad and should be replaced by supply chain throughout the 
directive. 
 
 
i) SME 
Companies that are more than 50% affiliated in terms of ownership must be aggregated 
under the current proposal. For the purposes of the provisions of this Directive, there 
should be no aggregation.  
 
The following definitions require extensive substantiation:  
l) severe adverse impact 
n) stakeholders (e.g. via a legitimate interest, following the OECD definition)  
q) appropriate measure 
 
r) Supplementary regulations for groups of companies 
The obligations under this Directive apply at the level of the individual company. No 
provisions are provided for groups of companies or other forms of organization. This is 
problematic, for example, in the case of a company with subsidiaries in different member 
states, because different member state regimes could be applicable and different 
authorities responsible due to minimum harmonization. Corresponding provisions for groups 
of companies must be created as a supplement. 
 
Article 4 Due Diligence 
The provisions on due diligence must be specified in concrete terms in order to create 
legal certainty. Due diligence obligations must be practicable, proportionate, and 
reasonable. This means, among other things, that Austrian companies should - if at all - 
only be held responsible for those violations of human rights and environmental standards 
of which they are or should be aware and which - and this is particularly important - they 
can actually influence. 
 
There is no limitation of due diligence to the first tier, i.e. the first stage of the supply 
chains, but responsibility for the entire value chain. This goes far beyond the direct sphere 
of influence of the companies. For the sake of legal certainty and the practical possibility 
of exerting influence, due diligence obligations must at least be limited to this sphere of 
influence. 
It is particularly important to answer the question of how compliance with the various 
obligations is to be demonstrated. Will confirmations from suppliers suffice? Under what 
conditions is good faith protection given? The immense bureaucratic effort inevitably 
involved in fulfilling all these obligations is perceived as particularly disadvantageous. 
 
Article 6 Identifying actual and potential adverse impacts 
The longer and the more international a supply chain is, the more time-consuming it is for 
the affected companies to identify and assess possible standard violations or risks. 
Ultimately, the affected company is dependent on information from the immediately 
preceding and - if the affected company is not the last supply chain link - the immediately 
succeeding supply chain link in order to even know which foreign companies the supply 
chain goes to. In practice, it will not be possible to obtain this information without 
disclosing trade secrets. 
 
 
accompanying measures for the exchange of information in accordance with competition 
law are necessary. Legal certainty would be provided if relevant information were 
collected, compiled, verified, and published by public bodies. 
 
Stakeholder consultations should only be carried out if they are necessary and purposeful. 
 
Article 7 and Article 8 Preventing potential adverse impacts and bringing actual 
adverse impacts to an end  
More concrete and feasible measures must be specified here. The legislation enormously 
restricts entrepreneurial freedom and freedom of contract, and a large part is impossible 
to implement. 
 
If a risk cannot be realistically assessed by means of the risk analysis, or if a risk is 
identified but cannot be remedied, the business relationship must be terminated as a last 
resort in order to eliminate the risk of an administrative penalty or even civil liability. 
Competitive disadvantages can be a consequence here not to be underestimated. If a 
business relationship has to be terminated, the business field is left to other players, which 
could lead to a much more precarious situation in developing and emerging countries. 
 
Stakeholder consultations in respect of this article should only be carried out if they are 
necessary and purposeful. 
 
Art 8(3) is inconclusive and makes little sense in economic practice. Encouraging indirect 
contracting parties to conclude contracts directly could lead to the intermediate link being 
forced out of the supply chain. Furthermore, this may be problematic under competition 
law. 
 
Support for SMEs is welcomed in principle, but these are government tasks that cannot 
simply be passed on to other private entrepreneurs. A mandatory cost-bearing regulation 
for verification by independent third parties represents a disproportionate encroachment 
on the contractual freedom of the parties. 
 
It should also be borne in mind that a temporary suspension or even termination of a 
business relationship may under certain circumstances constitute a breach of contract 
under the relevant statutory provisions and thus give rise to claims for damages. This 
would have to be taken into account at the time the contract is concluded and may 
become problematic in the case of old contracts and contracts to which the law of a third 
country is applicable.  
 
It is necessary to specify how the obligations under Art 8(3) relate to other enforcement 
measures of the Directive (in particular civil liability). 
 
Article 9 Complaints procedure 
A complaints procedure is excessive where unions and NGOs are allowed to intervene. This 
should be rejected above all because of the associated risk of overburdening companies. It 
would make sense to follow the mediation procedure of the OECD Guidelines. 
 
Article 10 Monitoring  
 
The specification of a 12-month interval is excessive. 
 
Article 11 Communicating 
The relationship with the CSRD needs to be clarified and streamlined. In particular, the 
content and timeframe of the delegated acts require coordination. 
 
Article 12 Model contractual clauses 
The Commission's commitment is welcome, but it would need to be put in more concrete 
terms:  
- There must be a mandatory timetable to ensure that the guidelines are published 
before the directive is applicable. Practicable implementation periods must be 
provided.  
- The involvement of relevant business representatives must be made mandatory.  
- Removal of liability or penalties if the model contract clauses are used as intended. 
(How do Art 7,8, 12 and 22 interact?). 
 
Article 13 Guidelines 
The issuance of the guidelines must be formulated as an obligation instead of an optional 
provision (cf. English language version "may"). The time schedule for and involvement in 
implementation must be specified (cf. Art 12). The question arises as to precisely which 
guidelines should adopted (e.g. specific sectors and specific negative effects)? 
 
Article 14 Accompanying measures 
It is questionable whether and to what extent the measures addressed offer effective 
protection against the passing on of obligations to SMEs. The measures need to be 
strengthened and made more specific in order to sufficiently mitigate the indirect effects 
on SMEs. 
 
More concrete obligations  e.g., for data sharing portals (Who provides the data? Who 
collects them? Who audits them? Who pays for the projects? Who maintains portals? etc.) 
and a timeline are needed.  
 
Regarding industry regulations and multi-stakeholder initiatives, a commitment (not an 
optional provision) and a concrete timetable are needed. An assessment of the initiatives 
should be made not only by the companies themselves, but by a public body. There must 
be a presumption of compliance if the company adheres to these standards. 
 
Article 15 Combating climate change 
The requirements are entirely unspecified. The obligations provided for here are too vague 
and cannot be implemented in practice. Moreover, in the overall context, the provision 
seems out of step with the system and should therefore be eliminated in its entirety. 
 
It is appreciated that no liability is envisaged in the event that a company should fail to 
meet its emission reduction plan or revise it.  
Enforceability with companies from third countries is questioned if EU law is not 
applicable. 
 
 
A regulation regarding the remuneration of the company management is not expedient. It 
is too vague and not necessary because such regulations are already provided for 
elsewhere (e.g. Shareholders' Rights Directive II). 
 
Article 16 Authorised representative 
This does not ensure the enforceability and tangibility of third country companies. 
 
Article 17 and 18 Supervisory Authorities and their powers 
For companies operating in several EU states, it appears unclear which authorities are 
responsible. There is a risk that such companies will have to deal with several authorities 
and a variety of national implementations of the directive. 
 
The competence of the supervisory authorities is limited to "supervise compliance with the 
obligations laid down in national provisions adopted pursuant to Articles 6 to 11 and Article 
15(1) and (2) " (i.e. not the corporate governance obligations in Articles 25 and 26). 
However, the powers are generally “related to compliance with the obligations set out in 
this Directive." A clarification is needed here. It should be noted that numerous positions 
and expertise will have to be created and the member states will have to reckon with 
corresponding costs. 
 
The principle of "advice instead of sanctions" is demanded. Provided that the company 
complies with the remedial measures imposed by the authority, there must be no 
administrative sanctions and no civil liability. 
 
Article 19 Substantiated concerns 
The chosen approach of giving every natural and legal person the possibility to submit 
"substantiated concerns" regarding the violation of the provisions of the Directive to the 
competent supervisory authority is too broad. A legitimate interest and direct concern 
should at least have to be demonstrated. 
 
Particularly problematic appears to be the granted power of appeal (Art 19 (5)), which 
should be rejected in any case, and which may ultimately lead to long and costly litigation 
for the companies concerned and considerably impair their public image. 
 
Clarification is required regarding the responsibility of the supervisory authorities (see 
above). Consideration should be given to safeguarding the interests of the companies 
concerned and, in the event of an unjustified accusation, to providing for a corresponding 
claim for damages in favour of the company - if necessary, also on a lump-sum basis. 
 
Article 20 Sanctions 
Administrative sanctions are, on the one hand, understandable and, on the other hand, 
arguably necessary to encourage companies to comply with the due diligence requirements 
set out in the directive. However, when implementing the directive at national level, care 
must be taken to ensure that the penalties are not unreasonably high. 
 
It needs to be clarified that the sanctions are directed exclusively against the company 
(not the management). 
 
 
For the imposition of fines, it is necessary that the substantive norms that could be 
violated be precisely defined (necessity of clarity and definiteness). In France, the Conseil 
Constitutional has declared unconstitutional the provisions of the French law (devoir de 
vigilance) providing for fines because the sanctions could be based on the violation of 
broad and vague terms (cf. Décision n° 2017-750 DC du 23 mars 2017). 
 
If sanctions are imposed on companies, consideration should be given to dedicating the 
funds to assist the companies in implementing the requirements with these funds or to 
assist with measures (e.g., environmental protection) that the intent of the draft is to 
promote. 
 
Article 21 European Network of Supervisory Authorities 
The application of the “once-only principle” is required in order to reduce administrative 
burdens. The European Supervisory Authorities must design their information exchange in 
such a way that companies do not have to repeatedly provide the relevant information to 
different authorities. 
 
Article 22 Civil liability 
The proposed draft is not suitable for enabling companies to make legally secure decisions. 
Vague formulations and unclear definitions do not reach the level of adequate legislation 
and should therefore be dispensed with altogether. 
 
Liability with regard to indirect suppliers must be eliminated in its entirety at the absolute 
minimum. It should be noted that liability for fault does not develop in the direction of 
strict liability. It should be made clear that the party liable to pay compensation must also 
be at fault for causing the damage. 
 
Companies should not be held liable for damage in their supply chain if they did not 
directly cause it, could not reasonably have known about it, or if they took the appropriate 
due diligence measures to prevent it. Legal requirements should not inadvertently lead to 
situations where companies are held liable precisely because they have taken reasonable 
due diligence measures. 
 
Furthermore, it is problematic that the draft does not contain any rules on the burden of 
proof. Since proof will be difficult to provide in many cases, it will play a major role who 
must bear the burden of proof. Especially in the application of the directive, there will 
probably be a plethora of special cases such as alternative or overriding causality. Since 
the liability provisions are not harmonized, considerable distortions of competition are to 
be expected on these points. A general shift of the burden of proof to the companies is 
strictly rejected.  
 
There is a need to clarify how the liability rules relate to claims between the individual 
parties, in particular joint and several liability and recourse claims along the supply chain. 
 
The provisions for pursuing claims must be consistent with the international legal 
framework. It should be noted that both the place of jurisdiction and the state where the 
company is based, must be applicable. Article 22(5) is legally impracticable: mandatory 
application of the provisions of the Directive can only be provided for in national law. The 
 
application of the provisions when the law of another state is applicable cannot be 
mandated by a nation state.  
 
In addition to any civil law consequences, a lawsuit, given the expected media attention, 
would be damaging to a company's reputation and could lead to a preliminary conviction - 
regardless of the outcome of the proceedings. 
 
Article 24 Public support 
It is a disproportionate effort for companies to actively prove that they are compliant. The 
respective funding body should verify this itself if necessary or specify it in the funding 
guidelines. 
 
Article 25 and 26 Directors’ duty of care and setting up and overseeing due diligence 
The obligation to act in the best interests of the company is standing law. Interference in 
national corporate governance models is neither necessary nor proportionate or suitable to 
achieve the goal of promoting corporate sustainability.  
 
Moreover, the obligations of company directors are much too broad; this is particularly the 
case because long-term estimates of the effects of actions are likely to founder on the 
impossibility of valid forecasts. 
 
Article 30 Transposition 
When the regulations come into force, a practicable timetable is required, i.e. vacatio 
legis. Since this is a minimum harmonization and the member states have a considerable 
amount of leeway, it is not practicable for the provisions to enter into force at the same 
time as they are transposed into national law. Companies need sufficient lead time to 
implement these due diligence processes. 
 
IV. Conclusion 
 
The WKÖ supports the goal of strengthening international human rights and environmental 
protection through a uniform legal framework. Companies must ultimately implement and 
comply with European legislation. In order to combat climate change or human rights 
violations, it is essential to use the strengths of the private sector. To do this, a workable 
regulatory framework must be created and measures that restrict competition and 
innovation must be avoided.

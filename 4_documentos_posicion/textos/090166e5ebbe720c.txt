Contribution by Stockholm Exergi in response 
to the EU Commission’s consultation on a 
Carbon Removal Certificate Mechanism  
(CRC-M) 
Stockholm Exergi applauds the CRC-M initiative. We believe that a robust certification 
mechanism is essential for volume deployment of removals with geological storage. Based 
on the recent IPCC report and the extra-ordinary climate value delivered by removals with 
permanent storage, and considering the challenges in scaling this industry, we believe that 
the EU should give high priority to these types of removals in the CRC-M. 
Increased ambitions are required to achieve the goals of the Paris Agreement, and this 
paper is ultimately aimed at enabling increased ambitions by facilitating for actors to make 
real projects happen, while maintaining the environmental integrity of the system by clear 
rules for booking, claiming and trading. 
In addition to our response to the 
 consultation questionnaire on a Carbon 
Removal Certification Mechanism, Stockholm Exergi would like to address the following 
questions specifically, which are essential for the certification mechanism to play a key role 
in future climate mitigation: 
• Double claiming vs double counting 
• Permanence and the value of removals 
• Framework and core content of the CRC 
• Additionality 
• Life Cycle Assessment 
• Reversal mechanism 
• Trade in mitigation outcomes 
Stockholm Exergi believes that the climate crisis can best be tackled if climate policy, 
wherever possible, leverage public-private resources to accelerate decarbonization and the 
introduction of new technologies and solutions. Through combined efforts, more climate 
mitigation projects can be realized, ambitions can be increased and the climate goals more 
easily attained. From this position, it follows that policy must encourage co-funding and a 
rulebook which promotes early realization of projects that have a direct physical climate 
positive impact.

Conclusion 
The conclusion is that the concept of double claiming ‒ when used to criticize that claims 
are made by nations and corporations towards the same mitigation outcome ‒ is illogical 
and works against the climate. The important integrity issue is instead to prohibit any type of 
double counting of mitigation outcomes within one single system of climate accounting, 
either that of nations or that of the corporate sector.1 Again: No two states should be able to 
count or claim the same mitigation outcome, and no two companies should be able to count 
or claim the same mitigation outcome. That would result in double counting and should be 
prohibited. But a state and a company should be able to co-claim the same mitigation

Framework and core content of the CRC 
The text below focuses on CRCs for BECCS and DACCS but could, with some modifications, 
also serve as a basis for other removal methods. 
The right to issue a CRC should be delegated to national competent authorities in the 
Member States. Such national authorities could delegate that right to certified organizations 
that would be Designated Responsible Bodies to issue certificates in line with national law 
that follows from European regulations and recommendations. 
To ensure a structured and transparent co-claiming between the corporate and government 
sectors as well as to promote global trade in mitigation outcomes, it is essential that for each 
tonne, two certificates are issued:  
• One for the corporate domain, called CRC 
• One for the NDC domain, proposedly named SMO (for Standardized Mitigation 
Outcome) 
The CRC should be issued to the capturing company upon verification that the tonne has 
been stored. Of course, the owner of the CRC will depend on the commercial agreements 
that are concluded throughout the value chain. The SMO should be issued to the nation 
where the capture takes place. The two types of certificates should be managed in a 
common register, ideally as an extension of the register structure already in place for the EU 
(COMMISSION DELEGATED REGULATION (EU) 2019/1122). The rational of applying two 
certificates is further explained in the last chapter of this paper, Trade in mitigation 
outcomes, including how SMOs can be traded/transferred between nations in line with the 
Paris Agreement.  
The CRCs and SMOs should contain the following information: 
• Serial number (connecting the CRC to the SMO and vice-versa) 
• Issuing organization 
• Issue date 
• Last cancellation date 
A certificate should reflect the physical properties of the stored tonne. To 
promote clarity between the actions of nations and corporations as well as 
facilitating transparent trade in mitigation outcomes, two certificates should, 
upon validation of storage, be issued for each tonne, one for the nation where 
the tonne is captured and one for the capturer.

Additionality 
Additionality is a controversial topic without a clear definition or method for establishing 
whether a mitigation outcome is indeed additional. Generally, additionality is meant to imply 
that in the absence of the funding provided, the mitigation measure would not happen. The 
the holder of the instrument the right to account for the climate benefit.  
Since such instruments have generally not made a distinction between whether they are 
used by corporations or nations, they have contributed to compounding the confusion 
surrounding the question of double claiming. A solution to this problem is presented in the 
last chapter in this paper on Trade in mitigation outcomes. 
Additionality is based on counter-factual outcomes of what would have happened in an

Important studies of the market for climate compensation also show that in realty, very few

Life Cycle Assessment 
In the list above, LCA is not directly included. The reason is to protect the physical nature of 
the certificate and the ability to ensure that the CRC and the SMO have the same physical 
value (typically a tonne). 
For nations, fossil emissions associated with the production of the removal will appear in the 
national emission accounts, so there is no need for deducting the emissions from the SMO 
certificate. 
For corporations, however, an LCA analysis should be undertaken and assessed quantitively 
as regards the mitigation project., since corporations target to compensate or neutralize 
their full residual emissions. This is best achieved by requiring the capturing company to 
cancel a certain number of CRCs corresponding to the additional fossil emissions associated 
with the production of the negative emission. Such a requirement may be part of the CRC-
M. 
It follows that while the LCA should not be a direct part of the CRC/SMO, it is of utmost 
importance that a common approach to LCA is agreed within the CRC-M, since the 
assumptions for the LCA calculation will have a significant impact on the result and the 
functioning of the market.  
An alternative approach would be to rely on some form of Best Available Technology hurdle 
for certification to take place. The implication would be that certificates would only be 
LCA is an important part measuring the climate impact of negative emissions. 
However, staying with the concept that the CRC/SMO should be evidence of the 
physical storage, the LCA footprint should not be a direct part of the CRC as 
such, but be compensated for separately.

Reversal mechanism 
Different removal methods have different risks of reversal. This question is too important to 
be regulated differently by all parties, be it corporations or nations. Thus, a common 
framework for how leakage should be treated is desirable.  
Limiting the question to BECCS, DACCS and CCS, leakage at geological storage sites should 
be treated in the same way for these three methods. It is our understanding that the current 
combination of the ETS and CCS directives already has a solution for leakage and that it 
neither makes a distinction between fossil and biogenic CO₂ nor between CO₂ stored within 
a compliance or a voluntary framework.  
A way to think of the current ETS/CCS framework is that, as of capture a new value chain 
starts and that value chain is responsible for its emissions. Considering the scientific 
evidence, as summarized by IPCC4, that 99% of geologically stored CO₂ is likely to remain 
after 1 000 years, the ETS/CCS directives, if correctly interpreted, constitute a strong 
framework for reversal. This would also allow for co-existence of and a smooth transition 
from the voluntary market set-up to an expected future compliance framework. 
If such an application of the ETS/CCS directives would not be feasible, two possible options 
remain: 
• An insurance requirement 
• The build-up of a buffer pool in connection with storage 
The requirement on such instruments would be based on the strong evidence for 
permanence and, of course, the individual quality of the storage sites, which would need to 
be secured as part of the certification mechanism.

Trade in mitigation outcomes 
It is generally accepted that trade in mitigation outcomes can reduce the overall cost to limit 
where so-called flexibilities between the Member States have been introduced. It is also 
reflected in the Paris Agreement, notably in Article 6. The same idea is embraced by the 
VCM, where hard-to-abate emissions of one company are expected to be neutralized by 
negative emissions typically produced by another company. 
Today, as outlined in the chapter on double claiming, there is a disconnect between the 
trade on national level and that on the corporate level. This disconnect hampers trade in 
mitigation outcomes and slows down the fight against climate change. 
To remedy this situation and contribute to much greater and needed transparency, the 
certification of a negative tonne should produce two certificates, one for the national level 
and one for the corporate level, named SMO (Standardized Mitigation Outcome) and CRC, 
respectively.  
While this proposal focuses on negative emissions with geological storage, the system with 
twin certificates could also serve as a more general basis for trade in any mitigation 
outcomes. 
By this proposal, co-funding of mitigation outcomes would be recognized as positive, and it 
would be a confirmation that such outcomes are naturally co-claimed. It would also allow for 
the on-going discussion about the VCM and the possible risk of lower ambitions in some 
nations, since the SMOs and CRCs would be managed in the same registry, connected 
tonne for tonne to each other by unique identities. 
Extensive trade in mitigation outcomes will accelerate the fight against climate 
change and drive increased ambitions. To foster such trade both among 
corporations and nations, encourage co-funding and avoiding double counting, 
two certificates should be issued for each tonne of CO₂ removed, one for trade 
among nations and one for trade between corporations.

SMOs would, unlike ITMOs, be standardized, typically a tonne of CO₂ as validated by the 
certification mechanism. SMOs would only be traded between nations, entirely compatible 
with rules of Article 6 of the Paris Agreement. If in a nation there would be a surplus of 
SMOs, that nation could sell them to another nation in need of negative emissions, and 
make a Corresponding Adjustment. From a UNFCCC GHG NIR perspective, the physical 
negative emission would be registered in the country where the negative emission has been 
produced, but with a Memo item that it is not included in the fulfillment of that nation
climate objectives (national, NECP and/or NDC). 
CRCs, on the other hand, would only be traded between corporations. In the simplest case, 
a twin CRC/SMO would be consumed (by cancellation) in the same country, by a 
corporation and the nation, respectively. Since the system keeps the two levels of 
accounting separate, if a foreign corporation would purchase a CRC, there would be no 
need for any Corresponding Adjustments. 5 Thus, the trade of CRCs on the corporate level 
would take place without consideration of national borders. 
If such a purchase of a CRC came with a requirement that the mitigation outcome must also 
be transferred to the home nation of the purchasing company, a Corresponding Adjustment 
would be required. In that case, the host nation would also have to transfer its SMO to the 
nation of the purchasing corporation. Whether the resulting SMO transaction would entail 
any monetary flow would likely depend on how the removal have been funded. 
The system would, almost by design, allow for full control of double counting, including 
double issuance and double use. On the national level through the combination of the 
registry and Corresponding Adjustments. On the corporate level by the ability to check 
whether more than one company has referred to the CRC identity in the fulfillment of its 
climate targets. 
The more liquid the market for trade in CRCs/SMOs is, the more efficient the market will be 
and the lower the cost for mitigation outcomes will be. It is thus essential to minimize 
frictions to trade. Consequently, CRCs/SMOs must as far as possible be standardized and 
free from non-essential, optional add-ons.  
The environmental integrity of Article 6 and the VCM would be taken to a new level, focus 
would be on real physical impacts on the climate, not on possible counter-factual impacts. 
The notion of double claiming, as discussed in previous chapters, ceases to be meaningful. 
Instead, co-funding and co-claiming would allow for an acceleration of climate mitigation 
measures and maximum participation of private funding, with no double counting taking 
place.

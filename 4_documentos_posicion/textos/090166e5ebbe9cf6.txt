FoodDrinkEurope contribution to the public consultation on EU rules 
for a certification of carbon removals  
 
1. General views  
 
FoodDrinkEurope strongly supports the European Union (EU) commitment to reaching net-
zero emissions by 2050, and as part of this target, to achieve a 55% reduction in emissions by 
2030. In this context, the Fit for 55 package sets net CO2 removal targets for each Member 
States, with the overall objective of a climate neutral land and agriculture sector by 2035. The 
European food and drink industry, processing 70% of the EU agricultural output, is committed 
to support farmers throughout this journey. 
 
The establishment of a framework for the certification of carbon removals is an essential 
steppingstone towards achieving a net contribution from carbon removals, in line with the EU 
climate-neutrality objective. The proposal for a European carbon removals certification must 
support a credible business model. Farmers who implement sustainable farming practices, 
including regenerative agriculture, and contribute to carbon sequestration – amongst other 
environmental and climate adaptation and mitigation benefits – should benefit from carbon 
farming and receive technical and financial support. As a key actor of the same value chain, 
the food and drink industry will play its part in discussions on a new framework for carbon 
removals. 
 
The EU certification for carbon removals should provide a regulatory framework which 
supports a consistent accounting of removals. If a market was to be part of the solution, it 
could help build trust in the market by setting standards, and prevent double counting and 
greenwashing. This initiative will allow for a harmonized certification across the EU, and ideally 
contribute to carbon markets globally.  
 
2. Policy coherence 
 
Alignment with other current and upcoming policies will be key to the success of this initiative. 
The framework to be developed under the certification for carbon removals must align 
with the Green Deal, Farm to Fork, and Fit for 551 objectives.  
 
The new Common Agricultural Policy (2023-2027), by introducing the eco-schemes, foresees 
a funding share for carbon farming and shows a European leadership in a transition towards 
more sustainable farming systems and food systems. 
 
It will be important for carbon credits to be taken into account under the upcoming rules for 
green claims and the corporate accounting requirements in the Corporate Sustainability 
Reporting Directive.

3. Decarbonisation of the agri-food chain 
 
Farmers are at the core of the transition to more sustainable farming practices and food 
systems. The industry is increasingly involved in the transition via premium prices, knowledge 
transfer, capacity building, specific contracts, or financial arrangements, such as cost sharing 
and/or loans for better adapted farm equipment for example. 
 
Agri-businesses have a significant role to play as they embark in their own journey to 
net zero. In line with the European Green Deal and the Farm to Fork Strategy (including the 
EU Code of Conduct), food chain operators are committed to reducing emissions to achieve 
net-zero targets – in line with the Science Based Target Initiative – and other corporate climate- 
related targets. The demand for carbon credits that will come from non-food companies will 
compete with the ability of food companies to reduce emissions within their own value chains 
(i.e. scope 3 emission reduction). In this context, it is of utmost importance to understand the 
risk of trading removals outside of the agri-food value chains.  
 
We support the development of a certification of carbon removals that encourages voluntary 
market solutions, where actors in the agri-food sector are able to keep carbon removals within 
the agricultural value chain, for the benefit of fulfilling high societal expectations of reaching 
net-zero targets, voluntary corporate targets, avoiding double counting and greenwashing, 
while also safeguarding farmer choice to make beneficial commercial agreements. In order to 
be able to achieve climate targets and ensure food security, collaboration along the food chain 
is key. As an example, agri-businesses could be granted preferential access to carbon credits, 
generated thanks to a joint effort of agri-food chain actors. 
 
FoodDrinkEurope believes that the EU certification for carbon removals would provide the right 
tools to decarbonise the agri-food chain. However, such decarbonisation must not jeopardize 
the supply of food in quality and quantity. The production of agricultural raw materials for food 
must remain the priority of farmers. If a carbon market is the tool identified to encourage carbon 
removals, it will also be important that the market mechanism does not discourage the growing 
of food, or does not encourage land managers to simply prefer cultures that can more easily 
sequester carbon. 
 
4. Methodology  
 
Different methods of certification and calculation of carbon reductions and carbon removals 
are already being developed by public and private actors. Existing schemes must be taken 
into account when developing an EU method for quantifying carbon removal and 
reducing emissions. The Commission should foster a dialogue with relevant actors which 
already carried out this type of activity. Such link will allow for specific guidance and 
identification of strengths and weaknesses of the different methods. For instance, the 
framework for the certification of carbon removals could build on the GHG accounting 
methodologies developed by World Resource Institute and the Science Based Targets 
initiative (SBTi) work, as they are already in use internationally and have a robust science 
foundation. 
 
Clear definitions of the baselines and additionality criteria, adapted to the different 
sectors (e.g. forestry, land use, industrial solutions) are essential for the upcoming 
regulatory framework for carbon removals certification. The Commission must set a level 
playing field for the calculation and reward of carbon removals.

However, frontrunners must not be left behind. There is a need for reward mechanisms and 
incentives for carbon previously sequestered. If not rewarded, those who have high carbon 
stocks would be encouraged to deplete them before re-stocking (e.g. by cutting down the 
current forest to engage then in a reforestation project). For this reason, a methodology for 
assessment and crediting of previous carbon removals should be introduced to incentivise 
landowners to maintain their carbon stock. 
 
Finally, the certification methodology must be coherent with the calculation used for CBAM, in 
view of the scope enlargement to all products covered by CBAM after 2035.  
 
5. Challenges to overcome 
 
 Additionality 
 
 MRV (Measurement, Reporting and Verification): Measurement and data collection 
is expected to be data intensive and expensive. It is therefore important that the method 
is kept simple, to avoid administrative burden for producers. 
 
 Permanence / risk of reversibility: It is essential for the European Commission to set 
a clear signal as regards permanence for carbon removals. This is of great importance 
for farmers' ability to plan and assess their opportunity and risk. 
 
 Double counting: when developing a certification scheme for GHG emissions, the 
Commission should make sure that there is no double counting for the carbon footprint 
of food products by operators. Double counting may happen between government and 
operators.  
 
 Costs: Carbon crediting seems to be both a new source of income for farming, and a 
source of additional costs (e.g., costs for reporting, modelling, accounting, certifying, 
soil sampling). Measures which would be implemented to increase sequestration levels 
may also affect the farm productivity and farming costs. It is important to make sure 
that such a certification scheme represents a long-term positive business model for 
both farming and processing activities.  
 
 Environmental co-benefits: the scope of the credits should reflect the environmental 
co-benefits that come with the carbon removal. Actions that generate carbon removals 
are critical to farm resilience and the associated environmental benefits to biodiversity, 
watershed health and soil health.  
 
6. Industrial solutions for carbon removals 
 
The potential of industrial solutions for carbon captures is considerable, although there is an 
essential need for a strong supportive framework that will trigger investments into these 
technologies for business to develop them at scale.

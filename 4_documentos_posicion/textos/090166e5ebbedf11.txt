EU rules for the certification

ECOS Position Paper 
Brussels, 2 May 2022 
 
 
 
 
Summary 
ECOS welcomes the European Commission’s ambition to develop a common EU standard for the 
transparent identification of activities that remove carb on from the atmosphere in a sustainable way.  
Although emission reductions remain the absolute priority , carbon removals will be increasingly 
needed to reach net zero emissions in 2050. ECOS believes that a certification framework is necessary 
to ensure that only real carbon removals are considered, and to avoid delaying climate action.  
In this paper, we detail ECOS’ views on this EU framework, outlining the main principles forming its 
basis (such as the criteria to determine whether a technology  results in negative emissions ), the 
governance structure, and more specific insights on the role of  nature-based and industrial solutions 
respectively 
The priority should be to start building a robust framework for the certification as a first step, and only 
when this framework is in place,  to start developing policy incentives.  We also suggest that t he 
certification and incentive mechanisms should be specific to each carbon dioxide removal ( CDR) 
method (nature-based solutions and industrial solutions).

I. Necessary principles in the framework 
In its Sixth Assessment report1, the WGIII of the IPCC addresses and defines carbon dioxide removals 
(or CDR) and it recognises its essential and unavoidable role to achieve climate neutrality2. There is a role 
for CDR in the short term to accelerate net reductions in addition to deep and rapid emission cuts, and in 
the mid- to long-term, to balance out residual GHG emissions and then sustain negative emissions. 
Given the strategic importance of CDRs for the EU’s climate strategy, we welcome the Commission’s 
initiative to set up a framework to certify carbon removals . Without such a framework the risk of 
greenwashing and delay in climate action is high, as voluntary carbon markets have a large variety of 
approaches and levels of robustness . Carbon credits are also widely used for the private sector to 
massively offset their emissions  and make  climate related claims,  instead of focusing on emission 
reductions. 
ECOS recommends that the following principles form the basis of this initiative. 
Emission reductions must come before removals and counted separately 
CDR is not a magic stick that will compensate all the GHG emitted annually . The EU should follow and 
foster strict adherence to the most effective climate mitigation measures  starting with emission 
reductions. When it comes to removals, one must take into account parameters such as the certainty of 
permanence, preventi ng perverse incentives  (such as from offsets), and co -benefits maximisation  
(particularly from nature-based solutions). Some of these measures are listed below which must be used 
complementarily, with the caveat that for the last three the risk of emission reversal must be prevented. 
• Reduce emissions, including through sufficiency measures; 
• Conserve existing carbon sinks (e.g. forests, peatland, wetlands, …); 
• Create permanent carbon sinks (e.g. geological carbon capture and storage); 
• Create nature-based carbon sinks (reforestation, agricultural measures, re-wetting, etc.); 
• Store carbon in long-lasting products (aiming for over 100 years). 
To avoid undermining emission reduction efforts, it is paramount that  emission reductions and removals 
should never be aggregated into a single number, whether in account reporting or in climate targets.  
The EU must set a clear definition of what counts as ‘carbon dioxide removals’ 
The certification framework put in place by the Commission should be extremely clear and strictly 
define what can count as a CDR and what cannot.

The IPCC defines carbon removals as “anthropogenic activities that remove CO2 from the atmosphere 
and store it durably in geological, terrestrial, or ocean reservoirs, or in products”. A 2019 study3 from 
Tanzer and Ramirez outlines four key criteria to determine whether a technology results in negative 
emissions:  
1. Physical greenhouse gases are removed from the atmosphere.  
2. The removed gases are stored out of the atmosphere in a manner intended to be permanent.  
3. Upstream and downstream greenhouse gas emissions associated with the removal and 
storage process, such as biomass origin, energy use, gas fate, and co-product fate, are 
comprehensively estimated and included in the emission balance.  
4. The total quantity of atmospheric greenhouse gases removed and permanently stored is 
greater than the total quantity of greenhouse gases emitted to the atmosphere. 
ECOS believes that these criteria should be the foundation of the certification framework. 
Carbon storage in products should be maximized to very long lifetimes (over 
100 years), but cannot be counted as ‘removal’ 
Since removals should be intended as permanent, d elayed emissions through temporary carbon 
storage (whether fossil or biogenic) in products should not be called ‘removals’. This means that the 
temporary storage of carbon in biomass (whether from agriculture, forestry or aquatic systems) cannot 
count towards removal.  
Longer-lasting products, and measures which effectively extend product lifetime and end-of-life 
emissions through durability, multiple cycles of reuse and recycling must be fostered to support climate 
mitigation objectives. Circularity of material flows and the cascading use principle remain paramount 
and cannot be omitted to pursue energy- and material-intensive industries tapping into virgin biomass 
resources. The expectation that a shift towards an EU bioeconomy will single-handedly enable us to 
save the climate by phasing out fossil emissions is a fallacy: the focus must remain on an absolute 
reduction of material and energy consumption to limit growth, with measures like phasing out short-
lived products (like single-use packaging) and virgin biomass burning. There are simply not enough 
natural resources to support a shift from fossil to bioeconomy. 4 
II. Governance and mechanisms of the certification 
framework  
Before any step is taken to d evelop an incentive system, a robust certification framework for  carbon 
removals should be in place. The system should be operated as much as possible by public authorities  
with ensured transparency and access to information at each step of the process. While every safeguard 
should be put in place to ensure that the new CDR certification fra mework can help contribute to more 
durable and effective CDR, this new framework should be tested and implemented progressively.  
Similarly to the Emissions Trading System , which required several revisions before it could function 
effectively, the methodologies for assessing, verifying and certifying volumes and permanence of CDR in 
different applications will undoubtedly require adjustments over time. 
The CDR framework should ha ve a built -in obligation to reduce emissions first , and then claim the 
removal of residual emissions . Abatable emissions should thus not be allowed to be ‘balanced out’ by 
removals. CDRs are too often seen as a climate ‘fix’ to activities which are material- and energy-intensive,

which use fossil fuels , or large amounts of virgin biomass. Activities which are material and energy -

In line with criterion 4, it is crucial that the scope of the assessment conducted prior to certification should 
encompass the risks of emission from systems indirectly related with the project, also called ‘leakage’. 
Removals occurring in the projec t’s scope may unintendedly c ause emissions elsewhere, leading to no 
net benefit. As a result, a ll possible unintended effects of CDR measures must be carefully studied to 
avoid certifying removals which in practice lead to leakages. This is the typical problem encountered with 
carbon neutrality assessments at the level of single projects, because in the end emission reductions 
matter on a global scale. 
The framework should be sensitive to the different types of removals in consideration of their relative risk 
of emission reversal . The incentive framework should  also reflect this risk with differentiated rewards 
according to the level of assurance provided with the CDR certificate. 
The system should be centred on  financial support such as tax breaks and  direct payment schemes. At 
any rate, the system should prevent a carbon credit ‘gold rush’ at the expense of emission reduction 
strategies. The certification framework should never lose sight of the long -term objective of balancing 
only residual emissions with carbon removals, therefore certified CDRs should not become a tool for 
companies to make claims around carbon neutrality by purchasing carbon credits to offset large portions 
of their emissions. 
The definition of  the CDR assessment and certification methodology should be developed by the 
European Commission itself, based on the four abovementioned key criteria from Tanzer and Ramirez . 
We call upon the European Commission to develo p its own set of rules for the CDR certification 
framework. If the Commission decide s to mandate standardisers from CEN to develop this approach, 
then the  mandate should include such cl ear requirements.  The process  should not rely on a direct 
transposition of private sector standards into EU rules, as this would risk biasing its design towards the 
creation of a voluntary carbon market. 
III. Nature-based solutions 
Carbon sequestration by agriculture, forestry and land res toration is a  necessary service for the 
preservation of the global climate. Incentives for this service should be broadly accessible to replicate and 
scale up efforts by land managers . This service should also be provided where it maximises social and 
environmental co-benefits./ This would  avoid unintended outcomes of  focusing on emission s while 
neglecting other vital ecosystem services like biodiversity or water quality. 
The new framework should secure a robust assessment and certification 
method for carbon removals from nature-based solutions. 
The certification process should minimise th e burden on farmers and foresters  in order  to increase 
participation and reduce societal costs of otherwise complex monitoring, reporting an d verification  
systems. The system should be robust enough nonetheless  to offer safeguards and incentives against 
the risk of emissions reversal from changing agricultural practices  or sudden and intensive forest 
harvesting. 
Such system can then form the basis of a future incentive -based system for carbon removals, inspired 
by direct payments under the CAP and the EU support mechanism for conversion to organic farming and 
maintenance of the organic status . Voluntary carbon markets as they exist today present many flaws, 
one being that only those entrepreneurs that apply to access the market and be visible to investors are 
likely to receive payments. Similarly to the mechanism under the CAP, we suggest that incentives should 
be directly accessible to anyone fulfilling eligibility criteria, such as a forester who conducts reforestation 
according to ecological principles  (new removals), or a farmer who already has practices in place which

favour carbon removals (preserving the sink). This would also reflect the EU financial support mechanism 
for organic farming,  which is provided for both conversion to organic farming and maintaining organic 
status.5 
Ensuring additionality is key to an effective CDR certification framework. The risk of no additional benefit 
compared to business-as-usual is very common in current carbon markets, particularly where it concerns 
forestry and agricultural credits. In forestry, credits have been show n to  be attribu ted for forest 
conservation where there was never any intention to harvest.6  
It is therefore crucial not to encourage land -use change-inducing practices by i ncentivising temporary 
carbon storage  in products to drive material consumption. Habitat protection is often a more efficient 
solution to protect carbon stocks and sequestration than active restoration .7 The increasing demand for 
forest product s is driving the projected decrease o f European forests’ carbon sink capacity by 30% 
between 2005 and 2030, even while forest cover is increasing.8 This means that the pressures currently 
placed on European forests is destroying their natural ability to sequester carbon.   
IV. Carbon storage in products 
Certification of carbon stored in products: only where sustainable sourcing and 
regeneration are assured, and storage lasts over 100 years. 
The EU should develop and support a strict but encompassing definition and certification of regenerative 
agricultural and forestry practices ,, including closer -to-nature forestry, and support such practices with 
public incentives. Regenerative practices are key to restoring sustainable carbon cycles, however they 
cannot be re duced to their role for carbon remo val. Their definition must include co -benefits for 
biodiversity, water cycles, ecosystem and social resilience. Forestry is regenerative if it employs selective 
harvesting techniques which preserve and enable natural growth, fosters flora and fauna diversity, and 
prevents the loss of ecosystem services such as flood prevention. Forestry is destructive if it entails clear-
cutting followed by tree monocultures, even when the harvested forest area is immediately replaced with 
new plantation.9 Similarly, agriculture is regenerative if  it clears water effluents from fertilising products 
to prevent eutrophication, creates habitats for pollinators, restores sustainable carbon cycles with 
practices such as cover crops, crop rotation, agroforestry, etc. 
The cert ification of carbon stored in long -lasting products produced from virgin biomass  must be 
supported by the enforcement of robust sustainable sourcing principles. The EU legislative framework 
for sustainable biomass sourcing is still in development. The use of virgin biomass should be the subject 
of robust due diligence enshrined in EU law. This requires the strict application of the due diligence 
requirements of the proposed Deforestation and Forest Degradation Regulation (DFDR). The use of

V. Industrial solutions 
Industrial solutions for CDR currently receive too much undue attention, at the 
expense of emission reductions  
When environmentally damaging industries are given credits for claimed carbon removals, the results 
can overall be worse for the environment than intended by the system. One example of this could be the 
incentivisation of  carbon removals  through bioenergy combined with carbon capture  and storage  
(BECCS), whereby woody biomass burning combined with CCS would be deemed ‘green’ because it  
removes biogenic carbon emissions. However, such a system would also encourage the rapid burning of 
slow-growing and therefore limited forest resources, leading to indirect land-use change and the loss of 
vital ecosystem services which forests provide.11 The whole scope of the activity must be accounted for, 
including its non-climate impacts. 
Technological solutions to carbon removal have been subject of promise and pilots, most of which were 
never brought to a significant scale or replicated. There is a massive risk of greenwashing ‘dirty’ activities 
with false promises from unsuccessful carbon removal projects.

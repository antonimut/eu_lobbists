The EU carbon removal framework should enable the 
market value recognition of sustainable farming practices 
and of the storage and long-term use of biogenic CO2 
 
 
The European Biogas Association  (EBA) supports the initiative of an EU framework for carbon removal 
certification . The EBA welcomes the plan of the Commission to make a legislative proposal before 2023. This 
EU framework must be coherent with existing EU legislation and practical, easy-to-use for market players. 
a. To be successful , it is critical that this EU framework is harmonized with other relevant European 
directives and regulations, especiallythe RED II (Directive 2001/2018), which contain rules on GHG 
accounting.  
b. The legal framework must also be manageable in practice and must not create excessive bureaucracy 
for the obliged parties and marker players. 
 
A Carbon Removal Certification Mechanism for sequestration of biogenic CO2 (with or without conversion) 
is strongly needed. Such Mechanism should: 
- Be grounded in a common definition of carbon dioxide removal 
- be applicable to both ETS and non-ETS sectors. 
- Should in particular define solutions for simple and fast achievable carbon removal (NBS: Nature-
based Solution). 
- include rules for the assessment of carbon dioxide removal (the permanence of the carbon storage) 
in products and construction material 
- propose monitoring and reporting rules for carbon removal with biogenic CO2 
- allow for the trading of carbon removal certificate on voluntary markets 
 
Additionally, the EBA calls on the European institutions to recognise in EU legislation biogenic CO2 whether 
it is stored underground as part of a carbon removal project (bio-CCS) or used in short -term or long -term 
products (bio-CCU and, when storage in products is long-term, bio-CCUS) to provide a basis for market value 
of biogenic CO2 from biogas upgrading. 
 
Definition of carbon dioxide removals 
The definition of carbon dioxide removals needs to be clear and comprehensive. As suggested in the reports 
of the German Federal Environment Agency, both areas (NBS: Nature-based Solution, TBS: Technology-based 
Solution) should be considered and measures described. It  should be considered that in some projects

Carbon removal by agricultural activities (NBS) 
The European Biogas Association  (EBA) supports the initiative of the European Commission to encourage 
carbon farming practices. 
Besides the lack of a concrete framework, there are two kind of barriers to carbon sequestration: the positive 
externalities are not sufficiently rewarded, and the negative externalities are not sufficiently penalized. 
Rewarding climate action and applying the “polluter-pays” principle would set the needed price signals 
 
The EBA urge to support practices that enhance soil carbon sequestration: 
a. Incentivising farmers to adopt management practices  leading to  healthier soil . Farming systems in 
combination with biogas plants are optimal for increasing the C-content in the soil.

plant this additional second crop can be harvested. The economic interest of the cover crops is low – its main 
goal is to protect soil and nutrients. However, their value increases if renewable energy policies allow their 
use for renewable energy production2 and if incentive schemes recognise their land-based carbon removals.

Carbon removal based on CCS/CCUS of biogenic CO2  from the biogas industry  
(Industrial-Based solution) 
A. The biogas industry is an “easy-to-access” source of biogenic CO2 
Biogas and biomethane are today produced from fresh organic materials (which   contain biogenic CO 2) 
through the anaerobic digestion process. The organic materials used as biogas and biomethane feedstock 
are themselves derived from biomass, directly (agriculture residues, intermediary crops, green waste etc.) or 
indirectly (sewage sludge, manure, etc.) and contain organic carbon. 
Biogenic CO2 from anaerobic digestion can be captured at two different stages in the process: 
1. During the biogas upgrading process, biogas is split into CH 4 and CO2 to get biomethane that can be 
used in the same applications as natural gas. The CO2 is captured at relatively low cost due to its high 
purity. 
2. On biogas plants fitted with combined heat and power (CHP), biogenic CO2 can be captured from the 
flue gas during the biogas combustion. 
Gasification of sustainable biomass can also be a source of biogenic CO 2. It converts dry, organic solid 
substances like ligno -cellulosic biomass into biochar and “syngas”, which is a gaseous  mixture containing 
mainly hydrogen, methane, carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide. 
Furthermore, the recently developed technologies for production of biohydrogen from the raw biogas of 
anaerobic digestion can be a third pathway to capture biogenic CO2. Biohydrogen can be obtained from other 
additional sources apart from anaerobic digestion and further contribute to the biogenic CO2 production.

B. Use of biochar produced by gasification of biomass 
Gasification of sustainable biomass also produces biochar. Biochar is a carbon -rich material that is 
produced by biomass heating in a low oxygen environment, a process called pyrolysis. Organic substances are broken 
down at temperatures ranging from 350°C to 1000°C, producing a gaseous, a liquid an d a solid phase. They can be 
used either for energy generation or as commodity materials. While the gaseous and liquid fractions are generally 
used for energy production, the solid fraction represents a valuable and highly versatile product.  
 
As long as the biochars are not thermally used (burnt or combusted), the carbon is kept over hundreds to thousands 
of years7. Instead, through the process of photosynthesis, plants capture carbon dioxide (CO2) from the atmosphere

and incorporate the carbon (C) while emitting oxygen (O2). By the conversion of biomass to biochar, parts (30-70%) 
of this carbon are made recalcitrant. 
 
Common uses for biochars are found in agriculture where they serve as feed amendment, stable bedding, for slurry 
stabilization, in biogas plants or direc tly in soils, e.g. as a carrier matrix for nutrients. New applications of biochars 
include their use in building materials such as concrete or asphalt, in plastics or in high-tech where they can replace 
fossil resources. 
 
The biochar market is growing exponentially. A recent market analysis of the European Biochar Industry Consortium 
(EBI) shows a 5 -year CAGR of 38% and a 3 -year CAGR of even 42%. In 2020, the growth rate was 70% and in 2021 
even higher rates are expected. 
 
C. Biogenic CCUS in products and construction material should be considered 
Biogenic CO2 can be stored in construction material and can generate emission credits that can be traded, 
including within the ETS.  When long -term storage is combined with the use of the biogenic CO2 to 
manufacture new materials, we refer to “biogenic CCUS” or “bio-CCUS”. 
 
CO2 can be used in the production of building materials to replace water in concrete, called CO2 curing, or 
as a raw material in its constituents (cement and construction aggregates). The CO2 is reacted with minerals 
or waste streams, such as iron slag, to fo rm carbonates (the form of carbon that makes up concrete). This 
conversion pathway is typically less energy -intensive than for fuels and chemicals and involves permanent 
storage of CO2 in the materials. Some CO2-based building materials can have superior performance compared 
with their conventional counterparts. A few applications, such as the use of CO 2 in concrete mixing, are 
already commercially available in some markets today (IEA, 2019). 
 
Mineralisation is a natural phenomenon, where Ca (calcium)- or Mg (magnesium)-containing minerals (e.g. 
olivine) react with carbon dioxide (CO2) to produce calcium or magnesium carbonate (CaCO3 or MgCO3), 
also known as limestone or dolomite, one of the most abundant roc k types.  This natural carbonation 
reaction, which happens in nature over thousands of years, can be purposefully accelerated to take only a 
few minutes in man -made manufacturing processes  (accelerated carbonation) by using high CO2 
concentrations and optim ized reaction conditions. The reaction is exothermic, meaning that it releases 
energy as heat and leads to the creation of stable products in which the CO2 is permanently captured. 
 
From a circular economy perspective, accelerated carbonation can bring a significant contribution to three 
major societal challenges:  the mitigation of climate change, the management of waste materials and the 
reduction of the consumption of natural resources.  
 
The CO2 used in the carbonation process gets permanently sequestere d (under the form of stable 
carbonate) in the end products and this CO2 is therefore no longer emitted to the atmosphere (climate 
mitigation benefit). Mineral waste coming from various industry sectors are transformed into valuable 
construction materials, so they no longer have to be disposed of in landfills (waste management benefit). 
Similarly, construction and demolition waste can also be looped back into the production of fresh 
construction materials and diverted from landfills. This also reduces the ne ed for extracting fresh mineral 
resources from quarries (natural resources benefit) 
 
Two main routes of mineralisation are currently under deployment or development:

1. concrete curing 
2. Mineral waste recycling 
 
Concrete curing 
CO2 can be injected during the curing of concrete in traditional ready-mix or precast processes without major 
changes needed to the process or ingredients with the effect of accelerating the hydration of the cement. 
Other technologies exist for curing of concrete with CO2 instead of water: this requires a novel cement and 
special curing chambers, but the amount of CO2 taken up in the product is higher – up to 250 kg of CO2 for 
every ton of cement used.  
 
Mineral waste recycling 
CO2 can also be injected as part of the recycling process of  a large range of Ca/Mg-rich mineral waste (e.g. 
slags, ashes, kiln dust, tailings, etc.) coming from various industrial sectors (power, steel, cement, mining, 
etc.) or from the construction/demolition sector to create diverse products such as aggregates (which can be 
used in road foundations or in the preparation of fresh concrete), construction bricks and blocks, concrete 
fillers, etc 
As an example, Sibelco and Rosetti Marino started to develop a new technology for the Sibelco Poviglio site 
(Italy) for the permanent CO2 sequestration by use of olivine. The produced Ca/Mg-rich carbonates will be 
reused in the cement and ceramic industries.

About the EBA  
 
The European Biogas Association is the voice of renewable gas in Europe since 2009. EBA advocates the recognition of 
biomethane and other renewable gases as sustainable, on deman d and flexible energy sources that provide multiple 
knock-on socio -economic and environmental benefits. Supported by its members, EBA is committed to work with 
European institutions, industry, agricultural partners, NGOs and academia to develop policies wh ich can enable the 
large-scale deployment of renewable gases and organic fertilisers throughout Europe, supported by transparent, well -
established sustainability certification bodies to ensure that sustainability remains at the core of the industry. The 
association counts today on a well -established network of over 200 national organisations, scientific institutes, and 
companies from Europe and beyond.

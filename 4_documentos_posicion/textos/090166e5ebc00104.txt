Stora Enso contributes to making the EU and world carbon neutral in following ways:  
• by capturing, or sequestrating, carbon dioxide in growing trees 
• by storage of carbon in harvested wood products 
• by substitution, when bio-based products and bioenergy replace other products that are fossil-based 
• by becoming the first forest products company to have its climate targets approved by the Science Based Targets initiative in 2017  
• In 2019, our total climate impact, including avoided emissions by product substitution and forest sequestration, was 12 million tonnes of CO2 
• In addition to that we also operate at the heart of the bioeconomy and contributes to a circular economy with our renewable and recyclable products 
Stora Enso 
Stora Enso is a renewable materials company that employs 
over 18 000 people in the EU. We deliver products that store 
carbon and provide alternatives to fossil-based products. 
While we are one of the biggest private forest owners in the 
world, we also source wood from other, smaller -scale 
private forest owners.  
 
The forest industry plays a key role in the green transition 
and tackling climate change. Stora Enso supports the 
Commission’s targets of reaching a 55% emissions 
reduction by 2030, and net zero  emissions by 2050. As a 
significant forest owner and purchaser, we have a great 
responsibility to contribute to these ambitions. We want to 
be a constructive partner to the Commission in the pursuit 
of the Green Deal’s vision. 
 
With our extensive expertise in the development of carbon 
storage products as well as in sustainable forest 
management, we would like to provide some suggestions 
on the establishment of a  regulatory framework for the 
certification of carbon removals. 
  
• Carbon removals vary, and the specificities of 
nature-based solutions should be adequately 
considered. 
• Technical solutions such as bio -energy with 
carbon capture and storage (BECCS) require 
greater incentives. 
• Carbon removals should be included in the 
accounting of bio-based carbon-storage products 
in LULUCF. 
• A flexible framework should be developed that 
ensures minimum standards on reporting and 
transparency are applied. 
 
Carbon removals vary, and the specificities of nature -
based solutions should be adequately considered 
Carbon removal solutions range from nature -based 
solutions enhancing ecosystem removals, such as forestry 
or agriculture, to technical solutions such as wood building 
products, or the permanent storage of CO 2 in geological 
formations or in innovative aggregates with carbon 
mineralisation. Different types of removals have different  
 
 
 
 
 
 
characteristics, costs, duration  (permanence) and risks  
(e.g. reversal) . Accordingly, there needs to be a 
differentiated approach. 
 
For example,  Puro.earth provides certificates for different 
“products” ranging from soil food (derived from industry 
waste streams) with a minimum storage period of 20 years; 
through to biochar for a period of 100-1000+ years. 
Nature-based solutions are not permanent and might 
require monitoring to avoid the risk of reversal, as could be 
in the case of a certified forest catching fire.  For example, 
in LULUCF, there is always a margin of error in calculating 
the carbon sink, and f igures are amended retrospectively . 
However, we believe that criteria defining the different types 
of carbon removals  and related certificates  should not put 
forestry at a competitive disadvantage and disregard the 
social and environmental co-benefits stemming from forests 
and forest-based solutions.  
 
Moreover, we believe that if setting criteria for carbon 
removals via existing managed forests, the focus should be 
on the long -term growth of healthy forests rather than on 
passive management of forests by e xtensively lengthening 
rotation periods . Such passive practices are not 
recommended for most forests  to improve their climate 
change mitigation effect  (Lundmark et al. 2018: Carbon 
balance in production forestry in relation to rotation length ) 
and should not be credited as carbon removal projects . 
Instead, we recommend promoting active management to 
increase forests’ growth by e.g., ensuring regeneration with 
appropriate tree species and seedlings from breeding 
programmes, the addition of growth-promoting nutrients as 
needed, and close monitoring and tending of forests to 
minimise damage from pests and pathogens.   
 
Given that growing forests capture more carbon, which 
continues to be stored when trees are harvested for 
products, it may make sense to limit carbon removal 
certificates to certain types or functions or forests.  For 
example, it may be advisable for certificates only to be 
created for the areas which are afforested/reforested for 
environmental protection purposes (e.g. restoration, 
erosion control etc.), and for which there is sufficient 
suitable land available.

Technical solutions such as BECCS require greater 
incentives 
Industrial solutions able to capture  and store CO 2 
permanently should support and complement nature-based 
removals in the long -run. There is agreement among 
academia and climate modellers that, without deploying 
technology-based carbon removals, it is unlikely that we will 
be able to reach the goals of the Paris agreement. BECCS 
technologies could even create net negative emissions; yet, 
the economic feasibility of this solution should be better 
incentivised to be fully scalable. 
 
Voluntary carbon markets such as Puro.earth are already 
testing out trading as a financial incentive. However, a 
carbon price of ca. EUR 100/T is needed for BECCS to be 
economically viable. This means that public investment will 
be an essential catalyst for scalability.  Whilst the EU 
Innovation Fund is a very welcome element in the funding 
landscape, it will not, alone, be sufficient. Stora Enso 
recommends monitoring developments in Sweden, where 
the Government is planning reverse auctions to kick-start 
full-scale BECCS projects.  Furthermore, Stora Enso 
recommends that, if BECCS is included in the carbon 
removals certification scheme, the Commission should 
prioritise operations where biogenic emissions are the 
result of production processes and not purely resulting 
from heat and energy.  This means that finance would be 
directed in the most effective way, with co-benefits to the 
emissions captured, as products provide additional 
benefits to society, especially if they replace fossil-based 
products.

A flexible framework should be developed that ensures 
minimum standards on reporting and transparency are 
applied.  
Stora Enso believes that the best approach is to create a 
framework on reporting and transparency with common 
minimum standards at the EU level  to avoid the risk of 
greenwashing. We agree with the European Commission 
that the certification rules should set scientifically robust 
requirements for quality of measurement, monitoring, 
reporting and verification of  the carbon removed from the 
atmosphere. We also agree that t he certification rules 
should put in place strong safeguards to make sure that 
carbon removal activities do no harm to biodiversity and 
other sustainability objectives. 
 
Areas deserving further discussion  
Designing a certification framework for carbon removals is 
a complex task and there are many outstanding questions.  
The following areas will require further analysis before 
concrete legislative proposals are published: 
• Ownership of carbon removals?  Value chains 
are often long.  For example, with cross-laminated 
timber (CLT) being sold for construction, is the 
owner of the carbon stored in that ultimate wood 
product the forest owner whose trees were 
harvested?  The company that produced the CLT? 
The building owner?  To whom does the revenue 
from the sale of the certificate go?  How is this 
decided?   Stora Enso believes that ownership 
should be linked to the economic  actor that 
enables the storage in the product. 
• Permanence: What is permanent and what is non-
permanent storage, in realistic terms?  What are 
acceptable periods for non-permanent storage?  
Not all products will store carbon for 100+ years, 
but they  still provide value and non-permanent 
carbon storage. All removals, permanent or non-
permanent, should be incentivised until we 
become carbon neutral as a society. After this has 
been achieved, non-permanent storage can be 
downgraded, but given the climate crisis, all 
options are  desperately needed. 
• How do we align carbon removals certification 
with e.g. GHG Protocol, LULUCF (accounting), 
climate neutral ISO standard, SBTI Flag 
initiative?  How do we ensure there is coherence 
across all these initiatives?  We recommend taking 
account of lessons / data from relevant pilot 
projects e.g. the GHG Protocol Land Sector and 
Removals Guidance currently being developed 
and tested (due for publication in 2023).   
• How do we ensure that carbon removals policy 
interacts with the Circular Economy agenda?  
For example, if a company takes  Stora Enso’s 
sludge (i.e., residue) from our operations, sells it to 
agroindustry to use as a nutrient like a fertiliser?  
This is giving a second life to a by-product.  It also 
reinforces the  issue of value chains in carbon 
removals.   
• Given the carbon re movals consultation 
questionnaire asks (Q5) if “labelling of sustainable 
products” should be an objective for certification of 
carbon removals , how do we bridge the gap 
between the system and the product?  The PEF 
process is complex, for example on issues such as 
on “guarantees of origin”.   If the Commission were 
to go down this route, we would recommend 
issuing  detailed guidance.   
Conclusions 
Overall, w e support the Commission’s aim of creating a 
framework to incentivise carbon rem ovals. Commonly 
agreed standards will provide more consistency and 
transparency and will provide an additional opportunity to 
channel public and private financ ing to carbon removals. 
The key objective is in fact creating or improving the market 
for carbon and channeling finance where it is most needed. 
We welcome that non -permanent storage in products is 
also being considered among potential options. We would 
suggest adding removals provided by bio -based carbon 
storage products. We are in a transition, and whilst 
permanent carbon removals are, of course, a priority, non -
permanent storage in products delays emissions and will 
buy us time to find and scale up even more solutions, such 
as BECCS.  We would also call on the Commission to 
carefully take into account the specificities of nature-based 
solutions, as well as consider further dialogue on issues 
where many questions remain. To reach climate targets, we 
will need to use all the tools at our disposal. In doing so, we 
should not lose focus on generating fewer emissions in the 
first place.

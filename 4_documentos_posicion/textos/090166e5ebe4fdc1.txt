Sitra’s statement on the European Commission’s proposal for the Data Act 
Sitra’s main observations: 
The Finnish Innovation Fund Sitra promotes the fair data economy model that is human-centric and creates 
benefits to all participants from all sectors. Our vision is in line with the European strategy for data (2020) and the 
attempt to create a single market for data built on European values.  
Sitra supports the Data Act as a core part of the fair data economy. We strongly emphasise the proposal’s 
importance in complementing the digital single market in the EU, strengthening competitiveness, boosting 
innovations, and empowering people and data-driven societies by increasing the availability and use of data 
across sectors and industries. 
Sitra supports the proposal in line with our comments to the previous hearing (Dec 2021). 
For individuals, the Data Act improves their position in the data economy by strengthening their right to data 
sovereignty and taking steps towards ensuring that they benefit from the added value from the data that they 
generated. The proposal targets everyone who produces and uses data, and it affects everyone indirectly as 
consumers, citizens, employees, and entrepreneurs. In terms of its significance, the Data Act is comparable to the 
GDPR, and it could create regulatory symmetry as data sets often are mixed, i.e., they contain both personal and 
non-personal information. 
For companies, the Data Act imposes obligations but also incentivises to make data available and accessible so 
that the data is interoperable and portable. This could create a new market in Europe (cf. Gaia-X). Currently, the 
costs of switching are high, which leads to undesirable lock-in situations. 
The society benefits from the Data Act. Data-driven innovation and solutions boost the competitiveness of the 
European markets, which should benefit the overall welfare and contribute towards the twin transition targets. 
The dual dilemma: Innovative data-driven societies and the regulatory burden: 
The data strategy recognised that fragmented data legislation between Member States poses a risk for the single 
market for data. The Data Act harmonises legislation with respect to accessing and sharing data more fairly 
between the actors in the data economy. It does so by imposing new rules that target primarily the business 
sector. 
Sitra emphasises that the regulatory burden especially for SMEs needs to be proportionate and facilitated. For 
businesses, new rules mean allocating resources to compliance to avoid being sanctioned by penalties by national 
competent authorities. According to Sitra’s survey (2021), regulation is the biggest obstacle for European SMEs to 
get involved in the data economy. The problem is not regulation per se, rather the clarity and simplicity thereof, 
as SMEs typically have less resources to allocate on regulatory sense-making than the bigger players. 
Furthermore, Sitra calls for further clarifying the relationship between the Data Act and other legislation relevant 
to the data economy. Alongside the Data Act, many proposals for legislation were introduced in the aftermath of 
the data strategy, and their relationships with each other as well as with the existing legislation needs to be in 
focus to prevent undesired regulatory burdens and overlaps with the many definitions and concepts. 
On the Data Act’s relationship with the GDPR, the Data Act aims to strengthen the existing access and portability 
rights of data subjects with respect to their personal data. It is important that the Data Act is well-consolidated

with the GDPR – as well as other instruments – to avoid conflicts or dilution of the rights under the GDPR (e.g., 
the legal grounds for processing personal data in the GDPR). 
On definitions, Sitra has discussed with stakeholders widely from both private and public sectors, and especially 
the definition of “data” in the proposal raises concerns. It remains uncertain what type of data is covered (raw or 
processed). As the starting point is the useability of data, the definition of data should not be prohibitively strict 
but focus on the use of data and tailored to the context. 
Moreover, the scope of application of the Data Act has sparked questions in Sitra’s experience. The proposal is an 
important step towards data sovereignty. The Data Act focuses explicitly on data generated by the use of IoT 
products or related service (recital 15). Based on stakeholder comments, it could be further clarified that what 
applications are excluded from the scope (recital 16). 
On regulatory coherence, Sitra recommends utilising other initiatives to clarify provisions and support the 
efficient application of the Data Act (e.g., the Data Support Centre). 
Fairness of data sharing: 
On data access and use, Sitra supports the prohibition of unfair contractual terms unilaterally imposed on SMEs 
and the Commission’s model contractual terms. In developing the model terms, Sitra encourages the Commission 
to work closely with the stakeholders in a bottom-up fashion and seek inspiration from the industry – many actors 
active in the data economy have already been proactive in this field (e.g., Sitra’s Fair Data Economy Rulebook, and 
Technology Industries Finland’s Data Sharing Model Agreement). The establishment of the Expert Group on B2B 
data sharing and cloud computing contracts is a welcomed initiative to develop balanced model contract terms. 
On compensation, businesses that share their data for other businesses or the government are entitled to fair 
compensation. The definition of what is deemed to constitute “reasonable compensation” (costs of making data 
available and reasonable margin) needs to be further clarified in the proposal. This would reduce legal uncertainty 
and the shifting the probability of dealing with disagreements ex-post in dispute settlement. 
On B2G data sharing, mandatory data sharing for the public interest could create benefits for the society. The 
proposal sets that businesses are entitled to compensation in situations of an “exceptional need” but not in 
situations of “public emergencies”. The definitions of the grounds for mandatory sharing are rather extensive and 
open for interpretation, which is not conducive for the business sector. To improve legal certainty, clarification of 
the qualifying instances are needed. 
Switching between data processing services: 
Sitra recognises that mandatory switching between data processing services is an ambitious and important step 
on the road towards a fairer data economy. This development emphasises the importance of interfaces, 
rulebooks, and standards, i.e., soft infrastructure. Soft infrastructure is urgently needed to facilitate the data 
flows envisioned in the Data Act and the European Data Strategy in general. 
The EU could ensure the deployment of a mission-oriented approach to well-functioning and fair data markets 
with appropriate funding instruments, which would promote and incentivise the development of the necessary 
soft infrastructure for a free flow of data in the EU. This is like the progress made with the GSM when it was 
developed in public-private partnership. 
       
   
   
   
 
Sitra has created, in close coordination with multiple stakeholders in Finland and across EU, an open-source 
rulebook and a reference architecture for data flows (the IHAN blueprint) which are used, e.g., in public sectors to 
spearhead the digitalisation initiative Virtual Finland. These could be used as references in EU-level work. 
Stakeholders: 
Sitra emphasises the importance of stakeholder engagement as a theme that should be considered horizontally in 
the assessment of this proposal. 
On European standards (interoperability), the work should be industry-led with a bottom-up approach. Where the 
Commission has jurisdiction to lead the work on European standards, the private sector needs to be heard 
actively and the work should be more industry-led rather than conducted top-down (cf. PSD2).

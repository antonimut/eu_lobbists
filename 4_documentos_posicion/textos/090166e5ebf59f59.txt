1. Introduction 
ASD welcomes the proposed European Chips Act, which has the potential to strengthen Europe’s position 
in the global semiconductor value chain and its security of supply. This can enhance the EU’s resilience, 
strategic autonomy, technological sovereignty, and industrial competitiveness. To achieve its objectives, 
the European Chips Act must ensure efficiency in resource allocation, coherence among all relevant 
initiatives, and close involvement of industry in its governance. A truly strategic approach to chips must 
support especially the strategically important defence, aeronautics , and space industries , with specific 
measures across all pillars of the initiative. 
 
2. Semiconductors in defence and aerospace 
Semiconductors are an essential component of defence, aeronautics, and space products, like for many 
other sectors. At the same time, the defence and aerospace segment of the chips market has certain 
significant specificities. First, it is of outstanding strategic importance, but accounts for only around 1% of 
the global semiconductor market demand. Secondly, defence and aerospace products contain both chips 
that appear also in non-strategic, commercial products , but also some specifically designed chips . 
Whereas commercial chip production focuses on cost-efficiency at large volumes of demand, production 
for defence and aerospace requires durability, reliable high performance under very demanding 
conditions, long-life supply, and security of information. Our defence and aerospace also generate much 
smaller volumes  of demand , including through  short production runs of high -value products (e.g. 
radiation-hardened or space-qualified designs). Consequently, our ecosystem has little ‘bargaining power’ 
and ability to influence related investments and market choices compared to higher-volume sectors, such 
as automotive and consumer electronics. 
The defence and aerospace ecosystem is particularly sensitive  to supply disruptions. It is already 
experiencing acutely the chips shortages  affecting Europe since  the outbreak of the coronavirus 
pandemic. Unable to meet global demand and driven by pure market logic, chips manufacturers have 
prioritised domestic markets and high-volume industries and products. As a result, current semiconductor 
capacity for defence and aerospace manufacturing is well below market demand . In addition, the 
deprioritisation of small-volume/high-value products creates the risk of permanent loss of manufacturing 
competence, with dire long-term impacts on security of supply. More generally, our industries regularly 
face difficulties with the short lifecycles of semiconductor components  incorporated in products with 
much longer lifecycles. Frequent obsolescence of components is therefore a key issue that the European 
Chips Act does not address adequately. 
Supply disruptions of semiconductors pose an economic, and potentially social challenge. However, when 
it comes to strategic sectors such as defence and aerospace, they also pose a security problem, as they 
endanger the delivery of products that are critical for Member States’ defence capabilities, the safe and 
secure functioning of critical transportation, communication equipment and infrastructure . This 
undermines the EU’s objective to enhance its strategic autonomy and is highly problematic in a context 
of rising geopolitical tensions and increasing weaponisation of trade and technology flows. Having 
recognised the geopolitical salience of semiconductors, other leading economies are already undertaking 
intensive efforts to strengthen their domestic design and production capacities. Against this background,

the EU must urgently reduce its strategic dependencies on non-like-minded geopolitical actors for critical 
components and critical raw materials, boost its competitiveness in state -of-the-art technologies, and  
ensure in particular the security of supply of chips for essential sectors like defence and aerospace, with 
their relatively limited market power. 
 
3. European Chips Act 
3.1. Horizontal aspects 
We support the European Commission’s focus on  next-generation technologies  for cutting -edge and 
sustainable semiconductors. Technologies such as transistor sizes below 2 nm, disruptive technologies for 
AI, ultra low-power energy-efficient processors, high performance FPGAs, electrification, photonics, and 
quantum chips will be key to addressing the future needs of strategic sectors. Hence, it is crucial to swiftly 
develop and protect European IP and to ensure that Europe has the necessary highly skilled workforce in 
these areas. At the same time, many European downstream industries will continue to require older 
generation and specialised larger feature chips. Indeed, the defence and aerospace sectors depend on 
mature and legacy technology nodes designed in the 1990s and 2000s , for which many chips 
manufacturers do not want to maintain or create additional capacity. It is therefore necessary to also 
support production and secure availability for more mature technology components, until the respective 
industries transition to the latest generation of chips. 
We also welcome the Commission’s aim to bolster security of supply by closing the gap “from the lab to 
the fab”.  We are concerned , however,  that Europe’s chips strategy does not sufficiently address raw 
materials and advanced packaging, which is a key dimension of security of supply , together with critical 
industrial assets such as foundries. The European Chips Act should feature specific measures to address 
these topics, such as the establishment of a strategic stockpile of raw materials to ensure the supply of 
material inputs for the production of chips. The European Raw Materials Alliance has an important role 
to play in the implementation of such measures. 
To deliver on its objectives, the European Chips Act counts on around €43 billion of policy-driven public 
and private investment up to 2030, including €11 billion of public investment under the Chips for Europe 
Initiative. We are sceptical that these amounts of public funding match the initiative’s level of ambition , 
especially given the funding that other economies provide to their domestic ecosystems. This is especially 
the case if the Commission intends to reallocate existing budget lines to finance these 11B€. Digital Europe 
and Horizon Europe , as well as  the already under -resourced European Defence Fund , have their own 
objectives that also support European sovereignty. Taking resources from these programs would certainly 
not contribute to Europe’s strategic autonomy. 
Against this background, making the best possible use of available resources is imperative for fulfilling the 
aims of the EU’s chips strategy. To that end, it will be key to ensure coherence and clear links between 
the European Chips Ac t and all relevant national and EU initiatives, including the Industrial Alliance on 
Semiconductors, the upcoming IPCEI on microelectronics and the future Observatory for Critical 
Technologies (OCT). While the envisaged advisory role of the Alliance on Semiconductors in the Chips for 
Europe initiative is a welcome step in this direction, greater clarity is still needed, for example, on the role 
of the OCT in the supply chain mapping and monitoring mechanism.

Making efficient use of finite resources may also require prioritising specific technologies and value chain 
segments. In this respect , w e believe that special emphasis should be put on design activities  and 
integration and test activities. Ensuring a strong presence of these two segments in Europe will enable 
access to foundries regardless of their location, while mastering these activities will also grant Europe 
independence in heterogeneous integration, which is a key area of interest for the near future. 
Since autarchy in semiconductor production is both unrealistic and undesirable , prioritisation must be 
coupled with coordination with trusted partners. This would ensure  complementarity of effort s and 
increase efficiency along the entire value chain. It would also allow addressing potential disagreements 
between partners, such as over the US Defence Priorities and Allocation System, which can prevent 
European access to core US electronic components. In pursuing cooperation, the EU should make the best 
use of existing fora, including the EU -US Trade and Technology Council. In this context, the close 
involvement of industry is essential.  
Industry will play a key role overall in the implementation of th e European Chips Act . Given its direct 
involvement in value chains, it is well placed to monitor market trends and assess potential remedies to 
supply disruptions. We are therefore convinced that industry, particularly the semiconductor and critical 
downstream sectors, should be closely involved in the governance of the EU’s chips strategy, including as 
full members of the Semiconductor Board and its subsidiary bodies. This would also improve coordination 
between upstream and downstream sectors , enabling industry to address supply disruptions without 
extensive market intervention. 
Finally, mapping and monitoring global value chain s requires an approach that reflects the ir complex, 
cross-border nature and en sures coherence of information. We therefore call for centralised data 
gathering by a single designated body at the EU level, to which companies across the Union would report 
following clear and streamlined requirements. To ensure the confidentiality of se nsitive information 
provided by industry, an appropriate data governance framework  should be put in place , covering data 
transparency, interoperability, sharing, access, and security. 
 
3.2. Critical and strategic sectors 
We welcome that the European Chips Act recognises the importance of ‘critical sectors’, including defence 
and space, and that it provides for their prioritisation in case of significant supply disruptions. However, a 
comprehensive strategy should prioritise these sectors under all pillars, rather than just in monitoring and 
crisis response. Specific measures to support critical sectors under the other two pillars should include 
priority-rate access to pilot lines , as well as  the possibility to prioritise orders from critical sectors to 
Integrated Production Facilities and EU Open Foundries  and the assurance that such facilities reserve 
production capacity to serve critical sectors, even outside ‘crisis mode’. 
Furthermore, the European Chips Act should specifically support the defence , aeronautics, and space 
sectors, following an integrated approach towards the ecosystem as a whole . Given these sectors’ 
specificities, their security of supply requires a criticality -driven approach, focusing on their strategic 
importance rather than on their market size. Thus, these strategic sectors should be prioritised over other 
critical and economically important sectors across all pillars of the initiative, but especially in the context 
of crisis response.

With respect to the first pillar , new chip and process designs developed under the Chips for Europe 
Initiative should take into account the unique requirements of semiconductors used in defence, space 
and aeronautical systems. The development of such designs should be incentivised, including by granted 
them priority access to the pilot lines  for prototypes. Defence, aeronautics, and space should also be 
among the sectors prioritised for develop ing reference certification procedures. Leveraging existing 
standards and developing new ones will be pivotal in this process. To maximise the potential for synergies, 
the Chips for Europe  Initiative could also support the development of joint military -civilian standards in 
the context of the European Standardisation Strategy. 
Concerning the second pillar, we welcome that the definition of the first-of-a-kind principle provides for 
supporting innovation in technology node as well as  in new integrated production facilities, system 
integration capabilities, process technology, performance, and sustainability. Additionally, the European 
Commission’s assessment of first-of-a-kind facilities and of state aid to production facilities could consider 
a facility’s commitment to support the defence and aerospace ecosystem, including by prioritising orders 
from these sectors or by reserving production capacity for chips that address their special needs (e.g. in 
terms of durability  and performance). Another measure to bolster security of supply  in the short -term 
would be to establish a strategic reserve for chips used in defence, aeronautics and space systems. 
As regards the third pillar, we welcome the emphasis on mapping and monitoring supply chains, which is 
vital for anticipating, avoiding, and responding to crises. To be effective, this mechanism should consider 
the specificities of each critical sector. For instance, certain risks are particularly relevant for defence and 
aerospace, including the impact of ESG policies, EU regulations (e.g. REACH), and the foreign and trade 
policies of countries where potential s uppliers are established (e.g. US ITAR/EAR).  Finally, it is crucial to 
secure semiconductor supplies for the defence and aerospace industries in times of crisis. During global 
shortages, these sectors compete against much higher-volume ones for access to the same products and 
production capacities. Therefore, it is important that the strategically essential defence and aerospace 
ecosystem is prioritised over other critical and ‘economically important’ sectors in terms of contracts and 
orders throughout the supply chain. As regards common purchasing, it would provide limited benefit to 
our industries, for which crises response depends on timely delivery combined with quality assurance, 
rather than on overall quantities. 
 
4. Conclusion 
The European Chips Act  has the potential to foster a vibrant, state -of-the-art European semiconductor 
ecosystem and enhance the  security of supply  of European downstream industries , including in the 
strategically important defence and aerospace sectors . It can thus provide a model for other sectorial 
industrial policy initiatives that aim to pursue common European objectives and boost the EU’s resilience, 
strategic autonomy, technological sovereignty , and competitiveness. To achieve these objectives, and 
with a view to providing a robust blueprint for future initiatives, we recommend that the EU’s chips 
strategy ensures the following: 
• Support production and secure availability for both advanced and mature semiconductor 
technologies used in Europe, and  better integrate the dimension of raw materials to security of 
supply. 
• Make best use of finite resources by prioritising specific value chain segments, particularly design 
and test and integration activities, and coordinating efforts with trusted partners.

• Ensure coherence between all relevant policy and funding instruments, taking care that other 
programmes contributing to the EU’s strategic objectives are not under-resourced. 
• Closely involve industry stakeholders from the semiconductors and downstream cr itical sectors 
in the governance of the initiative. 
• Prioritise, across all pillars, support for critical sectors and particularly the strategically important 
defence, aeronautics and space ecosystem. Relevant measures should include: 
o Pillar I: incentivise new chip designs that meet defence and aerospace needs, including 
by providing for priority-rate access to pilot lines and supporting joint civ-mil standards; 
o Pillar II: when assessing ‘first -of-a-kind' facilities and state aid, consider a facility’s 
commitment to support the defence and aerospace sectors, including through priority -
rate access and reserving production capacity for chips that cover these sectors’ needs; 
consider establishing a strategic reserve of chips for these sectors; 
o Pillar III: take into consideration the specificities of the defence and aerospace industries 
when mapping and monitoring supply chains and prioritise defence and aerospace 
contracts and orders throughout the supply chain during crises.

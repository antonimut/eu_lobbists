Enel’s position paper on the EU Commission proposal for a Data Act 
 
 
The Enel Group  is a multinational company and a leading operator in the power and renewables markets of 
Europe and worldwide. Enel realizes the energy sector is living a transition towards a new geopolitical paradigm 
in which legislators and consumers shall reconsider and adapt not only the Green Deal, but also Europe’s digital 
sovereignty strategy to a long-term, strategic rethink of existing and future EU policies.  
Availability of data may contribute to further energy efficiency, integration of renewables, grid resilience, and 
innovative businesses such as smart mobility, etc., that might enhance Europe’s energy and digital sovereignty. 
Digitalization can accelerate the decarbonisation of the  energy sector 1 by 20% in 2030 and make it more 
efficient.  
As a cornerstone to state the principle of data sovereignty – people and organisations to have self-determination 
with regards to data they generate  - the European Commission published on 23 February 2022, its draft for 
regulation to ensure fair access and distribution to data, the Data Act. Data protection and competition law  
requirements are interwoven in the proposal, with the Act drawing heavily on c ommon themes in recent 
European legislation and enforcement actions in the digital field. 
The Enel Group welcome the Act as a positive step  towards a Europe fit for the digital age , and see some 
possibilities to improve the holistic, practical, executional side of the Data Act.  
 
 Recommendation for policymakers

1. Legislators shall identify per sector, which are the EU’s sensitive data and emergency data 
needed for a better securitization, management, and analysis.  
To ensure legal certainty, the Data Act should provide clear and unambiguous definitions and be fully aligned 
with the purposes of boosting a data economy as required by the EU Data Strategy, while taking into 
consideration the protection of the EU essential services.   
• Ensure a consistent level of protection for critical infrastructure information , avoiding the share of 
highly sensitive data such as its configuration parameters and settings, logs, software level s; as 
well as detailed  information about  restoration or system defence plans ( Regulation (EU) 
2017/2196 establishing a network code on electricity emergency and restoration), and about the 
structure, contingences, (Regulation (EU) 2017/1485 establishing a guideline on electricity transmission 
system operation ), as well as technical supervision and control data regarding central remote 
management of end -point devices (e.g. smart meters and EV’s charging stations etc.). Further details 
are available in the Annex of this document. Unfortunately, the recent attacks to Ukraine’s critical 
infrastructure exemplify how relevant is to duly prevent any malicious activities against European ones, 
such as the energy supply chain. For this rea son, Enel calls on EU legislators to  create a data-sharing 
exemption for the highly sensitive data of critical infrastructures listed at the Annex of this document.  
To further enhance  legal clarity on critical infrastr uctures, the proposed Regulation on Artificial 
Intelligence should include a common EU definition for infrastructures considered critical and a clearer 
definition of the safety components affected. 
• Non-defined, or broadly interpreted ‘emergency situations’ could l ead to widespread expropriation 
of private data for public use, without a fair compensation for the transfer cost or the product given, 
by data holders . An exhaustive catalogue of situations describing what is considered a public 
emergency should be included in the Regulation.  
• A clear and comprehensive list of conditions under which public sector bodies can request access ‘on 
the public interest’, should be included in the legislation . Enel have serious concerns regarding the 
mandatory nature of the data access requirements  without clear parameters  subject to potential 
different interpretation in different member states . We believe that to boost the data economy, data 
sharing should only occur on a voluntary basis, supported by an enabling and incentivizing framework, 
so as not to create a chilling economic effect or free rider behaviours.  
• To prevent potentially high compliance cost and burden for data holders, the proposal shall clearly 
identify and limit public sector bodies entitlement to request access to data. As per the proposal, every 
public sector body (i.e. union institution, national, regional or local authorities and bodies governed by 
public law, or associations formed by one or more) could request accessing data, for (i) emergency cases, 
(ii) for the timely compilation of official statistics when data is not otherwise available or (iii) when the 
administrative burden will be considerably reduced; potentially causing a very high number of requests 
hence, a  high cost for business es and data holders . Enel is of the opinion that the  type of  public 
institution allowed to request information shall be restricted per type of data holders , defining 
geographical restrictions and a definite number of possible requests.  
• To prevent trade secrets exposure, the proposal shall not rely on good intentions. The Data Act will 
serve the EU's digital ambitions if it protects confidential business information , but the lack of 
guidance on which confidentiality measures those public authorities shall apply (and the extent to which 
the data holders may use / implement their specific non -disclosure or confidentiality agreements, 
including specific breach-related clauses),  might lead to situations of information disclosure and a 
reduced competitiveness of EU companies. Further, the fact that the information can be share d for 
research purposes, enlarges the risk. Enel considers the benefits of sharing trade secrets to the public 
sector bodies do not overcome the risks and consequently trade secrets shall not be shared for public 
interest purposes . In addition , data holders may not be able to evaluate the strict necessity for 
requesting such type of information.

• The exemption s provided in Chapter II to small or micro enterprises  on the data generated by 
users through IoT devices, might have privacy implications, and will create an imbalance power at the 
data market. In addition, it is recommended to establish clear and transparent rules regarding further 
sharing of the acquired data with third parties. 
 
 
2. Develop a complete stimulus to engage consumers and businesses to invest in data, and 
establish a fair cost allocation  
The EU Data Act aims to make more data available for reuse and the new rules are  expected to create €270 
billion of additional GDP by 20283. Enel supports the Commission’s ambition to untap the potential of industrial 
data by incentivising and supporting companies, instead of imposing strict measures. The provisions mandating 
the transfer of data might  undermine companies’ contractual freedom and have the opposite effect than 
intended. To boost data uptake, it is important that data -sharing agreements remain open and commercially 
viable. 
• The EU should  provide an enabling framework to support and  incentivize all actors (consumers, 
businesses, and governments) to share data such as tax incentives and, schemes allowing companies 
to closely cooperate and extract value from a data ecosystem,  in compliance with applicable laws and 
regulations (including Data Protection and Competition laws). Moreover, to make sure that commercial 
actors do not suffer disproportional economic disadvantages , fair remuneration schemes for sharing 
data with third parties shall have the right to involve the costs of data adaptations and transfer. 
• Non-binding model contract terms  on data access and use , will be useful for companies  without 
experience, legal, resources and capacity to handle contractual data sharing negotiations. The industry 
already involved in these negotiations should participate in the drafting of these models. 
 
• Draft legal principles for data adaptation and aggregation  shall be established,  to increase legal 
certainty and address the lack of digital skills or technologies , enabling an  effective data sharing  
framework. It is remarkable that, many times personal data are difficult to be filtered out from mixed 
datasets, and this hinders additional value proposition for B2B data sharing; Enel advice the Commission 
to tackle the governance of mixed datasets  at the issuanc e of model contractual terms. Moreover, 
likewise the EU Commission drafted different guideline s for business-to-business and business-to-
government data sharing, it would be valuable to have a separate approach also for business-to-
customer data access and sharing. The B2G and B2B data sharing models are meant to support business 
in their operations and to grow, contrasting with B2C and C2B models where business or users create a 
specific product or service. Therefore, the two models will be attached to diverse data protection and 
competition law (B2B data access in so far as it covers data belonging to competitors, shall be regulated 
in compliance with the prohibition of exchange of sensitive information set forth under antitrust law ), 
and their criteria for data monetization will diverge. 
 
• It is recommended to draft voluntary compensation guidelines for all actors considering factors such as 
the size of the company, an index to the market value of the data, sector -specific considerations, and 
the purpose of obtaining the data. Besides, as previously written, in case of access for the public interest, 
the guidelines should remember that trade secrets could not be part of the information shared, and the 
compensation shall clearly refer to one of the public goals defined and identified by the EU Commission.  
 
• The mandatory disclosure of the compensation should be erased as it  might undermine companies’ 
contractual freedom, and lessen the competitiveness necessary for creating a data market.

3. The reasons to allow the access of third country governments or companies to non-personal data 
shall be clearly listed and defined under the Data Act; avoiding Members States would be able to 
bypass them by: 
 
•  It is advisable to extend similar principles as for the international transfer of personal data by the 
GDPR, as well as by the EU Decision no. 2021/914.  In addition,  the Data Act should propose data 
protection guidelines for international transfers based on a risk -based approach and on a data 
classification level.  
 
• The Data Act mandates cloud providers to take all measures necessary to prevent any international access 
or transfer of non -personal data held in the EU that would be contrary to EU or Member State laws.  
Furthermore, any international access or transfer not explicitly permitted should be forbidden. In any 
case, the agreement of the data owner should be explicit , and international agreements can contain 
further details specifying the conditions of the access or transfers, established at the Data Act.  
 
• Finally, the Enel Group calls on EU legislators to consider the applicability of the sui generis right under 
the Database Directive, to databases containing machine generated data and, offer protection against 
third-party infringements (i.e., unauthorised use), avoiding the use of generated data by service providers 
without requiring authorization. 
 
 
4. Establish a harmonised interplay with existing or under drafting legislation  
 
Based on its current catalogue of obligations and requirements, the draft Data Act is related to  data 
protection and competition laws: 
• Data protection: Even though the draft Act aims to reflect some of the rights and obligations under 
the GDPR which apply to personal data, to the non-personal data; it is questionable whether a single 
breach could get cumulative fines (due to the GDPR and the Data Act), and how is the GDPR principle 
of further processi ng compatibility is aligned with provisions on unauthorized  use or disclosure of 
data. Moreover,  a significant loophole is evidenced at the current proposal for a Data Act, when 
allowing data holders to use data without authorisation if the us e did not cause ‘significant harm’ 
(Art.11), while the current General Approach for an e-Privacy Regulation prohibits the access and use 
of non-personal data that has not been previously consented or under very specific circumstances . It 
is advisable to include a clarification distinguishing the aspects relating  personal and to non -
personal data to avoid legislative loopholes. In addition, to better apply the sanctioning mechanism 
of the Data Act, it shall be also considered  the diff erent sanctioning mechanisms of related 
regulations. 
 
• Competition. The Data Act will apply in addition to new ways to deal with big digital platforms , such 
as the EU Digital Markets Act, that is expected to enter into force in 2022 and which will impose data 
access and portability obligations on core platform services (e.g., social networks, online marketplaces, 
or search engines) designated as “gatekeepers” , which will never be eligible data recipients and 
therefore must not receive any data shared under the Data Act.  
 
• Regulation on Artificial Intelligence (AI). At the actual proposal for a Data Act, it would be welcomed 
to explain whether and how data access requests will impact  high-risk AI systems and the legal 
positions of the AI system provider and the data access applicant 
 
• Besides, the Act complements the Open Data Directive of June 2019 laying down rules on the re-use 
of public sector information by addressing data that cannot be made available as open data. This 
concerns data attached to intellectual property rights, or other commercially sensitive information. It 
should be very well clarified if and how ‘exceptional needs’ can overcome IPR

5. To achieve a coherent i mplementation and enforcement , i nstitutional consolidation and 
harmonization efforts that cover the entire spectrum of data regulation in the EU are 
needed  
 
Generally, the existing and envisaged supervision national authorities under (i) the AI Act, (ii) the Digital 
Services Act Package, (iii) the EU Data Protection authorities, and (iv) the proposed competent authorities 
under the Data Act, create an ever more complex legal and compliance matrix.  
 
• For this reason, Enel encourages EU legislators to ensure a high degree of policy harmonisation and 
consistency between the many digital policies launched, adopting technological means  for: (i) 
monitoring the implementation, (ii)  privacy and data protection, ( iii) transparent reporting means on 
how the public authority used the data, (iv) interoperability instruments for an easier data exchange 
with the data holders and (v) data portability between cloud service providers promoting SWIPO codes 
of conduct for the implementation of Article 6 of the EU Free Flow of Non-personal data Regulation. 
 
• The Commission proposal for an EU Regulation, would become directly applicable without a need for 
Member States to transpose it into national law. The new provisions will be enforced by the individual 
EU countries however, it should be considered the inter play and common objectives with previous 
legislative digital dossiers such as the Digital Markets Act, where the Commission is intended as the sole 
enforcement authority. Hence, Enel advice the Commission to avoid creating a likely fragmented 
approach for infringements and enforcement, while drafting a revenue-based guideline on penalties 
through secondary legislation; instead of the recommended “effective, proportionate, and dissuasive 
fines”.  
 
• The Data Act also foresees Member States authorities will be prepared to enforce the Regulation and 
companies had implemented all the changes in their systems that this Regulation may bring in one year, 
for instance: to store and transfer data in new EU standards, new interfaces to allow consumer s share 
and erase machine -generated data, likely adaptations to share data with public bodies in case of 
emergencies, modify customers contracts specifying how to access and use generated data, the 
adoption of future EC model terms). Due to the number of modifications foreseen, Enel call on the 
European Commission for a more doable period of two years for the Regulation entry into force, after 
its adoption.

Background LEET Security is a Spanish cybersecurity rating agency founded in 2010 that started commercial operations in 2015. LEET Security is also accredited by ENAC (Spanish Accreditation Body) to perform ENS (Spanish National Security Scheme) certifications, so we have experience also as certification body.  The cybersecurity rating services that we provide are based on a proprietary methodology developed and tested during the last ten (10) years. Unlike other cybersecurity rating agencies that provides ratings only and exclusively based on the Internet footprint of the companies, our rating procedure thoroughly tests cybersecurity policies, procedures, methods and tools of companies to provide ratings based on the level of capabilities really implemented in services provided. For this reason, our cybersecurity rating is mainly used in process related with third-party risk management as it provides an efficient and effective mechanism of transparency about the level of cybersecurity implemented by the services that are rated. But, in general, it is suitable for any situation where one party of a transaction needs to know about the real cybersecurity posture of the other party (supervisor – supervised, M&A processes, insurance companies, etc.)  We would like to highlight to collaborations with Spanish institutions that will be relevant for better understanding our comments: • Ministry of Home Affairs – National Centre for Protection of Critical Infrastructures (CNPIC). Our collaboration is aimed to develop a cybersecurity certification framework for critical infrastructures operators. • Interbank Cooperation Centre (Centro de Cooperación Interbancaria, CCI). In collaboration with this Spanish Association that comprise the whole Spanish financial sector, we have just launched a collaborative service to assess and monitor the cybersecurity capabilities of service providers.  Insecurity is priceless: The reason for transparency  Cybersecurity has been perceived as something quiet technic and complicate, as in fact, it is, but behind this perception, there is an interest of not being transparent about the level of cybersecurity of products and services. Hidden this important characteristic to customers allows cheaper developments and easier put into production of services for all competitors together with a lack of responsibilities of all the supply chain.  As far as customers are not able to take into account the level of cybersecurity of products and services for their purchasing decisions, there is no incentives for suppliers to invest in cybersecurity (higher level of cybersecurity does not mean any kind of advantage to them).  For this reason, it is mandatory to assure transparency regarding cybersecurity levels of products and services that are put in the market in a language that can be easily understood by non-technical people. As cybersecurity is visible, customers are able to consider it for their purchasing decisions and suppliers have incentives to invest in it, as it will start to be a mechanism to segment the market, and which could allow suppliers to differentiate themselves from competitors.  In this way, there are examples from other markets that shown the path: energy efficiency, hotel stars, automobile safety (EuroCAP), Parker points for wine quality, etc. EC label is, of course, another reference, but as cybersecurity needs to show levels, it should be complemented with additional information.

Cybersecurity is not an absolute term  Nobody can claim that a product or service is “cybersecure” because “100% security never exists”. Cybersecurity is a matter of grades, even in different dimensions of information (confidentiality, integrity and availability). For this reason, there are so many certifications: As certification is based on standardization, and standards define the minimum requisites for a use case of technology based on risk assessments, we have ended after 20 years using certification with dozens or, even, hundreds of different IT standards (potentially, one per use case and risk profile).  This approach has some downsizes that have been the reason for approving the EU Cybersecurity Certification framework which is trying to change this. The fact is after some months of working on the developing of European certifications, we have ended in the same point: the process to standardization keeps being very long and we are going to have multiple certifications (cloud, IoT, 5G, etc.).  An alternative approach could be to develop a method to “measure” cybersecurity (with an arbitrary but consensual metric) that allows the service provider / vendors to show the level of cybersecurity of their services and products, and customers to decide which is the level of cybersecurity they need according to the criticality of the use case, as not all services and products should be “premium”, i.e., low criticality ones could implement fewer security capabilities.  We can assemble this to the way as investment are assessed in the financial markets. For example, insurance companies are required to invest part of their capital on high security investments (rated as AAA by financial rating agencies).  This approach focused on “measuring” cybersecurity has many benefits: ü Solve the problem of multiple certifications and regulations in different sectors and countries; ü It is easier to understand for customers (certifications are a complex world where only experts are able to understand what they mean and, even, if they are suitable for the context where they are used); ü Allows a clear way to define responsibilities, as vendors are responsible for showing their level of cybersecurity, and customers are responsible for choosing the right services according to the criticality of usage. ü It is compatible with certifications. Considering certifications as the set of minimum requirements that a product or service has to meet to be used for an specific use case, showing a certification serves to show adherence to minimum set of requirements and rating shows, once you meet the minimum, what level of cybersecurity it achieves.  We are in the position to support this as result of Pinakes® initiative developed with CCI as a cybersecurity rating mechanism that allows services providers to show their level of cybersecurity to the Spanish financial entities adhered to that service. And also, as result of our collaboration with Spanish Centre for the Protection of Critical Infrastructures (CNPIC) for developing a way to certificate the level of security of critical infrastructures.

Accreditation should be tied to potential impact  In the same way, that EU Cybersecurity Certification framework defines, there should be the possibility to “measure” the level of cybersecurity of products and services with different methods with different levels of assurance to give answer to different user needs.  In our opinion, the level of assurance of a measure should be something that customers have to be responsible for. We mean, depending of the criticality of the product / service for the customer, she should require one or other type of assurance (i.e., the higher the potential impact, the higher assurance that should be used). Of course, it could be also defined by the Administration for specific situations and to solve some market issues.  Additional considerations In our opinion, taking into account the growing importance these rating mechanisms are achieving, EU has a unique opportunity to define / standardize how this mechanism should work that could make EU lead this market in the future (unlike what happened with financial rating agencies). In fact, standardizing how cybersecurity rating agencies works could lead also to a new market where different rating agencies implement different ways of measuring cybersecurity levels, letting the market to decide which are the best options instead of having to standardize it (this mechanism would be more flexible and business driven that standards developed in a laboratory by cybersecurity experts). But always following European rules, because as this activity has not been yet standardized, it could be very easy that this European standard could become a global standard (as happened before with privacy legislation).  Of course, this does not imply that certification activities are useless. As happens in many other markets, certification and rating mechanisms coexist (for example, regarding quality): • Certification assure that service / product meets some minimum global requirements; and • Rating measures the level of excellence that service / product achieves according to a predefined scale.

The Spanish Banking Association welcomes the opportunity to contribute to the European 
Commission's feedback period on the Data Act & amended rules on the legal protection 
of databases. Our main comments on the proposal are set out below. 
 
I. General provisions - Which connected devices will be subject to the 
data access regime 
Regarding the scope and definitions, we believe they should be as clear and as 
prescriptive as possible in order to avoid misunderstandings. Clarifying what is in scope 
is fundamental to ensure the regulation can be properly enforced, and most importantly, 
that it actually focuses on the relevant connected products.  
For instance, Point of Sale (PoS) systems, Automated Teller Machines (ATM) and similar 
devices should not fall under the scope of the product definition. It would be important to 
clarify the exceptions as well (e.g., smartphones when user or repurposed to function as 
PoS systems). Their main purpose (primary function) is not to generate or collect data 
concerning its use or environment, or to store and process data; the regulation should 
not apply to products that generate data as a result of intentional human input to display, 
record, or transmit content. Services related to such devices should not fall under the 
scope either. Furthermore, virtual assistants are only in scope insofar as they are used to 
access or control a connected device, rather than being scoped in intrinsically. Thus, since 
virtual assistants from the banking and financial services industry are not related to a 
specific device, they should be out of the scope. 
 
II. Business to business and business to consumer data sharing  
● The Data Act includes new data access and sharing rights for individuals and 
businesses, but only for data generated by the use of connected physical products 
and related services. Throughout the regulation, SMEs are out of scope in several 
cases.  
● We support these new obligations, but we consider the scope must be 
broadened. The ineffectiveness of the existing right to portability is a problem for 
all data currently subject to this right, not only for machine-generated data. The 
Data Act itself already provides for a possible extension of the scope in the 
assessment that will be carried out two years after its application.  
● As a pragmatic first step, enhanced portability could include consumer data from 
telcos, utilities, e-commerce and mobility services, and tax and social 
security contributions data held by public authorities. This would add to the data 
held by large digital platforms (“gatekeepers”), which will be made available through 
the Digital Markets Act.    
● Regarding the modalities of data sharing, the proposal says that the data holder shall 
make available to the user the data generated by its use of a product or related 
service without undue delay, free of charge, and where applicable, continuously and 
in real time. This should be done on the basis of a simple request through electronic 
means where technically feasible. In our view, the operationalization of the data

● Expanding effective data portability for both individual and business users to a

energy installation. 
 
III. Obligations for data holders legally obliged to make data available 
● The proposal also includes general conditions that will apply not only to the above-
mentioned data sharing right but also to data sharing obligations established in 
future legislations. These general conditions, which we support, establish that the 
data holder shall agree with the data recipient the terms for making the data available 
– which will be fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory.  
● We support that there is at least some general horizontal approach (although very 
basic) to data sharing obligations 
● Particularly, we support the provision under Article 9 that establishes the 
possibility of receiving reasonable compensation for making the data 
available. Nevertheless, in relation to the specification provision that states that if 
the data recipient is a small or medium enterprise, the compensation shall not exceed 
the costs directly related to making the data available, we think that this should be 
extended to all data recipients, as it is a right of users to share their data with third 
parties.  
A fair distribution of value is key in terms of providing incentives for data holders to 
invest in data and this principle should apply to future sectoral initiatives such as 
Open Finance.  However, the fact that Art. 9(3) allows that other Union law or national 
legislation implementing Union law could exclude compensation or provide for lower 
compensation for making data available may lead to market asymmetries and 
incoherence between sectoral frameworks.  
The current regime under PSD2 where access by some market participants to data 
held by other market participants takes place free of charge, creating a situation 
where there is no fair distribution of value is an example of the risk of asymmetry.

IV. The possibility to exclude compensation for making data available or to provide it 
for lower compensation should therefore only be allowed in exceptional 
circumstances and should be duly justified. For example, the Digital Markets Act 
does not include compensation under the obligations touching on data access (Art. 
6(1)(h) and 6(1)(i)) however it is within a specific context and meets the 
objectives of this Regulation (the DMA). Unfair terms related to data access 
and use between enterprises  
We have some concerns in relation to art. 13, which establishes that a contractual term 
unilaterally imposed by an enterprise on a micro, small or medium-sized enterprise shall 
not be binding on the latter enterprise if it is unfair: 
● Firstly, in relation to the subjective scope of this chapter, we believe that micro and 
small companies have different characteristics from medium-sized companies, for 
which the approach should be different. 
● Regarding the objective scope, we consider that data necessary for the 
performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party should be out of the 
scope of this chapter. 
● As per Art. 13.4, we think that the conditions set to presume that the contractual 
term is unfair are vague and subject to different interpretations, causing 
uncertainty for contract negotiations 
● Finally, the provision that says that the contracting party that supplied a contractual 
term bears the burden of proving that that term has not been unilaterally imposed is 
challenging, as some of the negotiations may be verbal and this would represent an 
additional administrative burden to those a company already has. 
V. B2G data sharing 
● We support that a data holder shall make data available to a public sector body or to 
a Union institution in case of an exceptional need necessary to respond or to 
recover from a public emergency. Nevertheless, the obligation to make data 
available when the exceptional need means that the lack of available data 
prevents the public sector body from fulfilling a specific task in the public 
interest that has been explicitly provided by law could be problematic, as 
the scope of this circumstance can be too wide, and open to interpretation. 
Proportionality should be the principle that inspires any obligation to force data 
holders to provide data to the public sector. 
● On the other hand, the chapter states that a public sector body shall be entitled to 
share data received with individuals or organizations in view of carrying out 
scientific research or analytics compatible with the purpose for which the 
data was requested, or to national statistical institutes and Eurostat for the 
compilation of official statistics. In the case of personal data, this provision may 
be contrary to GDPR, which is very strict with the data processing for another purpose 
different for which the personal data are initially collected. In this case the controller 
must consider several factors before sharing those data with individuals or 
organizations.

VI. Improving portability for business users of cloud services 
We support that the cloud services users have recognized by law the right to freely switch 
providers and port their data from one provider to another. This is particularly important 
for financial institutions as we have regulatory obligations to keep exit strategies as part 
of our resilience plans. In addition, the future Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) 
will introduce minimum contractual provisions for arrangements with ICT service 
providers among others regarding exit strategies. According to DORA contractual 
arrangements shall provide for clear termination rights as well as dedicated exit strategies 
enabling mandatory transition periods during which service providers should continue 
providing their services and which allows the financial entity to migrate to another 
provider or change to in-house solutions. We welcome the alignment of the requirements 
set in this Chapter with DORA. However, we would make some considerations. 
○ About transition periods: On article 24, the mandatory transition periods defined 
in paragraph 1(a) and (c), and paragraph (c) are too specific. The timeframe required 
to migrate to another provider or back to internal premises will vary depending on 
the complexity of the service. While for IaaS or CaaS services a maximum period of 
6 months might appear reasonable, for complex SaaS services more time would be 
required. In addition, the time required will depend not only on the service provider 
but also on the capabilities of the customer. Contracts should include the right of the 
cloud user to request a longer transition period if needed and the possibility to 
request extensions if necessary, during which the obligations set out in paragraph 
1(a) shall apply.  
○ About minimum data to be exported: in article 24.1(b) it should be included 
among the minimum metadata that should be exported the interactions of end users 
with the service. In the case of SaaS this information is especially relevant, because 
it allows to identify e.g., which services are the most used, the least used, how many 
times they interact, usage patterns, etc. 
○ Wording on Article 24.2 needs clarification, especially regarding the 
reference to Article 25.2: A distinction should be made between the cost of the 
service during the transition period and the cost of switching. It would be necessary 
to clarify if the reduced charges are exclusively on the latter, where there should be 
a gradual withdrawal of switching costs according to article 25. With regards to the 
cost of the service, given that a cloud service is on-demand and scalable up and down 
-including during the switching period- the reduction in demand of resources should 
entail a proportional reduction of fees.  
○ About technical aspects of switching: on article 26.4 we welcome that the 
proposal refers not only to the data but also to the relevant data formats and data 
structures. Particularly in SaaS services cloud users usually do not know how the 
data is organized which is not trivial. It would therefore be necessary that the data 
processing service also provides technical tools that enable cloud users to exploit and 
migrate this information to an alternative service provider. 
All the provisions on portability contained in Articles 23 and 24 should also contemplate 
the possibility of porting the services and data back to internal premises, not only 
switching them from one data processing service provider to another.

VII. International access and transfer 
● We support that providers of data processing services take all reasonable technical, 
legal and organizational measures, including contractual arrangements, to prevent 
international transfer or governmental access to non-personal data held in 
the Union where such transfer would create a conflict with Union law or the 
national law of the relevant Member State. It is important to preserve a company's 
trade secrets and intellectual property.  
● In cases where personal and non-personal data are mixed, compliance with the 
provisions set out in the Personal Data Protection Framework should be sufficient to 
comply with Article 27. It will be key that the European Data Innovation Board 
coordinates with data protection authorities that extensively regulate international 
data transfers when they involve personal data. Any guidance for non-personal data 
should be consistent with personal data requirements.   
● According to the objectives outlined in the proposal, this article responds to concerns 
about non-EU/European Economic Area (EEA) governments’ unlawful access to data. 
It shouldn’t therefore be interpreted as an obligation to store non-personal data in 
the EU or to prevent international transfer of non-personal data. This regulation 
should provide legal certainty for cloud users to carry out such transfers based on 
the implementation of reasonable supplementary measures. Following a risk-based 
approach, those measures should be proportionate to the risk of the non-personal 
data. 
 
VIII. Interoperability 
 Although the interoperability chapter already includes essential requirements 
regarding interoperability of Data Spaces, we think that the Commission should go 
further and adopt delegated acts to specify these requirements.  
 The same applies to cloud services providers: it is important that standards are 
developed, and the Commission makes use of its power to recognize or 
certify the standards that CSP must grant compatibility with. In turn, this 
makes the request for development of standards by European standardization 
organizations under Art 29.4 fundamental for the successful outcome of the Data Act. 
 It is not clear what the text means by an “operator of a data space” as the term is 
not defined. This could mean that the final scope of interoperability rules remains 
unclear. 
 
IX. Enforcement  
The Data Act sees strong national enforcement, with national mechanisms for resolving 
disputes and overseeing the rules – new or existing authorities will be designated by each 
member as responsible for the application and enforcement of this regulation. However, 
as it stands, there is significant room for national divergence, with dispute resolution and 
enforcement mechanisms at national level (e.g., Member States have discretion over 
penalties). We could see different interpretations across the EU on open questions such 
as what qualifies as “reasonable” compensation for data and what is considered and unfair 
contractual term. This could be contentious especially in light of the growing pressure on

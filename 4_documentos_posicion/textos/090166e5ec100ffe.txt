 Cologne, 11 May 2022  Open-Xchange’s contribution to the online consultation on the Commission’s proposal for a Data Act  Open-Xchange, as the leading European open-source software company in the email and DNS space, and as the service provider of choice for some of the biggest Internet access providers in Europe, would like to thank for the opportunity to provide comments on the Commission’s proposed text for a Data Act. We would like to build on our first-hand expertise in providing one fundamental piece of any Internet connectivity service, the Domain Name System resolver, to some of the biggest telecommunication operators and Internet Service Providers in Europe and globally. We would thus like to address the issue of data sharing obligations for software and hardware products operating in the homes of European consumers, such as browsers, smartphone apps, smart TVs, IoT products etc, when connected to a home network with Internet access. To summarize our thoughts, we think that the proposal goes in the right direction but that it should be clarified and extended so to cover all relevant products including software applications, enforce interoperability and the use of open standards, and address the need for data sharing in the opposite direction, allowing third party data processing services to instruct these products on behalf of the end-user so that undesired data sharing activities are immediately stopped.  The problem: data transparency in home consumer networks We start by noting that consumers are increasingly disempowered by the service and technical architectures adopted by the makers of online software and hardware products, especially those made by the global Internet gatekeepers. These products collect and generate highly sensitive information, both because of their typical usage and purpose, and because they sit right inside the user’s home. In fact, a browser can track everything that a user does on the World Wide Web; a smart TV can track all content that the user likes to watch. Not just the data are privacy sensitive, but the metadata as well – such as the user’s IP address and their Internet destinations and usage patterns. The makers of these devices have business incentives to gather as much data and metadata as possible, independently from whether such gathering is useful to the end-user and from whether the user wants it. Malicious or cracked products could be even more dangerous. Products of this kind are often able to record audio and video from the room, with a significant risk of surveillance; as they are connected to the user’s home network, they can potentially attack other devices or monitor the entire traffic.

As the industry moves to using encrypted and obfuscated transmission protocols, it becomes impossible for the user to scrutinize these activities via the means that have been used in the last twenty years, such as firewalls, routers, DNS filters and the likes. Products increasingly create one or more private encrypted connections to remote servers in the cloud, transmitting data (Web pages, audio-video streaming, custom content…) and/or metadata (usage patterns, diagnostics, DNS resolution, network configuration…), often immediately moving the information out of the local jurisdiction. The content and features of these connections are only accessible to the maker – not to the end-user. Even if the device or application provides the user with information on its network activities or with configuration options, these have to be taken at face value; the user has no independent way to check whether the client actually does what it says it does. For these reasons, these products present a potential privacy and security risk for European consumers. To manage this risk, end-users should be granted two fundamental rights: 1. The right to access and scrutinize all the data and metadata that these products generate and collect, and, if they want, to automatically transmit these data to a third-party security product or service that will scrutinize this information for them; 2. The right to disallow each of these products from transmitting data via the home network to the rest of the Internet, including to the cloud-based servers of their makers. As per the GDPR, the second right should be granular; while disallowing transmissions that are necessary to the stated purpose of the product could imply the inability of the product to function, the user should be able to disable additional data gathering and transmission activities – for example, the extraction of personalized profiles for advertising – without prejudice to the functionality of the product. These rights, and the related data access measures, should be available to the end-users on an ongoing, real-time basis. Porting data from a product to a competing one can be useful to competition on certain services but does not address the security and privacy risks above. Learning that your privacy was being breached only when you switch to a different product would not be a meaningful remedy. End-users should be able to access or receive their data as soon as they are generated, through open, interoperable protocols and interfaces, so that any risk is addressed promptly. By the way, such a provision would easily promote a new market for third party security and data handling services, where end-users would direct their data for scrutiny, archival and further processing on their own terms – not on the terms of the original product’s maker.  How the Data Act addresses the first right The proposed Data Act indeed aims at addressing the problem that we just identified. However, by examining the current text, we note the following issues. Chapter II of the proposal introduces a right for consumers to access data and to share data with third parties that could process them on their behalf; this matches right #1 above and is a very useful provision that we fully support.

However, such right is introduced only for products, i.e. tangible objects (Art. 2(2)), and related services, i.e. software that is necessary to providing one of the product’s functions (Art. 2(3)). We find this limitation confused, inapplicable and discriminatory and we provide some examples. According to a literal interpretation of the text, a smart TV connecting to a streaming service like Netflix would be required to share the user’s generated data, but a browser providing the same exact service on a computer would not, because the first is a tangible object, falling within the proposal’s definition of “product”, while the second is a standalone piece of software which, on its own, is not strictly necessary to the functioning of the mobile phone or computer it runs upon. The latter situation would fall under the proposal’s definition of “related service” only if one would argue that accessing Netflix is one of the intrinsic functions of a computer. However, the only intrinsic function of a general-purpose computer is to run third party software, and it is generally marketed as such. Moreover, the computer and the browser are often produced by two different companies and acquired separately, so it seems hard to argue that the browser constitutes a function of the product. At best, the applicability of the proposal is unclear. Similarly, a specialized IoT camera recording everything that happens in the room would fall under the proposal’s definition, but an app using the smartphone’s camera to do the same would not; definitely, video surveillance is not one of the normally expected functions of a smartphone. In our reading of the proposal, the current text would introduce radically different treatments for identical digital services and products, depending on whether they are implemented on a specialized product or on a general-purpose computing device. This would fail to protect the consumers in the latter, very common case. We thus encourage the European institutions to make sure to include all digital consumer products and services under the purview of this law, including the intangible ones, independently from how they are implemented. Additionally, we commend the fact that Articles 4(1) and 5(1) mention an obligation to make data accessible “continuously and in real-time” when applicable. However, such obligation could be useless if the data were not made available in formats and protocols that are open and that can be freely understood and implemented by third parties to provide competing or complementary data processing services. We think that some language should be added to that effect, or that the provisions in Chapter VIII should explicitly include these cases as well. We also note that the “simple request through electronic means” mentioned in Article 4(1), depending on interpretation, could not be the best way for the user to exercise their right. The easiest and most effective way would be to require the makers of the affected products to provide the user with a configuration option right within the product’s installation and administration interfaces; such option should allow the user either to download the data, or to provide a standardized pointer (such as a URI) to a third-party product to which they want the information to be transmitted. Finally, we would like to stress the importance of including the metadata in the data sets that the user would gain access to. It is not just important to know which data are gathered, but also who they are sent to, when and how often. The user should have access to the details of

How the Data Act addresses the second right In our opinion, the Data Act entirely fails to address the second part of the problem: how the user could direct the product or service to stop certain types of data gathering and transmission activities that the user does not consent to. The GDPR does indeed assert the right of the user to stop additional data processing activities that they do not consent to, but as this Act addresses more specifically the sharing of data from a product to the end-user and to a third party acting on their behalf, it should also address the flow of information in the opposite direction – that is, introduce the obligation for the product maker to provide technical interfaces that would listen to the user’s automated indication of the provision or refusal of consent to each specific data collection activity. We would like to explain this with an example. Let’s say that a smart TV is connecting to Netflix, but then it is also connecting to its own maker’s servers to report the user’s watching activities. The Data Act would empower the user to receive copy of both these types of generated data, hopefully including the details on when and where they were transmitted out of their home network. However, once the user learns that the smart TV is tracking their watching patterns and sharing them with its manufacturer, they would not have any easy and immediate way to stop this from happening other than not using the smart TV. This is often an unacceptable proposal; in some cases, such as for the services of dominant Internet platforms, it is basically impossible to leave the platform without being severely impaired in the day-to-day usage of the Internet. It is true that the user, as per the GDPR, should have a way to disable these activities from the smart TV’s privacy controls in its configuration interface. However, this does not always happen, and when it happens, these forms are often well hidden, misleading and used as part of a “cat and mouse” game by the data industry. The experience with cookie popups, or with the recent on-by-default “legitimate interest” options that advertisers deployed as soon as people started to deny consent in cookie popups, shows that relying on manual consent forms does not effectively protect the consumer’s privacy. Users should have a way to automatically tell products to stop gathering certain types of data or transmitting any data to certain destinations. This functionality is already implemented in many third-party security services, such as security routers or personal ad-blocking DNS servers like PiHole; blocking the establishment of a connection, or returning a DNS reply that denies the existence of the destination server, are ways to signal the need to stop all transmissions to that specific destination. However, the global Internet industry is increasingly encrypting all transmissions and bypassing local DNS and firewall blocks, and this prevents the user from screening and blocking any traffic on their home network that they do not consent to. As breaking encryption is impossible and unadvisable, the only solution is to allow the user to detect and stop these activities at their source, that is, within the products and the software applications that perform them. Since digital manufacturers generally have a business interest in collecting data, this can only be accomplished through regulatory obligations. We thus recommend that Article 5 is revised to include a requirement for products and applications that perform online data transmissions, at least in the context of home consumer

We thank you for your attention, stand ready to answer any questions that you might have, and look forward to further opportunities to participate in this discussion during the future legislative course of this Act.  
About Open-Xchange Open-Xchange (OX) is a developer of secure and open communication and office productivity software, IMAP server software and DNS solutions. Since 2005, it has partnered with many of the world’s largest Internet Service Providers (ISPs), telcos and carriers to deliver open-source email and productivity solutions that include secure storage, file and document management. OX Dovecot is the world’s most popular IMAP server software and OX PowerDNS provides secure DNS services to telco customers and their users worldwide. Software developed by Open-Xchange is used by 200 million people globally. It is headquartered in Germany, with offices in Bremen, Cologne, Dortmund, Hamburg, Nuremberg and Olpe, and international offices in Finland, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands and the USA.

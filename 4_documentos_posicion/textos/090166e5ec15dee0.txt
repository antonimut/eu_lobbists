Executive Summary  
 
The Data Act constitutes a horizontal regulation for all IoT application fields even though there 
appears to be no apparent evidence of market failure or information asymmetries, or only in 
very specific market segments.  Therefore, such a broad regulatory intervention seems 
premature and could even create new obstacles for the desired market uptake of the European 
data economy in industry. 
Consequently, the re should be  no one size fits all approach with mandatory data sharing 
obligations for data holders. Data sharing in the B2B context should primarily be based on 
contractual freedom and market incentives. 
  
Some key points of concern:   
  
➢ Additional legal uncertainties due to high regulatory density , which may inhibit 
rapid market uptake in industrial B2B (IoT). The demarcation to other regulations, such 
as the GDPR, needs to be developed further. The regulation also weakens contract law 
as proven means for B2B data sharing.   
➢ Very broad and insufficiently defined understanding of data: It is not clarified how 
usage data is to be distinguished from trade secret/IP relevant and configuration data or 
from data for add -on services, that have to  be excluded. Similarly, the IoT device or 
product definition also remain unclear.  
➢ Broad and unclear scope of data processing services:  The obligations for data 
processing services, especially on cloud switching (Chapter VI), seem to target large 
hyperscalers. However, the current wording does not clearly exclude 
manufacturers. There needs to be a clear distinction between data processing services 
and application service s. An application service calculates/generates deriva tive data 
through a software process by proprietary software hosted on computer resources 
(Software as a service applications).   
Software as a service shall not be captured by the regulations – in particular not the 
obligations relating to facilitating the switching between data service providers.   
➢ Inadequate protection of trade secrets and intellectual property:  The obligation to 
share data with third parties poses a strong risk to trade secrets. The proposed means 
are insufficient to prevent re-engineering of algorithms, source codes or other relevant 
characteristics of a product, that have to be adequately protected in order to encourage 
investments and innovations.  
➢ Focus on the role of the asset user vs. non -consideration of the role of the 
component provider: Data Act seems to subsume this under the asset provider, who 
also has an interest in the usage data of his component, which he usually does not have 
in industrial practice (Art. 2).

➢ Assumption that the IoT asset provider is in fact the data holder: in many cases, this 
does not apply to industrial practice because the customer who uses the device usually 
holds the usage data and does not grant the manufacturer access to this data.  
➢ Administrative burden/cost: more difficult or more expensive to place IoT devices on 
the market by imposing additional product requirements,  thus creating additional 
obstacles to industrial digitalization and market uptake (Art. 2).  
➢ Access by design as a concept seems to be too broad and vague, thereby causing 
unintended side effects regarding the desired goals of the EU Data Act. In conjunction 
with the obligation to make data available to any third  party without adequate 
protection of trade secrets, this might potentially lead to asset producers reducing the 
ability of products to generate data (Art. 3).  
  
Siemens would therefore favor an industry - or sector-specific approach to data 
regulations. Furthermore, the definitions and concepts in the Data Act require further 
clarification. We have laid out our key concerns and recommendations for each chapter of the 
Data Act.   
          
Chapter I. General Provisions   
 
Article 2 – Definitions  
  
1) Data   
The definition of "data" is extremely wide and, in light of the multiple requirements imposed by 
the proposal, rather vague. The proposal does not consider that data generated by the use of 
or incorporated in industrial machinery may, inter alia,    
- differ in terms of their degree of maturity/processing (raw data vs. analyzed or 
processed data, metadata (structured data describing other data), data for add -on 
services);    
- contain trade secr ets and know -how or allow for conclusions to be made regarding 
such trade secrets and know-how.   
   
Where data is generated via related services, these regularly feature a certain degree of 
maturity/processing, which requires corresponding investments on the part of the service 
provider. It would undermine the service provider's business model , if the service provider, 
being required to disclose such data free of charge to the user, were no longer able to monetize 
its investments. This would, in turn, jeop ardize the objectives of the proposal to promote 
investments in innovations and the development of digital services.   
   
Where data incorporate trade secrets, know -how, and other sensitive information, the safety 
measures of the proposal, such as the conclusion of non-disclosure agreements, the prohibition 
of the development of competing products,  and tying the disclosure of data to a specific 
purpose, are not sufficient to meet the need for protection of specific data. Still, there is data 
that reflects the core of the business (such as source codes, recipes). Any obligation targeting 
the owner of such data to also unrestrictedly disclose such data would bear the risk of 
negatively impacting the business.   
   
It also remains unclear how to identify data that is the subject of user action and events (cf. 
Recital 14) and data that requires human input (cf. Recital 15), both in -scope of the proposal 
versus information that is derived or inferred from such data (cf. Recital 14) which shall be out-
of-scope of the proposal.

What constitutes “derivative data” needs to be clearly defined in the main text of the proposal 
– currently it is only addressed in Recital 14 – and it needs to be confirmed that this type of data 
is outside the scope of the regulation (no “should” but a “shall” provision). 
 
2) Product  
It appears unclear which physical asset components (e.g., sensors) fall under the definition of 
a product, especially in light of the accompanying Recitals 14 and 15.    
   
In industrial settings, individual components regularly cannot spontaneously generate data, but 
rather require a specific and customized configuration by the user or the engineering provider 
engaged by the user to generate data. Industrial components can only perform their functions 
based on certain human input configurations by the user. Recital 15 now exempts products 
which can generate relevant data only on the basis of human input from the scope of the Data 
Act. Against this background, it would therefore be preferable, for the sake of clarification, that 
(1) the additional requirement of "human input" is included in the product definition and (2) 
industrial applications (such as industrial controllers, like “Programmable Logic Controllers” 
(PLCs), as well as “industrial PCs” (IPCs)) are included in the examples of Recital 15  just like 
personal computers are excluded from the scope of the Data Act in the B2C environment.    
   
In addition, it would be preferable to clarify whether the individual component, by virtue of its 
design, must already be able to fulfill the technical requirements independently or whether it 
is only necessary for the technical requirements to be fulfilled in interaction with other 
independent modules. For example, industrial controllers by default do not establish network 
connections, but can only do so if the user connects an additional network module based on 
individual settings and considering its specific needs.    
   
Furthermore, it is unclear whose perspective should be decisive in the assessment  of the 
individual requirements of Art. 2 (2). Clarification is necessary.   
   
3) Related service  
The definition should focus on the service being essential for a product’s “basic function” rather 
than “a function”. The current definition does not contain a clear demarcation between related 
services and stand-alone software. In the railway infrastructure business, proprietary software 
and hardware (computers) are combined to achieve certain levels of safety integrity.  Such 
software is IP relevant and therefore, a clearer definition of related services is needed in order 
to exclude proprietary software from the scope.    
  
Hence, “including software” shall not mean that stand-alone software (not intended as service 
in the sense of monthly subscription fees) is also included.   
  
5) User & 6) Data Holder  
The proposal gives the impression of a very simplified and not practical understanding of the 
relationship between manufacturers and customers in the majority of industrial applications. 
The proposal does not take into account that in multilateral and multidirectional networks, the 
"user" of a physical asset is regularly the "data holder". The proposal states in its Explanatory 
Memorandum that "the manufacturer or designer of a product or related service typically has 
exclusive control over the use of data generated by the use of a product or related service" (cf. 
page 13 of the proposal). However, in most parts of the industrial sector this is not true. The 
formula "manufacturer = data holder" fails to reflect the realities in the industry environment. 
Rather, the user typically is the data holder.

The definition of the data holder appears to be at least partially circular, as the proposal itself 
defines the term "data holder" as "a legal or natural person who has the right or obligation, in 
accordance with this Regulation (...), to make available certain data" [cf. Art. 2 (6)].    
A more precise definition of the "data holder" is required as the one who de facto holds the 
data, pointing out that in the vast majority of industrial use cases this role is simultaneously 
with the user of the component in question. Thus, one-directional horizontal obligations (only 
holder to user) do not reflect industrial applications and thus seem not to be adequate. 
  
Example rail industry:  
In the railway industry, the product manufacturer produces field assets, which are sold to users 
(= railway operators) , who then own the products/assets. The data p roduced by the product 
pertains to the user of that asset, i.e. data is extracted by the product manufacturer only, if and 
to the extent allowed by the contract between the manufacturer and the user. Such data is then 
provided to the user in accordance with the contractual stipulations. 
  
The proposal does not provide definitions of the terms "manufacturer" or "service provider" in 
addition to the terms "data holder", "user" and "data recipient", although manufacturers and 
service providers are to be equally covered by the scope of the EU Data Act, cf. Art. 1 (2) (a).    
In general, (component) manufacturers do not play a prominent role in the overall conception 
of the EU Data Act. They are only required in Art. 3 (2) (d) to provide information on whether 
they themselves use the data generated by the product they s upplied or allow a third party to 
use the data, stating the purpose for which the data will be used. This particularly shows the 
proposal’s incorrect assumption that, in the industrial sector, the manufacturer of a product is 
to be qualified as the person who has the ability to make available certain data, i.e., the data 
holder. 
On the contrary, the (component) manufacturer is usually cut off from using the data generated 
by its components, both technically and due to contractual provisions. In this respect, it proves 
to be a conceptual gap in the proposal that the manufacturer is not recognized as having a right 
of access to data against the user, although such an interest in data use, particularly for the 
purpose of improving the manufacturer’s products, c ertainly exists in practice and the 
promotion of innovation and the (further) development of digital and other services is the 
declared aim of the proposal.  
  
11) Data Processing Service  
In general, the definition seems to indicate that it is supposed to  apply to cloud computing 
providers such as hyperscalers. However, the definition needs a clearer demarcation as it is 
unclear whether this applies to manufacturers in cases where they rent cloud infrastructures as 
a service from a hyperscaler, develop some specific apps for this vendor, and sell these apps as 
a service to customers. The EU Data Act should not be applicable to these scenarios.   
  
Chapter II. B2C and B2B data sharing    
 
The Data Act lacks a clear distinction between B2C and B2B applications. This creates additional 
regulatory density in industrial B2B that is not empirically justified by market failures or power 
asymmetries (unlike it may be in B2C).  
  
Service providers will benefit from data access rights while product manufacturers will not have 
similar rights, which distorts competition and market balance. There is a lack of consideration 
for loss of competitive advantage as a result of (non -European) competitors benefiting from 
data access through the Data Act without reciprocal rights.

The provisions in Article 3 on access by design sound very impractical, vague and too broad. 
Products can often produce more data than required for regular usage, but this requires 
investments that have to be protected. To make it “easily, securely and directly accessible” to 
all users, extra effort during the development process is required, which makes products more 
expensive and will lead to problems regarding functional safety and security in many industrial 
applications. It remains unclear which obligations stem from this regulation for manufacturers, 
e.g., are manufacturers required to develop interfaces? It can lead to the undesired side-effect 
that manufacturers try to refrain from developing products that produce data.  
  
Industrial components follow a very specific design that must meet the special requirements in 
the industrial sector. Modifications to the product design may significantly jeopardize the 
product's functionalities. 
 
Example manufacturing industry: 
If, for example, an industrial controller had to be designed in such a way that it had to grant 
data access by design - and not via additional modules - this would result in the loss of special 
properties of a controller (deterministics, equidistance). Precisely because industrial controllers 
on the I/O level have to focus entirely on their function, they are intentionally equipped 
exclusively with minimum functions that are required for commissioning the controller.   
   
The current provisions in Article 3 would give all users the right to access unfiltered raw data 
which then might lead to more warranty claims against the original manufacturer based on 
(potentially inconsistent) raw data. In addition, the requirement to provide information leaves 
the question open as to who must provide the relevant information.   
  
The underlying assumption in Article 4 is that the manufacturer is also the data holder, or in 
other words, that the data holder and the user are not the same person. This is rarely true for 
industrial B2B sectors such as manufacturing, the railway industry, the utility sector, and the 
medical devices sector. In these cases, the users are already possessing and utilizing the data, 
the data allocation is already in line with the intentions of the D A. Against this background, 
data sharing obligations for the machine/component supplier will not lead to an additional 
uptake of data usage/data economy. This should also be considered from a proportionality point 
of view.   
 
Trade secrets (Article 4.3)  
  
Trade secrets are currently vaguely defined and insufficient in terms of protection.  In general, 
trade secrets should be fully protected, and data considered as trade secrets should remain 
outside the scope (important to foster investments and innovation regarding generation and 
processing of data).   It is essential that trade secrets are not disclosed to third parties 
(competitors).   
The protection should not only cover trade secrets but also safeguard machine 
builders/component providers – often SMEs – from havin g to disclose data that allows 
conclusions on core domain know -how of their applications  (algorithms, machine 
configurations). Under normal circumstances such highly confidential know-how would not be 
made available to market participants at all.    
In add ition, concrete provisions on IT security are lacking.  The possibility to agree with 
customers and third parties on IT security measures may not suffice for preventing abuse. It is 
difficult for vendors in the mass market to have the negotiation power with  customers and 
monitoring capacities to ensure  adequate IT security for trade secrets by means of contracts. 
This applies even more to third parties (competitors).

The owner of the trade secrets will not be able to control adherence to the agreed measures, 
neither will he be able to enforce them adequately. This is especially difficult for software/data. 
Claiming damages is not an adequate remedy for any breach of the agreed obligations. Sharing 
data with third parties (including competitors) always has the risk of reverse engineering, which 
can hardly be evidenced.    
  
It is currently unclear what happens if the user does not agree with the NDA provided by the 
data holder. It must be clarified whether the data holder will be in breach of its oblig ation to 
disclose data.    
    
Article 4 (4) on competing products lacks a clear definition of said “competing products”. It 
should furthermore be extended to “competing services”. However, it is not possible to 
adequately enforce such a rule. In practice, it will hardly be possible to have a clear demarcation 
between a competitive product and a new product. Once the third party has access to the data, 
it can neither be controlled nor enforced that the third party does not develop competing 
products.  
  
Article 4 (6) intends to protect the asset user against (mis)use of operational data on the basis 
of a contractual agreement. A similar protection would be necessary in order to also clearly 
protect the asset/component provider from data misuse. As a general rule, Siemens only 
gathers data with the explicit agreement of the customer. Manufacturers struggle in many 
cases to agree data sharing with customers because usage data allows competition relevant 
insights. Also, after the delivery of the product, access to data is often limited, if not impossible, 
for the product manufacturer.   
  
The provisions in Article 5 on sharing data with 3rd parties need to be strictly limited as data sets 
may include proprietary IP and trade secrets that must not be disclosed to 3rd parties.   
This provision would enable third parties (for example, another supplier) access to structured 
data without making any investment of their own. This deteriorates the situation of the 
company that created the technical basis for the availability and analysis of the data. To reduce 
this risk, clear rules must be put in place as to who is the contractual user of the data. The access 
by third parties to the data generated in the B2B relationship must be restricted. Data holders 
and manufacturers should be able to claim damages for data misuse, e.g., for developing a 
competing product (mere deletion of the data is not sufficient).   
  
Chapter III. Obligations for Data holders  
  
In Article 9, the notion of “free of charge” data access must be clarified. It must also be clarified 
who will determine what constitutes reasonable compensation.    
  
Chapter IV. Unfair terms related to data access and use (B2B)  
  
The significant interference with contractual and entrepreneurial freedom in this chapter is 
unjustified. It is also not considered beneficial in view of the early stages of the development 
of the data market without proven market failures.      
  
The EU Data Act should therefore refrain from interfering with the “usually well -functioning" 
business relationships in complex industrial data value chains and leave sufficient room for 
tailored solutions. If there are imbalances or gaps, these should be addressed by EU competition 
law or sector-specific legislation.

The fairness tes t currently ignores the fact that contractual imbalances do not depend on 
company size alone but on specific circumstances such as buying power, substitutability, and 
overall diversity of supply.  
  
While the language on page 13/14 of the Explanatory Memorandum states that the parties can 
still individually negotiate a specific contractual term (“Furthermore, the parties can still 
individually negotiate a specific contractual term”), Article 13 (8) seems to restrict the parties 
from doing so. This needs to be clarified.   
 
Regarding the individual clauses:  
   
The general clause of Art. 13 (2) leads to considerable legal uncertainties. It remains unclear 
which criteria are to be used to determine good commercial practice in data access and use 
("good commercial practice") or to determine a significant deviation ("grossly deviates") and 
who determines these criteria.  
   
Art. 13 (3) (a) determines the unfairness of a clause that limits liability to intent and gross 
negligence or excludes liability altogether. At the same time, the clause does not determine 
what the reference point of liability is.  
  
It is market practice for cloud services to be provided based on general terms and conditions. 
This is necessary because a company cannot offer one and the same cloud service to different 
customers under different terms and conditions. If the use of general terms and conditions is 
prohibited according to this section, it would render it much more burdensome to offer such 
services.  
   
Example rail industry:   
In our standard terms for digital services, we limit the remedies in case of defects in the services 
to “using commercially reasonable efforts to restore the services” and offer the customer the 
right to terminate if we cannot perform the service as promised. That would not be possible 
according to Article 13 (3) This needs to be clarified.   
  
Chapter V. B2G data sharing  
  
The data provision obligations to the public sector in this chapter are unclear and potentially 
very broad (Art. 14), while compensation mechanisms need to be clarified (Art. 20). Especially 
the concept of “prevention of a public emergency” may be open to interpretation, also across 
EU Member States. The concepts of a “specific task in the public interest” and “exceptional need” 
are even more unclear and open to political influence by lawmakers on an EU, national or 
(federal) state level.   
 
Any extension of "B2G" data sharing obligations for research purposes should be limited to 
defined cases where public interest considerations clearly outweigh private interests (such as 
public emergencies) and to specific recognized and publicly funded research institutions.   
  
The exclusion of SMEs from such obligations is unjustified.   
 
The proposed Data Act should also address the risk of data , shared by the companies , being 
further re-used/re-shared as public sector data, e.g., in context of the soon-to-be-adopted Data 
Governance Act or pursuant to the existing Directive on open data and th e re-use of public

sector information. Given that some of such data may contain sensitive commercial 
information, be it trade secrets, know-how, or simply undergo a reverse engineering leading to 
disclosure of proprietary information, the Data Act should provide adequate safeguards for the 
protection of such information, as well as remedies in case of breach and/or misuse of data 
shared under the B2G obligations imposed by the proposed Data Act. 
 
Chapter VI. Switching between data processing services  
  
This chapter establishes rules for rapid data processing service provider changes (Art. 23ff.), 
which could have potentially serious implications for cloud and software as a service business 
model.  
  
The provisions laid out in Article 23 are probably intended to target hyperscalers. However, the 
current definition is too broad and there must be a clearer definition of data processing 
services in demarcation of application services to calculate or generate derivative data through 
a software process by proprietary software hosted on computer resources (SaaS applications). 
The latter shall be excluded from the scope of the Data Act as this would prevent many business 
models, especially as digital assets and applications provided by manufacturers may include IP 
relevant know-how which would have to be shared (at least in parts) with other providers of 
data processing services which could be direct competitors.  
  
Obligations to have a maximum termination period of 30 days to transfer all data to a 
competing service provider would considerably weaken the competitiveness of European cloud 
offerings.   
The combination of easy switchability and short -term customer contracts reduces any 
investment incentives into lasting improvements of data quality where data quality  is 
dependent on service performance.  
  
Art. 23 (1) (a) of the proposal needs clarification as to whether this provision prevents service 
providers from agreeing on minimum license terms. Would it still be possible to agree on a 
minimum term of, e.g., 24 months allowing the customer to terminate the contract with a 
maximum notice period of 30 calendar days after the expiration of that minimum term?  
 
The contractual terms laid out in Article 24 pose an unfair risk to the manufacturer, who has 
developed an infrastructure and a diagnostic system towards their competitors. The transfer of 
DICOM metadata to competitors amounts to a transfer of trade secrets and would enable 
competitors to train AI models in a similar quality but without the underlying investment.   
The term "metadata" is vague and may concern DICOM metadata, which are trade secrets and 
should not be affected. The transfer of configuration parameters, security settings, access rights 
and access logs amount to conveying detailed information about the internal processes of the 
service provider, e.g., company’s know-how.

Chapter VII. International contexts non-personal data safeguards  
  
This chapter creates additional hurdles for seamless data flows by imposing processes similar 
to the GDPR (Art. 27). This needs to be evaluated in detail.  
  
 
Chapter VIII. Interoperability  
  
This chapter lacks a reference to existing proven interoperability standards. Preference should 
be given to internationally recognized and proven standards which are industry -driven and 
sector-specific.   
  
In addition, there should be a clearer definition of data processing services in demarcation of 
application services.    
If application services are captured, guaranteed functional equival ence to competitors would 
be problematic for IP reasons (sharing specific knowledge about our products that our software 
service is supposed to monitor, analyze etc.).

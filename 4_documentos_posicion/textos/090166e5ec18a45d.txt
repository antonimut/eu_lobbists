Position paper on the proposal on 
harmonised rules on fair access to and 
use of data (Data Act) COM(2022)68 final 
 
Key messages  
• The Data Act is  a much needed step towards a fair and balanced legal framework for the 
data economy as it will guarantee access to and use of data for SMEs. 
• It is crucial that the Data is integrated with the regulatory principles of the DMA and DSA 
and that its implementation is not hindered by potentially competing legal principles of the 
GDPR.   
• The key principles for B2B and B2C data sharing will enable a competitive offer of 
aftermarket services and complementary products. 
• Stimulating the data market must come with enhancing the capacity of SMEs to participate 
in this data economy. This requires research, investments, education and training.  
• The list of unfair contractual terms concerning access to and use of data corresponds with 
the practices our SMEs complain about. However additional unfair practices must be added 
to the list. 
• Although we are very satisfied with the general proposal, we insist on a fast sector-specific 
legislation, especially in the automotive-sector.  There is a critical need for a stand-alone 
right for service providers to access the information, tools and resources required to develop 
competing services. 
• We have to ensure with all means that the Data Act will not be watered down during the 
legislative process and will be adopted still during this Commission mandate.  
• When authorisation for access to data is required, independent authorities should be in 
charge of the authorisation and authentication, not the data holder. 
• Given their limited resources and unfamiliarity with the topic of data sharing, SMEs should 
be a main target group for the promotion of the Regulation. This should consist of making 
available relevant and efficient support measures, including mo del contractual terms, for 
SMEs by the national authorities and industry stakeholders.

Introduction 
SMEunited very much welcomes the initiative to take a horizontal approach to lay down 
common basic rules for all sectors regarding data access. This is a  significant step towards a 
more competitive and innovative data economy, and it is crucial for SMEs to have  better and 
fairer access to data. However, sector-specific regulations remain necessary, in the areas of 
smart machines, consumer goods and vehicles, as one size does not fit all , and specificities of 
certain sectors must be addressed as well. 
1. Means of access to data (recital 21) 
Recital 21 leaves open the option for the manufacturer to either make certain data directly 
available from an on -device data storage or from a remote server  which can be the 
manufacturer’s own local server capacity.   
SMEunited fears this leaves the possibility for the manufacturer to impose means of access 
of their preference . Where on-device access is technically supported, an explicit requirement 
to make this accessible to third parties of the user’s choice should be included. Only such access 
could technically enable the aspiration for real time, continuous access to the full extent of data 
generated by a connected product. Without such a provision, third party service providers 
would always be kept at a serious structural competitive disadvantage, compared to the 
manufacturer.  
2. Definitions (article 2)  
SMEunited believes the definitions provided in the Data Act are instrumental for a harmonised 
implementation.  
Clear and unambiguous terminology, rights and obligations as well as clarity regarding the 
interplay with other legislations, such as the GDPR, is very important. The terminology around 
terms such as “data user” ,“data holder” and “ third party” and its implications needs to be 
clarified substantially as the proposal contains in its current form  contradictions on when and 
to whom to make  ”available certain data”. Data protection requirements deri ving from the 
obligations outlined in the GDPR and data access rights should not become two competing 
legal principles, rendering the overarching goal of the data act obsolete and counteract the 
original goal altogether, namely to facilitate SMEs’ access to data and thus offer consumers of 
interconnected products alternative repair and maintenance services.

Also, the definition of ‘data holder’  is too general as it is. For example, concerning the 
interoperability requirements, it needs to be clear that the data holder is the manufacturer of 
the IoT. We suggest the following: “ data holder’ means a legal or natural person who has 
ability, through the control of the technical design of the product and/or related services, to make 
available certain data”. 
Regarding the definition of ‘data’, the line between ‘raw’ and ‘processed’, ‘refined’ or 
‘aggregated’ data is in practice hard to delineate . Indeed , it would seem reasonable and 
practical that users and data recipients have unhindered access to data which is essential to 
the functioning, repairing or servicing of the connected products and related services. This 
would include all raw data and metadata but also other relevant sets of processed, refined, or 
aggregated data that does not include trade secrets. The relevancy of data could be specified 
in sector-specific regulation. Articles 2 and 3 would need to be redrafted to reflect this position.   
The suggested definition of ‘product’ includes “tangible, movable item, that obtains, generates 
or collects data concerning its use or environment, and that is able to communicate data via a 
publicly available electronic communications service ”. However, recital 15 excludes personal 
computers, tablets, smart phones, and other similar connected devices. The reason why these 
devices are ‘in contrast’ with products from recital 14 such as ‘physical products that obtain, 
generate or collect, by means of their components, data concerning their performance’ seems 
unclear. We need a clear unambiguous definition of ‘product ’ that makes it clear from the 
outset what it entails without having to rely on some examples in the recitals of what is 
excluded. 
 In line with the text of the Data Act (recital 16), the definition of ‘related services’ should include 
incorporated or  inter-connected digital services, including software, provided by primary 
sellers, renters or lessors or third parties, without which connected products could not perform 
their functions. The ‘function’ of a product should be outlined in a broad sense, from the front 
end to the back end. 
The notion of ‘public emergency’  remains vague and would be subject to multiple 
interpretation. Therefore, we suggest to add in the definition that it corresponds to the following 
situations only: 
a) Public health emergencies; 
b) Emergencies resulting from environmental degradation and major natural disasters, 
including those exacerbated by climate change; 
c) Major man-made disasters, such as major cyber security incidents.  
Additionally, we believe the Regulation must include a definition of ‘non-personal data’, both 
for natural or legal persons. If a generic definition appears to be impossible, the notion should 
be defined in sector specific legislations.

Lastly, we believe a definition of ‘trade secret’ should be added in article 2. The existence of a 
trade secret must be the condition for the application of confidentiality . To avoid legal 
uncertainty, the Data Act should define what is protected under the notion of ‘trade secret’.  
For the sake of harmonisation, we would welcome the addition of a paragraph stating that 
‘Trade secrets are defined in Article 2 of Directive (EU) 2016/943 of the European Parliament 
and of the Council of 8 June 2016 on the prot ection of undisclosed know -how and business 
information (trade secrets) against unlawful obtaining, use and disclosure.’ 
 
3. B2C and B2B data sharing  (articles 3 & 4) 
SMEunited welcomes the general obligation to make data generated using products and 
related services accessible, and the obligation to design and manufacture product s in a way 
that makes the generated data  on the identity of the holder, the means of access and the 
purpose of the data collection  easily accessible to the user by default. This appear s to be 
proportionate from a SME perspective. In addition, uniform data and customer interfaces 
required for thi s are to be anchored within the framework of EU internal market regulation 
(market approval directives, type approvals or similar). We also welcome the limit imposed on 
the data holder not to use data to undermine the commercial position of the user or the  third 
party. However, clarification is needed of what ‘undermining of the commercial position’ 
means in practical sense.  
In order to protect a free market, the granting of a lease or rent of the product should not be 
made conditional upon a “ take it or leave it” agreement with the consumer , obliging the 
consumer to accept the overall conditions set by the manufacturer regarding the access to data, 
by default. . 
Article 4 specifies the rights of users to access and use data generated by the use of products 
or linked services. Although we understand that electronic access should be granted where this 
is technical feasible, it is, however important that the Commission ensures that the argument 
of technical and technological barriers are not used as a pretext  to unduly restrict data 
access. 
SMEunited wants to ensure the text will not lose its efficiency and in order to do so, we suggest 
to add under Article 3, 2(c) the following: “how the user may access those data; which must be 
delivered in a usable format and in a simple, clear and free manner for the user”.  
We also welcome the protection of companies through the interdiction to develop a similar 
product based on the data received from the manufacturer.  However, clarification is needed 
of what ‘competing product’ means in practical sense, to make it clear that products that are 
not substitute of the original can still be developed using the data received.

4. Sharing with third parties (articles 5 & 6)  
SMEunited welcomes the obligation to make data av ailable to third parties at the user’s 
request. Real-time and free access should be guaranteed as much as possible to users and 
third parties with a simple electronic request.  
We want to stress that it is important for users to exercise their right to transfer data to a third 
party in an easy and straightforward way. Therefore, the information and consent possibilities 
must be easily understandable for the individual user . Intelligent consent management (i.e. 
already in the product interface) could be helpful in this regard. 
The Data Act is a horizontal legislation and would apply to all industry sectors.  However, sector 
specific rules are required to practically enable data owners to effectively exert their right to 
access data generated through the use of products, such as vehicles. This should be done under 
Type Approval regulation. There is a critical need for a stand-alone right for service providers 
to access the information, tools and resources required to develop competing services . 
Under the Data Act approach, all these service providers would only get a derived right, which 
neglects that these parties need, in the first instance, to know and test in advance what data 
and functions are in principle available and will be at their disposal . Therefore, only an 
autonomous and stand -alone access right to the tools and resources required to develop 
the means of access will enable the independent service providers to develop competing 
digital services in advance so that these ca n be offered, marketed and advertised to the 
consumers or other data co-generators.  
The interplay between the GDPR and the Data Act needs to be clarified. In addition, clarification 
for the handling of this kind of personal data should be created in the D ata Act. In practice, it 
would otherwise be difficult to increase the flow of data without creating access to personal 
data that does not originate from the user who is actually entitled to consent. Therefore, the 
Data Act , or sector specific legislation, where relevant,  should contain additional rights to 
process personal data.  
SMEunited also believes that article 6 should include a third paragraph clearly mentioning that 
competing repair and maintenance services are not covered by the exception  of article 6.2. 
Indeed it appears quite clear from the Explanatory Memorandum1 but the core of the text should 
stress this issue to avoid that competition protection is used as a pretext for not fulfilling the 
obligations under the Data Act.

5. Costs for SMEs (articles 9)  
Article 9 of the proposal foresee s a reasonable and non -discriminatory compensation for 
making data available. It has to be avoided that the data holder impose unreasonable costs on 
SMEs. In order to do this, SMEunited believes the notion of ‘making the data available’ should 
be clearly defined. Otherwise, the data holder could impose unreasonable costs on SMEs.  
Moreover, the notion of ‘ reasonable compensation’ should be explicitly defined or it will lead 
to legal uncertainty. 
6. Unfair terms related to data access and use between 
enterprises (article 13) 
SMEs are often in a situation in which they have no choice and have to accept unilaterally 
imposed contractual clauses due to their weaker negotiating position.  SMEunited positively 
welcomes the list of unfair or deemed to be unfair  clauses as it includes some practices our 
members have already identified. However, we see a need to include an obligation in article 13 
for a regular market investigation by the Commission to review and r evise the lists of unfair 
practices. In this way, new unfair business practices can be discovered quickly and the list can 
be expanded by means of a delegated act. 
In addition, the list should include additional widespread business practices. Indeed, if article 
13. 3.b includes ’the remedies available to the party upon whom the term has been unilaterally 
imposed in case of non -performance of contractual obligations’ , we would like to clearly see 
added the practice allowing one party to unilaterally break the contract if the other fails to prove 
a certain amount of turnover.   
SMEunited is convinced that if effectively implemented and enforced, the unfair test will reduce 
the adverse effects resulting from imbalanced bargaining relationship between large  
businesses and SMEs. Therefore, it is crucial to ensure that SMEs have the means to resort to 
the unfairness test and protect themselves from unfair contractual practices. Model 
contractual terms provided by the Commission are a necessary tool in this respect but need 
to be complemented with other support from the competent national authorities. Furthermore, 
the Commission together with the national authorities and other stakeholders must focus on 
raising SMEs’ awareness on the provisions that make up the unfairness test. SMEunited insists 
that these model contractual terms need to be available at the latest when the Regulation 
enters into force and even better before, in order to raise awareness among SMEs in a timely 
manner.

7. B2G data sharing (articles 14 & 15) 
Article 14 currently states that small and micro enterprises are exempted from making data 
available to public sector bodies demonstrating an exceptional need. In our views , this 
exemption should be extended to all SMEs as defined in the Commission Recommendation 
2003/361/EC. Otherwise, the requirements under the Data Act would burden SMEs in a 
disproportionate manner. 
Additionally, it appears acceptable to allow public sector bodies access data that is necessary 
to protect the public interest in exc eptional circumstances. However, the circumstances 
deemed as an ‘exceptional need’ should be clarified to leave no room for inconsistent 
interpretation. In that sense, we suggest to add a paragraph 3 stating that ‘the request to make 
data available for an exceptional need must be necessary, appropriate and proportionate to 
the need’. 
Public sector bodies must also ensure that the data the company entrusts with them remains 
safe, that it is not breached or leaked and that its use remains within legal and eth ical 
boundaries. 
The Data Act should in that sense support independent trusted organisations  to deal 
contractually, technically and financially with the sharing of data between businesses and 
governments.  
8. Switching between data processing services (articles 
23 to 26) 
SMEunited fully agrees on the importance to enforce clear rules to remove any obstacle to 
effective switching between providers of data processing services. This is why we also suggest 
to extend the list of obstacles to the legal obstacles companies may encounter when 
switching between data processing services, such as judicial proceedings between parties.  
Regarding the contractual terms, we very much welcome the requirement that the contractual 
clauses allowing the customer to change data processing service provider be set out in a 
written contract.  
Where article 24 mentions the notion of ‘ technically unfeasible’, we think this should be 
clearly defined, in order to avoid that this argument is invoked for any reason.  
Furthermore, a minimum period for data recovery of 30 days is too short  for SMEs. Indeed, 
SMEs are in most cases forced to use an external service to manage IT services, and must be 
able to benefit from a period of at least 60 days to recover its data.

The contractual terms should include the costs associated with the transfer of data and 
digital assets when changing service providers. It is essential that the customer is fully aware 
of the costs involved in such a transfe r. Such a clause would also limit possible abuse by 
providers.  
We would also like to underline that the 3 years period for the gradual withdrawal of switching 
charges appears arbitrary and without clear reasoning why 3 years are chosen. The 
withdrawal period could be made more proportionate.  
Finally, the guarantee that these costs do not exceed the costs incurred by the service provider 
which are directly linked to the process should in our view be strengthened. This is why we 
suggest the text to provide for clearly defined sanctions in the case of abusive behaviour.   
9. Safeguards related to international access and 
transfer of non-personal data (article 27) 
Although SMEunited agrees with the importance of monitoring and framing the flow of data in 
international contexts, we fear that the provisions of article 27 create undue friction in 
international data sharing and should therefore be reconsidered.    
We believe a less risky path is possible, by introducing at the EU level obligations for da ta 
processing providers to notify business users within 24 hours whenever they receive a data 
access request from third -country authorities , when allowed to do so . It might also be 
necessary to require providers to be more transparent about third-country laws to which they 
are subject, and which grant third-country authorities access to data they store or process.  
Furthermore, we believe third-countries authorities should be granted access to data only 
under circumstances described in article 27§2. This wo uld reduce the risk of different 
interpretation and ensure full protection of European data.  
In the end, business users should have the final say over which providers process its non -
personal data and whether it approves or disapproves. 
10.  Interoperability (articles 28, 29 & 30)  
SMEunited welcomes the set of requirements to ensure the interoperability of data spaces. 
However, as these requirements will be required from any party seeking access to data 
generated through the use of a product, they should apply not only to operators of data 
spaces but to additional data holders such as manufacturers.   
SMEunited also wants to highlight how essential it is for SMEs to be part of the process of 
developing norms and standards regarding technical standards and so -called ‘smart 
contracts’. The text of the Data Act should expressly stipulate the participation of SMEs and

their representatives, such as Small Business Standards, to reach an efficient harmonisation 
of European standards and common specifications.  
11. Penalties (article 33)  
To ensure efficiency of sanctions, SMEunited believes article 33 should give the possibility to 
reinforce the sanctioning  power and supervisory regime to the European Commission by 
means of delegated acts in the event that abusive behaviour is reported.

RELX welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on the European Commission’s proposed
regulation on fair access to and use of data (Data Act). RELX is a global provider of information-
based analytics and decision tools for professional and business customers. 
RELX is active in four market segments: Risk, Scientific, Technical & Medical, Legal and Exhibitions.
Risk provides customers with information-based analytics and decision tools that combine
public and industry-specific content with advanced technology and algorithms to assist them in
evaluating and predicting risk and enhancing operational efficiency. Scientific, Technical &
Medical provides information and analytics that help institutions and professionals progress
science, advance healthcare and improve performance. Legal provides legal, regulatory and
business information and analytics that help customers increase their productivity, improve
decision-making and achieve better outcomes. Exhibitions combines industry expertise with
data and digital tools to help customers connect digitally and face-to-face, learn about markets,
source products and complete transactions.
We support customers in 180 countries, have headquarters in London and Amsterdam and
extensive operations in the Netherlands, France and Germany. About a third of the 33,500
people we employ worldwide are based in the EU. 
Data is at the centre of the global digital economy and will help economic transformation, in
particular to achieve the EU’s ambitions of both green and digital transitions. Data is also
central to RELX’s businesses. We combine data and analytics with deep industry expertise to
help customers make better decisions and manage risk. We acquire data through commercial
arrangements or partnership agreements with other industry entities as well as gather data
that is openly available. The combination of our rich data assets, technology infrastructure and
knowledge of how to use next generation technologies, such as machine learning and natural
language processing, allows us to create effective solutions for our customers.
RELX supports the Commission’s aspiration to improve access to and use of data in the EU and
welcomes the proposal’s focus on creating legal certainty for data generated by products or
related services, in order to unlock data-driven innovation opportunities across the EU.
However, given that the interaction between actors in the data ecosystem can be complex and
multi-layered, we believe that the proposal requires further clarifications to ensure legal
certainty for businesses in terms of what products, what types of data and what actors would
be covered by the regulation. 
This paper outlines RELX’s initial views and recommendations for clarifying the scope of the
Data Act, in particular in the business-to-business (B2B) contexts, as well as the provisions
around unfair contractual terms and the mandatory business-to-government (B2G) data
sharing, ahead of further discussion with the EU institutions.

The proposal aims to confer the right to a user to request that their data is made available to
third parties, or data recipients to unlock unprocessed (raw) data from the Internet-of-Things
(IoT) context. However, the interaction between actors involved in the development of data
sets can be complex and multi-layered, particularly when creating large mixed datasets of non-
personal information, which are aggregated, analysed, and transformed into different kinds of
data-driven decision-making tools. Mixed datasets can consist of raw data combined with
inferred data, or of collated raw data that combines different sets of rasd data points from
multiple users, including some that could be subject to individual user requests.
These mixed datasets are not intended to be covered by the Data Act because, although they
may in part be derived from the IoT context, they have been combined with other datasets and
are no longer raw data. In these instances, the data is often collected from multiple sources,
including data holders, users or other third parties. In other instances, data that is collected
has been publicly broadcast prior to collection; for example, through Identification Friend or
Foe (IFF) transponders which transmit openly available data about the identity of an aircraft. As
currently drafted, the collection of data from these different sources could inadvertently be
included within the scope of the regulation. We therefore recommend providing more clarity
on the relationships that are to be covered by the Data Act to enhance legal certainty.
In addition, we need to ensure that the Data Act does not impact on pre-existing contracts
between data users and third parties. While it is our understanding that, on the basis of recital
15 and the inference in Article 3(2)(d), they would be excluded from the scope of the proposal
we would welcome further clarification that this is indeed the case. This confirmation is
important to prevent legal uncertainty and the unintentional extension of the scope into the
activities of the information industry business community, where contractual agreements for
data sharing are based on very specific objectives and already provide sufficient legal
safeguards.
Lastly, we would also welcome clarification that the Data Act, as well as protecting users, can
continue to enable them to provide permissions to a third party to make use of the data
outside of the regulation when they specifically wish to do so. As currently drafted, the
proposal imposes certain obligations (Article 6) on third parties, which would make any
permissions from the user unworkable. For example, it introduces obligations that a third
party cannot make data available to ‘another third party in raw, aggregated or derived form’ in
Article 6(2)(c). 
Subject matter and scope 
Articles 1, 2 and Recitals 14, 15
3
While we fully support the overall intention to prevent the unilateral imposition of unfair
contractual terms, we are of the view that contractual freedom should remain the fundamental
principle for B2B data sharing. Thus, any limitations to this principle should be approached
with great caution.
More specifically, we recommend clarifications to ensure any additional rules can be
implemented correctly. As currently drafted some of the provisions related to contractual
terms and in particular Articles 13(4) and 13(5) are too broad and open to interpretation. For
example, we believe it will be very difficult to prove terms such as “inappropriately limit the
remedies” Article 13(4)(a), or “an attempt to negotiate it” [a contractual term] Article 13(5).
Without further clarifications the provisions could risk impacting existing contracts and create
persistent litigation that would be detrimental to the whole European data economy.

Business-to-Government data sharing
Article 15
While we firmly believe that any flexible framework designed to facilitate B2G data sharing
arrangements should remain voluntary, we do understand the reasons behind the proposed
provisions to impose mandatory obligations in exceptional circumstances. At RELX we consider
it part of our commitment to corporate responsibility to respond to a public emergency. For
example, at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, Elsevier developed the Novel Coronavirus
Information Centre where researchers were provided free access to relevant research content,
which was also made available to global medical research databases for full AI-enabled data
mining.
We support the proposed provisions allowing mandatory B2G data sharing in exceptional
cases such as public emergencies, pandemics or disasters while providing safeguards to
ensure that such data sharing is proportionate and necessary. However, to prevent misuse of
the provisions we stress the need for clear safeguards. The scope of such requests must
remain narrow and the instances exceptional. 
We therefore welcome the provisions in Articles 15 and 17 that limit the basis of these
exceptional data sharing requests and minimise risks. The data request should be proportional
with the exceptional need and protection of commercially sensitive information. Public
authorities should demonstrate the exceptional need of the request, prove that they have
taken all measures outlined in Article 15 and be required to delete the data if no longer
needed for the requested purpose.

In addition, we believe there is a need for additional safeguards when it comes to misuse or
abuse of data. Any data request entails a risk of misusing, misreading, or misinterpreting the
data supplied by the private sector. There is also an increased cybersecurity risk in cases of
mandatory B2G data sharing and the greater the number of requests, the higher the risk.
Without the right additional safeguards this may lead to outcomes that are not anticipated or
desired, such as the erosion of a company’s intellectual property (IP) rights or an unfavourable
impact on the value of its partners, suppliers, and customers. We therefore concur with the
suggestion from the High-Level Expert Group on B2G Data Sharing that companies “should not
be held liable for the quality of the data in question or its use by public authorities for public-interest
purposes”.
Sui generis right under Database Directive
Article 35 and Recital 84
At RELX we are convinced that both the role of copyright in protecting creativity, and the sui
generis database right protecting investment, are key elements of the European vision to
encourage creation and investment. Although our business relationships with our customers
are primarily managed by contracts, the Database Directive confers additional protection
against third party infringers with whom, by definition, there is no contractual protection. 
In our view, well managed proprietary rights with the appropriate exceptions are the best way
forward and we therefore welcome the careful balance provided in Recital 84 between the
Data Act’s objective of unlocking more opportunities to use the unprocessed data and IP
protection. As currently drafted, Article 35 states that the sui generis right provided for in
Article 7 of Directive 96/9/EC ‘does not apply to databases containing data obtained from or
generated by the use of a product or a related service’. However, Recital 84 clarifies that the Data
Act doesn’t change the protection granted to ‘databases containing data obtained from or
generated by means of physical components of a product or a related service’ which qualify for the
sui generis right. We believe that this clarification should also be included in Article 35 of the
Data Act to ensure coherence and legal certainty.
Conclusion
RELX has been glad to have the opportunity to provide initial feedback on the proposed Data
Act and hopes the contents of this paper have been useful in highlighting certain areas where
some improvements could be made to the regulation. 
We look forward to engaging with the EU institutions on the Data Act as it makes its way
through the legislative process and stand ready to assist the EU in driving initiatives that foster
the development of innovative technologies that benefit society as a whole.

General remarks 
With its draft Data Act (Data Act-E), the EU Commission has presented far-reaching regulations 
for the access and use of non-personal data in the EU, with the aim of opening up data in 
Europe for the benefit of companies, consumers, public services and society as a whole. 
Software AG welcomes this intention. In our view, the Data Act as a horizontal regulation - if 
done right - offers the opportunity to shape an open and democratic data economy guided by 
European values, in which there is a fair distribution between those involved in the creation of 
value.  
However, we have the impression that no problem analysis was carried out in the run-up to the 
Data Act, which would have taken its fundamental importance sufficiently into account. In 
particular, the Commission's explanatory memorandum is largely based on assumptions - which 
are well-founded and comprehensible - but lacks empirical evidence. Without this, however, the 
Data Act has feet of clay. Moreover, in our opinion, the complex and multi-layered effects - and 
above all side effects - of the Data Act have not yet been adequately taken into account. Since 
the Data Act acts as a horizontal regulation across all industries and sectors, serious collateral 
damage is to be feared. 
Software AG is very concerned that the Data Act in its current form is associated with 
unnecessary effort, costs and legal uncertainties for companies. This risks deterring companies 
from investing in the development of IoT products and services. As a result, Europe would lose 
its competitiveness sooner rather than later. In order to prevent this, Software AG would like to 
contribute to the political debate on the Data Act with concrete proposals and show 
possibilities of how the - correct and important - goals of the EU Commission can be achieved 
on the one hand and how the negative (unintended) consequences can be minimized on the 
other. 
We are particularly concerned about the regulations on switching between data processing 
service providers in Chapter 6. We expressly support the EU Commission's goal of facilitating 
switching between service providers by reducing lock-ins and thus strengthening competition 
in Europe. However, the proposed regulations on switching go beyond what is technically 
feasible as well as what makes economic sense in many places. This applies in particular to the: 
• Notice period of maximum 30 days for terminating the contractual agreement  
• Transfer of all data, applications and data assets to the new provider 
• Storage and transfer of all data arising from the use of the service  
• Ensuring functional equivalence by the original provider 
• Commitment to port all applications and digital assets to an on-premise system  
These regulations are so far-reaching or could only be implemented - if at all - with such 
enormous technical effort that, on the one hand, it would become less attractive for European

companies to develop their own cloud and edge services. On the other hand, the 
implementation of the proposed regulations would drastically increase the prices for data 
processing services, especially due to the requirement to store all data generated during their 
use, including metadata, and to transfer it to the new provider when switching providers. This 
would unintentionally deprive many applications of economic ground, causing Europe to fall 
further behind in the use of data processing services. At the same time, this would destroy the 
industrial development of recent years. Especially in the B2B sector, numerous companies have 
begun to invest in the still young market. Not only would they have to fear that their customers 
would switch to another provider within 30 days, but they would also have to spend additional 
resources to make switching technically possible. The associated uncertainties with regard to 
the future viability of their own technologies and business models can be absorbed much more 
easily by established and large cloud providers. This would increase the data gravity towards 
the larger cloud providers. 
To mitigate this collateral damage and ensure a fair balance of interests, we propose the 
following amendments to Data Act E: 
Chapter 1 - General Provisions 
Define the term "service model" (Art. 2, No. 13) 
From Software AG's point of view, a fundamental problem with the Data Act-E is that many 
important terms are defined either inadequately or not at all. The Data Act-E leaves 
considerable ambiguity about the scope of application of the regulations. This concerns in 
particular the regulations on switching between data processing services. For example, the term 
"service model" is not defined, although it is of central importance for the definition of "service 
type" in the Data Act. Since there is also no uniform definition of the term "service model" in 
the IT sector, it can even be interpreted to the extent that it includes business model-dependent 
service configuration (as in the ITIL standard for IT infrastructure services). 
Chapter 2 - Business to consumer and business 
to business data sharing 
Consider undesirable industrial policy implications (Art. 3 - 7) 
The data access rights provided in chapter 2 could have undesirable consequences for the 
European industry. For example, although the data provided by the data holder may not be 
used for the development of competing products, data-based services are deliberately excluded 
from this prohibition on competition. We can understand the Commission's intention to 
strengthen the aftersales market with this regulation, but at the same time we see the danger

that this will lead to unintended effects in terms of industrial policy: The Data Act-E gives third 
parties access to the machine and factory data of European manufacturers. This previously 
protected industrial data is Europe's asset in the digital transformation or the data economy. 
Whereas European industrial companies were previously able to prevent the data originating 
from their machines and factories from being used against them, this would no longer be 
possible under the Data Act-E. This is critical above all because digital services are increasingly 
moving to the centre of the performance and value proposition and competition therefore 
takes place not at the level of the product but at that of the services. Put simply: for the 
customer, it becomes irrelevant whether he buys a machine from manufacturer A or 
manufacturer B, the main thing is that the desired digital service runs on it. The machine would 
then only be the physical and exchangeable carrier for the digital services - with the 
corresponding implications for the distribution of value creation. The Data Act-E would 
encourage this development - to the detriment of European industry. Above all, the Data Act 
should not intervene in an already functioning system. In the Industry 4.0 environment, for 
example, questions of data access can already be solved flexibly and fairly for all parties via 
contracts. 
 
In case of doubt, the Data Act would create new legal uncertainties, weaknesses in the 
protection of trade secrets and bureaucratic hurdles that prevent companies from investing in 
innovative business models based on their own data treasure. In order to prevent these 
unintended effects, the data holder must absolutely be able to keep his trade secrets contained 
in the data to be provided to himself and not - as provided for in the current draft of the Data 
Act - have to disclose them in the last resort if they cannot be removed from the data. 
Nevertheless, not all data may be declared a trade secret by the data holder in order to prevent 
the provision of data. Therefore, the Data Act should also specify what constitutes trade secrets 
within the meaning of the regulation.       
 
Furthermore, it is unclear to us what claims for compensation the data holder has against the 
data recipient in the event of unlawful use of the data, e.g. if the data provided is used to 
develop a competing product. The draft of the Data Act provides that the data holder may take 
technical precautions against the misuse of data and, in the event of infringement, demand that 
the data recipient destroy the data and remove the data, products and services based on it 
from the market. However, it is silent on the neuralgic question of whether the data holder is 
also entitled to compensation for lost sales. In our opinion, the Data Act should be amended as 
a horizontal regulation to include a dedicated liability regime in order to ensure a fair 
distribution of value creation.

Addressing legal uncertainties in the anonymisation and pseudonymisation of personal 
data (Art. 3 - 7) 
In business practice, there is great uncertainty regarding the separation of personal and non-
personal data. According to a study by the IW Köln, 85 percent of the companies surveyed 
describe "grey areas under data protection law" as an obstacle to the economic use of data.1  
Instead of taking this problem into account and reducing uncertainties, the Data Act-E 
exacerbates them. The regulations threaten new legal uncertainties and demarcation difficulties 
between personal and non-personal data. In this context, it is particularly urgent to clarify when 
personal data is considered to be anonymised in a legally secure manner.  
Chapter 6 - "Switching between data 
processing services". 
Restricting the scope of porting applications to another service provider (Art. 23 para. 1c) 
According to Art. 23(1c), all data, applications and other digital goods of a customer must be 
portable to another service provider. Since applications are not defined in the Data Act-E, the 
potential scope of the term “application” is very broad and would also include applications for 
which the customer only has the right of use. In a PaaS/SaaS environment, customers typically 
have access to and the right to use many different but generic functions such as dashboards 
and widgets, identity and access management systems, search and query mechanisms, storage 
functions etc . Giving the original service provider the responsibility to port all these 
applications to another service provider ("target service provider") basically means that its entire 
PaaS/SaaS offering must be transferable to any other service provider, regardless of the 
intellectual property rights and architectural differences of the services offered. 
In any case, the term "applications" in a cloud-native PaaS/SaaS environment often no longer 
make sense, since PaaS/SaaS providers regularly do not provide "applications" in the sense that 
one or more executable files are specifically executed on a computing node on behalf of a user. 
Instead, all cloud service providers today offer so-called capabilities, the provision of which is 
based on a distributed and elastic computing infrastructure. In terms of porting, we propose to 
replace the term "applications" with the term "functional logic", with the meaning of a 
connected set of data capable of influencing, amending, or changing the behavior of a data 
processing service after proper deployment.

Take technical differences into account (Art. 23ff.) 
Many of the far-reaching regulations for changing a service provider are technically unfeasible 
or difficult to implement. According to Art. 23 of the Data Act-E, service providers should be 
obliged to remove technical obstacles and support customers in porting their data, applications 
and other digital assets to another service provider. However, data processing services - even if 
they fulfil the same function - are usually and for good reasons realised in a wide variety of 
ways, for example in terms of data formats, data semantics, execution environments (Intel 
processors vs. ARM processors vs. GPUs), functional logics or internal IT architectures. Each of 
these differences can represent a potential obstacle in the sense of the Data Act-E. For example, 
if an application is to be ported to another service provider in the PaaS/SaaS environment, it is 
not sufficient to share the "basic data processing model" according to Art. 2, Para. 13, but the 
two service providers would have to use one and the same cloud architecture. A similar 
situation arises with the porting to an on-premise system provided for in Art. 24 (a). This is only 
realistic if the on-premise system has an almost identical technical architecture to the cloud in 
which the application to be ported was originally operated. Establishing such technical 
equivalence between service providers or between cloud and on-premise systems is often not 
possible in practice - especially if the original service provider is to bear sole responsibility for 
this. Against this background, it should be defined more precisely what is meant by a technical 
obstacle, which elements of the IT architecture these affect and how far the supporting 
measures on the part of the original service provider must go in order to make a change 
possible. In no case should service providers have to adapt their architectures to the 
architecture of other service providers for portability reasons. They should continue to have the 
freedom to develop their applications in the way that is best for them from a technical, 
economic and strategic point of view.  This freedom of providers also ensures freedom of 
choice for users. 
Extend maximum notice period (Art. 23, para. 1a) 
The maximum notice period of 30 days for terminating a contract would mean that service 
providers would no longer be able to grant their customers discounts on long-term contracts. 
This would unnecessarily and dramatically increase costs for all customers - including those who 
wish to enter into long-term contractual relationships - and severely restrict the development of 
partnerships, data rooms or other forms of data ecosystems. 
Limit the amount of data to be ported to a reasonable level (Art. 23 para. 1c) 
We acknowledge that the need to port data from on service provider to another is of 
paramount importance for a customer’s capability to effectively switch providers. However, in 
certain situations – such as IoT/IIoT/Industry 4.0 – the sheer amount of all data generated by 
the user (i.e., its devices or machines) over the course of the usage of a data processing service 
may be utterly too large to be continuously stored by the (initial) service provider to be 
available upon switching. Example: Wind turbines in a wind farm have several thousand sensors 
that can send sensor readings to a central data processing service (= typically an IoT platform)

hundreds of times per second. Typical European customers produce about 20-50 TB (terabytes) 
of raw data per month. These are usually analysed and aggregated if necessary, but not stored 
in the form of raw data. In the course of longer (multi-year) service contracts, however, the 
service provider would have to store all of this data, i.e. several petabytes (1 PB = 1,000 TB) of 
usage data, for the entire term of the contract, only due to the Data Act ("to port all data, 
applications and digital assets generated directly or indirectly by the customer"). As the cost 
of storing this data - which is normally not stored - is in the range of several million euros per 
year, European customers of IoT platform providers will not be able to compete with other 
(foreign) providers as they will be at a huge cost disadvantage. In addition, the storage of such 
amounts of data generates a high energy input, which should be avoided in the sense of the 
Green Deal. The specific amount of stored data to be ported should therefore be limited to a 
reasonable level - unless otherwise contractually agreed. 
Weaken the requirement for full continuity (Art. 24 para. 1a) 
Art. 24(1a) requires the original service provider to ensure full continuity in the provision of the 
respective functions or services in the context of the change of provider. This requirement 
ignores how migration projects for data or applications work in practice. In a migration project - 
and this also applies to the cloud - there is either a hard cut and a specific date or time when 
users move from one service provider to another (this is discontinuous), or some kind of 
switching gateway has to be deployed at both the original and the future service provider. 
Depending on the state of migration (or porting), this switching gateway dynamically 
determines whether a service request from a user should be addressed to the old instance of 
the service at the original service provider or to the new instance of the service at the new 
service provider. However, this does not take the form of a simple "switchover", but requires a 
full-fledged migration project to be set up and funded by someone. In our opinion, it is 
completely unreasonable - not least in view of the maximum notice period of 30 days - to place 
this effort on the original service provider. 
Introduce deadline for implementation of interoperability requirement (Art. 26 para. 3) 
According to Art. 26 (3), providers of data processing services shall ensure compatibility with 
European interoperability standards. Since there are currently no such standards, nor are any on 
the horizon, each service provider must subsequently modify its services and APIs as soon as 
the standards have come into force. For such an implementation, service providers need 
sufficient lead time - if only because of the large number of services and APIs.  
However, the wording in the Data Act-E suggests that service providers must immediately and 
without delay switch to a completely new (interoperability) standard as soon as it comes into 
force. This cannot realistically be achieved. Therefore, we suggest that service providers be 
given a reasonable period of time for implementation.

Chapter 8 - "Interoperability 
Consider data rooms with multiple operators (Art. 28). 
The current wording on data room requirements seems to focus solely on data rooms operated 
by a single operator, who would then have the sole obligation to comply with the requirements. 
Apart from the fact that the term "operator" is not defined in the Data Act-E, operation by only 
one operator does not correspond to the set-up of many data rooms. These typically consist of 
a managing entity that delegates the actual operation of the ecosystem services (e.g. identity 
and access management services, catalogue services, vocabulary hub services, trust anchor 
services, etc.) to a third party (sometimes referred to as the technical operator of the data 
room). In such a typical setup, the managing institution should be responsible, if at all. The 
proposal also does not consider federated (i.e. there is more than one operator) or 
decentralised data rooms (i.e. no group of operators can be identified). 
Do not introduce a declaration of conformity for smart contracts (Art. 30, para. 2) 
The Data Act should refrain from an EU declaration of conformity. No other type of software 
has to make such a declaration (not even embedded systems for autonomous driving or AI/ML 
applications), and it is not clear to us why this would be necessary for smart contracts. The 
existing laws on product safety and tort law are, in our opinion, quite sufficient.

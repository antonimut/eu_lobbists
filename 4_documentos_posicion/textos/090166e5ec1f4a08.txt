A. General 
We welcome the efforts of the European Commission to establish a flourishing 
data economy within the EU. The Data Act constitutes a main pillar of the 
European Data Strategy and is an essential milestone to achieve the single 
market for data.  
We further welcome the commitment of the European Commission that the Data 
Act should establish a “level playing field” (FRAND1 principles for data access) 
among all stakeholders of the data economy and place the users of products and 
related services at the center.  
Reviewing the draft Data Act from the  perspective of a key player in the 
automotive industry where data is also a critical ingredient for further 
development and innovation, we believe that several terms and provisions of the 
current draft need to be refined to provide more legal certainty fo r all 
stakeholders concerned. 
In some key product characteristics vehicles are very different from many other 
products subject to the Data Act: A vehicle is a complex and integrated product 
with high standards concerning the security, safety and physical integrity of its 
direct users as well as of any other involved road users. The automotive industry 
more generally is characterized by multi -tiered supply -chain networks, 
sophisticated requirements to ensure the safety of passengers and vehicles, 
complex IP agreements between different stakeholders and an average product 
lifespan of 15-25 years. Therefore, some of the provisions of the Data Act which 
aim to uniformly regulate all types of “smart products” are only practicable to a 
limited degree for the automotive sector. Whilst we appreciate that the Data Act 
cannot indivi dually address any and all sector -specific particularities, we 
hereinafter only focus on aspects which the Data Act does not sufficiently take 
into account and which should cause wider concerns even beyond  the 
particularities of the automotive industry.  
The main goal of the Data Act is to promote data -driven innovation across all 
sectors by facilitating access and use of data for the benefit of individuals, 
businesses, public sector bodies and researchers. However, the draft Data Act 
as it stands has also got a potential to create obstacles for exactly these goals.  
Our main concerns on the current draft of the Data Act, as further elaborated in 
this position paper, can be summarized as follows: 
• The definition of the key term data needs to be refined and clearly 
delimited from data which is not subject to the Data Act ’s rights and 
obligations (such as derived or inferred data and volatile data);  
• Access to in-vehicle data or real time access to data can only be granted 
where this is “relevant and appropriate” subject to a balancing of 
interests test; in particular, the holder must have a right to deny direct

access to in -vehicle data  where significant safety o r security 
vulnerabilities are at stake; 
• The crucial term competing product as used in Art. 4.4 and 6.2 lit e. 
should be defined explicitly to ensure the effectiveness of that provision; 
VW Group would clearly prefer to see the development of competing 
related services subject to the same restriction as competing products;  
• Similarly, other key terms such as user, data holder, data recipien t and 
related service should be further refined, or at least additional 
interpretative guidance should be provided in the Recitals; 
• The protection of confidential information, in particular trade secrets 
and unregistered IP of manufacturers must be safeguarded in a more 
differentiated and balanced way, adequately considering the trade secret 
holder’s fundamental rights and legitimate interests. This requires ( i) a 
proportionate and fair mechanism, allowing for the data holder to deny 
access (based on a reasoned request) in case a risk of loss of trade secrets 
materializes, (ii) a fair and efficient review and redress process to assess 
such issues, and (iii) minimum standards and further guidance on the 
specific confidentiality measures to be taken as prerequisite for enabling 
data access;  
• The temporal scope of the Data Act should be clarified to the effect that 
it applies only after a product has been placed on the m arket (to ensure 
that data and related trade secrets generated during the development 
phase are out of scope); 
• The geographical scope should be marked out more clearly, especially 
with regard to third parties outside the Union; 
• A clear basis for reasonable compensation must be established, not 
only for making data available to users and third parties but also for 
creating data, which are costly artifacts. Manufacturers create the 
possibility of the collection and use of data and the Data Act should 
therefore not eliminate any incentives for manufacturers to continue 
doing so. Where the Data Act blocks fair and reasonable compensation 
of the value of data, the Data Act may trigger (unreasonable and 
unintended) front-loading, and shift of data compensation to end users 
of products rather than where data are actually used during the life-span 
of a product; 
• There should be no liability arising for the data holder for any quality of 
the data to be made available under the Data Act (data which was not 
created originally for the use by third parties); 
• Potential loopholes allowing for a potential circumvention of 
restrictions foreseen under the Data Act should be closed effectively, for 
instance, in constellations where a user may provide data to third parties 
without the involvement of the data holder (in particular, to ensure fair 
compensation of the data holder and effective protection of trade 
secrets);

• Where restrictions foreseen under the Data Act or under contractual 
terms agreed with a third party have been vio lated to the detriment of 
the data holder, effective and deterring remedies should be explicitly set 
out in the Data Act; 
• The concept suggested in Art 4.6 should be amended to ensure that the 
Data Act does not foresee stricter requirements for the lawfulne ss of 
using non-personal data  than Art. 6 GDPR foresees with regard to 
personal data. Further, it should be clarified that the undefined term non-
personal data must not be mixed up with anonymized data within the 
meaning of GDPR; such anonymized data cannot be subject to the Data 
Act because reference to a particular requesting user would be 
impossible by definition; 
• The addressee of the upfront information obligations foreseen in Art. 
3.2. should be clarified and the scope of obligations amended to ensure 
practical feasibility also with regard to products (such as vehicles) with 
an average lifespan of 15-25 years or more; 
• The 12 months period currently foreseen as application date under Art. 
42 needs to be extended to 36 months to allow sufficient time for 
implementation of such far reaching regulation, especially in industries 
with long development and production times; further, it should be 
clarified that products / contracts which have been placed on the market 
/ concluded prior to the Effective Date of the Data Act should be out of 
scope; 
• To avoid another level of complexity , the Data Act should aim for the 
creation of a one-stop-shop type authority in relation to su pervising 
compliance with the obligations set out under the Data Act. 
While we are of the opinion that some provisions of the Data Act will need to 
be refined, we are convinced that data sharing as fostered by the Data Act will 
allow for mutual benefits an d lead to a growth in innovation. Therefore, VW 
Group is determined to engage in constructive discussions and contribute to a 
high-quality legislative process. 
B. Terms and Definitions 
I. Article 2.1: Definition of Data 
1. “Data” in scope 
The term “data” is the centerpiece of the Data Act. However, there is no widely 
established and universally applicable  definition of the term data. Thus, we 
welcome the effort of the Commission to provide a suitable definition within 
the context of data sharing under the Data Act. The current definition of “data” 
and consequently, the scope of the data holder’s obligation to provide access to 
data leaves room for uncertainty, especially in the automotive context. 
 739 
 
Modern vehicles generate and process a lot of data within their i nternal 
components. However, this does not mean that all data are (technically and 
legally) accessible for car manufacturers and used by them. Some data, for 
instance, is generated in components supplied by third parties to which the 
manufacturer has no ac cess at all (even and in particular if stored within 
components which would require reverse-engineering or dis-assembling to gain 
access to internally processed data).  
If a car manufacturer needed to provide real-time access to any data in a vehicle, 
this would require streaming and storage capacities way beyond current and 
foreseeable hardware settings in vehicles and backends of manufacturers. Even 
a substantially truncated offering of near -/real-time access to such data would 
exceed current mobile data subscription models and would generate high costs 
on consumers and their mobile data plans. Near -/real-time access would also 
run against the idea of sustainability due to high energy expenditure needed to 
make this possible. Even the vehicle manufacturer itself needs and actually has 
near-/real-time access to only a small fraction of all theoretically available data.  
VW Group would welcome a further clarification of the term “ data” and 
proposes to introduce such clarification in accordance with the follow ing 
principles: 
a) Only data generated by the use of a product or related service shall be in 
scope:  
First, we are concerned that the concept of “data” within the scope of the Data 
Act is not yet consistent throughout the main body of the draft act. Based on our 
understanding, the Data Act shall cover data that is generated by the use of a 
product or related service (and stored beyond the mere processing) (Art. 1.1). 
The obligations in Art. 3, 4 and 5 Data Act, make it explicitly clear that they 
refer to “data generated by their/its [product/s] use”, whereas for instance, the 
obligations under Art. 11, 13 or 15 et. seq. Data Act refer only to “data”, without 
explicit reference to the fact that only data “generated by the use of a product 
or related service” shall be in scope. 
We, therefore propose to explicitly include the requirement “generated by the 
use of a product or related service ” in the definition of “data” in Art. 2.1 (see 
lit.b below). 
b) Only data that is accessible, technically & legally, shall be in scope: 
Secondly, the Data Act should only apply to data that is accessible for the data 
holder from a technical point of view via an implemented access path . The 
concept of accessibility is already contemplated in Recital 28 (cf. 3 rd sentence: 
“…. as the data holder itself may be able or entitled to access from the use of 
the product or related service. ”) and should be further emphasized in the 
definition and in the respective Recitals.  Such an approach  would lead to 
consonance with t he current draft of the Digital Services Act (“ DSA”) which 
 839 
 
introduces the same baseline in Art. 31.6 lit. a when it comes to data access 
obligations of very large online platforms.2  
Further, only data that is legally accessible by the data holder should be in scope. 
Some data may be collected and stored in vehicle components of third party 
suppliers and access may be subject to contractual restrictions. For example, 
even if data is technically stored in the component, the vehicle manufacturer and 
data holder might not have physical access to such data. It might be possible for 
the data holder to gain access to such data by manipulating the component. 
However, this would be contractually or legally forbidden. Hence, such data 
would not be legally accessible.  Data should be considered technically and 
legally “accessible” if it is physically stored in the vehicle storage unit or on a 
backend server. By means of example, volatile data, which is generated during 
the use of the product but immediately disc arded af ter processing for its 
intended purpose, must not be considered technically “ accessible”. We 
therefore propose a clarification that volatile data is out of scope. 
Also, data would not be accessible for the data holder, once it has been deleted 
in a ccordance with the data holder’s legally compliant data deletion policy 
and/or data which is only available in archives in accordance with the data 
holder’s backup policy and thus not readily accessible fo r processing for the 
data holder. 
For the reasons set out above, we propose an amendment of the definition of the 
term “data” as follows: 
2.1 ‘data’ means any digital representation of acts, facts or information 
and any compilation of such acts, facts or information, including in 
the form of sound, visual or audio -visual recording generated by 
the use of a product or related service to the extent technically and 
legally accessible to the data holder and excluding (i) derived or 
inferred data and (ii) data t hat is discarded immediately after 
processing; 
c) Exclusion of derived or inferred data 
Thirdly, the Data Act should only be applicable to data in the simplest form and 
format generated by the use of a product or related service whereas “derived or 
inferred” data shall be out of scope to the extent that the generation of such 
derived or inferred data requires trade secrets (within the meaning of Directive 
(EU) 2016/943) or intellectual property rights of the data holder or required 
additional information that is not available through the use of the product or 
related service.

Data shall in any event be considered derived or inferred where already the input 
data required for such further processing of the derived or inferred data are 
accessible for the user or data recipient. We would not have understood the Data 
Act as a free ride for users and/or  data recipients to leverage the data holders’

For the reasons set out above we propose the introduct ion of a separate 
definition for derived or inferred data as follows: 
(xx) ‘derived or inferred data’ means any data (i) where the 
information contained in the data is modified involving trade 
secrets (within the meaning of Directive (EU) 2016/943) or othe r

additional information which is not generated by the use of the 
product or a related service or (ii)  which are processed from 
otherwise accessible data; 
d) Relevant and appropriate access to data 
Art. 3.1 of the Data Act stipulates that data shall be made available “where 
relevant and appropriate”. We welcome this reasonable approach of a generic 
description rather than venturing into an excessive list of scenarios to further 
illustrate the obligation under Art. 3.1 of the Data Act.  
However, for the design of products (and related services) – in our case of 
vehicles – we do not think that the Data Act is the right place to implement a 
far-reaching design and manufacturing requirement as stipu lated in Art. 3.1 of 
the Data Act. It seems nearly impossible to elaborate in sufficient detail how 
this should practically be done across sectors and across the diversity of 
products concerned by the Data Act. For vehicles, in particular, such design 
requirements would need to be harmonized with the conditions for type 
approval. Therefore, we believe such requirements would be better placed in a 
sector specific regulation on design and manufacturing of products. A mere 
generic principle beyond an enforceab le level of detail would not have the 
desired impact but would only create legal uncertainty.  
While we consider “relevant and appropriate” a reasonable concept, we believe 
the Data Act should give further guidance on the interpretatio n of those 
elements. We understand relevant to mean where possible and would propose 
to confirm that understanding by way of a Recital. Further, we believe the 
assessment and justification of whether access is relevant and appropriate 
should be with the data holder since the data holder provides and designs a 
product and not a data generating device in the abstract. 
 1039 
 
A core criterion to effectuate the relevant -and-appropriate-test of Art. 3.1 is 
whether and under which circumstances data are at all accessible to the data 
holder itself, i.e. where storage (beyond processing of mere volatile data) is 
technically and legally feasible and relevant for the data holder to the extent that 
it makes sense for the data holder itself to invest in the accessibility of the data. 
Where the data holder in its discretion decides to process and keep data, these  
data should be considered relevant and appropriate under the Data Act.  
Hence, we propose the following amendment of Art. 3.1 of the Data Act: 
3.1 Products shall be des igned and manufactured, and related 
services shall be provided, in such a manner that  Data shall be 
provided, in such a manner that - where relevant and appropriate -  
data generated by their use  they are, by default, easily, securely 
and, directly accessible to the user.   
VW Group would welcome further clarification in the Recitals of the Data Act 
making clear that the making available of direct access to data should not be 
considered appropriate where economically or technically unreasonable 
measures by the data holder would be required or where legitimate interests of 
the vehicle manufacturer outweigh the interests of the user, for instance, where 
giving access to the data would lead to significant vulnerabilities for the security 
as well as the safety  of the product. It seems worth mentioning that Art. 31.6 
lit.b DSA introduces a right to object to data access requests for very large online 
platforms, if such access presents security risks for their service. 3 We propose 
that the same approach should be implemented under the Data Act. 
In addition, we see no reason why different criteria should apply to user access 
to data under Art. 3 and 4. The relevant-and-appropriate-test of Art. 3.1 should 
also apply under Art. 4.1.  
Therefore, we propose the following amendment of Art. 4.1 of the Data Act: 
4.1 Where data cannot be directly accessed by the user from the 
product, the data holder shall make available to the user the data 
generated by its use of a product or related service - where relevant 
and appropriate - without undue delay, free of charge and, where 
applicable, continuously and in real-time. 
Since considerable economic and technical efforts may have to be mobilized to 
create the “resource” data in the first place , we are concerned about a n 
undifferentiated and unbalanced concept of user access free of charge , 
regardless of the kind of user  (natural person or commercial user) , the amount

2. Provision of data “as is” 
According to Art. 5.1 the data holder must make the data available to the third 
party in the same quality as it is available to himself. For the avoidance of doubt, 
we would welcome a clarification of the Commission as to the fact that data is 
always provided “as is” in a Recital. In particular, the data holder shall be under 
no obligation to clean, check or review the data provide d under Art. 3-5 in any 
way or to provide it in a specified quality or according to a specific standard. 
Furthermore, the data holder should not be held liable for any services or 
products developed by a third party using the provided data. In this context, VW 
Group would welcome the addition of clarifying wording in a Recital that any 
third party receiving data under Art. 3-5 remains obliged to check and assess the 
quality, fitness and appropriateness of any data received for their own purposes.   
In light of Art. 13.3 lit.a, VW Group would further welcome a clarification that 
contractual terms in B2B data sharing agreements, including in agreements with 
micro, small or medium -sized enterprises, with the effect of limiting warranty 
and liability of the data holder for data to be made available under the Data Act 
shall not be considered unfair under Art. 13 but shall be assessed under the 
general contractual standards for limiting warranties and  liabilities under the 
applicable law. 
II. Article 2.2: Product 
VW G roup generally follows the interpretation of the Commission that a 
connected vehicle as such is a product within the meaning of the Data Act.  
However, this must not imply that the manufacturer automatically is the data 
holder. The content of the contract with the user (cf. Art. 3.2 lit.e) seems to be 
the decisive element to assign the roles under the Data Act (e.g. if th e user has 
a separate contract on the provision of a digital service with an entity different 
from the manufacturer, this would allow for another data holder different from 
the manufacturer). 
III. Article 2.3: Related Service 
While VW Group acknowledges the need to also include related services within 
the scope of the Data Act, we believe that the definition should be refined. 
The definition of “related service” is rather wide and suggests that any service 
shall be included as long as its absence would prevent t he product from 
performing one of its functions. There is no further guidance as to the meaning 
of “one of its functions”. This open wording suggests that any function could be 
in scope, regardless of whether it is a core function required for the operation of 
the product or merely an ancillary or comfort function. Regarding smart 
consumer devices which have a limited set of functions this definition might be 
appropriate. However, in the context of a smart vehicle with a myriad of 
functions, this definition  raises concerns as to its practical handling. We 
 1239 
 
therefore propose that the definition of “ related services” should be lim ited to 
services necessary for the performance of “core” operational functions, whereas 
services necessary for the performance of “an cillary” or comfort functions 
would be out of scope. By means of example the core functions of a vehicle 
would encompass functions related to driving, ancillary functions would relate 
to mere comfort functions such as services offering the streaming of music. 
Recital 16 provides further background on the term “related service” and states 
that such service “can be part of the sales, rent or lease agreement, […]”. VW 
Group would welcome the approach to render the contents of the contract 
concluded with the user (whether it is a sale, lease or rent agreement) the 
ultimate and decisive element as to whether a service is to be consi dered a 
related service within the scope of the Data Act. We suggest clarifying this in 
the definition of “related service” in Art. 2: 
Art. 2.3 ‘related service’ means a digital service, including software, which 
is incorporated in or inter -connected with a product in such a way 
that its absence would prevent the product from performing one of 
its core functions and which is part of the sales, rent or lease 
agreement;  
Recital 16 explicitly mentions the situation in which the “related service” is not 
supplied by the renter, lessor or seller itself but is supplied by a third party under 
the sales contract. We would further suggest adding a clarification in this Recital 
that in such a case the third party could - subject to the content of the agreement 
and the setup of the service – be considered a data holder with respect to that 
particular service.  
On a side note, VW Group would welcome a harmonization of key terms used 
in recent EU legislation (and their definitions) when referring to digital services 
connected with a product since the regulatory environment becomes denser and 
interpretation more challenging; for instance, Directive 2019/771 established 
the term “goods with digital elements” referring to movable tangible items that 
incorporate or are inter-connected with digital content or a digital service in such 
a way that the absence of that digital content or digital service would prevent 
the goods from performing their functions as opposed to “one of its functions” 
under the Data Act.  
IV. Article 2.5: User 
1. Definition of “user” 
The user is defined in Art. 2.5 as the “ natural or legal person that owns, rents 
or leases a product or receives a service ”. From VW Group’s perspective the 
interpretation of the term “user” bears legal uncertainty and raises questions for 
a manufacturer of products which may be used by several persons (e.g. a family 
car used by several family members or a car used in a car-sharing context).  
Firstly, it should be clarified that a user under the Data Act can only be a natural 
or legal person who is a contracting party of the purchase, rent or lease 
 1339 
 
agreement with the data holder or in case the seller, renter or lessor is not the 
data holder, to whom the data holder has been made transparent under such 
purchase, rent or lease agr eement in accordance with Art. 3.2. By contrast, a 
person who is only authorized to actually use the product by the user should not 
fall under the definition of user (the decisive element should always be the 
contractual relationship of the user, not the a ctual use of the product). In this 
context, the term “ or receives a service ” as used in Art. 2.5 leaves room for 
ambiguity. It suggests that also another person which has only been factually 
authorized by the user to use the product or receive a related se rvice could be 
considered a “ user”. This would contradict the contractual element of the 
definition of “ user” as described before. Thus, we propose to clarify the 
definition of user as follows:  
2.5 “user means a natural or legal person that owns, rents or  leases a 
product or receives a service on the basis of a purchase, rent or 
lease agreement with the data holder or to whom the identity of the 
data holder has been made transparent under such purchase, rent 
or lease agreement in accordance with Art. 3.2.” 
Recital 30 (last sentence) insinuates that a user may become a data holder under 
certain circumstances and that, hence, the Data Act would be open for data 
holder and/or user chains. 
We have concerns that one sentence in Recital 30 is not sufficient to tackle such 
complex situations . Therefore, w e would welcome further guidance for 
interpretation of such constellations. From a data holder’s point of view , it 
seems crucial to clarify as a minimum that no liability may arise to the detriment 
of the data holder where a sub-user in a certain chain of users has not received 
information on the identity of the data holder (as required under Art. 3.2) or for 
lack of assignment of contractual rights of the initial user to the sub-user. 
V. Article 2.6: Data holder 
VW Group is concerned that the definition of “data holder” remains too vague: 
The definition suggests that there is only one data holder per product. It is our 
understanding of the Data Act that there could be several data holders regarding 
the product and the related service respectively.  
To fully reflect this understanding under the Data Act we would propose to add 
the following clarification to the definition of data holder: 
Art. 2.6:  “[…] through control of the technical design of the product and or 
related services, the ability, to make available certain data”.  
VI. Article 2.7: Data recipient 
The definition of data recipient refers to persons other than the user of a product 
to whom the data holder makes data available. The third party receiving the data 
is subsequently subject to certain restrictions laid down in Art. 5, Art. 6 and Art. 
9. 
 1439 
 
However, a situation could also arise where the user requested and obtained data 
from the data holder (under Art. 3 or 4) and later-on shares that data with a third 
party (Recital s 5 and  28). The current definition of data recipient seems to 
exclude this scenario from its scope; Art. 5 regards the scenario where the data 
holder makes available data to a third party upon request of the user. Art. 6 
directly refers to Art. 5 and also Art. 9 only mentions the situation where the 
data holder and the data recipient have been in direct interaction with each other. 
In order to preserve the intended protections and general restrictions laid down 
in Art. 5, Art. 6 and Art. 9 also when data is made available from the user to the 
third party, we would propose to either amend the definition of data recipient in 
Art. 2.7 (so that it should also apply in a situation where the third party receives 
data directly from the user) or to clarify directly in the text of the Data Act that 
the same restrictions shall apply when the third party receives data directly from 
the user.  
C. Main Body 
I. Article 1 – Subject Matter & Scope 
1. Temporal scope 
Vehicles are products with a typically long developme nt phase during which 
data may already be collected. Such data is crucial for the development of 
prototypes and/or new functions. During such phase, relevant products are not 
being disclosed to the public although sometimes they are technically “sold” or 
“rented” to a cooperation partner. Data generated during the development phase 
are typically confidential by nature.  Trade secrets are particularly sensitive in 
the research and development phase and related cooperation. To safeguard the 
protection of trade secrets it is of great importance that the development phase 
and data generated solely for the purpose of the deve lopment of a product or 
service shall be out of scope of the Data Act. VW Group, hence, proposes that 
the Data Act should only be applicable to products and related services from the 
point in time after they have been placed on the market. 
For that purpose, we propose to add a new last sentence in Art. 2, immediately 
following lit.e: 
“This Regulation applies to:  
(e) [...] in each case, after the product or related service has 
been placed on the market in the Union.“ 
We further propose to add a definition of “placed on the market” in Art. 2: 
Placed on the market means “the first making available on the 
market in the Union , whereas making available shall refer to 
any supply of a n individual  product or related service for 
distribution, consumption or use in the Union in the course of 
a commercial activity, whether in return for payment or free of 
charge;”.

2. Territorial scope 
VW Group believes that the territorial scope of application should be further 
clarified: the Data Act refers to the “Union” on several occasions (e.g. Recital 5 
or Art. 1.2).  
Art. 1.2 lit.a states that the Data Act shall be applicable to “ manufacturers of 
products and suppliers of related services placed on the market in th e Union 
and the users of such products or services”. Similarly, Recital 5 refers to “users 
of a product or related service in the Union”. We would welcome a clarification 
as to the scope of application in a scenario where, e.g: 
• the user is not domiciled in the EU but generates data within the Union 
by use of a product or related service placed on the market in the Union 
(e.g. in case of car rental)., 
• the user (domiciled in the EU) generates data outside the Union by use 
of a product or related service placed on the market in the Union,  
• a product has been manufactured in a foreign market (for example in 
China) and has then been imported into the Union. 
In addition, Art. 1.2 lit.b and lit.c refer to data made available to “data recipients 
in the Union”. Similarly, Recital 5 refers to the fact that this regulation ensures 
“data holders make data available to data recipients in the Union under fair, 
reasonable and non-discriminatory terms…”. From these provisions we derive 
that the data holder’s obligations to make data available to third parties upon the 
user’s request shall only apply to data recipients having their registere d seat 
within the Union. We would welcome a clarification in this respect. If our 
understanding is confirmed, we would propose to clarify that a data holder shall 
have the right to refuse compliance with a user request where access is required 
to non-personal data for a data recipient outside of the Union.  
In addition to the questions raised above, it also remains unclear in cross border 
situations whether the supervisory authority of Member State A or B shall be 
competent and what shall be the decisive element for local competence. 
3. Anonymized data 
Vehicles generate a vast amount of data while used by the user. Some data is 
processed right away and never stored, other data is stored in a way that it is still 
attributable to the usage of the vehicle at a specific point in time together with 
a unique identifier (vehicle identification number or similar ID) and can 
therefore be traced back to th e user. Yet, where reference to a specific vehicle 
or user is not needed or where it is not legally permissible to store this context, 
data may be technically anonymized before being stored in the backend.  
As a result of such anonymization, it is technically impossible for the data holder 
to comply with a user’s request to provide data  insofar as it is anonymized  – 
because the data holder simply cannot infer which part of the anonymized data 
set is attributable to the user requesting access.  Refraining from anonymizing 
 1639 
 
data which are not needed (or even not legally permissible to be stored in non -
anonymized form) would violate the principle of data minimization as  per Art. 
5.1 lit.c GDPR.  
Art. 3.1 and Recital 7 state that no provision of the Data Act should be applied 
or interpreted in such a way as to diminish or limit the rights and obligations set 
out in the GDPR . Thus,  we suggest a clarification in a Recital that the 
anonymization of personal data remains in the full discretion of the data holder 
and is not limited by the Data Act. Further, once data has been anonymized, it 
cannot be shared with users or data recipients under the Data Act anymore.  
II. Article 3 – Obligation to make data generated by the use of products or 
related services accessible 
1. Article 3.1: Access to in-vehicle data 
Art. 3.1 (in connection with Recital 21) foresees that data generated by the use 
of the product shall – where relevant and appropriate – be directly accessible to 
the user. In the automotive context, this would require access to in-vehicle data 
as due to technical restrictions or lack of relevance, data is often not transferred 
from the vehicle to a backend. While VW Group does not in principle object to 
the concept of in -vehicle data access, there are clear and objective limitations 
for such direct access as already outlined in section B.I.1.d). A vehicle is a 
complex product which bears an inherent risk for the user’s , passengers’ and 
other road users’ physical integrity. As a consequence, the safety and security 
of vehicle users must be safeguarded at all times and the manufacturer (and in 
many cases the data holder) is - for good reason - under strict regulatory scrutiny 
to maintain adequate car safety and security. Each third party interface granting 
access to a vehicle represents a potential point of vulnerability for car safety and 
car IT security. Therefore, VW Group would like to reiterate the importance of 
a clarification to the effect that data shall be directly accessible to the user only 
“where relevant and appropriate ”. We propose to introduce such clarification 
as explained in section B.I.1.d) by way of a Recital referring to a balancing of 
interest test to determine whether granting in-vehicle access would be “relevant 
and appropriate ”. Indeed, we believe that the manufacturer is in the best 
position to assess whether safety and security concerns prevail and whether the 
user should therefore be precluded from such direct access. Th e clarifying 
Recital could also include a requirement for the manufacturer to document the 
balancing of interest test including the main (commercial and technical) 
arguments against granting direct in -vehicle access in a transparent and 
comprehensible way. 
2. Article 3.2: Upfront information obligations 
The Data Act introduces new pre -contractual information obligations towards 
the user. From the point of view of the automotive industry, the current draft 
poses several practical questions as to how compliance with these new 
information obligations could be achieved.  
 1739 
 
Firstly, Art. 3.2 states that “information shall be provided” which leaves unclear 
as to who is obliged to provide the required information to the user (the 
manufacturer? the data holder? the selle r, renter, lessor? the re -seller?). In the 
context of vehicles, complex supply and contractual chains are typical. By 
means of example, a vehicle could be produced by the OEM, then sold or rented 
on a B2B level before being rented for the use of a natural person. We would 
suggest a clarification in Recital 23 that the information obligation generally 
follows the contractual relationships and that it is the entity who concludes the 
purchase, rent or lease agreement with the user who should comply with the 
information obligation. As already outlined in section B.IV.1 from a data 
holder’s point of view it seems crucial to clarify that no liability may arise for 
the data holder where a sub -user has not received information in accordance 
with Art. 3.2 (including wrong or incomplete information).  
Further, Art. 3.2 states that the information should be provided to the user before 
concluding a contract for the purchase, lease or rent of a product. This raises 
concerns as to the practical feasibility in light of the long lifespan of vehicles.  
Vehicles on average have a lifespan of 15 -25 years or more, much longer than 
typical consumer products. Hence, the question arises as to whether the 
information obligation must be fulfilled over the entire lifespan of the p roduct 
and whether the information provided to the user must be updated during the 
lifespan of the vehicle and if so, how this could reasonably be achieved.  
For instance, it is impossible to inform the user – 15-25 years in advance - about 
the nature and volume of the data which is likely to be generated by the use of 
the product or related services (Art . 3.2 lit.a) where such information is 
dependent on the usage pattern , future regulation  and by possibly changing 
configuration of the product by the user as well as in light of the fact that related 
services are constantly being developed and updated.  
Therefore, we propose to delete the upfront information obligation regarding 
the volume of the data as well as regarding the nature of data generated after the 
placement of the product on the market in Art. 3.2 lit.a.   
Another practical challenge which generically applies to products with a 
lifespan that exceeds the update interval of the product would be the pre -
contractual information obligation regarding the intended use of the data (Art . 
3.2 lit. d) by the manufacturer  or a third party and the purpos es for which the 
data will be used as functionality and usage patterns may evolve over time. It 
would further be helpful to add a clarification that the transparency obligations 
set out in Art. 3 .2 only refer to data that are actually subject to be shared in 
accordance with the Data Act (excluding in particular anonymous data which 
are often used for sensitive development purposes).  
Therefore, we propose to cut the requirement for information regarding the 
intended use of the data in Art. 3.2 lit.e down to  only comprise data usage 
reasonably foreseeable at the point in time of the placement of a product in the 
market in the Union. 
 1839 
 
In addition, we see practical challenges for implementing compliant and “user-
friendly” information due to parallel obligations under GDPR, the German 
Telecommunications and Telemedia Data Protection Act (“ TTDSG”) and the 
Data Act. More specifically, we see the danger of overwhelming the user with 
a “pile” of data related information under the Data Act, TTDSG and GDPR. 
This might c ause the opposite of the transparency effects as intended by the 
Commission.  
To ensure legal certainty, we strongly recommend aiming for a one-stop-shop 
authority and competence in relation to all questions referred to under the Data 
Act (cf. section XV for further detail). 
Lastly, we would welcome a clarification on the temporal scope of the 
information obligation under Art. 3.2, to the effect that the obligation to inform 
should only apply to products or related services which have been purchased, 
leased or rented after the  Effective Date of the Data Act (cf. section C.XVI 
regarding Article 42 below).  
III. Article 4 – The right of users to access and use data generated by the use of 
products or related services 
1. Access “continuously and in real-time” 
Regarding the requirement of access in “real-time”, we would like to point out 
that the development and operation of a system enabling real-time or even near 
real-time access for all data typ es would be extremely costly and complex. 
Currently, there is no system readily available on the market for the continuous 
and real -time provision of data. Further, the amount of data generated in 
connected vehicles is humongous and would exceed by far the  technical 
capacities of the vehicles ’ interfaces and the user’s processing abilities. Real -
time access to data should only be implemented where technically feasible and 
economically reasonable. As outlined in section B.I.1.d) above, we therefore 
propose to include the relevant -and-appropriate test also in Art. 4.1. Whether 
the provisioning of data in real-time would be “applicable” should be evaluated 
by the manufacturer using the same principles as developed for the asses sment 
as to whether direct access to data would be “ appropriate” under Art. 3.1. The 
general principle to make data available should not impose a commercial or 
design decision which would impede or even block the development of a 
product along the lines of the manufacturer’s discretion. In other words: Where 
the manufacturer decides to prioritize the usage of a capacity-restraint over-the-
air interface out of a vehicle for certain data over  others, this must not be 
considered a violation of any of the manufacturer’s or data holder’s obligations 
under the Data Act.  
2. Access free of charge 
We understand that with the current proposal the Commission aims at balancing 
the rights and interests of affected stakeholders with the general objective to 
facilitate wider use of data for a broad range of actors and at the same time, it 
aims to avoid undermining the investment incentives for the type of product 
 1939 
 
from which the data are obtained. VW Group welcomes this approach; however, 
by implementing a general standard according to which access for the user 
always has to occur free of charge, without differentiating between different 
categories of users and their purposes , the amount of access requests made, or 
the volume of data requested, we see an unbalanced outcome (in particular but 
not limited to natural persons vs. enterprises acting for business purposes). We 
would propose to open the free of charge standard for a more differentiated and 
balanced solution; as a minimum , where a data holder is willing to make 
additional investments to facilitate a specific access request, the data holder 
should also have the option to charge the user or data recipient for such 
expenses. 
3. Protection of trade secrets 
We welcome that Art. 4 of the Commissions’ proposal considers a prerequisite 
that all specific measures to preserve confidentiality of trade secrets must be 
ensured. However, we think that this does not sufficiently protect the data 
holder’s legitimate interest to protect its confidential information, especially in 
cases where the commercial value of the trade secrets involved clearly exceeds 
the value of the actual data and/or the user’s legitimate interest to access such 
data. Pre-determined exemptions for data that is typically reasonably considered 
subject to trade secret protection (e.g. derived, inferred, derivative data), a right 
for the data holder to deny access based on a reasoned request , a balanced 
mechanism to review such matters, and practical guid ance on minimum 
standards for “all specific measures to preserve confidentiality” are required to 
secure the proportionality and fairness in this regard.  We elaborate further on 
this point in section C.IV below. 
4. Competing product 
The Commissions’ proposal restrict s the use of data made available under the 
Data Act for the development of competing products by users (Art. 4.4) and 
third parties (Art. 6.2 lit.e). However, the term “ competing product ” is not 
defined. Given the enormous economic importance of this restriction regarding 
the protection of the investments of a data holder and the subsequent incentive 
for future investments and development we would ask the Commission for a 
definition as to what constitutes a “competing product”. Such definition should 
include guidance as to how to define the relevant market and product scope as 
well as the relevant temporal and territorial scope of assessing a “competing 
product” (e.g. would an autonomous vehicle be considered a competing product 
to a conventional car? Would a utility vehicle be considered a competing 
product to a sports car?). Further, it is unclear whether mere research would 
already fall within the scope of developing a “competing product”. Further, easy 
circumvention of the competing product restriction should be  impeded by 
addressing not only the user or third party but also their respective affiliated 
companies. 
This aspect ties into the remarks on the definition of a “ related service ” in 
section B.III above. As long as it is unclear what the relevant features and 
 2039 
 
functionality of a product for purposes of the Data Act are, it is difficult to assess 
whether a new development is actually competing with a product. 
Further, Art. 6.2 lit.e which lays down the restriction related to “competing 
products” for third parties references Ar t. 5. Ar t. 5 in turn only covers the 
scenario in which the third party has directly received data from the data holder 
(upon request of the user) but not the scenario where a third party has received 
data directly from the user  (as envisaged under Recit al 5 and 28) . As already 
outlined in section B.VI above, we propose to clarify that the restrictions related 
to “competing products” shall apply to all third parties, irrespective of whether 
the data was obtained through the data holder or directly passed-on by the user. 
Otherwise, by -passing this restriction would be easy and make it practically 
meaningless. 
5. Use of non-personal data 
Art 4.6 states that use of non-personal data by the data holder shall only be 
lawful on the basis of a contractual agreement with the user. Taking into 
consideration the regime applicable under the GDPR  for personal data , such 
restriction to having a contractual agreement with the user seems quite extensive 
and disproportionate. Even for personal data, Art. 6 GDPR offers a range of 
different options potentially serving  as a legal basis for lawful processing of 
personal data, i.e. besides a contractual arrangement with the data subjec t also 
the basis of legitimate interests. The processing of non-personal data, however, 
does not demand the same or even a stricter standard of protection than is set 
out for personal data under the GDPR, which is particularly in line with the EU 
Charter of Fundamental Rights setting out a fundamental right to the protection 
of personal data,  yet does not address the protection of non -personal data . 
Despite this clear distinction in the level of protection on the level of EU primary 
law, one could conclude that Art . 4.6 foresees stricter requirements for the 
lawfulness of using non -personal data than Art. 6 GDPR foresees regarding 
personal data. This cannot be the intention of the Data Act. Therefore, we 
propose to amend Art. 4.6 in such a way that the data holder’s right to use non-
personal data generated by the use of a product shall not be limited to having a 
respective contractual agreement with the user in place.  
At this point it is necessary to clarify the undefined term “non -personal data”. 
The term “non-personal data” as used in Art . 4.6 must not be confused with 
“anonymized” data (within the meaning of the GDPR), i.e. with data which are 
modified using techniques which prohibit to trace back the anonymized data to 
a natural person . Such anonymized data cannot be subject to the Data Act 
because it would be impossible by definition to establish a link between such 
data and a requesting user (cf. section I.3). Hence, non-personal can only mean 
data that can be traced back to an individual which is not subject to the GDPR, 
i.e. a legal entity. 
Taking this into account, we suggest clarify ing in a recital that the term “ non-
personal data generated by the use  […]” is meant to refer to data that is being 
generated by users that are not individuals, but does not refer to data that is being 
subsequently anonymized. 
 2139 
 
We further conclude from Art. 4.6 that the Commission intends to stipulate a 
restriction for data relating to identifiable legal entities, similar to the rules on 
profiling of personal data as per Art. 22 GDPR. While it is understood and 
appreciated that data holders in general should not abuse their position to seek 
a competitive advantage , the proposal set out in the Data Act is even stricter 
than the rules contained in the GDPR  for profiling as it does neither contain a 
balancing of interest element, nor does Art. 4.6 seem to allow such processing 
even with the user’s prior permission (despite Recital 25 explicitly mentioning 
“granular permission options ”). We suggest including a balancing of interest 
element as well as to explicitly state that such processing is legitimate based on 
the user’s permission. 
IV. Article 5 – Right to share data with third parties 
1. Background and concerns on the protection of trade secrets 
There is an obvious conflict between data access rights as envisaged under the 
proposed Data Act and the data holder’s legitimate interest to protect its 
intellectual property , confidential information , and know -how. This applies 
especially to intangible assets that require to be kept secret , in order to retain 
their commercial value, i.e. that are protected as trade secrets.  
Undoubtedly, data (whether in raw or derived form) can qualify as trade secret. 
This is obvious for derived data and data generated in the course of research and 
development (prior to any product being placed on the market). Considering the 
fact that trade secrets retain their value only as long as they are kept confidential, 
making data qualifying as trade secrets available – even under appropriate 
contractual prerequisites – disproportionally increases the risk  of loss of trade 
secrets (due to disclosure) and with it compromising the entire business value 
of such data. 
Trade secrets are protected by fundamental rights : For example, in Germany 
they are protected as a manifestation of freedom of profession and fre edom of 
property under Art. 12 and Art. 14 of the German Constitution. Trade secrets 
also enjoy protection under the European Convention on Human Rights 
(“ECHR”) and the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union 
(“CFR”). The European Court of Justice  (“ECJ”) considers trade secrets to be 
protected under the right to respect for private life under Art. 8 ECHR which 
flows from the common constitutional traditions of the Member States and 
which is restated in Art. 7 CFR. 4 In this respect, the ECJ refers to the case-law 
of the European Court of Human Rights, according to which the concept of 
“private life” can also include professional and business activities of natural and 
legal persons. Moreover, the ECJ considers the pro tection of trade secrets a 
general principle of Union law.5 Depending on the context, the principle of the 
protection of confidential information and trade secrets can also be protected

In particular in the context of access to confidential information, the ECJ has

other economic operators to the protection of their confidential information and 
their business secrets”.7 According to the ECJ, where several fundamental rights 
and principles enshrined in the Treaties are at issue, the principle of

balance between them:8 
“Legislation which entails an interference with fundamental

stakeholders’ fundamental freedoms under the Charter of Fundamental Rights

does not sufficiently consider this (cf. C.IV.2 below).

3. Proposed amendments 
To address the current imbalance and achieve compliance with the fundamental 
freedoms and rights involved, we would propose a number of amendments to 
be implemented into the Data Act, some of which have already been outlined in 
the previous sections: 
• Clarification of the temporal scope of application of the Data Act. The 
development phase of a product should be fully protected (cf. section 
C.I.1). 
• Clarification of the “data” in scope of the Data Act. The more data is in 
scope which is generated by the use of IP and/or trade secrets, the bigger 
the threat for trade secret protection; hence a clear carve-out of derived 
or inferred data is required (cf. this section as well as and B.I.1.c)). 
• Create an option for the data holder to deny data access due to risk of 
losing a trade secret (cf. this section as well as C.IV.5), coupled with a 
redress mechanism to allow for a fair and efficient review of such cases. 
• Specify the prerequisites for data access to trade secrets. It must be 
ensured that the data holder can put appropriate confidentiality terms 
and conditions in place as required under trade secrets law (cf. this 
section) – and in case such prerequisites are  not agreed by the user or 
third party, may refuse to enable data access. 
Scope 
As outlined above, data access requests under Art. 4 and Art. 5 seemingly also 
relate to data that is or can be a trade secret within the meaning of Directive 
(EU) 2016/943. However, these provisions do not further specify the scope of 
access to trade secrets intended by the Data Act. According to Recital 14 of the 
Data Act, “information derived or inferred from this data, where lawfully held, 
should not be considered within sco pe of this Regulation”. It does not seem to 
be sufficiently clear whether data access under the Data Act only applies to “raw 
data”, i.e. unprocessed data as supplied by a data source, or whether also forms 
of processed, combined, filtered or otherwise modified data are within scope of 
the Regulation. 
Further, Recital 17 states that “…Such data should include data in the form and 
format in which they are generated by the product, but not pertain to data 
 2639 
 
resulting from any software process that calculates derivative data from such 
data as such software process may be subject to intellectual property rights” 
However, irrespective of Recital 17, the current draft of the Data Act is not clear 
as to whether it  applies to “raw data” only, it does not even clarify what raw 
data are, or to what extent, for instance, also forms of processed, combined, 
filtered or otherwise modified data are within scope. 
In addition, software processes as mentioned in Recital 17 will, aside from being 
subject to intellectual property rights, also typically be subject to confidential 
information and know-how of the data holder , i.e. covered by applicable trade 
secret protection. Where access to data enables conclusions to be drawn ab out 
(confidential) company -internal processes (e.g. methods of analysis and 
information evaluation, use of algorithms, filtering of information, etc.), trade 
secrets (aside from intellectual property rights) will typically be at stake.  
The scope of mandat ory access to data should therefore be clarified in the 
normative text of the Data Act (cf. our proposal for the definition of “data” and 
“derivative data” in section B.I.1.b) and B.I.1.c)  as well as in Recital 17 to 
ensure trade secrets protection (aside from intellectual property rights): 
Recital 17 “Such data should include data in the form and format 
in which they are generated by the product, but not pertain to 
data resulting from any software process that calculates 
derivative data from such data as such software process may be 
subject to intellect ual property rights , trade secrets protection  
or otherwise proprietary or confidential to the data holder.”  
In any case, where access to data enables the recipient to draw conclusions about 
(confidential) company -internal processes (e.g. methods of analys is, data 
verification and information evaluation, use of algorithms, filtering of 
information, etc.), trade secrets will likely be at stake. Since such internal 
processes can hardly be considered data that is generated by the product and 
there is no legiti mate interest in making them accessible, it should be possible 
to exclude access to them or to make it subject to reasonable confidentiality 
measures meeting the requirements set out in Art. 2.1 lit.c Directive (EU) 
2016/943. 
Option to deny access 
The Data Act in its proposed form does not specify whether confidentiality risks 
with regards to trade secrets may hinder a request for data access. However, 
under Art. 31.6 of the EU’s proposed Digital Services Act, 12 very large online 
platforms can request an am endment of a data access request in cases where 
there are “ significant vulnerabilities  for […] the protection of confidential 
information, in particular trade secrets ”. Not least for reasons of consistency, 
where data access under the Data Act enables access or conclusions to be drawn 
 
12  Regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council on a Single Market for Digital Services 
(Digital Services Act) and amending Directive 2000/31/EC. 
 2739 
 
about a trade secret, a similar exception should be implemented in the Data Act. 
This applies even more, when access to such trade secrets is not covered by the 
data access request in the first place. 
We propose to apply such option in the context of Art 4. as well as Art. 5 , 
whereby in the context of Art. 4, data holders would need to provide the 
competent authority or settlement body “up front” with such refusal request, 
whereby in the context of Art. 5 , this would be carried out upon the user’s 
request. Art. 31 Digital Services Act can serve as a template  for developing a 
similar mechanism tailored to the Data Act environment. 
Moreover, an exception from data access should apply when a user or third party 
has already proven that it does not comply with confidentiality agreements or 
indicates that it does not intend to do so. In such cases, Art. 4.3 and Art. 5.8 of 
the Data Act, aiming at preserving confidentiality of trade secrets upon access, 
will run idle and a trade secret holder would have to knowingly risk the loss of 
the trade secret’s legal protection. 
In other cases, placing a product on the market or granting physical access to it, 
will inevitably increase the risk that any trade secret may be derived by means 
of observation, disassembly or testing of a product, i.e. by reverse engineering. 
While under trade secrets law, reverse engineering can be prohibited by contract 
(Art. 3.1 li t.b of Directive (EU) 2016/943), such contractual exclusion may 
conflict with Art 8.2 Data Act, which states that a contractual obligation shall 
not be binding if, in particular, it “ excludes the application of, derogates from 
or varies the effect of the u ser’s rights under chapter II”. Also here, the Data 
Act should provide for a clear and practical guidance on what information is 
covered by data access requests and whether reverse engineering can be 
contractually excluded, in particular to protect trade secrets that are not directly 
covered by data access requests.  
We would  therefore further propose to include an explicit  right for the data 
holder to deny access to trade secrets in specific situations as further outlined in 
section C.IV.5 below, inter alia. 
As out lined above, confidentiality and appropriate measures to maintain 
confidentiality of trade secrets are required by definition in Art. 2.1 Directive 
(EU) 2016/943. However, the Data Act does not specify sufficiently clearly in 
which manner and under what prerequisites data access can be granted while at 
the same time ensuring conformity with the requirements under trade secrets 
law: 
• In Art. 11.1, the Data Act states that data holders may apply 
“appropriate technical protection measures , including smart 
contracts, to prevent unauthorized access to the data and to ensure 
compliance with Art. 5, 6, 9 and 10, as well as with the agreed 
contractual terms for making data available.” 
However, such technical protection measures “ shall not be used as a 
means to hinder  the user’s right to effectively provide data to third 
 2839 
 
parties pursuant to Article 5 ” or any other right of a third party under 
Union law. 
• While Art. 4.3 states that trade secrets shall only be disclosed if all 
specific necessary measures are taken to pres erve the confidentiality, it 
does not specify possible measures. Furthermore, it is not sufficiently 
clear whether such measures would be a prerequisite of access, i.e. the 
data holder could make data access dependent on the fact of whether the 
user agrees to such measures (“[…] can agree measures to preserve the 
confidentiality […]”). The same applies to Art. 5.8, where such “specific 
necessary measures agreed between the data holder and the third party 
[…] shall be specified in the agreement between the data holder and the 
third party.” 
Since with regards to the protection of trade secrets it is crucial that 
appropriate confidentiality measures are maintained, the Data Act 
should clarify that – in case trade secrets are at stake – data holders can 
make the conclusion of an agreement on such “ specific necessary 
measures [...] to preserve the confidentiality” a condition for access. In 
order to provide more legal certainty for the parties affected and to the 
extent possible, it should also be explained in more detail and by way of 
practical guidance which measures could be regarded “ necessary” 
depending on the individual case (e.g. NDA, other technical protection 
measures, observing a need-to-know principle, etc.). 
From a practical point of view and to provide certainty for data holders 
and recipients alike, the Data Act should specify the (minimum) 
contractual measures which the data holder can make a prerequisite for 
access to trade secrets, in particular: 
• The data holder should be entitled to oblige the user to adequate 
non-disclosure of data received (i.e. user must not share trade 
secrets received under Art. 3 or Art. 4 with third parties; any 
sharing of trade secrets with third parties must occur via the data 
holder pursuant to Art. 5).  
• The Data Act should clarify that contractual clauses on non -
disclosure, reverse engineering, indemnification, and 
compensation must be considered fair and appropriate under Art. 
8 and Art. 13.  
Such clauses should also be part of the model contractual terms to be 
developed by the Commission (Art. 34). 
• Art. 3.1 requires data holders to make data accessible by design but does 
not contain any statement with regard to the protection of trade secrets 
that can be affected. Therefore, the  question arises whether ensuring 
confidentiality of a trade secret is a case where a direct access of the user 
is not to be considered “appropriate” within the meaning of Art. 3.1. 
If Art. 3.1 remains applicable in cases where trade secrets are at stake, it 
should be clarified that a data holder can make necessary confidentiality 
 2939 
 
measures part of the purchase agreement to avoid a possible conflict 
with Art. 8.2. 
• The Data Act should clarify that users are not free to provide confidential 
data to third parties, in particular not without observing and maintaining 
the necessary confidentiality measures. Therefore, Recital 28 seems to 
conflict with Art. 5.8: While Recital 28 seemingly provides for a free 
distribution of data received by the user, according  to Art. 5.8 third 
parties can only be granted access to the confidential data by the data 
holder on a respective request of the user and subject to certain 
conditions (disclosure is “ strictly necessary to fulfill ” an aftermarket 
purpose and “ specific nece ssary measures agreed between the data 
holder and the third party are taken by the third party to preserve the 
confidentiality”). We further propose clarifying that an indirect receipt 
of data (i.e. where the third party receives data from the user) must b e 
treated the same way as a direct receipt from the data holder. Inter alia 
for that purpose, we have also proposed to amend the definition in Art. 
2.7 (cf. section B.VI above so that the definition of data recipient does 
also cover third parties who receive data from the user). 
• In any case, where the Data Act commands for disclosure of trade secrets 
(Art. 4.3 and Art. 5.8), the data holder shall be entitled to claim in each 
case – as a prerequisite – adequate compensation along the lines of 
established FRAND principles. 
Unless clarified otherwise in the Data Act as suggested above, the mere 
possibility of the user to provide data received to third pa rties (Recital 28) 
without ensuring and observing confidentiality measures would most likely not 
be compatible with the definition of a trade secret as the data holder loses control 
over the access to the information.  
4. Gatekeepers 
Regarding the restrictions excluding gatekeepers as eligible third parties as 
foreseen in Art. 5.2, we are concerned that those provisions only refer to data 
provided by the data holder to the user under Art. 4; however, the user could 
also access data directly und er Art. 3 and in that case the same restrictions 
should apply.  
Therefore, we propose to include a reference to Art. 3.1 in addition to Art. 4.1 
in both Art. 5.2 lit.a as well as Art. 5.2 lit.c: 
“[…] that the user has obtained pursuant to Art. 3.1 or pursuant 
to a request under Art. 4.1” 
More generally, the implications of a qualif ication as a gatekeeper under the 
Data Act seem overshooting as they would apply even to the most innovative, 
fair and reasonable gatekeepers solely due to the fact that they trigger 
quantitative criteria as set out in the Digital Markets Act and even in the absence 
of any form of abusive use of a gatekeeper‘s powers. For instance , in the 
automotive sector even the handful of potential challengers for setting the scene 
 3039 
 
in autonomous driving in the Union would be deprived of access to critical data 
once trig gering the criteria and thus be de facto excluded from further 
participating in the race for innovative and data -driven research and 
development on autonomous driving. The respective exemptions for 
gatekeepers as set out in the Digital Markets Act are focu sing on more general 
aspects of hardship or public interest but do  not comprise data driven 
innovation, which is the particular focus of the Data Act. VW Group would 
therefore welcome further reflection on whether and how the Data Act can be 
amended, when taking reference to the gatekeeper concept, so that the vested 
interest of the Data Act itself remains intact. Fair and reasonably acting 
gatekeepers should not be deprived of any rights for access to data under the 
Data Act in the first place, and secondl y the Data Act should at least allow for 
gatekeepers to participate in data-driven innovation. 
5. Denial of access to data 
We propose to add a provision to the Data Act which explicitly specifies the 
circumstances under which a data holder may - on an exceptional basis - deny 
access to data, in particular: 
• Where such access is not relevant and appropriate (cf. B.I.1.d); 
• Where direct user access under Art.3 would jeopardize trade secrets  
protection (cf. C.IV.3 for further details); 
• Where a user or third party does not agree to adequate contractual or 
other measures to preserve the confidentiality of the accessed data  (cf. 
C.IV.3 for further details); 
• Where (regardless of such agreement) legitimate interests of th e data 
holder to safeguard the protection of trade secrets outweigh the interests 
underlying the access request (cf. C.IV.3 for further details);; 
• Where a user/third party has previously violated an existing 
confidentiality agreement (cf. C.IV.3 for further details); 
• Where the user’s / third party’s principal residence / registered place of 
business located outside the Union (cf. C.I.2); 
• Where a potential violation of Art. 101 TFEU could be at stake; 
• Where a violation of an embargo, sanction list, other applicable export 
control legislation or criminal law would be at stake. 
6. Identification of a party’s authority to act on behalf of the user 
Art. 5.1 provides that not only the users themselves, but also a party acting on 
behalf of a user may request the data holder to make available the data generated 
by the use of a product or related service to a third party. Whereas the user or 
the third party shall not be required to provide any information beyond what is 
necessary to verify the quality as user or as third party pursuant to Art. 5.1, it 
should also be addressed that the party (allege dly) acting on behalf of a user 
 3139 
 
provides information necessary to verify that such party has actually been 
authorized by the user to act on their behalf. 
V. Article 6 – Obligations of third parties receiving data at the request of the 
user 
In case the data recipient does not comply with the obligations laid down in Art. 
6, the data holder needs to have effective remedies. We have outlined our 
specific concerns and proposals relating to effective remedies in section C.VIII. 
One of our main concerns, however, is that the use of certain data to develop a 
competing product will be difficult to prove in practice. Hence, in case of an 
alleged violation we propose that the third party (user or data recipient) should 
bear the burden of proof that the restriction in Art. 6.2 lit.e has not been violated. 
VI. Article 8 – Conditions under which data holders make data available to 
data recipients 
The data holder should make data available to the data recipient under “ fair, 
reasonable and non-discriminatory terms”; further, the data holder should make 
the data available in a “ transparent manner ”. We would welcome further 
guidance on the interpretation of those terms in a Recital. 
Further, Art. 5.7 provides that any failure of the data holder and the third party 
to agree on terms for transmitting the data should not hinder the user to exercise 
his rights under GDPR, namely the right to data portability. We would welcome 
a clarification in Art. 8 stating that in case the data holder and the data recipient 
cannot agree on terms to make data available, the data holder shall not be forced 
to agree or to share data without an agreement. If the disagreement between the 
data holder and the data recipient should persist, the parties have the option to 
refer to a dispute settlement body (Art. 10) and the user would have the option 
to lodge a complaint according to Art. 32.  
Art. 8.3 provides that the data holder  should not discriminate between 
comparable categories of data recipients and obliges the data holder to 
demonstrate that there has been no discrimination. Firstly, we would welcome 
further guidance on what terms are considered discriminatory and note that even 
the Data Act itself introduces a certain discrimination between different 
categories of data recipients (enterprise vs. SME data recipient). Secondly, we 
do not see how the reversal of the burden of proof is justified in this case; it 
could make it t oo easy to approach data holders with (false) accusation of 
alleged discriminatory terms. Hence, we propose to amend the wording and 
clarify that it is the data recipient who bears the burden of proof. 
Art. 8.4 prevents the data holder from making data available to a data recipient 
under an exclusive license unless this is requested by a user. This is a very strong 
interference with the freedom of contract of the data holder. Also, it is unclear 
how an exclusive license grant at the request of the user should work in practice. 
For instance, it must remain possible for the data holder to use raw data for 
further processing in its product or related service (for example transfer of the 
 3239 
 
data to maintenance and repairs services) as well as for research and 
development purposes. In particular, it should be possible for the data holder to 
grant exclusive licenses for specific situations (for example for safety -related 
monitoring and optimization of the product and its components, for technical 
development and software updates). 
VII. Article 9 – Compensation for making data available 
Regarding the compensation concept envisaged under the Data Act we would 
like to express our concern that the Data Act does not seem to take the value of 
data sufficiently into consideration. Data has often been referred to as the “oil” 
of the 21 st century as it po wers innovation in technology and services of the 
underlying economic “machine”. While it is undoubtedly true that data can 
technically be reproduced with near to zero costs, it differs in a fundamental 
way from oil: Oil is a natural resource that is alrea dy available for further 
exploitation whereas data in itself does not merely “exist” but must first be 
created. In fact, considerable economic and technical effort may have to be 
mobilized to create the “resource” data in the first place. The value of data  is 
not limited to the costs incurred for making data available. Costs arising out of 
the transfer of data by way of data sharing (i.e. costs for making data available) 
may arise on top of the efforts to create the data in the first place. In addition, 
the data holder should have the possibility to include costs setting up the 
required technical infrastructure to enable data sharing. We would welcome it 
if the Commission could take all of these factors into account explicitly in the 
Data Act’s compensation concept.  Otherwise, any business built on the 
exploitation of data obtained from the use of a product would have to re -
consider the product pricing fundamentally and front -load the commercial 
remuneration for the envisaged commercial exploitation of data to the sale, rent 
or lease of the product to the user rather than leaving this revenue opportunity 
dormant until the product is actually used (and thus generates data which can 
then be exploited by the data holder). 
In this regard, we propose to clarify that the point of reference for compensation 
should generally be the value generated with the data and the focus should not 
be a cost-based approach only. This clarification in the direction of a fair and 
reasonable commercial consideration for making data available could be set out 
in Recital 46.  
Further, Art. 20.2 explicitly allows the data holder to request a reasonable 
margin in the context of sharing data with public sector bodies. The data holder 
should generally be entitled to claim a reasonable margin also from other third  
parties. Recital 46 states that parties can negotiate “ compensation if it is 
reasonable, taking into account factors such as volume, format, nature, […]”. 
We would propose to further clarify this Recital by adding th e wording 
“reasonable margin”.  
With regard to compensation limitations currently foreseen under the Data Act 
towards SME, VW Group would welcome further guidance as to how the 
Commission envisions to calculate direct costs related to making the data 
available and which are attributable to the individual request; a calculation 
 3339 
 
example would be helpful. If the data holder cannot even charge SMEs for 
infrastructure investments made to enable data sharing this seems highly 
disproportionate and excessive considering the Commission’s goal of protecting 
SMEs from economic burdens which would make it commercially too difficult 
for them to develop and run innovative business models. We would welcome it 
if the Commission could revisit the compensation concept in this  regard to 
ensure a fair balancing of interests. 
Recital 47, for instance, establishes the principle of marginal or incremental cost 
which - by definition - except for the very first request, would eliminate 
compensation for each and every setup cost for t he data holder to make data 
available. This cost standard is a regulatory remedy which is rarely and carefully 
used in cases of significant market dominance in regulated industries, and it 
seems disproportionate to apply it as a default rule under the Data  Act. Unless 
reasonable compensation of direct cost is chosen, we recommend to apply dis-
incentivizing cost standards  only to exceptional situations and tie it to further 
prerequisites and procedural rules (involving competent authorities). 
Another main area of concern relates to scenarios where the data holder makes 
data available to the user and the user subsequently shares that data with a third 
party. The data holder’s right to claim compensation from third parties could be 
circumvented in such constellations under the current draft of the Data Act. We 
strongly urge the Commission to revisit this concept and ensure the data holder’s 
right to claim compensation from third parties even where a user directly shares 
data with a third party. Otherwise, investment incentives for manufacturers and 
data holders for creating data generating products and making such data 
available will be undermined. We would propose to introduce an information 
obligation for the user to notify the data holder in case data is about to be shared 
with a third  party. Otherwise, the data holder would have no means to claim 
compensation.  
VIII. Article 11 – Technical protection measures and provisions on unauthorized 
use or disclosure of data 
Art 11.1 states that the data holder may apply appr opriate technical protection 
measures, including smart contracts, to prevent unauthorized access to the data 
and to ensure compliance with Art. 5, 6, 9 and 10, as well as with the agreed 
contractual terms for making data available. However, it remains uncl ear how 
the Commission sees the role of smart contracts under the Data Act in general, 
and what role in particular smart contracts may have with regard to ensuring 
compliance with Art.5, 6, 9 and 10 or contractual terms. We would welcome 
further guidance and clarification in a Recital in this respect, including technical 
specifications regarding the implementation and the electronic ledger. 
With regard to Art . 11.2 we are particularly concerned that the legal 
consequences foreseen for data recipients violating the Data Act or contractual 
terms agreed with the data holder seem rather mild and do not suffice to 
effectively deter noncompliant behavior. This is further aggravated by the fact 
that the data holder would have no contractual remedies against a third party 
 3439 
 
who has received the data directly from the user  as envisaged, e.g. under 
Recitals 5 and 28 (also see our concerns and proposals in section B.VI).  
We would propose to add at least the following additional legal consequences 
as part of Art. 11.2:  
• In case data was also shared with third parties the data recipient should 
explicitly be obliged to ensure that the data is also destroyed by those 
third parties in accordance with lit.a and that the production, offering 
etc. is stopped in accordance with lit.b. 
• The data recipient should be obliged to provide the data holder with 
evidence showing that he has effectively complied with the obligations 
set forth under lit.a and lit.b. 
In that context, we have strong concerns against Art . 11.3 lit.a which seems 
discriminatory and disproportionate to the detriment of the data holder. 
Therefore, we propose the deletion of the entire paragraph. In case of unlawful 
use of data by the data recipient it should be irrelevant how significant the harm 
caused to the data holder was; further, it seems unfair that despite a violation of 
the data recipient the data holder should have to bear the burden of proof that 
the harm caused was significant. Whether the harm is significant also seems 
rather arbitrary since it depends heavily on the point in time when the 
assessment is made. For the same reasons we have concerns against Art. 11.3 
lit.b. As a minimum, we see the need for clarification as to which circumstances 
would be considered “disproportionate” in the sense of Art. 11.3 lit.b. 
In the event of noncompliant behavior of the data recipient we further propose 
that the Data Act should explicitly grant the data holder the following rights:  
• Terminate any data sharing transactions with the data recipient 
concerned, including right to deny future data access requests for such 
data recipients. The same rights should apply for the data holder in case 
of risk of compromise of trade secrets; 
• Seek immediate relief by way of a preliminary injunction; 
• As the legal consequences foreseen in Art. 11.2 will not compensate for 
all of the damages incurred, the data holder should - in addition to claim 
damages - be entitled to claim any profits arising out of the noncompliant 
behavior from the data holder. 
IX. Article 15 - Exceptional need to use data 
Increasing access to data does not only create benefits for the private sector but 
it can also contribute to alleviate or mitigate public emergencies. We understand 
that with chapter 5 the Commission aims to formalize the business to 
government (“B2G”) data access. It is important to note that up until now B2G 
data sharing has been carried out on a voluntary basis. Voluntary data sharing 
needs to be taken more into account in the Data Act as it allows for quicker data 
access with less bureaucratic barriers. 
 3539 
 
In any event, the scope of public sector bodies’ access to data in the current draft 
is too broad and leaves room for much legal uncertainty. First, it needs to be 
clear that only public emergencies reaching a certain level of “criticality” should 
be in scope. Recital 57 already points in this direction by referring to “ major” 
natural disasters. However, it should be clarified that not any “ public health 
emergency or emergency resulting from environmental degradation” is in scope 
but rather only such emergencie s which reach a certain threshold of affected 
people or estimated damage.  
Second, mandatory B2G data sharing should be the ultima ratio. This approach 
is in line with Art. 15 lit.a and Art. 15 lit.b  Data Act which state that the data 
requested must be “ necessary” to prevent, respond or assist the recovery of a 
public emergency. Nevertheless, we would welcome a clarification in Recital 
56 that “necessary” should be understood in the way that the public sector body 
was unable to obtain the requested data by alternative means, including by 
purchasing it on the market. Hence, the public sector body should demonstrate 
that requesting the data in line with the  procedure laid down in chapter 5 is in 
fact the ultima ratio. 
Contrary to Art. 15 lit.a and Art. 15 lit.b, Art. 15 lit.c opens the door for public 
sector bodies to request access to data whenever it is “convenient”. Public sector 
bodies are allowed to request data if it is needed to fulfill a specific task in the 
”public interest”. However, the term “ public interest” is very broad and could 
also encompass building new speed control systems on the highways. This could 
hardly be considered an “ exceptional need” within the meaning of Art. 14.1. 
Therefore, the scope of the “public interest” needs to be narrowed down to tasks 
reaching a certain level of criticality, eg. of equal “impact” as a public 
emergency. 
Even if the data access request by public sector bo dies is further restrained by 
the requirements of Art.15 lit.c.1 and Art.15 lit.c.2, it still remains too broad. 
For instance, it is not clear how to determine whether data could have been 
obtained by the public sector body at “market rates” in situations where such a 
market price might not be easily identifiable or not exist. In a similar way the 
alternative requirement in Art.15 lit.c.2 is extremely vague. It is not clear how 
B2G data sharing according to chapter 5 could substantially reduce “ the 
administrative burden for the data holders ”. It is further not clear who would 
determine if this were in fact the case.  
X. Article 17 – Requests for data to be made available 
When requesting data, the public sector body must specify the deadline by 
which the data holder must comply with the request. The level of discretion that 
can be exercised by the public sector authority should be clarified in the 
respective Recital. For instance, the public sector body should take into account 
the nature of the data requested, t he amount and whether data needs to be 
anonymized. We would propose to specify that the deadline must be reasonable 
and would propose to adapt the wording of Art. 17.1 lit.e as follows: 
“[…] e) specify the reasonable deadline […]” 
 3639 
 
Further, it is not clear how a data holder would actually receive a request from 
a public sector body. According to Art. 17.2 lit.f a public sector body must make 
a data request publicly available online without undue delay. However, it must 
be clear that the data holder has  no obligation to regularly consult dedicated 
websites or similar resources to identify potential requests by public sector 
bodies. It also remains unclear how the data should be provided to the public 
sector body. It must be clear that the public sector body is not entitled to request 
the data in a specific format and that the data holder is not obliged to provide an 
API dedicated to the access for public sector bodies. Art. 20.2 allows the data 
holder to request compensation where necessary for the “ technical adaption”. 
Again, this should under no circumstances be understood as introducing any 
obligation for the data holder to undertake technical adaptations to facilitate the 
data sharing with the public sector body. 
According to Art. 17.1. lit.d, the requ esting public sector body must state the 
legal basis for requesting data. Regarding personal data being shared with users 
as per Art. 4 or third parties as per Art. 5, it is clearly set out in the Data Act that 
such personal data shall only be disclosed if there is a valid legal basis under the 
GDPR. This is not set out explicitly for personal data to be made available to 
public sector bodies. However, Recital 24 states that “ this Regulation [i.e. the 
Data Act] does not create a legal basis under the GDPR for the data holder to 
provide access to personal data or make it available to a third party”. It follows 
from this Recital, that also in the context of sharing personal data with public 
sector bodies, such data shall only be disclosed if there is a v alid legal basis 
under the GDPR.  As neither Art. 4, nor Art. 5 speak of “making data available”, 
but this is the notion used in Art. 14, it follows that also Art. 14 is not intended 
to create a legal basis as per Art. 6.1 lit. c GDPR. It is to be doubted anyways if 
Art. 14 would fulfill the requirements as per Art. 6.2 and 6.3 GDPR. 
Thus, we would propose to interpret the term “ legal basis” used in Art. 17.1. 
lit.d as referring to both, (i) the legal provisions entitling the public sector body 
to request t he respective (personal or non -personal) data and (ii), insofar as 
personal data is concerned, the legal basis governing the transfer of personal 
data to the public sector body as per Art. 6 GDPR. We further suggest clarifying 
this accordingly in a Recital. 
XI. Article 18 – Compliance with requests for data 
The data holder may decline the request for data by a public sector body if the 
data is “ unavailable”. However, it is not clear when data is considered to be 
“unavailable”. We would propose to interpret this term in line with the 
conditions laid down in section B.I.1.d). Only data where the access is relevant 
and appropriate should be considered “available”. Further, we propose to use 
the term inaccessible instead of unavailable in Art.18.2 lit.a.  
Art 18.5 provides that the data holder shall take reasonable efforts to 
pseudonymize personal data, insofar as the request can be fulfill ed with 
pseudonymized data. It follows from Art 5.1 lit. c GDPR that if pseudonymized 
data is sufficient for a specific data processing or a specific data transfer, then 
respective personal data may only be processed in pseudonymized form. So, if 
 3739 
 
a request can be fulfilled with pseudonymized data, the data holder is prohibited 
as per the GDPR to make the data available to the public sector body in its full 
personalized form. In this respect, the time periods set out in Art. 18.2 might in 
practice be too sho rt for the data holder to effect ively pseudonymize the data. 
We propose to interpret the term “take reasonable efforts” to take into account 
that in situations in which a request can be fulfil led with pseudonymized data 
respective personal data must in any  case be pseudonymized before it is made 
available to the public sector body and if this cannot be done within the timelines 
set out in Art. 18 that this does not constitute a violation of the data holder’s 
obligations set out in chapter V to make the requested data available to the 
requesting public sector body. 
XII. Article 20 – Compensation in case of exceptional need 
Art. 20.1 excludes compensation for data made available to a public sector body 
to respond to public emergencies. A public emergency is a special situation for 
everybody, including the data holder. Excluding compensation in such a 
situation by default seems disproportionate as the data holder might as well be 
affected by the emergency. For instance, a public emergency might last several 
years (such as the Covid -19 crisis) and might lead to significant costs for the 
data holder. Furthermore, if the data holder can’t expect any compensation, not 
even the technical and organizational costs, he would have no incentive to make 
an effort in complying with the request. In situations of public emergencies this 
would be counterproductive as the willingness to help, and a solid cooperative 
behavior will most likely lead to better results. We would propose a compromise 
that in cases of public emergencies pursuant to Art. 15 lit.a the data holder 
should receive compensation only for the technical and organizational costs but 
is not entitled to request an additional margin. 
XIII. Article 21 - Contribution of research organizations or statistical bodies in 
the context of exceptional needs 
We support the Commission’s intention to foster scientific research and 
analytics by sharing data made available pursuant to chapter 5 with research 
organizations. However, since the data holder does not receive any 
compensation fr om the research organizations and the data made available 
might be commercially valuable or sensitive, the data holder should have a right 
to refuse such data sharing. If a public sector body intends to share data with a 
research organization, it must firs t notify the data holder. The data holder can 
object to the data sharing within a reasonable time by providing a reasonable 
justification (for example the protection of trade-secrets). 
Furthermore, research organizations should also implement technical and  
organizational measures to ensure the adequate protection and integrity of the 
data received. The data holder should be entitled to request proof of such 
measures prior to agreeing to the data sharing. 
Lastly, Art. 19.1 lit.c should also be applicable to research organizations. In the 
same way as public sector bodies, research organizations should also be obliged

to destroy data as soon as it is no longer necessary for the stated purpose and 
inform the data holder that the data has been destroyed. 
XIV. Article 30 – Essential requirements regarding smart contracts for data 
sharing 
In its Art. 30 the Data Act addresses basic requirements f or smart contracts as 
well as on their possible standardization and certification but leaves the actual 
scope of application of smart contracts largely open. With a view to the vast 
number of envisaged requests of users and data recipients for the making 
available of data, we recommend that the power of smart contracts also be 
leveraged to increase and provide an efficient path to legal certainty for all 
involved actors on compliance of arrangements between users, data holders and 
data recipients. This coul d apply in particular to the automated checking of 
clauses for compliance with Art. 8 (FRAND), Art 13. (fair) or the compensation 
provisions in Art. 9 of the Data Act. Technically, this could be effectuated by 
AI-based, semantic compares of contractual arrangements against model 
clauses and evolving decision practice. Enhancing legal certainty via a partly 
automated process would also accelerate the creation of economic value of data 
at scale and thus directly support the goals of the Data Act. 
Moving in this direction would, in our view, command certain prerequisites 
such as (i) authorized and certified algorithms for AI-based, semantic compares 
to provide legal certainty for this automated compliance check, (ii) a rebuttable 
presumption of compliance with the Data Act once such an automated 
conformity check has been passed; (iii) a strict time -barring and reversal of 
burden of proof for alleged non -compliance and (iv) a one -stop shop principle 
and competent authority for ruling on compliance with the Data Act. 
XV. Article 31 – Competent Authorities 
Art. 31 of the Data Act confers to Member States the possibility to designate 
one or more competent authorities as responsible for the enforcement and 
application of the Data Act. Further, Art. 31.2 states that the authorities 
responsible for monitoring the application of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 shall 
be responsible for monitoring the application of the Data Act with respect to the 
protection of personal data. While we fully agree that matters of protection of 
personal data should be enforced by the competent data protection authorities, 
we are concerned that a split of responsibilities between several authorities 
would lead to additional administrative burdens.  Separation of responsibilities 
between different authorities would create legal uncertainty and operational 
friction.  Thus, we would welcome the concentration of responsibilities in the 
hand of one authority acting as a “one stop shop”, which we believe would lead 
to more efficient monitoring.  
We would suggest a clear separation between the scope of competences of the 
relevant data protection authorities and the authorities responsible for 
monitoring the Data Act. Data Protection authorities should only be responsible 
for monitoring matters related to the protection of personal data, but they should 
not be competent for monitoring the general compliance with obligations laid 
 3939 
 
down in the Data Act (in particular chapters II, III and IV). We would invite the 
Commission to clarify this understanding. Since the referred chapters contain 
provisions related to contract and competition law (for example assessing unfair 
contractual terms) we do not see these topics within the specific area of expertise 
of data protection authorities and would rather see these questions within the 
competence of authorities who already have exposure and know-how in dealing 
with such questions (e.g. competition authorities). In addition, we would 
welcome further clarification as to the local competence of authorities in cross-
border cases (also cf. section C.I.2). 
XVI. Article 42 – Entry into force and application 
The current draft of the Data Act stipulates that the Data Act should apply from 
12 months after the date of entry into force of the Regulation (“Effective Date”). 
However, given the extensive requirements laid down in the Data Act we 
believe that the suggested lead -time is too short, especially for the automotive 
industry with long development and production times to adapt adequately to 
such large-scale regulation. Vehicle manufacturers will have to implement new 
compliance procedures, re -assess business models, possibly adapt sales 
channels and undertake strategic re -orientation of operations. The lead -time 
given for the implementation of the GDPR, for instance, was 24 months. GDPR 
presented many challenges for businesses across all sectors, and it is to be 
expected that a far-reaching regulation as the Data Act will require unparalleled 
efforts from all stakeholders. Thus, we propose a lead-time of at least 36 months. 
Further, we would welcome a clarification that the Data Act will only apply to 
(i) products placed on the market, and (ii) data sharing contracts concluded on 
or after the Effective Date of the regulation. Vehicles and contracts which have 
been placed on the market or concluded prior to the Effective Date of the Data 
Act should generally be out of scope. 
Further, all data generated in the development and/or production phase of a 
product shall be out of scope. Only data which is produced after the product has 
been placed on the market shall be subject to the Data Act  (cf. section C.I.1 
above).

CropLife Europe feedback on the Data Act proposal 
 
 
CropLife Europe members are committed to delivering truly sustainable agriculture. Innovative farming 
tools, used in combination with good agronomic practices, are needed to meet ever-evolving consumer 
demands for food that is safe, diverse and affordable. 
We believe agriculture data plays a major role in evolving our agricultural system and improving its 
environmental impact. Recent global crises have taught the world the importance of food security – this 
is a fundamental role of farmers and the agricultural value-chain. Digitalization in agriculture, powered by 
data, enhances the farmers’ tool box, enables higher precision in the management of inputs and it is 
opening the door for innovative business models.  
Clarity and value generation are key for the digital economy – no different for the development of the 
digital agriculture market. The members of CropLife Europe believe that it is essential that farmers are at 
the heart of the decision making of how their data is used and how it is shared. Looking to the future we 
believe it is essential to continue to build trust between technology providers and farmers in regard to the 
use of agriculture data – facilitating data sharing in a transparent and reliable manner is key to this.  
The draft Data Act intends to put users of technology in greater control of who their data is/can be shared 
with, while protecting trade secrets and accounting for the cost of investments by early movers. We 
welcome the objectives of the Data Act and believe this is a fundamental aspect for the long term 
success of digital and precision agriculture in Europe. There are however still to be resolved 
challenges when it comes to agriculture data, such as a clear differentiation between personal and non-
personal data, as well establishing the needed connectivity in rural areas of Europe so that the digital 
transformation of agriculture can become a reality for every farmer.  
We support the deployment of digital tools to assist the European Commission’s ambitions of a digital 
and green recovery. In this context, the members of CropLife Europe have committed to significantly 
invest in precision and digital technologies in order to support farmers in the more targeted use of 
agriculture inputs such as crop protection products and seeds. We therefore greatly appreciate the 
opportunity to participate in the public consultation on the Data Act and look forward to further discussing 
with the EU Institutions and relevant stakeholders. 
Key Messages  
1. The Data Act and future sectoral legislation should remain consistent with relevant sector-
specific initiatives, such as the Code of Conduct on agricultural data sharing by contractual 
agreement 
2. Businesses struggle to differentiate between personal and non-personal farming data. A clear 
differentiation, including via the creation of a new category of “Farm Data”, would eliminate 
the existing ambiguity 
3. The new rules should only apply to raw data. Processed data, software, output data and 
information derived or inferred from raw data should remain out of scope 
 
4. Any trade secrets or intellectual property rights should be protected in handling the data

1. General Comments 
• The Data Act provisions should remain consistent with the C ode of conduct on 
Agricultural data Sharing 
 
Across the industry, significant steps have been taken to agree upon and give clarity to how farm 
data should be used. The EU Code of C onduct on Agricultural Data Sharing by Contract 
Agreement1 (“Code of Conduct”) was agreed upon within the agriculture industry -including the 
farmers association- to provide support and guidance on use and sharing of agricultural data. The 
core principle underpinning this initiative is that farmers need to have control over who uses the 
data that is produced on their farms and for what purposes , as well as to make sure that data 
benefits them and their businesses.  The Code of Conduct offers important insight into access, 
control and portability of agriculture data, as well as  protection of trade secrets and  intellectual 
property rights. 
 
CropLife Europe welcomes the Code of Conduct and its members support its principles. We 
therefore commend the intention to build the Data Act and future sectoral legislation on relevant 
developments in specific sectors, such as the Code of Conduct.2   
 
• A clear differentiation between personal and non-personal agriculture data is needed  
 
The draft Data Act creates a dilemma in view of personal data rules: a company which wrongly 
classifies non-personal data as personal and therefore refuses the sharing of data as required by 
the Data Act, would be in breach of the Data Act.  
 
On the other hand, if a company wrongly classifies personal data as non-personal data and 
therefore shares data without a legal basis pursuant to the GDPR, it violates the EU data privacy 
rules. Accordingly, the Data Act forces every company to assess each piece of information and 
decide whether it is personal data or not. In particular in digital agriculture such an exercise would 
be difficult, because there are many grey areas. 
 
A solution could be the introduction of a new data category “Farm Data”. Data belonging to this 
category could be defined in the Data Act or future sectoral legislation as non-personal data. For 
example, data which are generated by tractors, automatic irrigation systems and other connected 
equipment as well as soil characteristics, land utilisation and other location data should explicitly 
qualify as non-personal data even if they are remotely attributable to an individual thereby being 
also arguably considered as personal data. 
 
Such a new category of data would eliminate the existing ambiguity in categorizing Digital 
Farming data and would bring legal certainty for all actors. 
2. Specific Comments 
• The scope of the Data Act should remain limited to raw data  
 
We fully agree with the principle that the rights regarding data directly collected and generated 
by IoT equipment should be accessed and used extensively by users. However, the definition of 
data provided by the draft regulation3 is too broad.

It should be very clear in the legal text that only the so-called raw data collected and/or generated 
by users is in the scope of the Data Act (i.e. data that is collected by IoT products/related services 
without editing or any other form of processing).  
 
An example:  
 
▪ Company A sells a  smart sprayer, which uses image recognition technology to identify  
plant diseases. The device processes in real-time images and other available data  (like 
humidity, wind speed and direction, as well as temperature) in agronomic models and 
applies the right product (e.g. a fungicide) at the right time and rate in the respective zones 
of the field. 
 
▪ Crops images are converted into a machine-readable data format so that the processing 
software, based on the respective algorithms, generates output data, i.e. where to spray, 
which products and at which rates. 
 
▪ The software, the processed data and the output data are trade secrets of company A. If 
these data were shared further, users, competitors and other third parties could identify 
the models and algorithms of company A through reverse engineering and duplicate these 
for their own benefit. Furthermore, other processed data such as information derived or 
inferred from the raw data, e.g. in aggregated or anonymized form, should be maintained 
out of the scope.  
 
The clarification that aggregated and anonymized data do not fall within the scope of the Data 
Act is even more important considering the applicability of the act to non-personal data. Privacy 
laws allow the clear distinction between personal data (to which they apply ) and non-personal 
data (including, among other things , aggregated and anonymous data , to which they do not 
apply). T he Data Act , in contrast,  might be interpreted as also governing the processing of 
aggregated/anonymized data if such data originate from legal persons owning, renting or leasing 
a product/receiving a service thereby also qualifying as a “user”.  This can, however, hardly be 
the intention of the Data Act considering, in particular, that, as to such data types , there is no 
identifiable user that could request data holders to grant access to the respective data or make 
them available to third parties.  
 
A more precise definition of Data would  therefore better reflect the spirit and objective of the 
regulation, in line with its Recital 14 which clearly states that “data represent the digitalisation of 
user actions and events and should accordingly be accessible to the user, while information 
derived or inferred from this data, where lawfully held, should not be considered within 
scope of this Regulation.” The Code of Conduct provides several examples of raw data referring 
to farms and farm operation, including agronomic data (e.g. soil data, input data) and compliance 
data (data required for control and enforcement in relation to competent authorities).  
 
The definition of related services is another concept that seems to give rise to misunderstandings 
about the scope of the draft regulation. 4  As the focal point of the Data Act is data 
collected/generated by Internet of Things equipment, it would be preferable to specify that related 
services are services which may generate data themselves or that are incorporated in or 
interconnected with a product in such a way that the absence of the service would prevent the 
product from performing the core function of collecting and/or generating data . Without 
such a clarification, complementary services which do not concern data collection/generation or 
transmission would eventually be included into the scope of the new rules.

in the Code of Conduct , users/data originators need to have the right to reuse and freely move 
the data they generate across different services and platforms. This right would complement the 
data portability provisions provided under the GDPR, which only apply to personal data and 
natural persons. As stated above, differentiating between personal and non-personal data in the

The Data Act allows the data holder to set reasonable compensation to be met by third parties

• Adequate protection of t rade secrets , confidential business information (CBI)  and 
Intellectual Property Rights (IPRs) is fundamental  
  
The protection of trade secrets, CBI and IPRs is critical to fostering innovation and protect 
innovative business models. As stated in Recital 28 of the Data Act, “any trade secrets or 
intellectual property rights should be respected in handling the data.”   
 
The Trade Secrets Directive 2016/943 is a  suitable tool to protect undisclosed business  
information against unlawful acquisition and disclosure.  We fully support the exclusion of trade 
secrets from the general obligation to share data  under the Data Act 5 and the possibility to 
preserve confidentiality of the shared data, in particular with respect to third parties, via private 
confidentiality agreements.6  
 
As an example for the crop protection/agriculture sector, a non -disclosure agreement may be 
applied to preserve the confidentiality of  information collected by a user who is for instance  
providing services to a company through the use of IoT products (e.g. crop protection field trials 
that are subcontracted to a farmer who collects relevant R&D information – which certainly 
qualifies as trade secret- through an IoT device. In this case , the farmer would not use the IoT 
device to carry out ordinary farm operations but to provide a separate service which entails 
collection of R&D data useful to turn an invention into a marketable product).  
 
In this context we consider that the user’s right to disclose trade secrets of the data holder on the 
sole condition that those are necessary to fulfil the purpose agreed between the user and a third 
party is disproportionate .7 This possibility should be subject to the consent of trade secrets 
owners. In any case, we believe that a confidentiality agreement adopted pursuant to Article 4(3) 
of the Data Act would be able to derogate from this provision and therefore keep the information 
confidential.

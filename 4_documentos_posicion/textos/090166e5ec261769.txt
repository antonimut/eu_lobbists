Harmonised rules on fair access to and use of data (Data Act) 
Common position of business user associations 
Introduction 
Beltug, Cigref, CIO Platform Nederland and Voice-e.v. jointly support the Commission’s ambition to ensure 
fairness in how the value of data is allocated among actors who are active on different levels of the data 
value chain. Data is not only a core component of the digital economy, but also a strategic asset of business 
users, in all sectors. All organisations within our associations have many years of experience with  digital 
transformation and the processing of data. Therefore, our members have high expectations regarding the 
Data Act proposal. Indeed, clear rules across the single market are essential to foster innovation, growth, 
and trust. In that purpose, our associations have also endorsed the work led by Cigref with its members for 
three years on trusted cloud. Trust is the cornerstone to free data circulation, and it is based on 3 pillars: 
cybersecurity, mastered dependency from IT vendors, protection against third-States legislations. Data Act 
must deploy the appropriate legal framework to guarantee these pillars. 
The Data Act is largely welcomed by our four associations and their professional IT user organisations, with 
a very positive stance on chapter VI article 23 (Switching between data processing services), chapter VII 
article 27 (International contexts non -personal data safeguards) and chapter VIII article 28 and  30 
(Interoperability). The provisions are well in line  with calls made by our associations for years. It will be 
critical to ensure that the unfair and prejudicial contractual, technical and commercial practices of supplier 
relations are well encompassed by the legal formulations of the text, in coherence with the DMA.  
Nonetheless, several provisions would require clarifications, especially considering that many businesses 
are both users of digital solutions and data producers. Balancing trade secrets or confidentiality to protect 
companies' assets with open access and fostering innovation is a complex mechanism. In this document 
our associations highlight some of the questions that were raised by their members during a dedicated 
discussion on the Data Act. We understand that some of our concerns could be lifted thanks to an open 
dialogue with the European institutions and we are open for further discussion.

1) Our understanding of the Data Act and preliminary open questions 
Our associations support the overall principle of the Data Act that business users and consumers should be 
able to access, manage and share the data they contribute to creating when using a connected device or a 
service such as virtual assistants. 
We understand that the Data Act aims at providing IoT device users more control over the data they 
generate and its use. Yet, the reality is often complex  and we encourage the consideration of multiple 
sectoral use cases, some brief examples of which are mentioned in the annexes. Besides, the scope of right 
access to data under the Data Act could be broadened to data beyond data of IoT -products and related 
services. 
 
1.1) Questions and comments with regards to Data 
• The definition of data is very broad: the reference to "non-personal" data is not sufficiently precise, 
nor is the scope of the data concerned (in particular, what data the user or the third party of his 
choice will have access to: raw, processed, etc.). The notion of "commercially sensitive data" also 
needs to be precisely defined. 
The definition of data holder is also very vague and needs to be clarified. In addition, it may diverge 
from the one retained in the Data Governance Act. 
• Article 3 of Chapter 2 on the obligation to make accessible data generated by the use of related 
products or services between companies also seems too broad. The contours and limits of "data 
generated by the use of linked products or services" essentially need to be clarified (possible risk 
due to the costs incurred to meet this new requirement). 
• With regard to the sharing of data with the user, and to a third party (Chapter II), once again, the 
provisions seem to be too broad and insufficiently protective,  particularly in the face of industrial 
espionage practices and data confidentiality. 
1.2) Questions and comments with regards to IoT 
• What would be the obligations for IoT object assemblers? Clarification would be appreciated for 
companies that are at the same time, IoT product manufacturers and suppliers of related services. 
In addition, business users should be granted full access to their data (i.e. the data owned by them 
or generated by devices or related services used by them) stored in the cloud of a software vendor 
in order to comply with their obligation towards users. 
• Moreover, several companies work as data integrator. Who holds the data in the context of an 
ecosystem of connected objects? 
The Data Act imposes requirements on the design and use of IoT products. According to the provisions, the 
data holders should create by default an interface where users can easily access and manage their data at 
no extra cost.  
• What will be the perimeter of these obligations?

• Will the Data Act apply retroactively to non-compliant sensors because they were designed before 
the entry into force of the text? Indeed, a high num ber of IoT devices are already in use in 
companies, without such interface. The cost of change for these can be very high. A clarification on 
the concrete implementation of the text is required.  
The Data Act describes the o bligations to make data generated by the use of products or related services 
accessible. Users can demand access to information that would fall under the scope of the Trade Secrets 
Directive. Despite the provisions, it is unclear how effectively any trade secrets will be protected in practice. 
The Data Act should protect European businesses from the predatory approach of non -EU actors . We 
believe that the data sharing should be an obligation only if all the confidentiality rules are truly 
respected. Companies should be able to determine what is or is not a trade secret. We regret that the Data 
Act relies excessively on the good faith of third parties.   
We understand that the Data Act will interact with several EU laws, from the GDPR to the database 
directive, or the Data Governance Act and the Digital Markets Act. It is sometimes difficult to understand 
how each legislative framework will interact with the others. This may create to legal risk and inconsistency 
for companies. 
Our associations welcome the provision that prohibits the transfer of data to any undertaking designated 
as a gatekeeper under the Digital Markets Act.  It is important that the sharing prohibitions apply to all 
group companies of such undertakings. It will be critical to ensure the co mpliance with the rules and to 
ensure that gatekeepers will take the necessary measures to comply with the rules.  
The rules would be directly applicable to all sectors and across the EU as minimum standards. Yet, as future 
revisions of sectoral regulations may go beyond these rules , our associations would like to highlight the 
importance of coherence and legal certainly for businesses to operate and invest.   
Finally, we would like to raise our concerns about the enf orcement of the regulation. Indeed, national 
competent authority will be responsible for applying and enforcing the regulation. This would generate 
discrepancies and thus uncertainties in the application of the text. This could diminish the internal market 
and create unfair competition for companies according to their localisation. This is especially true when it 
comes to penalties as they will be decided at national level. We call the Commission to ensure the maximum 
coherence among the Member States with clear guidelines. 
 
2) Switching between data processing services, a long-awaited ambition 
Our associations fully support the provision s by which data processing service providers (cloud and edge 
services providers) will be obliged to facilit ate user switching between data processing services. The 
definition of data processing services is broad enough to include a wide spectrum of cloud and edge 
services. The Data Act should also facilitate ‘inter cloud data transfer’ of (partial) data sets between data 
processing services  during normal operations . The EC proposal for the Data Act only includes the full 
switching between data processing services of complete data sets, for instance at the end of a contractual 
relationship.

We support the ambition to remove contractual, economic, and technical barriers to switching of data 
processing services, as well as the obligations set in article 24 . Provisions on functional equivalence when 
switching to a new I T environment are important as well as the proposed timeline to eliminate charges 
related to switching three years after the Data Act comes into force (article 25). It will be critical to ensure 
that the providers will comply with the rules by imposing high penalties. In addition, our user 
organisations believe that the phase-out period for switching fees should be shortened. 
 
3) Unfair contractual terms, a reality also for large businesses 
The Data Act will prevent the exploitation of contractual unfairness that hinder fair access to and fair use 
of data by micro, small or medium -sized enterprises. Contractual terms must be automatically deemed 
unfair when unilaterally imposed. Our associations support the vision that the burden of proof is reversed 
to the data holder to show before a court or conflict resolution body that the terms are reasonable and 
non-discriminatory. 
Yet, our associations would like to highlight that, large businesses are also affected by unfair contractual 
terms regarding digital services . Provisions developed in article 13 could be rightly extended to all 
companies. Even large companies are suffering from margins of interpretation of the contract on which 
suppliers rely.  
In this perspective, our associations welcome the creation of the  expert group on Business -to-Business 
(B2B) data sharing and cloud computing contracts . This group will have a positive rol e by elaborating a 
practical tool which  could shape how data is shared between businesses in Europe and boost fair 
contractual conditions. Standard contractual clauses are provided with instructions for use. We believe that 
a similar approach could be adopted here. The idea would be to specify conditions within contractual 
documents with rules of interpretation that support the spirit of the c ontract. This would prevent the 
capacity of cloud providers to circumvent contracts by imposing specific conditions which take precedence 
over the general clauses.  
It is important for the data economy , to ensure fairness of the standards terms and conditions for all 
businesses, regardless of their size.  The definition of size is very relative in the context. In comparison with 
a lot of cloud providers, a company with e.g. 1.000 employees – considered to be large in the EU definitions 
– is very small, and has no or very limited power. In the cloud context the unbalanced relations between 
cloud providers and their business customers are certainly not limited to micro, small and medium -sized 
businesses. 
 
4) A necessary complement to the GDPR on data portability 
Our associations share the objective of the Data Act to unleash the untapped potential of vast amounts of 
industrial data that are currently not being used.  We believe that t he Data Act will provide an additional 
framework on data portability, while the EU General Data Protection Regulation remains the core text for

personal data. Indeed, access to data is a key source of profit and of market power. Therefore, it should be 
carefully framed. 
The recitals make clear that the Data Act should not be applied and interpreted in any way that would 
diminish the protection of data provided under the GDPR . If the principle is right, the legal compliance 
with data-sharing obligations and data protection rules might prove challenging for business end-users. 
Indeed, the line between personal and non -personal data is not always clearly defined , and personal and 
non-personal data use is intertwingled in the applications.  
The scope of the right to data portability is broadened and includes any data generated by the use of a 
product or related service regardless of the nature or origin of the data (personal or non-personal, passively 
or actively provided) or the legal basis for its acquisition under the GDPR. Although the principle is positive, 
the concrete implementation requires a clearer vision.  In particular, the Data Act should more clearly 
explain the terms of the right to transfer "non-personal data". This is especially relevant as many forms of 
data could or may be regarded as personal data if it is relatable to a person in any way. For instance the 
person that has agreed to the contract needed for the data transfer or that has started the data being 
collected by using the device or service generating the data. When does such data become ‘non-personal’? 
Guidance is needed.  
 
5) Clarity on the scope of obligations on data holders, a requisite for adhesion and concrete 
implementation  
It clearly appears that the Commission's ambition in the Data Act is not to deal with data ownership. 
Nevertheless, we observe that companies still find it difficult to distinguish between "owner" and 
"holder". The confusion remains  strong and the debate vivi d. The fear of companies about their 
responsibilities and obligations must be lifted to ensure full support to the regulation. Trust, understanding 
and consensus in the definition are key to foster the data economy. According to our members, the notions 
of data holders versus data owners  would deserve validated and contextualized definitions through 
representative "use cases" and a glossary for this purpose. 
The Data Act aims at ensuring that users of a product or related service in the Union can access, in a timely 
manner, the data generated by the use of that product or related service  and that those users can make 
use of that data, including sharing it with third parties of their choice. The introduction of the "FRAND" 
standard, mean ing data hol ders are obliged to make data available under  fair, reasonable and non -
discriminatory conditions is positive, but requires clarifications. Indeed, the question as to what constitutes 
FRAND terms is complex and has led to various litigations.  
Our associations support th e vision on Business -to-Consumer and Business -to-Business data sharing 
obligations. Yet, a careful risk analysis is needed to ensure that businesses will fully benefit from these 
provisions, as data is a key corporate asset . For instance, we understand that the access to data from 
connected devices by the owners and users of the devices  will include permitting the sharing of that data

to other services, including analytics. This should be handled with great care as we potentially speak about 
critical/sensitive business information. Moreover, the practical implications are likely to be burdensome. 
Where a data holder is obliged to make data available to a data recipient, the regulation sets out the 
principle of a reasonable compensation for making the data available. The regulation would have greater 
effect if it defines the perimeters to calculate the compensation, allowing a harmonized vision throughout 
Europe. Clarification of the interpretation of "reasonable compensation" is needed. In addition, the cost of 
making data available must take into account the entire value chain of production costs. The "reasonable" 
margin must also be specified in a harmonized manner at the European level, at the risk of seeing significant 
differences in remuneration for data exchange between member states, which would run counter to fair 
competition. Likewise, the ambition to make available data, where applicable, continuously and in real -
time, should be better defined, again for the sake of common vision. The regulation needs to be more 
specific regarding the question of “undue delay” in order to create rights and obligations that are 
understood by all in the same way. This effort in terms of definition and clarification is needed to prevent 
interpretations that would end in disputes in courts. 
Finally, it is unclear to understand how the Data Act effectively protects the user that generates data from 
commercialization of their data . The Data Act needs also to protect businesses  in Europe  from the 
unwished monetization of the data they generated. The users might decide to share that data with a third 
party, although the data holder might at the same time protect trade secrets and other confidential 
information. A provision stating that the user can use the section on abusive clauses to oppose the use and 
monetization of the data generated , would increase the legal certainty of the regulation.  Indeed, we 
understand that despite safeguards, an authorized third party might be a direc t competitor of the data 
holder. While these organizations are forbidden from using the obtained data to develop a directly 
competing product, they will be able to use it to create an alternative service to the one of the data holders. 
The boundary seems very tight to apprehend and could end in litigation. Provisions that prohibit the use of 
data to gain insights about the economic situation of users or other similar business sensitive information 
must be strengthened. 
Related to this issue, our members would flag that the third parties may be establis hed outside the EU. 
Although the Data Act might prohibit certain uses of the data, it is unlikely that the EU or (EU -companies) 
can enforce their rights against companies from such third parties. Thus, business users need to be provided 
with adequate protection against misuse of (their) data by third parties situated outside of the EU as 
enforcement of the Data Act outside the EU will be limited. 
  
6) Interoperability standards, a question of credibility 
Our associations support the development of interoperability standards to enable the re-use of data. It is 
critical to address the lack of harmonised standards by using minimum essential requirements and provides 
for further legislation to implement common specifications.

7) Clear rules for international data transfers  
Our associations welcome the provisions in chapter VII and the clarification that data processing services, 
namely cloud service providers, will have to take all reasonable steps to prevent government access to or 
transfer non -personal data that would breach EU or national law. It is important to  define safeguards 
against unlawful data  transfer, especially when this is done  without notification by cloud service 
providers.  
We support the obligations (technical, legal, and organisational measures to prevent the data transfer) 
described in the Data Act, but the guarantees currently proposed do not go far enough to ensure effective 
protection of non -European laws. The question of enforcement will  also be critical. It was important to 
clarify that not processing personal data cannot be a way to escape the EU rules on international data 
transfers. In this perspective, we regret that unlike the DMA or the GDPR, sanctions for breach of the rules 
prescribed by the Data Act fall within the national sphere.  We recommend the reading of Cigref’s Trusted 
Cloud Reference Document (public document) which gathers the needs of companies to reach confidence 
in cloud and proposes criteria to match these requirements. 
 
We are the Belgian, Dutch, French and German CIO -associations; the communities of Chief Information Officers (CIO’s) and other 
senior leaders that are responsible for digital technologies and digital transformations within private or public organisations. All our 
members are business users of digital technologies; they acquire, implement and use digital technologies in practise. We do n ot 
represent ICT providers and consultants.  
 
Business users of digital technology are a key link in the digital transfo rmation of society and economy: they rely on the digital 
technologies they use, either hardware or software, and need these to be secure and safe to use and compliant with EU laws an d 
regulations. Business users also play an influential role in keeping digital technologies used in their organizations and across Europe 
secure and compliant with European standards and values through their combined purchasing power. To reap the benefits of digital 
technologies in Europe, the position of business users - companies and government organisations - of digital technology must be 
taken into account.

As mentioned in the introduction, “Balancing trade secrets or confidentiality to protect companies' assets 
with open access and fostering innovation is a complex mechanism”.     
Some examples of Use cases and questions  
1. Smart electric meter data  
With large investments, electricity companies install digital smart electric meters in each home.

Knowing that today the “smart grid” data are certainly a competitive advantage -  and this is exactly why the 
electricity companies make the large investment s in the installation of the smart electric meters  - should 
these companies be forced to disclose all the gathered dat a? And if so, what would be reasonable 
compensation for such large investments? 
2. Data from sensors in cars  
Car manufacturers have developed clever systems to analyse and react to data from sensors installed in each 
car. The objectives have evolved over time from planning the engine maintenance to helping the driver and 
nearly self-driving cars.  
Knowing that today the self-driving functionalities are certainly a competitive advantage on the car market, 
should the car manufacturers be forced to disclose t he data flows captured by the sensors in the cars to 
generate the self-driving functionalities? Or should only specific kinds of information be openly shared (for 
instance on road conditions, or environmental impact)? Several industrial business and use cases should be 
deepen in order to propose adapted rules. 
3. Data integrated in building management  
A company specialised in smart building management installs sensors to measure permanently the 
temperature and humidity all over a building. These data feed an automated air conditioning system to 
produce a clever air flow in the building taking into account many parameters, for example the position of 
the sun.   
As a user of the sensors, should the company specialised in smart building management receive free  of 
charge the data generated by the sensors?  
Should the company specialised in smart building management, considered as a Data Holder of the 
integrated temperature and humidity information, be forced to give the data to the owner of the building or 
eventually to a third party? Can individual workers in the building also demand to get the data? 
4. Harvest yield data  
A producer of combine harvesters has equipped his machines to measure the yield of the work done. Today 
a farmer interested in these data must pay to receive them.  The producer of the machines receives 
automatically all the data from all the machines at work. With these numbers compared with previous years 
he could try to foresee the volume of the harvest and, perhaps also the price on the market.  
As a user, should the farmer receive for free the yield data for his farm?  
Should he also get compensation for providing data that the producer of the harvester can then put to use? 
Should the producer of the machines be forced to provide the harvest yield data to third parties?  
5. Research data, patents

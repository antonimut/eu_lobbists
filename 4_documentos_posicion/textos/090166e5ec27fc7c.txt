HERE comments to the European Commission’s proposal for a Data Act 
 
About HERE Technologies 
HERE Technologies is the leading global provider of location data and services, headquartered in the 
European Union. From high -definition maps for automated driving, to our open B2B Platform, 
designed to enable data exchange and foster innovation, HERE is a fully data -driven company. Our 
innovations have led the development and deployment of advanced connected and automated 
vehicles solutions. Today, 100 million vehicles are equipped with HERE systems (4 out of 5 vehicles on 
European and North American road s). With HERE, journeys become faster, more efficient and safer; 
fleets optimize deliveries; supply chains become more predictable; and services become location 
intelligent. Our vision is to create an autonomous world for everyone, by facilitating the exch ange of 
data as a critical prerequisite for innovation, future business models, societal well-being and economic 
growth around the world.  
Introduction 
HERE Technologies appreciates the opportunity to provide feedback on the proposed EU Data Act. 
Enhancing data availability, access and re-use are key prerequisites to develop a fully-fledged EU Data 
Economy and pave the way for a true EU Digital Sovereignty, that both EU Member States and the 
European Commission are aiming for.  
However, we do believe that the text, in its current form, could lead to a high level of legal 
uncertainty, which we should avoid at all costs if we want to preserve business predictability and 
innovation in Europe. On top of the questions related to a rather unclear scope and definitions, the 
challenges posed by including both personal and non-personal data in a same regulation are deemed 
critical.  
 
1. Scope & definitions 
 
a. Scope of related services & products 
From the point of view of a third-party automotive supplier and digital map provider, a key question 
remains around the notion of “related services” which are in scope of this draft regulation. As we 
understand that a “related service” actually enhances th e functionality of the product at sale, we 
would conclude that embedded navigation services may fall into the scope of “related services”. At 
the same time, Recital 15 of the proposal introduces exemptions, i.e products which are not in scope

of the regulation such as smartphones and related content e.g digital maps. This de facto creates an 
unfair situation where any raw input data from embedded services would have to be shared to any 
user requesting it whether it being a consumer or a business competitor,  whereas data originating 
from an app on a smartphone, used for precisely the same guidance and routing purposes as the 
embedded services, would fall out of scope. 
In order to avoid any unfair competition scenario, we suggest narrowing the scope of product 
functions to core functions only and the scope of “related services” to services that enhance the core 
functionality of the product.  
b. Definition of Data holder 
When looking at the definition of “data holder”, i.e  a legal or natural person, who has , through 
control of the technical design of the product and related services, the ability, to make available 
certain data, it appears that in order to qualify as a data holder, an entity must be responsible for 
both the technical design of the IoT hardware product AND its related services. Therefore, third-party 
service providers would not be considered as data holders per this regulation and would not be 
subject to the requirements of Articles 4 and 5. Only the parties that collect s ource data are able to 
make source data accessible to third parties, not service providers that license such source data to 
use within their own  services.   We do request the European Commission to provide some clarity 
regarding this concept of “data holder”, notably because th is regulation covers both personal and 
non-personal data. There might be scenarios (i.e with non personal data/ anonymised data 
reidentification) where the role of a data holder is switching to that of a data controller. Therefore, 
it is of utmost importan ce to clarify the relationships between those different notions and ensure 
consistency and applicability of both the Data Act and the General Data Protection Regulation. 
c. Source data vs Service-level data 
It is necessary to refer to 'source data' which means data directly collected from and by the original 
source and 'service data' which means source data that has been subject to data refinement and 
made available as an IT service which can be covered by IP rights if it meets the relevant legal criteria. 
We therefore recommend restricting data sharing obligations to product manufacturers that collect 
source data from the original source and not third party service providers that license and aggregate 
source data. 
We would like to emphasize that when considering data access and defining obligations for entities 
to make data accessible and available for re -use, it is cr ucial to correctly and accurately define the 
various sub-categories of ‘data’. There is no singular concept of data, specifically in a B2B context. It 
goes through a journey where it starts off as source data and then completes a data refinement 
process. This involves the process of inspecting, cleansing, transforming, modelling and/or 
interpreting source data towards forming information. Thereafter, it becomes service data, that has 
been subject to data refinement and made available as an IT service. These activities require 
extensive know-how, ressources, and are part of commercial and competitiv e business models that 
must be preserved. Our concern with the current definitions of “data” in Article 2 and “data

generated” in Article 4 is that they are extremely broad: there is no distinction made between source 
data and data that has gone through a refinement process. Recital 17 does imply a narrower scope, 
by excluding data resulting from any software process that calculates derivative data from such data 
as such software process may be subject to intellectual property rights. If we do recognize the validity 
of this “derived” vs “intentionally provided” separation, it should be underlined that the notion of 
“derivative data” remains too vague from our point view. As an example, map editors provide direct 
feedback e.g “this street is closed”, this information is then derived in the processing chain to reflect 
the feedback, but the feedback itself is pretty similar to the compiled output. Such legal uncertainties 
could hamper any collaborative model, which is however paramount for the development of a D ata 
economy. 
We therefore request a clarification in the core regulation which better reflects this distinction 
between raw data and service-level data, i.e data that has been subject to a commercial service.  
The definition itself of “data” should be revi ewed to ref lect that “ ‘data’ means any digital 
representation of acts or facts or information and any compilation of such acts or facts or information, 
including in the form of sound, visual or audiovisual recording, which has not gone through a process 
of refinement ;  
Also, it should be made clear that the notion of “generated data” (whose definition is missing) only 
covers accessible data, which would be requested in a digital and machine-readable format, available 
at the existing interfaces of the data holder. 
 
2. Technical limitations to making data available to users 
 
As the world leading provider of location data and services, HERE is fully committed to comply with 
privacy and data protection regulations. In order  to comply with the General Data Protection 
Regulation (GDPR), our company has to undergo processes to isolate and partition the data into 
various components., which leads to technical hurdles to trace the data back to the user. The same 
applies to the pri nciples of anonymization and pseudonymization that we as a company do 
implement in order to comply with GDPR and related existing regulations. A further complication is 
that persistent identifiers in our datasets are removed or changed and nearly all data is aggregated 
prior to further use.   These techniques are applied to reduce the chances of reidentifying the user 
based on the data and manage the data in a cost-effective manner. With the current wording of the 
Data Act, we are concer ned that we would need to remove these data protection measures and 
personalize the data collected, in order to make that data available to users. 
What constitutes legally effective anonymization and pseudonymization is an important issue, 
leading to legal  uncertainty and potentially expansion of the existing GDPR -uncertainty to non -
personal data.

Also, from our perspective, the essential distinction which is completely missing in this draft 
regulation is the notion of user context versus non -user context, that is however recognized in the 
GDPR. We do request more alignment between the GDPR and the Data Act on this fundamental 
distinction. In a navigation context, as we do not want to relate location-based use-cases to any user, 
there is simply no user conte xt, on purpose. Only the product side has a user context. The service 
cannot build a user -context that does not exist. Therefore, obligation has to be put on the product 
when there is a user -context, not on the service that cannot build a user -context that  simply and 
intentionally does not exist. 
 
3. Conditions for data access, sharing and re-use 
 
Reasonable compensation for data holders should be possible in general in order to  stimulate the 
data economy and incentivize data sharing. Otherwise, it appears cleat that any new innovative data-
based service in the IoT world would be jeopardized even before being put on the market. We should 
also have in mind that increasing complian ce costs might actually as a perverse effect restrict and 
penalize B2B data sharing.  
We do welcome the Commission’s proposal under Article 6 to prohibit the third party receiving the 
data to develop a product that compete with the product from which the accessed data originate or 
share the data with another third party for that purpose. The prohibition to use the data received 
from the data holder for the development of a competing product should, in our opinion, not only 
apply to products, but also to related services. There is no obvious reason to restrict the scope of Art. 
6 para 2 pt. e to products only while data can also be obtained from related services. It is important 
to state that a prohibition to compete with the product or related service that t he data originated 
from does in no way prevent a third party from offering an aftermarket service that may be in 
competition with a product or related service other than the one the data originated from. Hence, 
the pro-competitive effect of the Data Act would be preserved. 
Regarding Article 3 on obligations to make data generated by the use of a product or related services 
accessible:  in a B2B value-chain, scenarios are much more complex. When a supplier, acting as Tier 
1 or Tier 2, makes interfaces availa ble to business customers and ultimately OEMs, it is very often 
the case that, at the end and for technical reasons during product integration, this interface has been 
modified. Therefore, what is the responsibility of the different actors in a B2B chain i f the data is 
deemed non accessible or difficult to access?

A crucial barrier for B2G data sharing is often the very high costs associated with it. Therefore, putting 
in place attractive compensation mechanisms or respecting commercial data licensing agreements 
for companies would be a more efficient way to achieve the desired objective of the Data Act. These 
incentives could be direct (e.g., monetary) or indirect (e.g., reputational) and actually foster voluntary 
data sharing. 
On top of this, the scope of the covered data, the set of scenarios under which mandatory B2G data 
sharing would be required as well as a proper definition of “public interest” are crucially missing in 
the proposed text. As it stands, the notion of public interest could cover anything from traffic 
management to statistics. Also, it should be clarified which responsible government entities could 
exercise such public interest, i.e the case of entities acting on behalf of a public authority. 
The interference with existing sectorial regulation, such as the newly revised delegated act on Real 
Time Traffic Information, should be more closely looked at to avoid any c ontradiction and legal 
uncertainty. As an example, a clear reference to FRAND principles (such as in RTTI) should be 
introduced in the Data Act. 
Such an uncertainty with regards to the data sharing conditions could hinder the effectiveness, both 
in terms o f quality and timeliness, of the emergency response from a company perspective, which 
will still have to invest in data processing without any incentive whatsoever in return , and often, as 
we have seen recently, at a time of crisis.

 
 
 
 
Commission Feedback 
Data Act & amended rules on the legal protection of databases 
EFPIA welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback to the proposed Data Act and amended rules 
on the legal protection of databases, published on 23 February 2022. 
 
There are many aspects of the Commission’s proposal which EFPIA fully supports, including the 
overall goal of facilitating the re-use of data. These include the clear statement that data protection 
considerations will continue to be governed by the GDPR, the recognition of the need to respect 
intellectual property rights, support for private law approaches, the limitation of data in scope to 
unprocessed data and increased support for data portability. 
Each of the following aspects of the Data Act will need to be further elaborated and refined if they 
are to  function effectively in supporting innovation. In particular: 
• The scope of data covered by the Act could be made clearer 
• EFPIA would welcome a clearer distinction between data that is generated for non-specific 
purposes as part of the functioning of a device and the situation where data is being 
gathered through the device for a specific purpose such as a scientific research study (for 
example the use of a wearable to gather data for a clinical trial). In the latter case, even the 
release of raw  data sets may be commercially sensitive. The measures enabling holders to 
protect proprietary knowledge should be sufficiently robust. At present this is not the case 
and while the Commission’s intention to respect Intellectual Property Rights is stated, the 
Act should be strengthened in this area, particularly in regard to confidential data, 
irrespective of whether those data would be considered as trade secrets. 
• Further work will be needed to align the Data Act’s concepts of user, holder and recipient. 
Real life business models are more complex and diverse than presented in the Act. In 
particular, it is common for data to be held not by the device manufacturer but the user. It 
may be useful to further focus on the manufacturer as holder, which we understand to be 
the context which the Commission is seeking to address. 
• The Data Act relies on the distinction between personal and non-personal data. While the 
Commission has clarified the treatment of mixed data sets in the past, the distinction 
between the two terms remains a grey area. The same is true for the distinction between 
personal and anonymised data.

• International data transfers of research data have been adversely-affected by general 
concerns about the adequacy of safeguards in third countries, despite the fact that it is 
rarely the case that research data is the focus of concern. EFPIA would be concerned if the 
provisions of the Data Act made worse an already difficult situation. 
• The pharmaceutical sector does not develop connected devices as a core part of its business 
activities, but it may be reliant on such devices to gather data in support of its activities 
(examples would be the use of devices and/or software to track usage of medicines or the 
use of medical equipment to assess the impact of a novel medicine). The Data Act addresses 
connected devices and associated software. One of the consequences of this is that the 
coverage of the health sector by the Act is partial. Data repositories are out of scope, as are 
consumer devices which provide a platform for gathering information direct from patients. 
There is the potential for a segmentation of regulatory frameworks for data access in the 
Health sector which will require further evaluation 
• Under the Commission’s proposal, access requests for personal data can only be initiated by 
natural persons (data subjects in GDPR terms). While consistent with GDPR, this process will 
be very difficult to implement in relation to large scale data access requests by users. The 
data intermediation structure proposed under the Data Governance Act may have a role 
here and some form of cross-referencing between the legislations could be considered. 
• The Data Act proposes that data be made available for purposes that do not infringe the 
interests of the holder. Even though there are clauses that define that third-party recipients 
of data must not use such data to develop products or services that compete with the 
product or service from which the data originates, how will this be enforced and monitored 
is a key risk. The Act’s proposals also provide no clear protection where data are being 
gathered for activities that precede product development, the disclosure of which would 
compromise commercial strategy. 
• The proposal envisions compulsory disclosures of data to public sector bodies (B2G data 
sharing) in cases of “exceptional need”, such as public emergencies. Under the Data Act, 
there is a risk that if the “emergency situations” are not clearly defined, or if they are 
generously interpreted, this will act as a dis-incentive for companies to invest on data 
collection. EFPIA also has concerns that these powers may be abused or lead to the 
expropriation of enterprise data. 
 
The Data Act represents a horizontal framework which will be supplemented by more detailed 
provisions with the legislation on the Health Data Space. Best practice in data-sharing and the 
preservation of innovation incentives in the health sector differs from the general model laid out in 
the Data Act in many respects. The Act should be permissive of adaptation to reflect the specificities 
of the Health sector perhaps by allowing some latitude to the governing bodies of the sectoral 
spaces.

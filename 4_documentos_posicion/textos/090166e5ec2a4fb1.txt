E.ON supports the plans of the European Commission for a twin green and digital transition of our 
economy. The transformation of the energy sector, with data and digital solutions as key enabler, is at 
the heart of Europe’s climate ambitions. E.ON therefore we lcomes the proposal for a Regulation on 
harmonised rules on fair access to and use of data (Data Act) . The file should lay out a legal and 
regulatory framework that keeps up with the pace of technological developments  and be consistent 
with enacted as well as proposed legislative initiatives on the sharing of data. 
 
Relation between sector-specific legislation (e.g. in the energy sector) and horizontal legislation 
• We encourage providing clarification how the Data Act relates to other EU regulations and other 
sector-specific requirements, e.g. the following example for s ectoral legislation applicable to the 
energy sector: 
o Pursuant to Art. 23 and 24 of Directive (EU) 2019/944 on common rules for the internal market 
for electricity (“Electricity Directive”) , secondary legislation pertaining to an interoperable 
framework for an easier data access and exchange using harmonized EU standards are 
currently being drafted and will facilitate the full interoperability of energy services within the 
EU. This work is supported by a wide group of experts from the energy sector and shall serve 
as a reference for data exchange in the energy sector. For example, the way how smart meters 
data should be shared with third parties (see Art. 5 Data Act) shall be defined specifically in 
Implementing Acts  to the Electricity Directive . Art. 6  Data Act  provides clear rules on 
obligations of third parties receiving data at the request of the user which so far are absent in 
the Implementing Acts. In any case, a clarification seems important to avoid inconsistencies by 
multiple, potentially, conflicting rules. 
• In that respect, it should be stressed again that data exchange processes with eligible parties, 
including data consent management related to metering and consumption data are to be defined 
in the dedicated Implementing Acts, and that this shall be comprehensive. Smart meters shall not 
fall under the “product” definition pursuant to Art. 2(2). 
 
Definitions (Art. 2) 
(1) ‘ Product‘: It is not clear whether smart meters will be in the scope of the Data Act or not. The 
Implementing Acts to the Electricity Directive, shall be comprehensive and take precedence over the 
Data Act. 
(9) ‘Public sector body’: The current definition is too broad, since it covers the public sector in all its 
forms leading to an almost unlimited number of beneficiaries regarding the right of access to  / 
availability of data. It should ideally be limited to a single public institution on Member State level or at 
least a manageable number. 
(10) ‘Public emergency’: The definition is too extensive, there should be conclusively described cases, 
in which a public emergency shall be regularly deemed given.

Obligation of the data holder (Art. 3, 4) 
• The ability of the data holder to gain value from data generated by the use of a product and/or 
related services it provides, is too limited and entails the risk of lack of incentives for the data holder 
to innovate. After all, it is at least also due to t he da ta holder’s investments and creativity in 
developing and implementing data generating products/related services that value generation 
based on data is possible. 
o Usage of data for defined purposes (i.e. innovation and product development) of the data holder 
without prior consent by the user shall therefore remain possible ( contrary to the current 
wording of Art. 4(6) of the Data Act). 
• Trade secrets: The limitation of data holders’ rights is too far-reaching, at minimum regarding data 
holders’ right to define technical means of protection of data holders’ trade secrets to be applied by 
the user/data recipient (Art. 4(3) of the Data Act). Otherwise, this might jeopardize trade secrets of 
the data holder . This would also be more in l ine with the principles set forth in Directive (EU) 
2016/943. In the current proposa l, the safeguards for intellectual property rights of the data 
holder, including trade secrets, seem far too limited. 
• Technical feasibility (Art. 4(1) of the Data Act): The obligation of the data holder regarding technical 
means for any data transfer should refer to the utilization of the most common 
programmes/interfaces only (compatibility, not interoperability). It cannot be the general obligation 
of the data holder to ensure technically any data transfer. 
• The Data Act proposal contains too l imited financial incentives and protection of data holders’ 
investments in data-driven products and services. After all, it remains true that the making of such 
products and related services requires the investment of considerable human, technical as well as 
financial resources. This continues to justify the recognition of certain rights of use of the data 
holder to the data as well as an overall better recognition of the legitimate interests of the data 
holder. 
• Remuneration: Related development costs as well as the costs incurred for the data transfer itself 
(changing data formats, anonymizing, and assuring the transfer of data, etc.) must be adequately 
taken into account to the benefit of the data holder. 
• The data holder should not be under any obligation to transfer data to any of its competitors, which 
should therefore specifically be excluded as potential recipients of data. 
• Permitted use by users and third parties of data must be limited, e.g. usage including by third parties 
in the interest and for the purposes of the user as agreed between data holder and user only. Neither 
the user nor any data recipient shall have the right to make use of the data for the development of 
a product or a related service competing to the product or related servic e as provided to the user 
by the data holder. 
• Clear rules must be added to the Data Act for liability of both the user and the data recipient for 
cases of abusive use of data. 
• The data holder shall only be under the obligation to transfer data to the user “as is” (as generated 
by the use of the product/related services) with no liability as to the content quality of such data. 
 
Obligation of user and data recipient (Art. 5, 6)

limited to the interests of the user as well as the purpose for which the user originally procured the

Obligation in case of public emergency (Art. 14-16) 
• Unclear and potentially (very) broad applicability of the exceptional needs and public emergencies 
the Data Act proposal refers to regarding B2G data sharing . E.ON calls for clearer and specific 
definitions of cases of exceptional needs under which data must be shared by data holders. 
• It seems questionable whether legislative measures, such as the proposed ones, are required in 
light of the existing legal situation as there does not seem to be a lack of data available to the public 
sector. 
• The g roup of data  beneficiaries must become significantly smaller. Ideally , there should be one 
single institution serving as single point of contact, which could pass on the data to the respective, 
directly affected public sector body or at least a manageable number of such bodies. 
• Besides, there shall be no data transfer to another public sector body/or even third party, without 
any authorization or justification (cf. Art. 17(4)). 
• In any case, any transfer to any public sector body or EU institution of data obtained under Chapter 
V of the Data Act and their subsequent processing must at all times happen in respect of applicable 
information security and privacy requirements (e.g. stemming from critical infrastructure-specific 
legislation), intellectual property rights of data holders or any other applicable laws. 
 
Personal data 
• The data holder should not bear any legal responsibility as a “controller” for personal data of the 
user / employees of the user; in general, it should be up to the applicable data protection law (GDPR) 
to determine, which of the involved actors assume which role as set out in the GDPR. In that regard, 
Recital 24 to the Data Act appears to be misleading. A different result might be justified in case of 
related services that may themselves generate data of value to the user independently of the data 
collection capabilities of the product with which they are interconnected. This applies in particular 
to a related service that is not provided by the seller, renter or lessor of the product itself, but is 
supplied, under the sales, rental or lease contract, by a third party, in which case such third party 
might be the controller in the meaning of Art. 4 (7) GDPR. This seems to require further clarification 
in the Data Act, which appears to be in disregard of such constellation. In any case, the supplier of 
the product is not always the (only) data holder, in particular since related services may be provided 
by one or more service providers that are distinct from the supplier of the product. 
• A statutory permission should be introduced to the Data Act for any transfer of personal data from 
the data holder to the data user and/or any third party. 
• Legally secure anonymisation processes must on short notice be developed to enable effective data 
sharing as otherwise the distinction between non -personal data on the one and personal data on 
the other hand will remain an obstacle to effective data sharing, in particular of mixed data sets.

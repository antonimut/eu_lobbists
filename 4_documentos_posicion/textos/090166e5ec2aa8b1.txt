B. MAIN OBSERVATIONS & RECOMMENDATIONS 
 
1. Ensuring legal certainty for & finance ability and scalability of European SMEs 
a. Digital Decade 2030 and its values, principles, objectives and targets are for once initiated 
to support European organisations, including existing and new SMEs, to become, benefit and 
remain fit, resilient, sustainable, and to flourish. 
b. Both existing SMEs need or may need to transform and adjust to this Digital Age, where new 
SMEs - either early stage companies, or new small or medium enterprises within or just 
outside existing organisations -. This also goes for SMEs that are on the data proc essing 
services side of the market.

c. Already for many years, these SMEs are building their businesses with their own funds and 
resources, as well as those of all kind s of investors, corporate finance, financial institutions 
and other partners. 
d. Their market valuation  in this digital domain  is generally based on contractual annual 
subscriptions with customers, and forward-looking, the upcoming, returning revenue from 
those subscriptions. This is for instance known as ARR: Annual Recurring Revenue. Based on 
the ARR, these SMEs  are able to negotiate funding, loans, equity deals and the like.  With 
such clear metric - which is the currently standard and good practice, understood by each of 
the stakeholders - it is quite difficult to obtain funds or any kind, and it is quite difficult to 
build, achieve and sustain a strong European SME infrastructure, community, and for once 
also difficult to reach the envisioned digital sovereignty within the EU and EEA. 
e. Article 23 of the proposed Data Act provides certain provisions for the ‘ Removing obstacles 
to effective switching between providers of data processing services’. In particular, paragraph 
(a) envisages the possibility of ‘[..] terminating, after a max imum notice period of 30 
calendar days, the contractual agreement of the service [..]’. 
f. Despite agreeing with what envisaged in Recital 69, that ‘ The ability for customers of data 
processing services, including cloud and edge services, to switch from one data processing 
service to another, while maintaining a minimum functionality of service, is a key condition 
for a more competitive market with lower entry barriers for new service providers’, we would 
like to suggest, in consistency with Recital 44, ‘to protect micro, small or medium- sized 
enterprises from excessive economic burden’ - both from demand side as well as supply side 
- to reframe the proposed Article 23 a bit. 
g. This provision, so formulated, could otherwise lead to customers using this clause as a 
potential excuse to terminate an agreement with such European SME data processing service. 
This for instance as Article 23 can be read by a customer that any commercial or contractual 
obstacle can justify terminating an agreeing -  at will - where the ‘obstacle’ is not linked or 
relevant to data portability or the ability to use customer data with or in other data processing 
services. For instance, the fact that a subscription agreement has a term of three (3) years, 
which is not uncustomary, could at  some point be seen by a customer that signed for it, as 
an obstacle. 
h. Said otherwise, the current text could lead to legal uncertainty and economic instability for - 
even the end of -  SMEs that rely on the stability of contracts  to ensure a predictable cas h 
flow, and to have ability to raise funds, negotiate valuations, get funded without losing 
control, and to scale and flourish. 
i. Therefore, we propose to insert specific safeguards for SMEs in the language of this provision 
as shown in the table below:

j. In addition, the proposed legislation could add this exclusion for SMEs dealing with data 
processing services with entities - other than SMEs - in the recitals of the proposed Data Act. 
This solution would be in line with the exclusion provided in current recital 36, which excludes 
‘Gatekeepers undertakings’ from the beneficiaries of the data access right. 
 
2. A technology-neutral and -agnostic definition of smart contracts 
a. Article 2(16) defines ‘smart contracts’ as a ‘a computer program stored in an electronic ledger 
system wherein the outcome of the execution of the program is recorded on the electronic 
ledger’. 
 
b. Given the relentless  technological evolution  of products, systems and services,  and in 
consideration of the aim of ensuring that this legislation remains future-proof, we would like 
to suggest a technology-neutral and -agnostic definition: 
 
Current Text Proposed Text 
Article 2(16) 
 
‘smart contract’ means a computer program 
stored in an electronic ledger system wherein the 
outcome of the execution of the program is 
recorded on the electronic ledger; 
 
 
Article 2(16) 
 
‘smart contract’ means a digital mechanism that 
facilitates the authentication and validation of 
certain contextual data, such as a computer 
program stored in an electronic ledger system 
wherein the outcome of the execution of the 
program is recorded on the electronic ledger , or 
by means of an attributed -based mechanism 
such as zero -knowledge proof, where the 
outcome of the execution is recorded 
electronically through a tamper -proof system 
that ensures the authenticity and integrity of the 
data, in addition to the accuracy of their data and 
time, and their chronological ordering.’ 
 
 
C. CONCLUSION 
a. Arthur’s Legal believes that this legislation could untap the potential of the vast trove of data, 
structure data (information) and to be used and deployed information (knowledge and 
experience), which is already present but locked and not yet readily available, accessible or 
interoperable. 
b. However, despite lowering barriers to accessing data and services is the right thing to do, we 
would like to draw attention to the perils of subjecting to the same obligations SMEs as well 
as established larger market players, with the chance of not achiev ing the objectives of the 
Digital Decade 2030. 
c. Moreover, we envisage that a broader definition of ‘smart contracts’ would enhance legal 
certainty and ensure that this legislation remains tech neutral and future-proof.

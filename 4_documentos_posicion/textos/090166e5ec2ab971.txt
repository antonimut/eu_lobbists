   EU Data Act Position Paper  Executive Summary The BMW Group welcomes the European Commission’s Data Act proposal (hereafter Data Act or DA) and its ambition to sustainably foster the European data economy, by increasing data sharing and data usage.  We strongly believe in the goals set by the EU Strategy for Data and thus, that innovation will strive when data is shared and used on a fair reasonable and non-discriminatory basis (hereafter FRAND). Furthermore, the European Single Market will enable this necessary digital transformation and thereby creating new (digital) opportunities for Europeans citizens and businesses.  We are actively supporting these goals by participating and proactively shaping digital data sharing initiatives like Catena-X Automotive Network, the German Mobility Data Space and Gaia-X.  In support of these goals, the BMW Group is already sharing its data – based on the FRAND principles and whilst having implemented all necessary (cyber) security measures. Thus, we did implement and successfully rollout the necessary Extended Vehicle Concept (OEM backend) and consequently shared more than 750 million vehicle data sets with multiple relevant parties once the necessary customers consent is provided.  However, we are concerned that some of the provisions of this proposal overreach by imposing requirements, which result in tremendous technical and legal challenges that are currently not realizable. Such requirements will not deliver on the objective of securing the consumers’ rights on their data and will create legal uncertainty as to how business sensitive data will be handled and further shared once it is disclosed. This will likely have a discouraging effect on the quantity and quality of the data businesses gather and process and thus contradict the Commission’s intention to promote the European data economy. We believe that legislation should nurture the market driven approach, underpinned by the proportionality principle and create an environment in which both users, data holders and third parties are safe in sharing data. The Data Act should not become a regulatory obstacle for European companies to grow and invest in high-quality data

Business to Customer and Business to Business Data Sharing (Chapter II, III, IV, X) The BMW Group welcomes and supports the European Commission’s objective to broadly establish and facilitate data sharing for Internet of Things (IoT) products and related services. However, it needs to be noted that currently proposed data sharing obligations by the DA might – but ultimately should not – result in prohibitive effects and thus deter companies from offering IoT products, id est products or solutions that share data in the first instance. It must be recognized that data is often rivalry as its use carries potential and actual economic value.  Scope of a ‘Product’ and Product Design Reflecting the scope and definition of a ‘product’ there is difficulty recognizing the rationale behind the dedicated exclusion of exemplary smartphones – as smartphones and their ecosystems are de facto the epitome of data generating IoT devices and should consequently be included in this regulation. An exclusion as currently anticipated would favour non-European market participants, which are dominant on the smartphone/OS market. Existing ecosystems already use their dominance to force contractual access to IoT generated data of European market participants. The Data Act must create a level playing field. Requirements of product design shall not impede innovation. We believe it essential for users to know what data can be accessed and shared from their products. Nevertheless, there are inherent limitations to the amount of data that can be processed in a vehicle and transferred to an interface where it can be accessed.  A lot of the data generated by the internal components of a product are not intended to be extracted. Rather, they are local functional data, the purpose of which is for the product to work as it was designed to do. T ransferring additional data points often requires deep modifications of the physical architecture and the software of a product, which in turn will require ex ante oversizing its computing power. Such measures have a significant impact on the total cost, resources optimisation and on the environment. To access more vehicle data, it would be necessary to physically bring that data to an interface through its internal networks. In order to transfer this data, each ECU in the chain would be needed to be modified, from the point where the data is generated up to the point where it is communicated to the outside world, the telematics unit. Furthermore, transferring data continuously would implicate that each vehicle would need to transfer dozens of Gigabytes of data – data that generally cannot be easily read and interpreted by the customer – per day. Trade Secrets & Competing Products  Innovation is based on investments and has in the past enabled Europe to be in many industry sectors the paramount originator of world-wide used products, especially the automotive industry. Whilst recognizing the need to create clear rules on data sharing and usage and creating incentives to increase data sharing – thus enabling a thriving EU data economy – the core of the innovative EU industry might be severely negatively affected if only the ‘data’ itself fall under the definition of trade secrets, but moreover ‘functions’, which read, process and write data should be protected as well. This data, respectively these functions are often intellectual property of either the manufacturer and/or of a manufacturer first-, second- or third-tier suppliers. Most severely, these data may be misused, e.g. for: - Reverse-Engineering: By using modern technologies (e.g. machine learning) the input and output data can be used to create an almost perfect replica of the function from where the data generates. - Security Vulnerabilities: Data to some extent can be used to actively attack or manipulate the product. E.g. for mileage manipulation or vehicle tuning with the consequence of higher emissions.  Unclear relationship between DA, GDPR and data term  With its broad understanding of ‘data’, personal data are equally covered by the specifications of the DA. Necessary clarifications and harmonization with the GDPR are missing. At least regarding the vehicle identification number (VIN) for motor vehicles, there are overlaps between the two regulations in various places. Here, a clear definition occurs to be of necessity. The opportunity to address one of the central practical obstacles faced by corporates and European citizens – within the data economy – was not leveraged to its full extent. Therefore, to achieve an effective empowerment of individuals with regard to their personal data, the concept of user in Article 2.(5) of the Proposal and throughout the text needs to be integrated and specified as follows:  (a) adding in the definition of users ‘’and the data subjects’’  (b) clearly differentiating the situations where the user is the data subject from the situation where the user is not the data subject. Moreover, as both the EDPB and the EDPS rightfully recommend a clarification to where the user is not the data subject, any personal data generated by the use of a product or related service shall only be made available to the user in compliance with

Trade Secrets Based on the predominant obligation of a manufacturer to design and build safe and secure products, thus ensuring users safety and integrity, certain limitations result e.g., due to necessary cyber security protection measures. Although we clearly recognize the need – and clearly support this need with current and previous developments of our products – to increase the data generation as well as data usage, it needs to be recognized that the OEM has obligation by its own against its component suppliers, which impacts the data generated out of these components and functions. Exemplary, a product of a certain tier 1 supplier contains trade secret relevant data that is property of the tier 1 supplier. However, the tier 1 product consists of functions provided by further tier n suppliers – again with trade secret relevance. Furthermore, not only the data itself is sensitive, but by continuous monitoring and accessing the input and output data of certain functions the product itself might be ultimately reverse engineered. Thereof, we would recommend specifying Art. 4.1 and Art. 5.1 as well as adding new Art. 4.5. and 4.5.. Obligations for Data Holders, Compensation & Unfair T erms The data economy is a highly competitive market, which did prove in the past – although quite young compared to the classical IoT industry – that especially small and medium enterprises developed already under existing regulation into market dominating enterprises. The BMW Group recognizes that further data sharing, respectively a further use of data will create benefits for all involved parties – especially for the customer. Nonetheless, the current proposal needs to find the appropriate balance between the divergent parties’ valid interests and thus mitigate significant future imbalances arising due to the exclusion of medium enterprises under Article 9. Generally, it should be noted that outside of Data Acts scope of application individual parties should be able to enter into data sharing agreements under the principle of full contractual freedom as outlined in recital 39. Consistently we propose to amend Article 9.2., Article 9.4. and for consistency Article 13.(1). Furthermore, to amend Article 11.3. and Article 13.4.(a): Art. 9.2.:  Where the data recipient is a micro, or small or medium enterprise, as defined in Article 2 of the Annex to Recommendation 2003/361/EC, any compensation agreed shall not exceed the costs directly related to generating, processing and making the data available to the data recipient and which are attributable to the request. Article 8(3) shall apply accordingly. Art. 9.4.: The data holder shall provide the data recipient with information setting out the basis for the calculation of the compensation in sufficient detail so that the data recipient can verify that the requirements of paragraph 1 and, where applicable, paragraph 2 are met. Where a data recipient considers the compensation agreed to exceed the costs directly related to generating processing and making the data available to the data recipient and which are attributable to the request, it shall be for the data holder to demonstrate that the compensation did not exceed such costs.

Making Data Available to Public Sector Bodies (Chapter V) We recognize that clarity on data provision based on transparent and defined exceptional needs – id est in case of natural disasters – would in case of these events generate efficiencies for involved parties. The currently outlined however appear to be to broad and thus should be limited. Furthermore, currently proposed rules are creating further legal uncertainties, explicitly considering trade secrets and further third parties. Therefore, further clarification is needed. Processing and transferring data is technically dependent and limited to existing resources. Thus, providing data for exceptional need may cause damages in terms of deprioritizing use cases and thereby contractual obligations. On later part, we would recommend amending Article  2.2.: Art. 2.2.:  Where the data holder claims compensation for making data available in compliance with a request made pursuant to Article 15, points (b) or (c), such compensation shall not exceed damages and the technical and organisational costs incurred to comply with the request including, where necessary, the costs of anonymisation and of technical adaptation, plus a reasonable margin. Upon request of the public sector body or the Union institution, agency or body requesting the data, the data holder shall provide information on the basis for the calculation of the costs and the reasonable margin.

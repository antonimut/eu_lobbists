 
Philips feedback to Data Act proposal 
 
Philips welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on the proposed Data Act which aims at building 
a single market for data and making Europe a global leader in the data economy.  
 
Data is the cornerstone of digital transformation. At Philips, we help healthcare providers unlock data 
from different systems and devices to make it available at the point of care in an integrated and 
meaningful way – providing actionable insights at scale, when and where they are needed. End-to-end 
mechanisms that govern how data is collected, shared, and used across the health continuum are vital. 
 
While the main objective of the proposal  is to create a regulatory framework  ensuring fairer value 
allocation from data and fostering access to and use of data, it should:  
 
- preserve incentives to invest in ways of generating value through data  in a balanced and 
proportionate way;  
 
- ensure consistency with the existing legislation, in particular the General Data Protection 
Regulation, the Medical Device Regulation and the ‘New Legislative Framework’, including the 
Product Liability Directive ( up for revision in Q3 2022 ), which already include terminology, 
definitions and requirements covering various aspects of the proposed Data Act. 
 
In the ongoing legislative process, the current proposal should therefore be further clarified to provide 
clear definitions, requirements and standards  that are aligned with the existing EU legislation  
considering the following elements:  
 
- The definition of ‘data’ in the proposal  is very broad and includes both personal and non -
personal data, which we understand is an indispensable concept to define the scope. Further, 
it creates an exception for information derived or inferred from this data, which we very much 
welcome seeing this as a key enabler/ facilitator of innovation. However, unfortunately,  it is 
not (yet) clear how the concepts of device-generated and user-generated data can be clearly 
separated from derived or inferred data while applying the obligations of the Data Act.  
To ensure legal clarity and certainty, the definition of ‘data’ should be further clarified and 
narrowed down to data created through user actions, i.e., data actively created by the user. 
It should not include data generated without any action by the user as such data may be 
subject to trade secrets and intellectual property rights.  
  
- There are already significant legal and practical concerns regarding w hen a dataset could be 
considered sufficiently ‘anonymous’ and/or ‘non-personal’ due to the lack of clarity/common 
understanding of these concepts across the EU.  
Guidelines at EU level are needed to create more consistency and clarity on the concepts of 
personal/non-personal data and anonymization to ensure legal certainty for stakeholders as 
to what the implications of the Data Act vis-à-vis different datasets could be.  
 
- The proposal does not differentiate nor assign the roles of data controller and data processor, 
and also does not clarify the delineation between these and the data holder. This should be 
clarified as to the interplay/relation between these and the data holder in line with the GDPR. 
 
- The distinction between ‘data holder’, ‘user’, and ‘data recipient’ should be clarified  as the 
data and IoT value chains are very complex. For instance, a medical device manufacturer may 
not be the data holder and in control of data.

- The territorial application of the proposed Data Act should be further clarified  as to how it 
will interact with the territorial jurisdiction of other regulations, including e.g., the GDPR, MDR, 
ePrivacy Directive and any future relevant legislation, in particular:  
 
i) whether users of products and related services falling within the scope of the 
Data Act have enforceable rights thereunder against providers of such 
products and services in the EU, regardless of where the users are based;  
 
ii) what factors must  be taken into consideration while determining whether a 
provider is an establishment based in the EU; and  
 
iii) to what extent will the activities of global corporations fall within the scope of 
the Data Act. 
 
We would therefore suggest to re-use/leverage the already existing definitions of the New 
Legislative Framework legislation, e.g., ‘placing on the market’, ‘making available’, ‘distance 
sale’, etc. 
 
- The proposed international data access and transfer requirements are at risk of imposing data 
localization and resulting in non -EU jurisdictions to implement, as a counter reaction, data 
localization as well leading to more data fragmentation.  
The terms ‘access to data’ and ‘transfer’ should be clarified to provide more legal clarity and 
certainty. 
 
- The obligation to share data under the Data Act should in no way compromise the safety , 
security and performance of medical devices required under the EU legislation, in particular 
the Medical Device Regulation and GDPR. When this may be the case, the  data sharing 
obligation pursuant to the Data Act should not apply to protect patient safety.  
 
- The proposal does neither provide a clear differentiation between B2C and B2B , nor does it 
explicitly stress whether both are covered.  It shall be clarified as it  may have a  significant 
impact when it comes to the protection of trade secrets. 
 
- The obligation to share data should not hamper innovation . As currently proposed, i t may 
discourage companies to invest in expanding technology if they are at risk of having to share 
what they have built up , not only in terms of data, datasets but also IP, trade secrets and 
valuable know-how as all these may be embedded in the data having to be shared . While we 
recognize that data holders are not obliged  to disclose trade secrets and other proprietary 
information when making data accessible to non -user third parties, there is a risk that users 
on large scale are going to sell their data, being incentivized, or requested  to share their data 
with a competin g business. As such risk is unlikely to be effectively addressed by this 
legislation, the above exclusion should have a broader scope, not only in relation to non -user 
third parties.

- We also have to be mindful of and avoid the risk of collusion and violat ion of antitrust rules. 
Sharing data with other businesses may have a limiting effect on competition, depending on 
the size and number of the companies present in an industry. While we understand that the 
transparency or access to large sets of data concentrated in the hands  of relatively few 
companies will potentially increase competition and innovation by giving access and potential 
to rise for new/smaller competitors, in the case of markets with few larger competitors , the 
likelihood of collusion between those may increase and raise antitrust concerns.  
 
- Article 4.6 of the proposal forbids the data holder  to use data generated by the use of the 
product or related service in a way that would undermine the commercial position of the user. 
It should be clarified what is meant by ‘undermine commercial position of the user’. 
 
- When it comes to  sharing of data with third parties , the proposed provision of Article 5.8  
regarding the disclosure of trade secret s is formulated too broadly and offers insufficient 
remedies to the data holder to prove that the user or third party has used trade secrets beyond 
the scope of confidentiality arrangements as the harm to the data holder is done the moment 
the data is shared.  
Trade secrets should be fully protected, and data considered as trade secret should remain 
outside the scope of the Data Act to foster investments and innovation regarding generation 
and processing of data. Furthermore, the protection should not only cover trade secrets but 
also the potential ‘reverse-engineering’ and obtaining trade secrets from, for instance,  
machine-generated data that as such (i.e., before being analysed or reverse -engineered) do 
not contain trade secrets. 
 
- The proposed provision of Art 6.2 (e) raises  concerns as to how will the data holder whose 
information has been shared demonstrate that a third party developing a competing product 
uses their information. This should be further clarified, and the term ‘competing product’ 
should be defined as this may hamper innovation.  
 
- Regarding the B2G data sharing, the definition of ‘public emergency’ should be narrowed 
down to specific cases  to ensure that it does not go beyond public emergency. The same 
applies to the definitions of ‘public interest’, ‘prevention’, ‘recovery’ and ‘exceptional need’.  
 
- The proposed provisions on interoperability  should refer to  the existing global 
interoperability standards and give preference to these internationally recognised  
interoperability standards. A mechanism should be put in place to ensure  cooperation and 
coordination with  all relevant European and international standards development 
organizations (SDOs) of all sorts, including industry consortia, and not only with the legally 
recognized ESOs (CEN, CENELEC, ETSI) or their global equivalents (ISO, IEC, ITU). This must be 
organized with due stakeholder engagement, in particular with the industry. An important goal 
would be to align on shared views on the need for standards as input for SDOs, who could then 
base their own priority setting on better and more homogeneous market demand insights.

- Besides all the above, requirement for data ‘accessibility-by-design’ also raises the question 
of the interplay with the provisions of the M edical Device Regulation as it may  add an 
additional layer of product design requirements to medical devices, already strongly regulated. 
 
- The proposal, similarly, to other current legislative initi atives (e.g., AI Act) as well as existing 
legislation (e.g., GDPR, MDR), calls for the creation of subject matter dedicated (regulatory) 
bodies and authorities. It also refers to the potential need for cooperation between those, i.e., 
designated authorities under the D ata Act and data protection  ones established under the 
GDPR to ensure a consistent application. Further, we refer to the system of authorities 
dedicated to the existing MDR and AI Act proposal. The notion of multiple competent 
authorities, considering their establishment and operationali zation (not to mention 
potentially divergent approaches and agendas ), may give rise to serious compli ance 
challenges for companies but also represent unnecessary high and duplicated cost on society 
and taxpayers. We would therefore suggest combining these bodies with the existing ones  
to avoid unnecessary and costly proliferation of additional entities.  
 
- The proposed transition period of 12 months  should be extended to at least 48 months  to 
allow for the necessary adjustments to be made by all the participants to the data ecosystem 
in scope of this proposal. 
 
We look forward to further engaging with the EU institutions in the legislative process. 
 
About Royal Philips 
Royal Philips (NYSE: PHG, AEX: PHIA) is a leading health technology company focused on improving 
people's health and well-being and  enabling better outcomes across the health continuum – from 
healthy living and prevention, to diagnosis, treatment and home care. Philips leverages advanced 
technology and deep clinical and consumer insights to deliver integrated solutions. Headquartered in 
the Netherlands, the company is a leader in diagnostic imaging, image -guided therapy, patient 
monitoring and health inform atics, as well as in consumer health and home care. Philips generated 
2021 sales of EUR 17.2 billion and employs approximately 79,000 employees with sales and services in 
more than 100 countries.

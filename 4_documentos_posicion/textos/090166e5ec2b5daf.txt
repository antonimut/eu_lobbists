The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise position on the Data Act 
proposal 
 
 
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise brings together 60,000 companies and 50 industry 
and employer organisations. We work on issues that are important to all companies, 
irrespective of sector and size. But our role is also to provide a voice to all those companies 
and sectors that do not yet exist, but that may emerge in the future if the conditions and 
climate are right. We give Swedish business a voice in the EU. 
 
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise welcomes the initiatives in the Data Act with the 
objectives of on the one hand increasing the availability and interoperability of data and on 
the other hand incentivizing more data sharing. The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise 
agrees that coordinated EU action can add greater value to the European economy and to 
European society compared to actions taken by individual Member States. In this regard, 
businesses need clear and proportionate rules that are - as far as possible - principle-based 
and technology-neutral to ensure predictability, encouraging innovative power and creating a 
positive investment climate. 
 
While a detailed analysis of the proposal is still in progress, the Confederation of 
Swedish Enterprise wants to highlight the following points in particular: 
 
✓ Imposing obligations without clear market failures will have a negative impact on the 
competitiveness of economic operators present in Europe. 
✓ The Data Act risks overburdening businesses in sectors where no problems have 
been identified.  
✓ Based on the Impact Assessment, the Commission have both a) potentially 
underestimates the likely costs, and b) potentially overestimates the likely benefits of 
the proposal. 
✓ The proposal remains unclear as to whom the provisions will apply, and which kind 
of data is affected. Further clarification is needed. 
✓ B2B and B2C data access should be separated to avoid legal uncertainty.  
✓ The safeguards proposed are insufficient and risk undermining incentives to invest in 
data collection.  
✓ The Commission should propose stronger and concrete safeguards for trade secrets 
to create a framework for companies to feel safe in sharing more data.

Do not undermine the competitiveness of economic operators present in Europe  
 
As the data economy is not confined to the borders of Europe the Data Act must avoid 
imposing specific obligations which may have unintended consequences on the 
competitiveness of economic operators present in Europe.  
 
Businesses are concerned about data sharing to any unknown third-party, not least in these 
uncertain geopolitical times. Any data sharing obligations must take into account the risks of 
hacking and espionage. Data Act, and also the AI Act, will lead to sharing and storage of 
data on a significantly greater scale, which entails increased costs, energy consumption and 
cybersecurity risks. One can question how to meet the goals of cybersecurity, the green and 
digital transition and at the same time promote innovation and competitiveness . 
 
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise has recently published a report entitled “EU data 
strategy and data sharing”1. The purpose of this report is to add knowledge and insights on 
what will make it easier for business and industry to share and make better use  of data in the 
internal market and help drive digital progress. As stated in the report, the concept of data 
sharing is a relatively new area, and as a result most legislation in this area is also 
completely new. Markets need time to operate and adapt to the legislation that already 
exists.  
 
The EU Commission and other Stakeholders should refrain from introducing additional 
horizontal legislation on data sharing until it has been possible to properly evaluate the 
impact of already introduced initiatives on data sharing, not least the most recent initiatives 
within the Directive (2019/1024) on open data and the re-use of public sector information 
(“Open Data Directive”), the Data Governance Act and the forthcoming Digital Markets Act. 
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise questions whether the EU Commission has taken 
due consideration to these initiatives in its Impact Assessment, which seeks to identify the 
marginal costs/benefits associated with the Data Act specifically. 
  
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise acknowledge the legitimate need for additional 
legislation on data sharing in some sectors. But with broad horizontal rules covering all 
sectors, the Data Act risks overburdening businesses in sectors where no problems have 
been identified and where well-functioning contractual arrangements are already in place. 
Concrete gaps identified in specific sectors could be handled better with separate initiatives 
that are already in the process of being drafted. To avoid undermining the competitive ness of 
companies and European industry, the Confederation of Swedish Enterprise strongly caution 
the Commission to limit the current sharing requirements to situations and sectors where 
such data is indeed indispensable.2

The Impact Assessment potentially underestimates the costs associated with 
mandatory data sharing 
 
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise questions whether all costs and benefits of the 
proposal have been correctly identified and assessed in the Impact Assessment. Given the 
difficulty in estimating the relevant costs and benefits associated with imposing mandatory 
data sharing rules, a cautious approach to the estimation of costs and benefits is warranted. 
Despite this, it seems that the Commission both a) potentially underestimates the likely 
costs, and b) potentially overestimates the likely benefits. 
 
Taken together, the Confederation of Swedish Enterprise questions if the proposed 
regulation and its associated Impact Assessment achieves its objectives in a proportionate 
way that provides net benefits to European consumers and businesses. 
 
The Impact Assessment estimates just two types of costs associated with the Data Act, both 
relating to the development of technical solutions for data sharing: 
 
1. a one-off cost of 410 million EUR and  
2. a recurrent costs of 88 million EUR per year.  
 
These costs have been extrapolated from cost estimates for a single market – the fitness 
tracker market – for which costs were estimated to 83.4 million in one-off costs and 18 
million in recurrent costs).3  
 
There are reasons to believe that the Impact Assessment, which includes only these two 
cost types substantially underestimates the true costs associated with introducing mandatory 
data sharing.  
 
First, the Impact Assessment potentially underestimates costs by ignoring the fact that 
mandatory data sharing could undermine incentives to invest in data collection. This could 
constitute a major cost to the economy as a whole if/where the regulation means that 
investments are never made, implying either that valuable data is never collected or in the 
worst case that an entire product/service never reaches the market.

The Commission notes that the Data Act’s “benefits apply to a bigger and broader range of 
stakeholders compared to the costs”. However, this would no longer be the case if/where 
costs are not limited to technical data provision costs but also include broader costs to the 
economy from data never being generated and products/services never reaching the market. 
These costs would be borne by many firms and consumers across the economy. 
 
Mandatory data sharing could harm innovation in data collection if/where firms’ incentives to 
invest rely on their ability to potentially generate a competitive advantage from that data. 
Mandatory data sharing would likely reduce firms’ ability to generate a competitive 
advantage – by allowing rival firms to replicate their services more closely – meaning that 
firms might never invest in data collection in the first place. 
 
In the worst case, entire business models might rely on the ability to generate a competitive 
advantage from the collection of data. For example, a manufacturer may be considering 
entering the market for factory robots. Its entry into this market may be conditional on its 
ability to generate revenues on the aftermarket (e.g. repair services). If, due to mandatory 
data sharing, the manufacturer can no longer expect to generate the same revenues on the 
aftermarket (because it would have to share data with rival firms), it may not enter the market 
in the first place, which would undermine not only data collection but also the production of 
factory robots. Similarly, a firm may also be deterred from entering a market if/where 
mandatory data sharing reduces its ability to guarantee a certain quality of service in the 
aftermarket, thereby undermining its ability to protect its brand in the primary market.  
 
The incentive to invest in data collection may be particularly stifled on emerging markets 
where returns are uncertain and/or where the full value of data has not yet been realised. 
This point was raised by interviewed stakeholders from various sectors who warned that 
regulatory intervention at this stage could significantly block the potential for future value 
creation since data sharing is a growing field and companies are still examining ways of 
creating value from these data sets.4 
 
It is standard practice across many sectors of the economy to carefully protect incentives to 
invest. For example, patents grant exclusive rights to sell a certain product for a limited time. 
 
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise notes that the Impact Assessment has omitted 
these costs despite explicitly noting the risk of harming innovation: 
 
“Furthermore, mandatory access rights potentially created by the legislation are also likely to 
reduce incentives to collect data, as well as to upset the current business models of smart 
machinery manufacturers in certain industry sectors.”5

Second, the Impact Assessment also potentially underestimates costs by disregarding the 
annual costs of around 6 billion EUR that were estimated in the cost-benefit analysis 
underlying the Commission’s Impact Assessment. These were the costs of data holders 
associated with developing data management agreements and administrative overhead 
costs and were estimated as part of the cost-benefit analysis for policy option 2 (the 
Commission’ s preferred option). In its own Impact Assessment, the Commission argues that 
these costs are not relevant due to legal and technical safeguards included in the proposed 
regulation. Hence, they have been excluded from the costs of the proposed reg ulation.6 
 
However, it is not clear why the legal and technical safeguards referred to by the 
Commission would not have been considered in the original cost-benefit analysis underlying 
the Commission’s Impact Assessment. If the underlying cost-benefit analysis evaluates a 
different proposal than the currently proposed regulation, then this should be clarified and a 
discussion on how this may affect other estimates should be included. Moreover, even if new 
legal and technical safeguards would reduce costs, it seems unlikely that the annual cost of 
6 billion EUR would be reduced to zero.7

There are several reasons to believe that this approach substantially overestimates the true 
scale of potential benefits associated with the regulation. 
 
First, the estimate of a 15% increase in effectiveness and productivity relies on input from 
the potential beneficiaries of data access – stakeholders who would have a clear incentive to 
inflate the potential scale of benefits. Furthermore, neither the Impact Assessment nor its 
supporting study reveals how many interviews were conducted to support the estimates.  
 
Second, the 15% estimate in any case seems disproportionate with any reasonable estimate 
of what could be expected from a single piece of legislation – it corresponds to 
approximately eight years of baseline growth in productivity (GVA growth for EU27 du ring 
the period 2010-2018 was also around 15%).9 The Impact Assessment thus implicitly claims 
that the Data Act will give a productivity boost that will facilitate an eight-year jump in one go. 
 
The Commission itself acknowledges that it is difficult to quantify the expected costs and 
benefits associated with the proposal, and that the scale of benefits is essentially based on 
“speculative thoughts”:  
 
“Therefore, while it was possible to collect qualitative feedback from the public and  private 
sector on the provisional policy options for each subtask, it was more difficult to quantify their 
costs and benefits, e.g., because case numbers are still small, or the data sharing practices 
are just emerging and stakeholders themselves do not yet know their scale and/or costs of 
making data available. In addition, the stakeholders consulted do not yet have a final and 
consolidated perception on for example the potential benefits they could draw from 
increased data use and availabilities in their respective domain, besides speculative 
thoughts.”10 
 
Given the great uncertainty associated with the scale of potential benefits, the Confederation 
of Swedish Enterprise would strongly encourage careful scrutiny of whether the proposed 
regulation would still provide net benefits subject to conservative assumptions.

The proposal remains unclear as to whom the provisions will apply, and which kind of data is 
affected. Recitals 14 to 17 intended to provide further clarity on what products are in scope 
and how data should be interpreted, but further clarification is needed.  
 
The proposal clearly targets IoT data to allow users of connected devices to gain access to 
data generated. This contrasts with provisions on B2G data and the chapter on international 
data transfer and access where all types of business data fall under the scope of the 
proposal. The proposal covers smart TVs and voice assistants while excluding deskto p 
computers and smart phones; yet these tend to fulfil in many cases similar functions and can 
access the same services.  
 
It remains unclear which components of a physical asset fall under the definition of a 
“product”. It is also unclear what constitutes a "competing product". In the absence of a 
definition, it could refer to only physical and movable objects but exclude "related service", 
i.e. software and data-driven services. If the understanding is limited only to physical 
products, then the Data Act poses a risk that software providers or service providers could 
benefit indirectly by developing software-driven products or services based on the extracted 
data, which then compete directly with the original product or a service.  
 
The definitions of “related service” fails to address the responsibilities in the supply chain and 
who is best placed to provide the access to data. It will be important to clarify which 
economic operator will be responsible to share data from certain types of connected objects 
and with whom. 
 
More generally, the definition of data in the Data Act and the definitions of data in other 
regulations are incompatible. As for now, there are regulations with different concepts and 
legal starting points of data (GDPR, directive of trade secrets, Open data directive, Data 
Governance Act etc). For those companies that must apply the rules, this becomes 
problematic. As Data Act have not put the issue of concepts and legal starting points in 
focus, this problem persists.  
 
 
Chapter 2 – B2C and B2B Data Sharing  
 
In the B2B and B2C relation, the regulation imposes the obligation on data holder to make 
data available to users and third parties nominated by the user in certain circumstances. 
 
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise agree that micro and small enterprises should be 
exempted from the new data sharing obligations.   
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise strongly argues that B2B and B2C data access 
should be separated to avoid legal uncertainty.  
In the case of data collected through consumer products, data protection regulation will 
continue to be a de facto major barrier to data sharing and, on the other hand, data 
protection regulation already provides users the right of data portability . 
Regarding B2B data sharing, voluntary data should prevail to allow for data sharing to take 
place in different ways depending on which underlying technical solution is used, which 
business models’ parties have, and the specific legal regulations that are applied.  Tools, 
such as the Commission proposals on voluntary model contract terms are welcome , 
8 (10) 
 
especially for smaller companies that often lack the necessary legal resources and capacity 
to negotiate contractual data sharing agreements. The Commission should involve industry 
actors in the drafting of these models. 
 
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise do not agree that any market failure that would 
warrant the proposed mandatory data sharing requirements in the Data Ac has been 
presented. To put such invasive requirements in a horizontal regulation can cause 
unforeseen consequences in different sectors and harm the innovation and investment 
climate and the competitiveness of European companies.  
 
 
Chapter 3 – Obligations for data holders legally obliged to make data available  
 
The proposal obliges data holders to make data available to data recipients in the Union 
under fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms and in a transparent manner. The 
proposal however leaves open the question of what happens if the data recipient and data 
holder cannot agree on reasonable protection measures. 
 
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise agrees that micro and small enterprises should be 
exempted from the new data sharing obligations.   
 
The safeguards for sharing data are either unenforceable or insufficient as to guarantee the 
protection of trade secrets. The proposal does not indicate how the legitimate interests of 
data holders would be protected in the event of unlawful use by third parties or how the data 
holder would even be aware of this. It appears the burden of proof that such an abuse took 
place would be on the original data holder, which may be practically difficult.  
 
The proposal has several shortcomings for how data holders should be compensated for 
data sharing. The proposal allows data holders to agree on a “reasonable compensation” 
with larger companies.11 However, the proposal does not sufficiently specify what constitutes 
a reasonable compensation. The data holder must also provide a calculation of the 
compensation, which is detailed enough that it can be verified by the recipient.12 As it is 
difficult to quantify the costs related to data sharing, this will likely be a difficult exercise for 
the data holders.  
 
The failure to specify what constitutes a reasonable compensation is also something that has 
been criticised by the Regulatory Scrutiny Board.13 The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise 
would welcome further clarification on this point.

Chapter 4 – Unfair terms   
 
More clarity should be provided regarding “good commercial practice in data access and 
use” and “unilaterally imposed” in this context. The lists of conduct that is always unfair and 
presumed unfair should be further specified to ensure legal certainty. 
 
 
Chapter 5 – B2G data sharing – Making data available to public sector bodies 
 
In B2G relation, the regulation ensures that data holders make available to public sector 
bodies of the Member States and to union institutions, agencies and bodies, where there is 
an exceptional need, the data that are necessary for the performance of tasks carried out in 
the public interests.  
 
B2G data sharing already takes place, on both voluntary and mandatory basis . The 
Confederation of Swedish Enterprise recognize the objective of the proposed rules to create 
a harmonized framework for the use of such data. Also, companies agree that for specific 
use-cases with a clear public interest B2G data sharing are necessary with the appropriate 
safeguards. However, with the current proposal, the public sector bodies would be entitled to 
require data “to fulfil a specific task in the public interest that has been explicitly provided by 
law”. This use-case is unclear and disproportionate. It does not fit the concept of “exceptional 
need”. The scope for B2G sharing obligations with the current proposal is therefore too wide 
and unpredictable.  
 
Companies have a responsibility to ensure that corporate data remains protected. The more 
data being shared, the greater the risk of, for example, data breaches in one's own or the 
recipient's database and the greater the risk of violating e.g. privacy rul es or disclosure of 
trade secrets. Companies don't always rely on public sector privacy and security protections. 
In addition, there is a lack of trust in how the information shared will be used. In this regard, 
Sweden and Finland have additional challenges with B2G data sharing due to the principle 
of public access to information, a principle that makes companies often hesitant to hand over 
sensitive information to authorities. It must therefore be legally ensured that information 
shared by companies, where relevant, is covered by confidentiality and thus cannot be 
passed on to other actors. In addition, the recipient should always be transparent about how 
the information will be used. 
 
 
Chapter 7 – International access and transfer of non-personal data 
 
The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise opposes the Article 27 and believes it should be 
removed. The approach of the Data Act to mirror the GDPR on non -personal data held in the 
EU appears excessive and disproportionate. What is necessary to protect is in tellectual 
property rights and business secrets and this should preferably be dealt with in existing and 
planned international agreements such as the WTO, the UN, the OECD, the ILO and the 
UNESCO. This Article would be an overregulation that would hinder t he free flow of data and 
opportunities to get desired services to the best price.

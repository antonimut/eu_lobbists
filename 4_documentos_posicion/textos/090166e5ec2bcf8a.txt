Siemens Healthineers’ feedback to the European Commission’s proposal for a 
Regulation laying down harmonised rules on fair access to and use of data 
(Data Act) 
 
Siemens Healthineers  welcomes the initiative of the European Commission to facilitate access to and use of 
data, including business -to business and business -to-government. We too share the ambition and support the 
measures which would ensure fairness in the digital environmen t, stimulate a competitive data market, open 
opportunities for data -driven innovation and make data more accessible for all . Data is instrumental for the 
digital transformation of diverse European economic sectors, including healthcare systems. 
Particularly for healthcare, data is a source underpinning innovation making healthcare delivery more effective 
and targeted , including via digitalisation and datafication. These processes are fundamental for  healthcare 
transformation and granting access to healthcare for everyone. This may happen by: (i) bringing healthcare and 
expertise to remote and resource -poor locations in industrialised  countries as well as developing nations, 
thereby removing or lowering barriers to accessing health services; (ii) telemedicine, where one can access care 
more easily, reducing waiting and travel times; (iii) knowledge transfer between healthcare professio nals i n 
more convenient ways; and (iv) AI enabling broader access to diagnosis and treatment at a lower cost globally.  
Data is a driver for digital health tools which can have a substantial social impact. Such tools provide patients 
with better access to healthcare and personalised treatment, based on multifactorial diagnosis and monitoring, 
in a more cost- and time-effective way. Data-driven tools connect patients to healthcare staff in remote areas. 
Data-driven decision-making and measures based on current and accurate data are especially needed in times 
of a pandemic, thus improving the quality of health services. Lastly, data forms an integral part of a promise of 
patient-centric and precis ion medicine ( for example , digital twins of patients ’ organs and entire organism s 
facilitate the monitoring, prediction, and better understanding of patients ’ health status, the prediction of 
changes, the simulation of individual therapy outcomes, and the discovery of new treatments). 
The above illustrates t he importance of data – both personal and non -personal – for innovation. Healthcare 
innovation in Europe has enormous potential. 1 To foster and support the success of healthcare innovation  
(which has also contributed significantly to the fight against the Covid-19 pandemic), any policy on data flows 
and AI should strike the right balance between the protection of fundamental rights and legitimate interests of 
Europeans and bolstering Europe as an innovation hub.  
In view of this, we would like to caution the policymakers against a premature regulatory intervention in markets 
where a market failure is not yet evident. An overly prescriptive approach coupled with convoluted frameworks 
might further drive  European innovators towards other – more favourable and clearer  – jurisdictions for 
introducing their products, such as medical technology software.2 As we are experiencing with the AI Regulation

General remarks 
The proposed Data Act sets out rules governing cross-sectoral data sharing in various settings , including 
business-to-business, business-to-consumer, and business-to-government. Data sharing pursuant to the existing 
body of rules is already governed by a considerable number of regulations, ranging from the 2016 General Data 
Protection Regulation (GDPR), the 2019 Directive on open data and the re-use of public sector information, and 
the 2018 Regulation on the free flow of non -personal data, to the soon to be adopted Data Governance Act , as 
well as the 2021 eID proposal and the newly proposed European Health Data Space (EHDS).  
Already today, innovators face a need to navigate an ever-growing number of regulatory frameworks  with the 
broad aim of fostering seamless data flows in Europe . However, these frameworks tend to put more brakes on 
the uninhibited circulation of data  in the EU and globally  and make the regulatory environment less clear and 
certain without addressing the hurdles already created by previously adopted regulations . The recently 
proposed Data Act, while in theory aiming at unlocking the potential of data generated by connected products, 
in practice will create many challenges and uncertainties for the manufacturers of such products , on which we 
will elaborate below. 
In addition, such a broad regulatory intervention appears to be premature in view of the current, early 
development stage of the data sharing market where market failure is not  yet apparent. Such an intervention 
may adversely affect the data sharing market by creating new hurdles and disincentivising investment into the 
development of data infrastructure and data generation.  An industry - or sector -specific approach to data 
regulation would be more justified in this case to address issues in specific markets without unintended spill-
over effects chilling innovation in other sectors. For instance, the EHDS has the potential to set the level playing 
field, in addition to addressing the existing regulatory fragmentation affecting the cross-border sharing of data. 
I. Definitions and scope 
One of the core issues with the draft Data Act stems from the vague and broad definitions it relies on. In practice 
this would cause much legal uncertainty not only for manufacturers, but also for users of connected products. 
For instance, the  definition of the term “data” is very broad and needs to be clarified and narrowed down.  
Understanding the intention of the European Commission to exclude any “derivative data”3 from the scope, the 
term as defined in Article 2(1) should be amended accordingly. Thus, only “raw” data should be  included, 
expressly excluding volatile data, processed data, and data entitled to IP/trade secrets protection. Here the Data 
Act remains ambiguous on how to separate data on the use of a product from trade secrets/other proprietary 
information. It is also not clear how to distinguish data that is the outcome of user actions and events4 (in scope)

Further to that, the definition of “product”  is too broad and imprecise  as well: simply put,  any product may

essential to define what is understood under the term of “competing product” 7, which definition is currently 
missing.  
Similarly to the above, the definition of a “related service” is extensively broad as it may cover any functionality 
of a given product. This is disproportionate, and we suggest that the focus of the definition should be on such a 
service being essential for a product’s ‘basic function’, rather than ‘a function’. In addition, as stated above, the 
text of the proposal does not provide certainty on the delineation of a stand-alone software from software as a 
service in this regard. 
The understanding of ‘user’ and ‘data holder’ , and consequently ‘data recipient’ , under the draft Data Act is 
convoluted. It does not account for the complexity of the (e.g., IoT) value chains where the device manufacturer 
may not be the data holder and in  control of data, and thus may not be in a position “to make available certain 
data”8 (which is even more complex where the devices are used in medical setting involving hospitals, clinicians, 
and patients , where handling of  special categories of personal data  is concerned ). In practic e, although a 
manufacturer designs a certain product, it may not be entitled to hold data generated by it  due to both legal 
and security concerns. Further to that, with such a definition, even a cloud processing service provider which 
stores and holds data for a data user may be understood as a data holder in the meaning of the Data Act and 
thus subject to the data sharing obligations. 
The interplay of the GDPR and Data Act is also not sufficiently clear, in particular on the issues of data portability, 
data subject rights,  and machine generated data containing personal  (health) data. In light of that, the 
differentiation of data controller and data processor is missing. Given the frequent occurrence of mixed data 
sets when using a connected device, such clarification would be essential.

II. Business-to-consumer and business-to-business data sharing 
 
a. Lack of delineation of B2B and B2C 
The proposed Data Act  sets out the rules for data sharing in these two different contexts  but lacks a clear 
distinction between the two since it blends the B2B data sharing with B2C2B. 
b. Impact on competition and incentives for innovation and R&D 
In addition to the erroneously concepted roles of different actors along the value chain and their ability to make 
data available, the draft Data Act enables another market distortion. Service providers by virtue of the proposed 
data access rights , would obtain the  benefits from such quasi -mandatory data sharing , while the product 
manufacturers will not have similar rights  and opportunities in respect of service providers . This would lead to 
imbalances in market power and adversely affect competitiveness by means of regulatory intervention. 
Further to that, t here is also a lack of consideration for the loss of competitive a dvantage as a result of non -
European competitors benefiting from data access rights through the Data Act, without any reciprocal rights for 
the European manufactures. This not only enables market fragmentation, but it also weakens the incentives 
for both investing and developing products for the European market.   
As a result of the proposed extensive B2B data sharing obligations,  there is a risk that ( non-EU) companies and 
competitors will benefit from data generated by products of EU manufacturers, including medical devices, to an 
unacceptable degree. The significance of digital services for the service and value propositions of manufacturers 
cannot be over -estimated, which goes far beyond  after-market activities. Where product manufacturers  are 
developing and generating data through related services – thus becoming service providers themselves – such 
data usually has a ce rtain degree of maturity/processing, which requires corresponding investments. It would 
undermine the business models of EU manufacturers and result in considerable loss of competitive edge if they 
were required to disclose such data free -of-charge to the user and were no longer able to capitalise on their s 
investments. This would in turn jeopardize the objectives of the proposal to promote investments and foster 
innovation and the development of digital services. 
The proposed Data Act tends to exempt micro and small enterprises from the relevant B2C, B2B and B2G data 
sharing obligations. While we understand the intent to foster and support the innovation in Europe by creating 
favourable conditions for start-ups to grow and scale up, the provisions on reasonable compensation for making 
data available should be equally applicable to all data sharing market participants. Therefore, instead of 
imposing caps on compensation (i.e., “not exceeding costs  directly related to making the data available to the 
data recipient”9), FRAND (i.e., fair, reasonable and non -discriminatory) terms should be the general applicable 
rule. If the compensation caps rule were to survive, such cost-basis compensation  and related provision of 
evidence may require disclosure of a product manufacturer’s  sensitive financial information , which would be  
disproportionate.

of gatekeepers from B2B data sharing) will only apply at a certain point of time. SMEs may easily be acquired by

Finally, as stated above, a definition of “competing product” is missing from the proposed Data Act. It must be 
noted that, i n practical terms, it will be challenging to define a clear distinction between a competing product 
and a new product in general. Once a third party has access to the data, it can neither be controlled nor enforced 
that this third party will not develop competing products. Also, a limitation to “competing products” would not 
preclude competitors from developing any competing related service. Therefore, we once again caution against 
such broad and invasive regulatory intervention. At least  a wide definition of competing products and services 
should be included in the legislative text to limit the weakening of the competitive advantage of European 
manufacturers. 
c. Contractual freedom and fairness test 
As stated above, the market failure that the proposed Data Act intends to address, is yet to be proven. In view 
of that, such a s ignificant interference with contractual and entrepreneurial freedom can produce unintended 
adverse effec ts due to overly prescriptive provisions regard ing the data sharing contracts . The regulatory 
intervention into cont ractual content may be even  contrary to civil law of certain Member States.  It also 
noteworthy, that the assumed c ontractual imbalances in practicality do not depend on certain manufacturer’s 
size alone but on specific circumstances such as buying power, substitutability and overall diversity of supply. 
We would also appreciate seeing in the legislative text a clarification that s tringent requirements, for instance 
on IT security or data privacy, should not be considered unfair and thus would not be affected by the prohibition 
of unfair terms.  These terms are essential for preserving data integrity, especially if data sharing may contain 
any sensitive data. 
d. Intellectual property rights and the related issues 
The Data Act proposal does not appear to factor in that data generated by the use of a product may, inter alia, 
differ in terms of its degree of maturity/processing (raw data as compared to analysed or processed data) , as 
well as to whether or not such data contain trade secrets and know-how or allow a data recipient to get insights 
into them based on data. 
The provisions of the draft Data Act do not offer any adequate protection to data which may constitute trade 
secrets or other proprietary information.  For instance, measures aimed at trade secrets protection are 
mentioned in Article 4(3) but they remain to be vaguely defined and insufficient in terms of protection, although 
a strong protection of trade secrets is instrumental for incentivising innovation. Therefore, trade secrets should 
be fully protected and data which may be considered as trade secret should stay outside the scope of the data 
access rights.  Therefore, in principle, d ata sharing obligations should not be imposed on data holders with 
respect to their proprietary information. This is important to foster investments and innovation in products and 
services based on the generation and processing of data  without losing a competitive advantage . It is thus 
necessary to avoid situations where competitors may get unrestricted access to manufacturers ’ know-how 
through such data access rights.

e. IT security 
In addition, the protection of trade secrets and know-how is further weakened by the lack of concrete provisions 
on IT security. The possibility to agree with customers and third parties on IT security measures may not suffice 
for preventing abuse. It is difficult for vendors in a mass market to negotiate with all customers and have the 
monitoring capacities to ensure adequate IT security for trade secrets  by contractual means. This is even more 
pertinent in case of third parties ( e.g., competitors). Holders of trade secrets will neither be able to control 
adherence to all agreed measures with every individual customer or third party, nor to enforce them adequately. 
This is especially difficult for software/data. Claiming damages is not an adequate remedy for any breach of 
agreed obligatio ns, as any loss or misuse  of data  belonging to trade secrets  entails considerable losses in 
potential innovation and competitive advantage and even goodwill, which losses are challenging to quantify and 
compensate for.  
III. Business-to-government data sharing  
The data sharing obligations in the B2G context are vaguely defined and potentially very broad10. Especially the 
concept of “prevention of a public emergency” may be open to interpretation, also across EU Member States.  
The concepts of an “exceptional need” and “specific task in the public interest” are even more unclear and open 
to political influence by lawmakers on an EU, national or (federal) state level.  Also, the permitted re-sharing of 
data received from data holders with individuals or organisations carrying out scientific research or analytics 11 
is impacted by these definitions being so broad and open to interpretation . There should be a requirement to 
limit such a re-sharing to defined cases , where the public interest considerations clearly outweigh private 
interests (such as public emergencies) and to specific recognised and publicly funded institutions . This is 
particularly important considering the insufficient guarantees under the Data Act to protect sensitive data (e.g., 
trade secrets).

Further to that, obligations of data recipients in B2G data sharing raise additional concerns. For instance, the 
Data Act only requires of them to “ not use the data in a manner incompatible with the purpose for which they 
were requested ”12. Therefore, the risk of data shared by the companies being further re -used/re-shared as 
public sector data, e.g., in context of the soon -to-be-adopted Data Governance Act or pursuant to the existing 
Directive on open data and the re-use of public sector information, should be adequately addressed. Given that 
some of such data may contain sensitive commercial information, be it trade secrets, know -how, or simply 
undergo a reverse engineering leading to disclosure of proprietary information, the Data Act must limit the use 
of shared date to the purpose ir was requested for, as well as provide for adequate remedies in case of breach 
and/or misuse of data shared under the B2G obligations 13. 
As is the case for the proposed B2B data sharing obligations, micro and small enterprises are excluded from B2G 
obligations. We find this exemption unjustified. The data to be shared for the prevention of a public emergency 
or in case of exceptional need should cover all the relevant sources. Over 90% of European companies are SMEs. 
In the medical technology sector, approximately 95% of innovation happens within the SMEs14. Exempting them 
from B2G obligations may lead to the omission of significant insights indispensable to address any public 
emergency. 
It should be noted that this rather broad and prescriptive regulatory intervention regarding B2G data sharing 
appears to be premature in view of yet to be evidenced market failure. The issue with sharing data in such cases 
may be frequently linked to limitations and ambiguities of the GDPR, where data holders may hesitat e to share 
data in view of GDPR concerns  and liability . Therefore, the biggest issue to address is legal uncertainty on 
handling data which may be potentially classified as personal. In particular, one should account for the data 
which may be considered identifier(s) especially if combined with additional sets of data, within one or more 
data holders. Finally, the proposed Data Act requires that the provision of data under the B2G obligations must 
be made “without undue delay”. This should be replaced by “within a reasonable period of time” or at least an 
indication that the required data preparation time in the context of such a request must be adequately factored 
in.  
IV. Product requirements  
One of the measures provided for the Data Act to facilitate making more data available concerns product  
requirements, namely the accessibility of data by design 15. Such product design requirements create an 
additional layer of product requirements  already provided for medical devices through the Medical Devices 
Regulation and In Vitro Medical Devices Regulation. The interplay with those sectorial regulations is unclear, as 
well as governance and enforcement of such requirements . In addition, it is not clear  whether an individual 
component, by virtue of its design, should already be able to fulfil the technical requirements independently, or 
whether it is only necessary for the technical requirements to be fulfilled in interaction with other independent 
modules of a device.

In addition to data sharing obligation s, the proposed Data Act establishes a right to a swift change of data 
processing providers. The proposal lacks clarity on whether the proposed provisions on removing obstacles to 
an effective switching between cloud providers would effectively preclude service providers from agreeing on 
minimum license terms with customers. Already the proposed obligation to have a maximum termination period 
of 30 days for transferring all data to a competing service provider would considerably weaken the 
competitiveness of European cloud offerings.  The combination of easy switchability  and short-term customer 
contracts reduces any investment incentives into lasting improvements of data quality where data quality is 
dependent on service performance. 
The contractual terms concerning switching between cloud providers pose a risk to any manufacturer which has 
developed an infrastructure and a diagnostic system.  Transfer of configuration parameters, security settings, 
access rights, and access logs amounts to conveying detailed information about the internal processes and know-
how of the service provider . Also, the term “ metadata” is rather vague and in the medical sector  may include 
DICOM (i.e., Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) metadata, which are trade secrets and in no way 
shall be affected. The transfer of DICOM metadata to competitors would (amongst others) effectively enable 
competitors to train AI models in a similar quality but without the underlying investment into R&D and 
innovation. 
In addition, imperative conditions of Article 13 (“Unfair contractual terms”) of the proposed Data Act preclude 
cloud service providers from utilising standard ‘terms and conditions ’ by imposing an obligation to offer each 
customer to adapt conditions. Consequently, it will be nearly impossible to provide cloud services that offer the 
same product to all customers. 
Beyond that, the proposed requirements can only be fulfilled if a large-scale harmonisation of functionalities of 
data processing services is achieved on a cross -vendor basis. Besides competition concerns t his would 
considerably stifle innovation. 
VI. International data flows 
Article 27 of the proposed Data Act  intends to establish a regime for transfer of non -personal data to the third 
countries which is similar in nature to the one under the GDPR.  The volume of global transfers of personal data 
has plummeted  due to Schrems judgements and legal uncertainty around GDPR even within the EU. In the 
future, we expect this tendency to continue, which contradicts the intended goal of increasing data sharing 
within EU . T hus, we believe that the imposition of  processes similar to thos e under the GDPR but for non -
personal data would only create hurdles for seamless data flows. Given that many economic actors are already 
operating on a global scale, and many SMEs aim to scale up, the imposition of measures contributing to 
fragmentation rather than regulatory convergence would only disincentivise innovation in Europe and push 
relevant actors towards other markets.  
VII. Liability  
The proposed Data Act provides users with express protection against the data holder deriving insights about 
the economic situation, assets  and production methods of or the use by the user that could undermine the 
commercial position of the user in the markets in which the user is active . A similar protection of data holders 
against a misuse of their data is lacking, however. Data holders should also be able to claim damages for data

Conclusion 
We believe that this (non -exhaustive) list of the proposed Dara Act’s inconsistencies and regulatory 
shortcomings, including concerning its interplay with the existing data laws and sectorial regulations on medical 
devices, demonstrates that this proposal may increase regulatory density and complexit y and  raise legal 
uncertainty and compliance costs. Ulti mately, this would only stifle medical innovation. We would rather  
support a targeted and sector -specific approach for addressing any potential challenges in data sharing and 
proven market failures. In the medical technology sector, the biggest challenge i s the regulatory fragmentation 
stemming largely from the GDPR implementation which inhibits seamless data sharing, both when needed for 
patients (especially, in cross-border cases) and for innovation and healthcare transformation purposes.  
We are looking forward to engaging with the European institutions and stakeholders in the upcoming dialogue 
on this legislative initiative and stand ready to provide any necessary expertise  and jointly work on its 
improvement.

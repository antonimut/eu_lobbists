 
 
Response to the European Commission’s  
Request for Feedback on the Proposed Data Act 
May 13th, 2022 
Workday welcomes the opportunity to respond to the European Commission’s request for feedback on its 
proposed Data Act (‘the Act”). Workday is a leading provider of enterprise cloud applications for finance 
and human resources, helping customers adapt and thrive in a changing world. Workday applications for 
financial management, human resources, planning, spend management, and analytics have been 
adopted by thousands of organizations around the world and across industries—from medium-sized 
businesses to more than 50% of the Fortune 500. Headquartered in Pleasanton, California, Workday has 
more than 3,200 employees in our 19 European offices and more than 2,000 customers headquartered in 
Europe. 
Workday shares the Commission’s goal of enabling people and organizations to unlock the full potential 
of their data. Our software provides enterprises the tools they need to gain new insights from their 
financial, human resources, and other data and to generate new value from it. A fundamental principle 
underlying our offerings is that our customers should maintain full control over the data they input into 
Workday applications. 
Consistent with that principle, we are long-standing proponents of facilitating interoperability and porting 
between services. One example of this is the Workday Integration Cloud, a set of Workday services that 
enable customers to synchronize data stored in Workday with other third-party systems. With the 
increasing expansion of data-driven technologies and services, the importance of enabling enterprises to 
engage in data sharing and data-driven innovation cannot be overstated. At the same time, Workday 
believes data protection is a fundamental right, and we have built privacy and data protection into the 
fabric of our products since our founding. 
We therefore broadly support efforts to remove barriers to data sharing, analysis, and reuse, while also 
ensuring that fundamental rights to data protection and privacy are fully respected. As the Act seeks to 
advance these goals through legally binding obligations, however, it is vital that the Act is clear in scope 
and application, and that it takes adequate account of the distinct roles of different actors in the data 
ecosystem, and the realities of current market standards. To this end, this response offers targeted 
suggestions for clarifying certain key terms in the Act, and refining certain provisions, so that regulated 
actors are clear on whether and how the Act applies to them, and can take the necessary steps to 
comply. Part I of this response addresses the Act’s provisions applicable to data processing 
services, while Part II addresses its provisions applicable to data holders. 
Workday appreciates the opportunity to share our views with the Commission on the proposed Data Act. 
Please do not hesitate to contact Jens-Henrik Jeppesen, Senior Director, EMEA Public Policy, at 
jens.jeppesen@workday.com.

I. Data processing services 
• Clarify Chapter I (scope) definition of “data processing service” to reflect the diversity 
of SaaS technology. 
The Act would impose a number of switching, portability, interoperability, and other obligations on 
providers of “data processing services” (“DPS”) (see Chapters VI – VIII). Recital 72 states that the goal of 
these obligations is, among other things, to ensure that customers can port their “digital assets” from one 
service to another, including both “data” and “applications.” This goal is consistent with Workday’s own 
practices; we strive to make our applications capable of running on various cloud infrastructures and 
interoperable with third-party and customer-built integrations, providing customers with meaningful 
flexibility and choice. 
Although Recital 72 indicates that cloud applications should be considered digital assets—i.e., among the 
things that customers should be able to port from one DPS to another—other aspects of the Act make the 
classification of cloud applications less clear. In particular, Article 2(12) defines a DPS, in the relevant 
part, as a digital service “which enables on-demand administration and broad remote access to a scalable 
and elastic pool of shareable computing resources of a centralized, distributed, or highly distributed 
nature.” If the Commission intends the DPS definition to include cloud-based “software-as-a-service” 
(“SaaS”) applications, then important clarifications and nuance must be introduced into the definition to 
accurately reflect the nature of SaaS services and the diversity of provisioning models. 
Although some SaaS applications may be provisioned through “a scalable and elastic pool of shareable 
[and centralized] computing resources,”—i.e., in the cloud—they may not themselves “enable on-demand 
administration” of those resources, when that capability is controlled by the provider of the underlying 
cloud platform or infrastructure service, a commonplace arrangement for the provision of SaaS 
applications. If it is the Commission’s intention to include SaaS services within the scope of DPS, 
legislators should amend the definition such that it does not specifically assign “a scalable and 
elastic pool of shareable computing resources” as a necessary attribute of SaaS services 
themselves.  
• Refine Chapter VI (switching) obligations to account for the unique nature of SaaS 
services. 
To the extent that legislators decide that certain of the Act’s DPS obligations should apply to providers of 
cloud-based SaaS applications, we urge them to refine Chapter VI obligations such that they are 
workable for the nature of SaaS technology. When applied uniformly to the extremely diverse ecosystem 
of SaaS offerings, many of the obligations set out in Chapter VI could slow innovation, reduce competition 
and choice, and inhibit growth in the EU’s SaaS sector. 
Examples of the Chapter VI provisions that require adjustment to effectively work for SaaS offerings 
include: 
 
 
  3 
One-size-fits-all requirements. Chapter VI contractual requirements fail to account for the unique nature, 
complexity, and customer requirements of enterprise software. For instance, while Article 24(1)’s 
requirement that DPS providers enable customers to terminate or switch services within 30 days may be 
workable for relatively simple or homogenous services, it is infeasible and unrealistic when applied to 
more complex SaaS products, in particular for enterprise software, where services are specially deployed 
to be deeply integrated into, and interoperable with, the customer’s broader IT system, and implemented 
over long time frames ranging anywhere from 6 to 18 months. In our experience, it is commonplace for 
contracts in our sector to have a duration of anywhere from 3-5 years, with an option to transition to a 
new service over 3 months, or longer. Enterprise customers overwhelmingly welcome the stability and 
certainty of these terms, and few have the technical ability to switch to a new service within 30 days. They 
are therefore extremely unlikely to request to terminate a contract within this short timeframe, given the 
tremendous disruption to their internal operations that could result. In our experience, customer requests 
regarding contractual length during switching are nearly universally for transition period extensions, 
beyond the standard 3 months. To the extent the Commission seeks to impose contractual terms on 
providers and customers of SaaS offerings, it must ensure that these terms reflect the practical 
realities of enterprise SaaS implementations, the longer time frames required at the enterprise 
scale, and customer needs. In addition, Article 25 should clarify that billing regular service subscription 
fees during the course of a transition is not considered as a switching charge, when the fees are billed for 
the continuance of the service, and not for the switching process itself. 
Enabling vs. doing. Several provisions in Chapter VI require providers of DPS to do various things—e.g., 
to “assist and, where technically feasible, complete the switching process” and to “ensure full continuity in 
the provision of the respective functions or services” (Art. 24(1)(a)). However, most importantly, 
incumbent providers do not have detailed information about the technical requirements of the new 
provider and how data must be migrated and formatted across a complex array of interrelated systems to 
make the transition successful. Only the customer or its third party knows what the specific requirements 
of a transition are. Further, if providers were required to directly manage (“complete”) the switching 
process, it would necessitate net more granular provider access to potentially sensitive customer data. In 
general, Workday customers are the controllers of their data, and Workday is the processor, acting on the 
controller’s instructions; Chapter VI, however, would fundamentally alter this relationship by requiring the 
processor to, at least jointly, determine the purposes and means of processing. Finally, despite these 
barriers, if providers were still legislated into this role, they would need to be fully remunerated for 
undertaking this costly and resource-intensive work—to the extent that today, as explained below, 
systems integration is its own industry. Instead, the Act should require providers to enable switching 
by providing customers and customer-engaged third parties the tools and resources they need to 
switch to ensure a successful transition and continuity of service provision.  
Customer & 3rd party roles. In our experience, customers expect their provider to enable the switching 
process, while customers primarily manage the switching process themselves—often with the help of third 
parties such as system integrators, consultants, and others that specialize in helping customers 
implement and transition IT systems. Particularly for enterprise software, switching involves deep 
complexity and requires such a high level of specialized expertise and investment that involvement of 
 
  4 
third parties dedicated to managing the transition has become industry standard. These systems 
integrators are experts on not just one but multiple industry providers’ systems, and are therefore best 
positioned to manage transitions between different providers. Requiring providers to take on certain roles 
outlined in Chapter VI, including those currently filled by dedicated third parties, would functionally require 
providers to enter into new lines of business by law, while undermining the business models of third party 
systems integrators—including many European companies that could be put out of business. Rather, the 
Act should take the above approach of requiring providers to enable such transitions, and should 
explicitly account for the role of customer-engaged third parties that support switching as 
possible recipients of such enablement, in addition to the customer itself. Such enablement could 
include: ensuring generous standard transition periods (e.g. 3 months), liberally granting transition period 
extensions, making data available in a format mutually agreed upon between the parties, and packaging 
and porting data back to the customer or its systems integrator. This would strike the best balance 
between reducing barriers to switching, while avoiding unnecessary disruption to large shares of the IT 
market, including the many small- and medium-sized European companies that support IT 
implementations and switching as their primary revenue source. 
In general, the market realities described above reflect broadly accepted industry standards. Workday is 
proud to maintain an industry-leading 97% customer satisfaction rate, demonstrating that our industry-
standard contractual terms and enablement-focused approach to switching are overwhelmingly in line 
with our customer’s expectations and needs. 
• Clarify and refine Chapter VII (international safeguards) obligations for non-personal 
data.  
Chapter VII (Article 27) of the Act would place a number of limitations on DPS providers transferring non-
personal data outside of the EU. Yet the problem that the Commission seeks to solve in proposing these 
limitations on the transfer of non-personal data is unclear, and several aspects of Article 27 are confusing 
and could create challenges for regulated DPS providers and their customers, as well as for regulators 
seeking to enforce these rules. 
First, Article 27 poses potential problems for enterprise providers who act as a processor of customer 
(controller) data. Customer data belongs to customers—but to meet the requirements of Article 
27(1), DPS providers will need to gather much more intrusive information about this data from 
their customers, including whether it is non-personal and whether it is the type of data likely to be 
subject to specific protections under EU law. Enterprise customers would strongly object to sharing that 
information—and many may refuse altogether. In general, such an approach contravenes a key value 
proposition of enterprise SaaS services: that customer data, whether personal or non-personal, is safe 
with the provider because of the strong protections that safeguard customer data from unlawful provider 
access or transfer. Further, for most SaaS providers whose access to customer data is strictly limited 
(deliberately), all customer data is treated as personal data as there is no way to distinguish between 
personal and non-personal data. For example, usage, logging, and telemetry data, which are critical to 
ensure the proper functioning of the service and security, cannot necessarily be distinguished between 
personal and non-personal data without intrusive provider access. 
 
  5 
Beyond this challenge, other aspects of Chapter VII require further clarification. Article 27(1) would 
require DPS providers to take “all reasonable technical, legal and organisational measures, including 
contractual arrangements'' to prevent “international transfer or governmental access to non-personal data 
held in the Union'' where such transfer or access “would create a conflict with” EU or Member State law. 
However, the Act does not define what constitutes a “conflict” with EU or Member State law, leaving DPS 
providers uncertain as to when the Article applies, and good faith compliance efforts up for interpretation. 
(While Recital 77 lists a very broad category of interests that Article 27(1) may be intended to protect—
including fundamental rights, Member State security interests, and commercially sensitive data—the 
Recital provides no further specificity, nor does it explain how a particular conflict with these rights and 
interests would arise.) The Act also does not define what constitutes “reasonable technical, legal and 
organisational measures.” 
Other challenges arise with respect to obligations imposed by Article 27. For instance, Article 27(3) states 
that where a DPS provider receives a third-country order to transfer or grant access to non-personal data 
“in the absence of . . . an international agreement,” such as a mutual legal assistance treaty (MLAT), and 
complying with that order “would risk putting the addressee in conflict with” EU or Member State law, the 
DPS provider shall only transfer or provide access to the requested data where the third-country legal 
system satisfies certain conditions set out in the Act. These obligations appear to apply separately from 
those in Article 27(1), but again, the Act does not define what constitutes a “conflict”. Further, read 
literally, Article 27(3) does not apply to any third country with which the EU or a Member State has an 
MLAT or similar agreement—meaning that providers cannot rely on Article 27(3) to transfer non-personal 
data to countries with which the EU or a Member State has such an agreement. This is unlikely to be the 
Commission’s intended outcome, and should be clarified. 
We encourage EU legislators to consider carefully whether the limits proposed to transfers of non-
personal data are necessary in the first instance. Ultimately, the responsibility for resolving conflicts 
of law between countries should fall on governments, and not private organizations. If the 
conclusion is that such limitations are appropriate nonetheless, we recommend amending Article 27 to 
be precise about the specific conflicts covered by the Articles 27(1) and (3) and the measures 
required under Article 27(1) to prevent them. Article 27(3) should also be amended to make clear 
that it applies in relation to transfers to all third countries, and, as the EDPB-EDPS Joint Opinion 
notes, the interplay between Article 27(1) and 27(2) and 27(3) should be further clarified. Finally, 
the Act should specifically define “reasonable technical, legal and organisational measures.” 
• Ensure Chapter VIII (interoperability) provisions account for the nature of 
competition in the SaaS market. 
As is the case for Chapter VI, certain Chapter VIII obligations on interoperability may be sensible for 
“infrastructure-as-a-service” (“IaaS”) or “platform-as-a-service” (“PaaS”) offerings, but are not workable for 
SaaS specifically. For example, the Article 29 requirement for data processing service interoperability 
specifications and standards to guarantee “functional equivalence between different data processing 
services that cover the same service type” is not practical for the SaaS sector, in which providers primarily 
compete on differences in service offering. Requiring SaaS providers to offer the same functionality, 
 
  6 
features, or qualities of service as their competitors would remove their incentives (and even ability) to 
innovate, and would leave customers with less choice, directly undermining the Act’s objectives. While 
some level of service variability may also exist among IaaS and PaaS offerings, such variability is 
relatively more prevalent and core to competition in SaaS markets, in which software is tailored to the 
varying needs of specific business functions, industries, and geographies. 
A “functional equivalence” requirement also fails to recognize that many cloud applications are specifically 
designed to enable customers to configure applications to their own needs—for many customers, a key 
value proposition of SaaS applications, especially in enterprise software. Beyond the general 
unworkability of “functional equivalence” for SaaS, it is not clear how this requirement would operate 
specifically with respect to application configurability chosen by the customer, a core attribute of many 
SaaS applications. In general, mandating technical specifications or a fixed “common denominator” level 
of functionality across the entirety of the software market would be detrimental to user choice and 
meaningful provider competition, risking worsening the problems the Act intends to solve, and threatening 
the ability of European providers and customer enterprises to innovate and generate value from data. The 
Article 29 “functional equivalence” provision should be clarified to only apply to IaaS and PaaS 
services, in line with the Commission’s approach taken in Article 26 in the context of switching. 
II. Obligations on data holders 
• Ensure Chapter III (data holders) specifies the EU laws under which a data holder is 
obligated to make data available.  
Chapter III of the Act sets out various obligations that apply in cases where a “data holder” must make 
data available to a “data recipient” (which Article 2(7) defines to mean a third party other than the “user”). 
Article 12(1) (like Article 8(1)) states that these obligations apply “where a data holder is obliged under 
Article 5, or under Union law or national legislation implementing Union law, to make data available to a 
data recipient” (emphasis added). 
In Workday’s experience, it is extremely rare that Union law would require us—or indeed most other 
providers of cloud services—to share a customer’s data with a third party, other than on the customer’s 
instruction. Indeed, to the extent the data in question was personal data, the GDPR might preclude us, 
acting as a processor of this data, from sharing such data in many instances, absent an instruction from 
the customer to do so. 
Without specific guidance on the particular EU laws that the Commission believes to impose such 
obligations, it could be extremely challenging for data holders to determine whether or how Chapter III 
applies to them. Given the sensitive and even confidential nature of the data that our customers typically 
process in Workday applications (e.g., human resources and financial data), we think it is vital that 
providers in Workday’s position have maximum clarity on the laws that the Commission believes would 
impose such obligations, the specific third parties to which such data sharing obligations would extend, 
the precise conditions under which we would be legally obliged to share the data, and the types of data to 
which these obligations would apply. 
 
  7 
To this end, legislators should consider the approach adopted in the Commission’s Proposal for an 
Artificial Intelligence Act. Annex II of that Act lists all of the EU laws applicable to “products” that will be 
classified as “high risk” under the Act, which has the benefit of giving manufacturers crucial clarity on 
whether their products will fall within scope of the Act. We urge legislators to adopt a similar annex to 
the Data Act that lists the specific legislative measures, and the specific articles therein, that 
impose a legal obligation that would be within the scope of Article 8(1). This approach would ensure 
legal certainty for data holders, in keeping with the Act’s objectives, and mitigate the significant ambiguity 
in Article 12(1) as currently drafted.

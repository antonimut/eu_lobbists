 
 
 
Microsoft Response to the European Commission’s 
Request for Feedback on the Proposed Data Act 
13 May 2022 
 
 
Microsoft recognizes that greater collaboration, access to, and re -use of, data will play a key role in 
the EU’s economic future, and can help address some of society’s biggest challenges, driving 
innovation, efficiency, and productivity. In particular, the availability of greater amounts of data, along 
with improved access, emerging analytics tools, and state of the art technologies, will allow European 
businesses and researchers to better leverage the data they already possess while improving 
outcomes through enhanced collaboration with international partners to advance Europe’s digital 
goals and global competitiveness.  
  
Microsoft has already taken significant steps to promote data sharing, as reflected in our Open Data 
Campaign to help address the “data divide” and help organizations of all sizes to realize the benefits 
of data and the new technologies it powers. We’ve also adopted a set of principles to guide our data 
collaboration efforts, based on the themes of openness, usability, empowerment, security, and 
privacy. These principles are also reflected in the goals of the Data Act and we look forward to working 
with the Commission and the co -Legislators to ensure that the Regulation’s provisions best achieve 
these shared objectives.  Microsoft welcomes legislative efforts that improve access to data and 
innovation for all. While we applaud the broad objective of the EU to foster data-driven innovation by 
facilitating greater data sharing across sectors, some aspects of the proposed Data Act risk falling short 
of this objective and could more likely have a detrimental impact on data innovation in Europe.  
 
We welcome the European Commission’s objectives to foster business -to-business (B2B) and 
business-to-consumer data sharing (B2C) by expanding rights for users to re -use the data generated 
by their use of connected devices and related services. Better access to more data typically generates 
better insights and greater value. Because few organizations generate sufficiently large volumes of 
data on their own, it is imperative to make it easy to share data and for organizations to be able to 
use this data. In fact, Microsoft has participated in experimentation with the concept of European data 
spaces using open source collaboration with European companies and research institutions.  
 
Given the broad scope of both data and the players implicated, it is currently unclear how the exercise 
of the new user rights would work in practice. Particularly in a consumer IoT context, it’s unclear which 
generated data would not be personal data already falling under GDPR rules.  Meanwhile, provisions 
should encourage the proliferation of data that can be made open, while at the same time 
safeguarding privacy and confidentiality. It is unclear whether there is any obligation to share trade 
secrets, and i f the intention is to compel companies to disclose and share trade secrets, this would 
represent a clear erosion of trade secret protection.  This could be addressed by clarifying explicitly 
that there is no obligation to share trade secrets . This would not prevent companies from shari ng 
information confidentially if they choose to do so and when the appropriate safeguards, as they deem 
fit, are in place.   
 
The current text, rather than clarifying, risks creating ambiguity  whether protections for customer 
data and trade secrets are weakened. In the context of cloud services, customers are provided 
assurances, both contractually and technically, that they control their data. Given the new concept 
and definition of “data holder” it is unclear what role is expected of such companies tha t would be

considered data processors under the GDPR, particularly in B2B environments and contractual 
arrangements where many different platforms and services are implicated in complex ecosystems of 
“products” and “related services”. This could be clarified by limiting the definition of “data holder” to 
the party that manufactures the device and operates the related service, excluding infrastructure or 
software providers who may be contractually prohibited from producing the data. 
 
Microsoft also supports the introduction of a flexible, voluntary framework that would facilitate the 
access and use of data in a B2G context, while safeguarding privacy, security, and commercially 
sensitive information. However, the potential introduction of a right for governme nts to access 
privately held data for public interest purposes and/or exceptional needs, absent robust safeguards, 
raises legitimate privacy and security concerns around sensitive information. We welcome, therefore, 
the additional clarity the European Commission has provided as to what would constitute “exceptional 
needs” and support the exclusion of this right for activities done in a law enforcement context which 
is already addressed in separate EU legislation. Any requests from public authorities should also 
always be directed to the entity responsible for making decisions about the data at issue or to the 
party who has a contractual right or obligation to comply with any such requests , as other entities 
should not be forced to share customer data with pu blic-sector organizations, p articularly without 
customer knowledge or consent, nor risk violating their contractual obligations or their obligations 
under EU or Member State laws. 
 
Regarding data portability , the concept is  well established and indeed is increasingly expected by 
customers and cloud users, and there is significant and increasing competition amongst providers to 
develop customer tools to better enable effective data portability and interoperability across services. 
However, data portability  and interoperability  are different from switchability, and the rules and 
expectations set out in the Data Act may not accurately reflect the technical realities of the services 
captured. The proposal includes a general prohibition on “obstacles” to switching but does not provide 
a definition nor require s that an obstacle meet a certain threshold of significance. As a result, any 
commercial, technical, contractual, or organizational factor – left to the discretion of the user - that 
might dissuade a customer from switching services could potentially amount to a prohibited obstacle, 
which would create an impossible compliance obligation. The contractual requirements also risk going 
beyond what is reasonable and common practice in a B2B (and even B2C) context. The Data Act largely 
places the switching obligations on the exporting provider, but effective switching requires the co -
operation of both the exporting and the importing data processing provider, as well as the customer, 
and this could be clarified further in the text.   
 
Modern ICT applications are built on a rich and constantly evolving set of resources that offer choice 
in terms of capability, performance, cost, and other factors.  Imposing requirements on data 
processing services  so that all services are interchangeable or mandating technical specifications 
and/or a fixed set of common denominator functionality, would in practice result in requiring all 
services to be effectively id entical. This would lead to a loss of innovation capacities for European 
companies and users. Requiring all data processing services to use the same specific technologies or 
data formats would result in greater uniformity of software services that could lead to reduced choices 
for customers and likely impede the development of more innovative offerings in the EU.  Customers 
choose their data processing service often based on the special features offered. It is impossible for 
data processing services to have  adequate awareness of the functionalities of other data processing 
services to ensure the “functional equivalence” envisioned in the proposal.  
 
International data flows are a cornerstone of the data economy, and companies rely on them to 
enhance their global competitiveness, develop products, and gain insights on issues that are global in 
nature, such as the COVID-19 pandemic. They are equally important for addressing societal challenges 
such as public health, environmental sustainability, and cybersecurity, as well as medical and scientific 
 
research. While aiming to ensure appropriate safeguards are in place to protect non-personal data, 
the Data Act should also contribute to enabling, rather than restricting, the free flow of data, to 
facilitate cross-border data sharing and leverage its collaborative benefits. Companies should be able 
to leverage the state-of-the-art technologies available and service providers to reap the full benefits 
of the data economy. Data localisation requirements, intentional or unintentional, could also hurt 
sectors that do not participate heavily in international trade such as healthcare, where up to a quarter 
of inputs, for example, consist of data -reliant products and services. They may also be inconsistent 
with commitments the EU has made under international trade agreements. 
 
We also welcome the European Commission’s clarification in Article 35 that  the sui generis right 
should not be extended to databases made of machine -generated data. Intellectual property and 
access laws should not be interpreted to unnecessarily restrict legitimate access to and use of lawfully 
accessed data. Expanding protection of the Database Directive to include unstructured or machine 
generated data would create and entrench data monopolies and  stifle the free circulation of 
information and ideas.

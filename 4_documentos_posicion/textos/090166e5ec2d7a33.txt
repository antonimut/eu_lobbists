ACT | The App Association feedback to the European Commission’s proposal for a regulation on harmonised rules on fair access to and use of data (Data Act) I/ Introduction and statement of interest  ACT | The App Association (hereafter ‘App Association’) submits feedback to the European Commission in response to its roadmap for the legislative proposal for a regulation on harmonised rules on fair access to and use of data (Data Act).  The App Association is an international not-for-profit trade association located in Brussels, Belgium, that represents more than 5,000 small and medium-sized application developers and connected device companies located across the European Union (EU) and around the globe. We are committed to European leadership in innovation, as well as supporting competition and growth in the Digital Single Market (DSM). The App Association dedicates itself to creating an economic and regulatory environment in which small and medium digital players can thrive. Today, the ecosystem the App Association represents—which we call the app economy—is valued at approximately €830 billion globally and is responsible for millions of jobs across the Member States of the EU. Alongside the world’s rapid embrace of mobile technology, our members have been developing innovative hardware and software solutions that power the growth of the internet of things (IoT) across modalities and segments of the economy. The App Association’s members include many EU-based innovators who develop mobile technology products in both established and emerging markets. How our members can use and share data affects how their products function and how consumers engage with those products and services. Our members handle and work with data every day, so they are directly affected by European data governance.  Data plays an essential role in the EU’s objectives for its digital future, such as job growth, innovation, and competitiveness. As such, we welcome the Commission’s goal of ensuring fairness in the allocation of value from data among actors in the data economy and fostering access to and use of data. At the same time, clarifying the legal liabilities of those who use data responsibly, including the legal protection of databases, is just as important as removing the barriers that hinder safe data sharing across the EU. In general, fostering data sharing between businesses as well as between the public and private sectors while preserving strong data protection would promote a useful balance between data use and incentives for new investments in data generation.  As a representative of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), we very much appreciate the Commission’s focus on protecting SMEs against unfair contractual terms to enable them to negotiate balanced data-sharing agreements and exempting them from certain clauses in data sharing or use contracts that would make data sharing practices unattractive. SMEs could especially benefit from this Commission initiative and easier data access. Data sharing is the basis of interoperability, which is what helps SMEs to maintain their presence in the IoT market.  However, while we acknowledge that many markets that could especially benefit from enhanced data sharing are still developing, we caution the Commission against developing overly rigorous and prescriptive regulations, such as mandatory data sharing or transparency obligations in business-to-business (B2B) settings, that are responsive to demonstrated problems. Other issues we invite the Commission to consider as it reviews the current proposal are how the Data Act may interact with the GDPR, how to ensure that trade secrets and intellectual property are protected, and which information security requirements apply to sharing sensitive information. Below we provide specific comments on the chapters of the proposed regulation.

II/ Chapter 1: General provisions and scope The App Association welcomes the Commission’s overall goal of unlocking the value of data across the EU and establishing a single market for data. We believe the scope of the Data Act, which covers manufacturers, data holders, data recipients, public sector bodies and EU institutions, and providers of data processing services, is appropriate. The key aims of the proposed Act are worthwhile goals, including: • Facilitating access to and the use of data by consumers and businesses, while incentivising data value generation; • Providing for the use by public sector bodies and Union institutions, agencies, or bodies (Public Bodies) of data held by the private sector where there is an exceptional data need; • Facilitating switching between cloud and edge services; • Safeguarding against unlawful data transfer without notification by cloud service providers; and • Providing for the development of interoperability standards for the reuse of data  However, as mentioned above, we have concerns about how the Data Act will interact with other regulations, most importantly the General Data Protection Regulation, but also other initiatives like the Digital Markets Act, the Platform-to-Business regulation, the e-Privacy Regulation, the e-Privacy Directive, and the Artificial Intelligence Act; as well as further sector-specific related efforts such as the Commission’s Health Data Space proposal. As the Data Act will have effects on personal data processing, we urge the Commission to clarify how the Data Act will interact with GDPR Article 6(1) and the legitimate interest purpose. Initiatives like the DMA, the e-Privacy Regulation, and the AI Act are still going through the legislative process, and it will be crucial to ensure legislative coherence with all these proposals as they all include certain provisions on data sharing.   III/ Chapter 2: Business-to-consumer and business-to-business data-sharing Our members are innovators who develop mobile technology products. Their use and sharing of data with others affect how their products function and how consumers engage with those products/services, so business-to-business data-sharing is immensely important to them. They share data with other companies to optimise their supply chain, predict maintenance needs, move to circular production, train algorithms for AI, design innovative solutions/products, and many other purposes. Responsibly increased B2B data-sharing would boost the emerging EU data economy and help job growth, innovation, and competitiveness and SMEs could especially benefit.  Chapter 2 sets out the rights to data access by establishing a list of obligations. The Act states that products and services should, by default and design, make data that a user, individual, or organisation is generating available to that user or individual. The user should then be able to use this data or freely share this data, while the data holder is obligated to make it available to third parties upon the user’s request. The data made available cannot be used to develop products in competition with the data holder and users or third parties may not share that data with organisations designated as gatekeepers under the DMA. Trade secrets may only be disclosed to third parties subject to an agreement, only to the extent strictly necessary to fulfil the agreed-upon purpose, and only when specific, agreed-upon measures are taken by the third party to preserve the confidentiality of the trade secret.   We welcome the increased transparency and data access the provisions above will generate. Prohibiting the sharing of data with designated gatekeepers could have pro-competitive benefits for SMEs. However, although micro and small enterprises will be exempt from these obligations, we note that many SME products and services rely on data and infrastructure provided by larger companies. These obligations, while well-intentioned, may have negative impacts on the development of future technologies like IoT

devices or AI. The nascent IoT market is developing and using many new solutions including proprietary, standards-based, and open-source services. As these markets change and develop, there is a potential for challenges to market fairness to emerge. However, such an assessment should never be easily or broadly made and should only be based on a properly scoped market assessment and a strong evidence base. The obligation to not use data that has been made available to compete with the data holder’s product or services doesn’t hinder third parties from competing with the data holder by providing a related service or product that is different from the one that the data originated from. Data carries economic value and excessive obligations related to data sharing may steer companies away from developing new technologies. Disincentivising companies that SMEs rely on to share data could have dangerous ripple effects as obtaining and retaining data may become a legal and competitive risk that companies aren’t willing to take.   Specifically, the Data Act could make personal data available in anonymised formats to all interested parties if the data is legally processed and shared and fulfils the user’s needs. Concerning non-personal data, particularly trade secrets, we believe more restrictions and a clearer distinction between types of data is necessary. For example, eligible market participants should not have access to all machine-generated data without personal reference. Data holders should be reasonably compensated for such data as an incentive and encourage new data-based services and products. The Commission should come up with a balanced framework and clarify what constitutes personal and non-personal data in the context of the Data Act and GDPR. As chapter 2 covers B2C and B2B data-sharing, we encourage the Commission to provide clear liability terms in cases of data misuse, e.g. for data holders to claim damages from the user or data recipient.    Concerning Article 4 and 5 of the Data Act, we support the right of portability but strongly urge against legislative intervention into markets where there is no strong evidence base established that clearly identifies a portability problem. Commission intervention into markets where it is not needed would significantly distort those markets and should be done carefully, if at all. Should a clear problem be identified, only then should the Commission consider the tools like standard APIs, open standards and interoperable data formats, timeframes, and other technical elements. Absent clear evidence of a market failure, the Commission mandating any such elements would distort that market and upend competition and innovation. The right to portability should entail the ability of a business user to reasonably access, change, control, and move their data. While such a right is important, it is another matter as to whether Commission action is needed to enforce that right. Commission assistance concerning data portability for business users should thus be approached on a case-by-case (market) basis. Such a need may be present, for example, in the digital healthcare context, an area where the Commission is separately proposing legislation at this time.  Further, we again request the Commission clarify how this provision will interact with Article 20 of the GDPR. How can a company designated as a gatekeeper under the DMA receive data from individual users who are exercising their Article 20 rights, when the Data Act prohibits the same company from obtaining such data? The Commission must clarify which law takes precedence. Considering the provision on trade secrets, we note that App Association members rely on the full range of tools available to them to protect trade secrets. All are essential, particularly to SMEs. Although SMEs are exempt from Chapter 2 obligations, we emphasise that disclosing trade secrets to any third party is highly risky. Companies could decide simply not to collect data and the enforcement of such agreements could disadvantage SMEs. We urge the Commission to ensure that parties cannot be compelled to share trade secrets under the Data Act’s requirements; alternatively, the Commission should clearly explain which trade secrets, in practice, are subject to sharing agreements. Further, we note again that the Data Act currently does not include safeguards like liability terms for data holders to ensure that users, third parties, and public authorities do not disclose or unlawfully use their trade secrets. The Commission should revisit this issue to provide additional mechanisms to protect trade secrets.   IV/ Chapter 3: Obligations for data holders legally obliged to make data available Chapter 3 essentially mandates B2B data sharing, obliging data holders to make data available to a data recipient on fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory (FRAND) terms. The provisions specify that the data holder and data recipient should agree on the terms for making the data available, including reasonable compensation for access to the data. The burden of demonstrating that the terms of data availability are non-discriminatory is on data holders. The Act also provides for access to dispute settlement bodies to settle disputes for data holders and data recipients concerning the determination of FRAND terms and the transparent manner of making data available (Article 10).  We strongly believe that data sharing mandates on ‘fair, reasonable, proportionate, transparent, and non-discriminatory conditions’ may raise significant competition policy issues across EU industries by designating the data subject to the rule as an essential facility. Such a designation should only find a basis in an identified problem supported by strong evidence. We urge the Commission to recognise that ‘FRAND’ has a special meaning within the standardisation and patent licensing space where patent holders choose to declare their patents as, in effect, essential facilities for purposes of using an open standard. The FRAND licensing approach to standard-essential patents (SEPs) licensing is not easily applied to general B2B data-sharing as in SEP licensing scenarios, a licensor party voluntarily trades the opportunity for significant market power and access to a broader licensee pool for curtailing their full options as a patent holder. Outside of the standardisation/SEP context, such a dynamic does not map to any B2B scenarios (instead, in the context of the Data Act, the Commission is effectively declaring data sets subject to its rules as ‘essential’). We, therefore, strongly urge the Commission to avoid using ‘FRAND’ in any context outside of SEP licensing and remove this language from the Data Act as FRAND terms should not be applied to general data access.   Further, it is unclear whether these provisions apply to micro-firms and SMEs that are data holders. If so, they could not make a profit from sharing data with other micro-firms or SMEs, because the Act states that costs of obtaining data for SMEs cannot exceed the cost the data holder incurs for making it available. In that case, any data sharing between micro-firms and SMEs would be a zero-sum game and seriously hinder their growth as they lose out on profits. Larger companies sharing data with other large companies, however, would continue to receive profits, further widening the gap between them and SMEs. The Commission must ensure that micro-firms and SMEs can make profits from sharing their data with other industry players of their size.   V/ Chapter 4: Unfair terms related to data access and use between enterprises Chapter 4 covers the contractual terms of data sharing, stating that in cases where one party unilaterally imposes contractual terms on a micro-firm or an SME, those terms must be FRAND. Unfair contract terms that a data holder unilaterally imposes on a micro-firm or SME would not be binding under the Data Act.

We believe that the application of an ‘unfairness test’, to prevent unilateral imposition by one party of unfair contractual terms on another, could contribute to increasing data sharing between businesses.  We appreciate the intention behind this provision is to reduce power imbalances in negotiations and ensure larger companies cannot take advantage of smaller companies. Although we again caution against applying the FRAND concept to data access/sharing, applying it to contract terms may be more feasible, as it resembles how FRAND applies to the concept of SEPs. We reiterate, however, that in the SEP context FRAND licensing is something a licensor voluntarily commits to in exchange for significant market power and access to a broader pool of licensees for their SEP. The market power of SEP holders, in our view, is different from that of large data holders and taking the FRAND concept outside of the standardisation context is something the Commission should re-examine and consider very carefully. We also invite the Commission to examine contractual imbalances between large companies and between micro-firms and SMEs. In the data context, company size isn’t always the deciding factor, but rather the value, substitutability, and diversity of the data a company owns.   Chapter 4 further requires the Commission to ‘develop and recommend non-binding model contractual terms on data access and use to assist parties in drafting and negotiating contracts with balanced contractual rights and obligations’ (Article 34). While the App Association believes that model contract terms for voluntary use in B2B data-sharing contracts could contribute to increasing data sharing between businesses, it depends on the details of the model contract terms and the data sharing situations to which they would apply. The rights of all parties to freely negotiate must be preserved.  VI/ Chapter 5: Making data available to public sector bodies and Union institutions, agencies, or bodies based on exceptional need Chapter 5 covers the situations in which businesses should make data available to government bodies. As such, the Data Act mandates that a data holder should provide data upon request to a public sector body or an EU institution, agency, or body demonstrating an exceptional need. Exceptional needs could be caused by public emergencies such as public health emergencies, major natural or human-induced disasters, or where a lack of available data prevents a public body from fulfilling a specific task in the public interest. The Act specifies that these requests have to be proportionate and must be used for the intended purpose as specified only. Businesses are expected to provide data to respond to public emergencies for free, while in other cases of exceptional need data holders will receive reasonable compensation.   We agree with the Commission that overall B2G data sharing is currently limited and could increase to serve the public interest. Currently, B2G data sharing lacks a framework that would provide transparency and harmonisation across the Member States. We note, however, that in crisis situations and public emergencies businesses have voluntarily cooperated with public sector bodies and shared data, and there is not currently a reason why this would change (and the Commission has not demonstrated systemic problems with B2G data sharing in such circumstances). Rather than mandating B2G data sharing, we encourage the Commission to develop structures that incent businesses to voluntarily make more of their data available to the public sector. These incentives could be monetary or non-monetary, e.g. tax incentives, increased know-how and innovation through co-creation with public bodies, reputation/public recognition programmes (e.g. corporate social responsibility), or investment of public funds to support the development of trusted technical tools for B2G data-sharing. The EU’s B2G data sharing report includes many examples of B2G data sharing.2 When public sector bodies clearly outline the purpose for their reasonable request, businesses are often more willing to share their data on reasonable terms. Commission should thus prioritise voluntary data exchanges as they are less bureaucratic and can happen faster. Since the Commission has not identified a market failure in this context, we encourage the Commission to either justify this intervention or remove it from the proposal.  In terms of definitions, it remains unclear which data is covered and what constitutes ‘public interest’. The Commission should narrowly define both terms to increase legal certainty. Similarly, the overly broad definition of public emergencies/exceptional needs could leave room for abuse of this right if the B2G data sharing mandate remains without revision. The EDPB has also expressed concerns about the right of public sector bodies to request company data in ‘exceptional’ situations without further specifying what qualifies as a ‘public emergency’. Further, we urge the Commission to implement appropriate safeguards for B2G data sharing, including data security measures and protection of commercially sensitive information, specific rules on proportionality and reasonableness of the request, transparent reporting on how the public authority has used the data, and limitations regarding how long public bodies may use or store specific datasets before having to destroy them. The current draft does not seem to include sufficient protections for privacy, security, and intellectual property rights, which are all issues that inhibit data sharing that the Data Act could address. If the Commission moves forward with this B2G data sharing mandate, it must also ensure that public sector bodies have the capabilities to securely and effectively handle and process the possibly large amounts of data businesses provide. For transparency purposes, we believe it would be useful to oblige public sector bodies to (1) share the intended ways for such data to be shared, and how it will be secured; and (2) report to data holders on how the data they made available was used.   As stated previously in this document, the Data Act must preserve trade and professional secrecy for intellectual property protection and thus should exempt such data from B2G sharing obligations. We also reiterate our previous observation that liability terms are unclear. Businesses should not be liable for the data they share with government bodies, and we encourage the Commission to clarify this.   While the App Association shares the Commission’s desire to promote fair, reliable, and transparent access to and use of big data held by private companies for public services and policymaking, we do not believe that legislating public sector rights to access privately held data is the correct instrument to achieve this goal. Instead, a flexible framework for data access and use that includes requirements for data sharing addressing feasibility and the need for scaled risk management, among other factors. In particular, we encourage the Commission to bring together private and public stakeholders to reach joint agreements and to ensure the most efficient and productive debates in this forum and any policies that would result.   VII/ Chapter 6: Switching between data processing services Chapter 6 covers cloud switching and interoperability, providing that contracts should contain clauses that support switching, interoperability requirements, and a transition period to prohibit data processing service providers from charging any fee for switching. We welcome that the proposal does not mandate specific technical standards or interfaces and requires services to be compatible with existing European standards or open interoperability technical specifications. We advise the Commission to continue to refrain from binding obligations like mandating Standard Contractual Clauses or using high-level legal requirements for cloud providers in the Data Act. Developing more specific legal requirements depending on the distinct conditions in each case that are tailored to a cloud provider’s contractual, technical, and economic situation seems like a more useful solution. We also urge the Commission to refrain from mandating that data must be exported in a structured, widely used machine-readable format, as this may not be possible for smaller providers in particular. Similarly, the maximum allowed timeframes to complete porting should be flexible and scalable to companies of different sizes.

 Generally, we appreciate the Commission facilitating the process of switching between data processing services. As it finalises this draft proposal, we encourage the Commission to define terms in this chapter more narrowly, e.g. what is an ‘application’, or what does ‘whole service offering’ or ‘functional equivalence’ mean? When defining these terms more clearly, the Commission must also consider practical implications for cloud providers.   VIII/ Chapter 7: International contexts non-personal data safeguards Chapter 7 requires data processing service providers to take all ‘reasonable legal, technical, and organisational measures’ to prevent the international transfer of government access to non-personal data held in the EU that would conflict with European or national laws. Companies can only transfer data internationally if a legal basis exists, e.g. a mutual legal assistance treaty (MLAT). Without an MLAT, the decision or judgement must meet other conditions such as proportionality.  International data flows are critical to a successful business environment for SMEs. Scenarios in which a cloud computing service or other data processing service provider that is processing data on our members’ behalf is subject to an order or request based on foreign legislation for the mandatory transfers of your company/organisation data present a risk to our members as an order or request may lead to the disclosure and/or misappropriation of a trade secret or other confidential business information.  Generally, the App Association supports introducing safeguards against non-lawful access requests to non-personal data. In the proposed Data Act, what constitutes ‘legal, technical and organisational measures’, who would assess their reasonableness, and how remains unclear. To eliminate legal uncertainty for businesses, we urge the Commission to clarify these aspects. Such clarification could be achieved in the form of a list of suggested/acceptable safeguards (taking into consideration existing frameworks and standards) for cloud and data processing service providers.   Another clarification we would welcome concerns the verification process of requests from non-EU/European Economic Area public authorities. Businesses need clear guidelines on how to undertake such an assessment and how to verify a request satisfactorily. To develop such guidelines, the Commission should consult industry stakeholders to make sure verification is practically possible.   IX/ Chapter 8: Interoperability To enable operability, the Data Act introduces several requirements in Chapter 8. All operators of data spaces must sufficiently describe the contents of a dataset, use restrictions, etc., to allow data recipients to find, access, and use the data. Operators of data spaces must also publicly describe the data structures, data formats, and classification schemes; as well as sufficiently describe the technical means to access the data, such as application programming interfaces (APIs); and enable the interoperability of smart contracts. The European Commission also reserves the right to prescribe common standards. When using smart contracts to make data available to others, the contracts must be robust, safely terminable and interruptible, able to archive or continue data, and cover access control. First, we note that the current draft does not define ‘data space’ or ‘operator’, hence it is not clear to which entities these obligations apply. The draft also does not consider that there may be more than one operator per data space, which is frequently the case in many existing data spaces that consist of a governing entity and a contracting operator. In cases of decentralised data spaces, no operator may be identifiable.

We appreciate the inclusion of smart contracts here, as they are a nascent field that offers great potential, particularly for SMEs. However, we believe that smart contract standardisation should occur organically from the bottom up, based on a strong consensus of impacted stakeholders, via open and consensus standards bodies (and not driven by top-down mandates from government entities), rather than Commission-prescribed standards. Smart contracts could support the data economy by facilitating the creation of a standard baseline for data sharing, which also has the potential to decrease SMEs’ legal fees for contract review. If implemented across the EU, smart contracts (based on voluntary standards developed through a European standards organisation) could facilitate continuous, automated data sharing and pooling.  We urge the Commission not to reserve the right to prescribe common standards for itself or formally request that European standardisation development organisations design standards or the necessary APIs before concluding a properly scoped market assessment. Similarly, we advise the Commission to refrain from specifying standard APIs, open standards, and allowable data formats through the prescription of common standards unless a thorough market examination, using a strong evidence base, responsibly concludes there is a problem the Commission should address. Further, there are numerous international standardisation efforts in existence (and that are being created all the time) that provide bottom-up stakeholder consensus solutions to portability problems. Government-mandated, top-down requirements on European standards bodies raise the significant risk of creating EU-only standards that would put technical barriers to trade in place unnecessarily, ultimately harming European businesses trying to compete globally, as well as European consumers.  X/ Chapter 9: Implementation and enforcement The Data Act leaves enforcement and infringement penalties to the Member States, while the Commission would recommend voluntary model contractual terms concerning access to and use of data.  We are concerned that leaving the design of liability, remedy, and penalty regimes up to the Member States will cause unnecessary complexity, fragmentation in the single market, and diverging implementation of the Data Act across the EU. A lack of fully specified enforcement rules risks creating gaps and inviting forum shopping in different states, and we encourage the Commission to reconsider establishing an EU-wide enforcement entity. If the Commission decides to move forward with new competent Member State authorities, we would further request a clarification of the division of responsibilities between those authorities and the Data Protection Authorities.   XI/ Chapter 10: Sui generis right under Directive 1996/9/EC Chapter 10 states that the sui generis right (Article 7) does not apply to databases containing data obtained from or generated by the use of a product or a related service in order not to hinder the exercise of the right of users to access and use such data (Article 4) or of the right to share such data with third parties (Article 5).  Although we believe an update of the Database Directive could be useful to clarify its scope and increase legal certainty in terms of access regimes for databases and consider new aspects like machine-generated data and IoT to address existing legal uncertainties around data access and sharing, especially in B2B and B2G contexts, the proposed Article 35 seems to restrict database protection and related investment incentives. Instead of eliminating database protection altogether, the Data Act should exhaust the database protection right only in scenarios where it allows data access or use.

XII/ Conclusion  We share the ambition of the European Commission to facilitate data use, access, and sharing across the European Union and appreciate the positive emphasis on SMEs in the proposed Data Act and encourage the Commission to address the issues highlighted in our contribution. When specifying the policy proposals in the Data Act, we urge the Commission to continue to keep SMEs in mind and consider the flexibility and scalability of the proposed requirements. The Data Act is a complex piece of legislation, and it will have an impact on the day-to-day operations of many companies doing business in the EU.   The App Association remains at your disposal to provide further input and would welcome the opportunity to work with the Commission to develop scalable solutions that benefit all actors in the European data economy. We thank the European Commission in advance for its consideration of our submission, and we look forward to engaging further in the future.   Sincerely,

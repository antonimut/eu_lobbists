1. Medical fitness to drive: importance of vision and regular eye examinations 
 
The task of driving requires appropriate visual, motor and cognitive skills to safely and 
effectively operate a vehicle. The Driving Licence D irective specifies requirements on visual 
functions. These medical requirements are there for a reason; vision affects how a person 
drives and of course applies throughout the full driving career. Indeed, without adequate 
vision, driving is dangerous and undermines the EU’s long -term goal to move road deaths 
close to zero by 2050 ("Vision Zero"). 
 
However, not only is there poor compliance with the existing requirements, but the Directive 
is currently implemented inconsistently across the EU (“ECOO Visual Standards for Driving in 
Europe” A consensus paper (2017)  https://www.ecoo.info/wp-
content/uploads/2017/01/Visual-Standards-for-Driving-in-Europe-Consensus-Paper-
January-2017....pdf). This is clearly a result of the fact that the requirements of Annex III are 
open to interpretation. A number of non-technical changes to Annex III are needed to clarify 
the existing requirements (see section 2 below).  
 
Added to this, we know that visual functions deteriorate with age, this is natural and applies 
to everyone. It is therefore surprising that there is no requirement for visual functions to be 
examined on regular basis. 
 
We therefore call upon the Commission to take both the implementation of the visual 
requirements and the need for regular eye examinations for driving into account when 
revising the Directive. We would note that this can be easily achieved. Screening for visual 
fitness is most effective when integrated into a person’s normal requirements to check their 
eye health regularly and they could simply demonstrate this upon renewal of t heir driving 
licence. 
 
While it is difficult to breakdown the multifactorial nature of accidents and uncover to what 
extent poor vision plays a role in road safety, studies have shown that vision is an often -
overlooked variable in accident investigations.1  
 
This however does not mea n that there is no evidence to support the case that vision is 
important for safe driving. There is an accumulating body of research reporting that poorer 
visual function is associated with reduced driving performance (e.g.2,3,4,5,6,7), and, importantly, 
that with improvement of vision, accident rates are reduced .8,9,10,11 Furthermore, in-person

A list of references to studies supporting the importance of vision for safe driving is provided 
below. Some  highlighted examples of evidence from studies that have looked at this matter: 
 
• Prof Joanne Wood, a recognised world expert in vision and driving, conducted a recent 
seminal systematic review of the evidence of vision disorders on driving and accident 
risk.13 Synthesising the evidence from several studies she reported that there is a 
significantly increased motor vehicle crash risk with binocular visual field impairment. 
This review acknowledges the need for future studies to further explore the impact of 
vision disorders and impairment on driving outcomes to inform evidence-based policy 
and fitness to drive guidelines. 
• While younger drivers are overrepresented in crashes, when one takes account of 
experience (i.e. exposure to driving), it becomes evident that older drivers are more 
likely to be involved in accidents .3 As we age, our visual capacity diminishes and our 
risk of ocular abnormality increases. However, because these changes are gradual 
they may go unnoticed. While some drivers choose to self -limit in terms of the time 
of day and conditions when they  drive, many drivers are known to overestimate their 
visual capacity at night 14 and lack insight into their visual limitations .15  A large-scale 
study of 1,000 people in Belgium aged 60 years and over found that 10% of them did 
not meet the visual standard for driving with their present, uncorrected vision.16  
• Cataract is a common visual disorder and has a gradual onset with age. It is treated by 
surgery to restore vision and is one of the most common surgical procedures in 
eyecare.  Several studies (most notably Meuleners et al.9 in a whole population study 
in Western Australia) have investigated crash rates pre- and post-cataract surgery, and 
consistently report that cataract surgery decreases accident rates and provides cost 
savings. 
• A recent study, commissioned by National Society for Road Safety in Sweden used a 
driving simulator to measure driving performance in younger and older people with 
and without visual impairment.17 They report that those who had impaired vision saw 
pedestrians or vehicles later than those with better vision. This resulted in risky driving 
behaviour because they reacted to the object (braking or stopping) later or were 
driving faster than was safe.  These findings are also supported by a US study using 
naturalistic driving data which measured visual processing speed and found an 
elevated risk of crashes and near-crash involvement in older drivers.18  
 
 
2.  Annex III: non-technical changes 
 
The following changes to Annex III clarify the visual requirements to avoid the confusions and 
discrepancies across the EU that currently exist. These do not change the actual requirements 
and simply clarify the current standards.  
 
 
 
 3 
• Clarification on method to assess Visual Acuity:  The measurement of visual acuity is 
a core established measure of visual function. The only accurate way to measure visual 
acuity is by means of a validated visual acuity chart. This should be clarified to avoid 
the use of other methods, such as the Licence Number Plate Test which does not 
measure visual acuity and is not carried out in standardised conditions.  
 
• Clarification on professionals able to conduct an assessment of vision for driving : 
The Directive states that a ‘competent medical authority’ should assess vision for 
driving. A cross Europe a  variety of professions deliver eye -care services, including 
medical docto rs (such as ophthalmologists), optometrists and opticians. This is 
defined by legislation in each Member State, including whether or not these eye care 
specialists are classed as medical practitioners. Using only the term ‘medical authority’ 
in an EU Directive can therefore inadvertently result in excluding appropriately trained 
and qualified professionals from a role in driver vision assessment. The wording 
‘appropriately trained and qualified eye care professionals  authorised by national 
law’, which is the phrasing used in other EU legislation  (see for instance the Medical 
Devices Regulation), should be added.  
 
• Clarification on units of measurements for Vision standards : In Annex III, the 
minimum vision is expressed using decimal notation; however, there are two different 
methods of measuring visual acuity that can be expressed as a decimal. This creates 
discrepancies and confusions across Europe. Visual acuity measurements need to be 
expressed as both the Decimal notation and the LogMAR equivalent.

End FGM EU’s reaction to the European Commission’s Proposal for a 
Directive to combat violence against women and domestic violence 
End FGM European Network (End FGM EU) represents 32 organizations across 15 European countries. End 
FGM EU strongly welcomes the European Commission’s Proposal for a Directive to combat violence against 
women and domestic violence, and in particular the ad dressing of specific forms of gender -based violence 
and harmful practices including female genital mutilation and forced marriage.  
This text provides landmark provisions  for ensuring increased prevention, protection, prosecution and 
integrated policies  at the EU level , in line with the pillars and the provisions of the Council of Europe 
Convention on preventing and combating violence against women and domestic violence (Istanbul 
Convention), with the further ambition to provide higher minimum standards and  harmonization across the 
EU. 
We particularly welcome the comprehensive approach that the Directive aims to take to combat violence 
against women. 
While criminalization of female genital mutilation ( FGM) with specific provision s in national laws is key to 
ensure legal harmonization across Member States,  this needs to be accompanied by  the scaling up of  
prevention and protection measures according to the highest minimum standards. The proposed Directive 
importantly rules for increased support services for survivors tailored to their specific needs . With a specific 
reference to FGM, it is important that Member States are required to increase the accessibility to holistic care 
and information and that reconstructive surgery, when the patient makes an accompanied and informed 
decision to proceed with it,  is performed free of charge and is integrated within multidisciplinary holistic 
care.  
We welcome the recognition coming from the analytical framework of intersectiona lity that violence can 
intersect and be triggered or worsened by other forms of discrimination, such as nationality, race, colour, 
ethnic or social origin, genetic features, language, religion or belief, political or any other opinion, membership 
of a national minority, property, birth, disability, age or sexual orientation. The intersectionality lens should be 
an overarching principle guiding the translation into practice of all legal provisions. It could be further 
strengthened in the text, by being included in the legal provisions as a tool for effective transposition and 
implementation of the Directive. This approach should  also be accompanied by  concrete provisions on its 
applicability. In particular, when  it comes to  specific forms of GBV like FGM, it is key that the response is 
appropriate, ensuring the involvement of community members and that there is no bias (especially racist 
bias) in reporting or response mechanisms. To increase consistency with othe r EU policies, th e text of the 
Directive should also refer to the Anti -Racism Action Plan  2020-20251. An example of intersecting 
discrimination for a FGM survivor could be that of an undocumented migrant, with a linguistic barrier, needing 
access to specialist support and healthcare. Member States should ensure universal health coverage for 
all migrants regardless of status: undocumented people living with the consequences of FGM should not be

Additionally, alternative forms of public fund should be established to help migrants living with FGM to access 
the necessary support and health care services2.  
The training of professionals outlined in Art. 37 is key. On this point, the Directive should encourage Member 
States to include community health workers among the officially recognised part of the national health system, 
and make more funds available for either NGOs or health care institutions to employ interpreters and cultural 
mediators3. The need to accelerate all support and preventive measures aimed at achieving the 
abandonment of harmful practices such as FGM must be backed by adequate funding . Adequate funding 
means more funding and more accessibility to funding mechanisms in particular for small grassroots 
organisations who are often led by volunteers.  In this regard, as far as forms of GBV such as FGM are 
concerned, the Directive should be in line with global commitments of achieving elimination of harmful 
practices by 2030 as provided for by Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 5.3.  
When it comes to providing support with a one -stop-shop approach, it is also important to guarantee that 
these centres are accessible and geographically widespread on the territory. 
We welcome the importance attributed to tackling gender stereotypes from e arly education, aimed at 
eradicating the harmful norms that stem from different beliefs and are enabling factors for the justification 
and perpetuation of gender-based violence. A gender-transformative approach to end GBV and FGM should 
be seen as the most worthwhile preventive measure to achieve the sustainable elimination of the practice4. 
To eradicate GBV and F GM in particular a strong Directive should harmonize prevention and support 
measures in Member States, involving affected communities at all stages. Harmful practices that 
disproportionately affect women, such as early and forced marriage, are perpetuated as a consequence of 
the same roots and norms based on gender inequality. A stronger Directive should tackle all harmful practices 
that represent a form of GBV, including obstetrical violence and lack of access to SRH services, not only in 
the recitals but within the articles.  
The Directive would be strengthened by having gender-based violence as recognized as a Eurocrime within 
Article 83(1) TFEU  and will continue to support  actions aimed at  achieving this. Full accession of the 
European Union to the Istanbul Convention should continue to be pursued. 
The Directive implementation and monitoring framework should be clearly outlined, in line with the 
Communication on EU law: Better results through better application5 to enforce thorough transposition of the 
provisions at the Member State level and  systematically integrate civil society to ensure correct application 
and enforcement as well as State accountability.

Cyber security of recharching infrastructure for electric vehicles 
 
Poor cyber security of recharging points is a threat to the electricity grid 
 
The number of electric vehicles (EVs) and recharging points in Europe is growing rapidly. The vast 
majority of recharging points are directly or indirectly ‘connected’ to the internet and they collectively 
represent a very large electric power demand. Good cyber security of recharging points is therefore 
crucial for the stability of the electricity grid. 
 
In order to prevent overloading of the electricity grid at certain times and locations, and to properly 
align the recharging of EVs with the availability and price of renewable energy, Smart Charging is or 
will be applied. Recharging points are then controlled remotely from a back office. Home recharging 
points can be connected to a home energy management system which in turn is connected to a 
central system.  
 
If the cyber security of recharging points is not well managed, this is a potential risk not only to the 
mobility system itself, but also to the stability and security of the public electricity grid. A hack of a 
large number of recharging points could cause damage on two levels: 
 
1. A disruption of the national system balance. If a hacker were to suddenly switch off a very large 
number of recharging points, the question is whether the TSO, would be able to adapt production 
capacity and/or demand quickly enough. If this is not successful, a national blackout could be the 
result. At a switchable power capacity of 3 GW, the stability of the entire European electricity grid 
could be at risk. 
 
2. Local overloading of the distribution grid. If the regional grid operator uses smart charging to tailor 
recharging to the maximum grid capacity and a hacker were to override those control signals, 
components in the power grid such as cables or transformer stations could be overloaded and, in 
extreme cases, catch fire. 
 
Recharging eco-system 
 
Recharging points are one component in a recharging eco-system which includes several new market 
roles. Most recharging points are connected to the backend of a Charge Point Operator (CPO). Most 
end users have access to public recharging stations through an e-Mobility Service Provider (EMSP). 
CPO’s and EMSPs may be participating in (international) roaming platforms. Smart Charging Service 
Providers (SCSP) may aggregate a large number of recharging points in order to adjust the recharging 
process to the energy markets, both for supply of (renewable) energy and for flexibility/balancing 
services as requested by DSO’s or TSO’s. Some market parties do not develop the software required 
for their operation themselves, but procure SaaS from companies offering white label fulfilment for 
multiple clients. 
 
Since EV-recharging is a relatively young market, new market roles may appear over time.  
 
As a result, cyber security of recharging infrastructure does not stop at requiring security 
measurements for the recharging stations themselves, but should also cover all these new market 
roles and the interaction between various parties.  
 
EU-cyber security legislation must therefore cover the hardware and (embedded) software of the 
recharging point, as well as the connection to the backend system and the interfaces between the 
systems used by various market parties in the recharging eco-system. It should also cover future 
technological and market developments.

Legislation 
 
At this moment in time, there is no comprehensive EU-legislation yet for the cyber security of EV-
recharging infrastructure.  
 
Several EU-instruments may apply to or refer to recharging points for electric vehicles: 
 
- NIS (Network and Information Security Directive): this is the main legal framework for cyber 
security in the EU. It applies to Operators of Essential Services (OESs) and Digital Service 
Providers. These entities are required to implement security measures, will be supervised and 
are obliged to report cyber incidents to the national Computer Security Incident Response 
Team. In the energy sector, TSOs and DSOs are OESs however Recharging Point Operators 
are not. 
The EC is now preparing an update of the NIS defining Essential Entities (EEs) replacing the 
old category OES.  
 
- AFID (Alternative Fuels Infrastructure Directive): the AFID mainly focuses on public recharging 
points which member states must implement and specifies the technical requirements for such 
recharging points.  
The EC is now preparing an update which will get the status of Regulation rather than 
Directive. 
 
- EPBD (Energy Performance of Buildings Directive): this includes rules for implementation of 
private recharging points for newly constructed buildings and major reconstructions. For the 
specification of the recharging points reference is made to the AFID/AFIR.  
 
- NCCS (Network Code on Cyber Security): this is a draft network code for cybersecurity 
aspects of cross-border electricity flows submitted by ENTSO-E and EU DSO entity to ACER. 
 
- ITS (Intelligent Transport Systems): this directive aims to integrate telecommunications, 
electronics and information technologies with transport engineering in order to plan, design, 
operate, maintain and manage transport systems. 
 
- Radio Equipment Directive: this addresses the secure communication between information 
systems and devices. 
 
None of the above-mentioned EU-instruments specifically addresses the cyber security of the 
recharging infrastructure at this moment in time. The EU-policy regarding the cyber security of 
recharging infrastructure seems scattered and there is no comprehensive approach. In this context, 
the proposed Cyber Resilience Act may act as a safety net to fill in the legislative gaps which are not 
covered by specific instruments. Thus it covers the entire recharging ecosystem, today and in the 
future. 
 
Furthermore, the CRA offers important benefits: 
 
1. The horizontal approach covers a wide range and includes new market roles and system 
interfaces that may appear in future. 
2. SaaS can be included. 
3. The CRA covers the whole life cycle of a product (please note that for a recharging points this 
may be 10 years or longer). 
4. Conformity assessment rules can be based upon a risk-based approach (please note that the 
risk of a blackout of the electricity grid is the product of a chance that may be very small and 
an enormous societal and economic impact).

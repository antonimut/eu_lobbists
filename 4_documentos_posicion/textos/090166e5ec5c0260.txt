Statement of the Austrian Raiffeisen Association 
in the context of the consultation on the European Commission's proposal for a Corporate 
Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD) 
 
 
The Austrian Raiffeisen Association is the umbrella organisation of the Austrian Raiffeisen 
Group. In particular, it represents the interests of the Raiffeisen Banking Group as well as 
those of the agricultural processing and commodity cooperatives. 
 
In th e tradition of Friedrich Wilhelm Raiffeisen, Raiffeisen places people at the centre of 
economic activity. We therefore support in principle a harmonised legal framework for 
corporate compliance with relevant human and environmental rights. This legal frame work 
must be practical, proportionate, legally secure and effective. 
 
The present draft does not meet these requirements, which are self -evident and 
fundamental from the point of view of the business community.  
 
Massive overload of SMEs through bureaucracy and certification requirements 
 
The draft continues the existing trend of excessive bureaucratic Commission "legistics", which 
characterises a number of  the Green Deal projects, especially in the area of sustainable 
financing, as follows: 
• Massive burden on SMEs due to bureaucratic and impractical requirements as well as 
increasing outsourcing of official tasks to companies. 
• Legal uncertainty, legal fragme ntation and massive technocra cy in the legislative 
process through increased references to future delegated acts, guidelines and 
standards, so that democratic participation is reduced to "shell legislation” 
• Failure to coordinate the individual projects of the "Green Deal" without taking into 
account that all proposals of the various DGs are to be implemented in a single 
undertaking (in the proposal, for example, the equally extraneous and superfluous 
inclusion of climate requirements, which are already regulated in other legal acts)  
• Outsourcing of legal interpretation and control to private certification bodies without 
appropriate legal remedies  
• Systemic cost and information advantages of very large companies and thus 
strengthening their market power over SMEs 
 
Increasingly, a legal framework is  being drafted that is geared to investor interests and can 
only be implemented by very large listed companies in terms of cost and effort. The SME 
driven economy  economy in the EU is already being left behind from the very first Commission 
drafts. However, without a strong, competitive SME economy, the necessary implementation 
of the climate policy goals of the Green Deal cannot be realised . It is urgently time that the 
economic pillar of sustainability, especially for SMEs, is taken into account in all Commission 
drafts.  
 
The relief for SMEs provided for in the draft is well -intentioned, but remains ineffective due 
to the "trickle-down" effect. In the reality of increasingly indirect legislation via market (thus 
beyond the principle of legality while strengthening market power), SMEs are forced to

"voluntarily" introduce corresponding due diligence system in order to be open and attractive 
for new business relationships in competition.  
In practice, it remains unclear why an obligated company should support SMEs in developing 
a standard if the associated effort/costs also benefit competitors who wait to enter into 
"established" business relationships for the time being.   
 
General applicability requireme nts are ineffective and create massive legal uncertainty in 
addition to bureaucracy 
Already in the consultation on sustainable corporate governance, we strongly advocated a 
sector- or topic-specific rather than a general approach, as only this can be specific enough to 
establish clear and thus legally secure as well as effective standards or facts. 
It must be taken into account that due diligence obligations for companies along supply chains 
are a new area of law, which is predominantly characterised b y voluntary standards. There is 
no experience with such a liability and sanctions regime outlined in the draft. The national 
procedures and instances are also not equipped for such a transfer of state tasks to 
companies.  
 
Lack of international integration 
In the adoption of the OECD Guidelines for Responsible Supply Chains, there are already 
contact points for each contracting state with complaint possibilities for those affected. It 
would seem obvious to strengthen the validity of the OECD Guidelines and their 
implementation in the EU by means of agreements between the OECD contracting states and 
to develop sanctionable and liable offences primarily within the scope of the OECD. 
Particularly in view of geopolitical developments, an international approach to the protection 
of human rights seems necessary. 
 
For the internal market, there must be protection of confidence for businesses and citizens  
The provisions of the draft should also apply to purely national or common market value 
chains without any refer ence to third countries. EU citizens and companies must be able to 
trust that the relevant human and environmental rights within the EU and the common 
market will be guaranteed by the states and their authorities. The European Commission has 
indicated in the explanatory notes to the draft that this is not necessarily the case. In order to 
protect confidence in the common market, full transparency of the facts and, if they are true, 
a fundamental political discussion of the protection of human rights within the EU is required. 
 
Far beyond the goal of minimum harmonisation 
The draft even goes far beyond the requirements in national supply chain laws that exist in 
some EU member states: e.g. in relation to the entire value chain - not supply chain, in the 
sanction and liability regulations, in the definition of obligated companies, trickle -down to 
micro-enterprises... 
 
Independent basis for claims in national labour, environmental and discrimination law 
The validity of the draft for intra-community and national value chains in conjunction with the 
trickle-down effect also leads to the creation of an independent and parallel basis for claims  
in national labou r, environmental and discrimination law,  including procedural and party 
rights.

The problem of the impact on national regulations and the coordination of interests in 
national policy by social partners and civil society is massively exacerbated by the 
indeterminacy of the actual obligations, by the burden of proof, but also by procedural rights. 
 
For example, Z 21 of Annex 1 contains - in addition to the often indeterminate specific facts 
of the individual articles of the Annex - a general applicability clause for a number of 
conventions.  
 
The important question of the burden of proof is not addressed in the draft, but even from a 
legalistic point of view, a burden of proof for potential victims in the area of relevant human 
and environmental rights violations can hardly be argued. It is therefore to be expected that 
the indeterminable liability risks will be passed on to SMEs in accordance with market power. 
 
Victims will continue to be punished 
From the point of view of agriculture, there are additional points of criticism of the proposal, 
which are in particular a consequence of t he validity of the directive also for purely regional, 
national and community value chains. 
 
In a global comparison, European agriculture is characterised by a high eco -social standard, 
which will be massively raised by the Green Deal. Corresponding import  protection in the 
sense of equivalent standards is therefore necessary. However, recent experience has shown 
that in trade agreements the EC is willing to sacrifice these interests for the export 
opportunities of other sectors.  Therefore a coordination on  EU trade agreements with the 
contents of the annexes is necessary. 
 
The "high impact" sectors mentioned are precisely those sectors within the EU that are 
exposed to massive competitive disadvantages due to eco -social dumping of imports from 
third countries.  
In the food industry, for example, agricultural production and processing are faced not only 
with imports, but also with the concentrated market power of retail.  
In the discussion of sustainable food systems, fair prices and fair contractual conditions are 
therefore central for the food industry. 
 
It is the consequence of a regulation without restriction to a necessary third -country 
reference, that these companies in a high-risk sector such as agriculture and the food industry, 
which with their national and community value chains are already at a co mpetitive 
disadvantage compared to imports, are now to be further burdened with bureaucratic 
requirements.  
 
A dairy with 250 employees /40 mio € turnover  - hardly a multinational company - would be 
obliged to set up a corresponding system of due diligence,  especially in its "established" 
business relations, i.e. with its regional milk suppliers, and to transfer the obligations to 
comply with the annexes of the draft to them via the contractual clauses.  
 
On the other hand, it is unclear - as is the case wit h so much of this draft - how the contract 
clauses will be formulated on part of retailers , how far the necessary inspection  and 
monitoring rights will apply and how the data on supply and value chains will not increase the

market power of retailers. These questions against the background of the increasing share of

The provision in the draft that national implementation should be concerned with competition 
law problems can at best be interpreted as a placeholder for an in depth assessment. 
 
Conclusion 
 
Against the backdrop of massive disruptions in global trade, questions of security of supply, 
disruptions in supply chains and an intensified multipolar world politics, the protection of 
human rights is a core political task of the European Union as well as of the member states. It 
is also the task of the EU to create a non -bureaucratically targeted, legally secure and 
constitutional legal framework for businesses - which are primarily SMEs. Within the EU, the 
principle of trust must apply, trade agreements must be coordinated with the validity of 
international conventions and only in a subsidiary manner, where there is no state influence 
of the EU or the member states, must the due diligence obligations of comp anies be applied 
in a practical and effective manner. 
 
In summary, a fundamental revision of the proposal is necessary, so that individual provisions 
cannot be addressed in the context of an initial consultation of the proposal. The necessary 
revision of the proposal should also be oriented towards the "cross-sectional material" of the 
existing national regulations in the EU, such as those in France, the Netherlands or Germany, 
in the sense of a minimum harmonization.

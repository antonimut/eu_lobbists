EU Commission proposal

Diligence (COM(2022) 71 final) 
Fair working conditions and better environmental and climate protection in the production and 
trade of goods around the world: these are the most important goals of the European Due Diligence 
Act. The TÜV Association highly welcomes the European Commission’s proposal to make due 
diligence obligations for responsible supply chains mandatory for companies operating in the EU. 
The European Union has now a historic opportunity to create clear rules and a level playing field for 
compliance with human rights and environmental due diligence obligations for all companies active 
in the European Single Market. Not least, the coronavirus pandemic illustrates the need to make 
supply relationships responsible and resilient.
Some European countries (France and the Netherlands, for instance) have already implemented 
mandatory measures. In Germany, a law on corporate due diligence in supply chains will enter into force in 
2023. According to a recent study by the TÜV Association 1, most companies welcome regulatory action 
for more sustainability in a globalized economic world. 56 percent of companies in Germany with 25 or 
more employees are in favour of a supply chain law. Only 29 percent oppose the law and 15 percent are 
undecided.  
However, it is also clear that individual companies cannot achieve this alone. Therefore, in the view of the 
respondents, the same requirements should apply to all competitors. Three out of four companies 
welcome that the legislation may create uniform standards for supply chains and that it may encourage 
investments in sustainable development. Furthermore, according to the respondents, the due diligence 
legislation will create a level playing field with equal rights and obligations, whereby paying attention to 
sustainability will no longer represent a competitive disadvantage. Another positive effect will be the 
creation of increased legal certainty. According to 69 percent of respondents, the due diligence 
legislation might also lead to a change in awareness of corporate behaviour. A good one in two companies 
is of the opinion that business relationships with suppliers are improving and that, thanks to risk 
management, the legislation will contribute to its economic success in the long term.

The TÜV Association welcomes the EU Commission's plans to establish binding requirements by 
considering the entire supply chain. Welcomed is also that the proposal includes comprehensive social 
and human rights, as well as environmental and climate-related due diligence obligations.  
For a successful implementation of the Due Diligence Act, the EU legislator should not only focus on 
providing comprehensive requirements, but also on ensuring that the requirements are effectively 
complied with. We strongly welcome that the proposal assigns an important role to independent third 
parties assessing compliance with due diligence obligations along the supply chain. Certifications and on-
site audits by independent conformity assessment bodies are important instruments to ensure 
compliance with social and environmental standards. Independent third party assessments can create the 
necessary confidence in the statements of the individual elements in the supply chain – thereby 
supporting companies and authorities, creating transparency for consumers and, after all, helping to 
contribute to an equitable and sustainable world.  
The TÜV Association is now calling for a swift legislative process. Given the long delays in the preparation 
of the proposal, the European Parliament and the Council should seek to adopt quickly their positions.

1. Scope (Art. 2) 
Apply a consistent risk-based approach 
We welcome the EU Commission's proposal to oblige in particular larger companies to comply with their 
due diligence obligations. Small and medium-sized companies are not directly in the scope of this 
proposal. However, a blanket exemption for SMEs could be problematic, as their economic activities can 
also lead to human rights violations and negative environmental impacts. Furthermore, thousands of 
European companies would be out of the scope of this legislation, undermining a much-needed level 
playing field and the overall effectiveness of Due Diligence obligations. Therefore, it is very positive that 
the Parliaments proposal2 includes those SMEs, which can be considered „high-risk” (textiles, agriculture 
and extraction of minerals as proposed by the EU Commission). However, small and medium-sized 
companies in particular often find it difficult to verify compliance with social and ecological standards in 
the third, fourth or fifth stage of their supply chain. They lack the human and financial resources to do so. 
Therefore, SMEs should face proportionate and reasonable obligations, and should receive additional 
support such as best practice training in understanding their responsibilities. 
Ensure an international level playing field 
We welcome the inclusion of companies from third countries that generate turnover in Europe. This will 
mitigate expected competitive disadvantages for European companies and is a first step towards an 
international level playing field. However, the proposal does not seem to provide a true level playing field. 
Threshold values for non-EU companies are much higher than for EU companies (e.g. 150 million EUR 
turnover inside the EU for third country companies, while for EU companies 150 million EUR worldwide). 
This means that EU companies that must comply with the directive are likely to be much smaller in practice 
than companies in third countries are. The EU Commission should work towards a true level playing field.

2. Due Diligence (Art. 4, 5-11) 
Strong focus on environmental due diligence as a right step 
We highly welcome the fact that the EU Commission is giving great attention to environmental due 
diligence with several environmental conventions being listed in the proposal. The included provisions go 
further than national due diligence legislation such as the German Supply Chain Act, thus fostering the 
Green Deal and the new Circular Economy Action Plan. According to a representative Forsa survey 
commissioned by the TÜV Association 3, a majority of German companies agrees that environmental and 
climate protection should lie on equal footing as human rights protection.  
No limitation of due diligence obligations to established business relationships 
The draft directive limits due diligence to established business relationships: „[…] take appropriate 
measures to identify actual and potential adverse human rights impacts and adverse environmental 
impacts arising from their own operations or those of their subsidiaries and, where related to their value 
chains, from their established business relationships.” (Art. 6)
While this limitation may at first glance appear reasonable from a business perspective, it runs counter to 
the UNGP and OECD Guidelines. They follow a risk-based approach and deliberately do not limit the scope 
of due diligence to a particular set of closely related business relationships.  Limiting due diligence to 
„established business relationships” creates a clear risk that in practice the focus of due diligence will not 
be defined by where the most severe risks and impacts occur in a company’s value chain, but by whether 
or not a business relationship can be characterized as „established”. Furthermore, this creates incentives 
for companies to avoid relationships that would need to be categorized as „lasting”. 
While the concept of „established business relationships” may have value as a basis for defining the scope 
of potential civil liability (Art. 22), using it to define the scope of the responsibility to carry out due 
diligence is problematic. In our view, it makes sense to limit civil liability to „established business 
relationships”, but aligning the scope of the duty to do due diligence to „business relationships”. This 
would also align with the Parliament’s recommendations in its initiative report of March 2021 to require 
due diligence to the full scope of the value chain in line with international standards, and to limit civil 
liability to a narrower set of situations.

3. Preventing and ending actual adverse impacts (Art. 7 and 8) 
The Commission proposal refers to two important instruments that companies should utilize in order to 
prevent or end actual adverse impacts (Art. 7 and 8): 
a) contractual assurances from a business partner with whom it has a direct business relationship 
that it will ensure compliance with the company’s code of conduct and, as necessary, a prevention 
action plan, including by seeking corresponding contractual assurances from its partners, to the 
extent that their activities are part of the company’s value chain (contractual cascading). 
b) the contractual assurances or the contract shall be accompanied by the appropriate measures to 
verify compliance. For the purposes of verifying compliance, the company may refer to suitable 
industry initiatives or independent third party verification systems. 
Both contractual terms and verification mechanisms are core elements of corporate due diligence 
practices. The TÜV Association welcomes that the proposal assigns a role to independent third parties 
with regard to the assessment of the contractual assurances or the contract itself.  
Independent conformity assessment bodies have become key actors in the implementation and 
verification of human rights and environment-related due diligence obligations of companies. This applies 
both to their procedural and organizational ex-ante obligations, and to the assessment of compliance 
with due diligence practices on-site.  
The value of third party assessments in the due diligence process 
The Commission proposal includes the obligation for companies to demonstrate, and for judicial and 
administrative bodies to assess, compliance with due diligence requirements. The involvement of 
conformity assessment bodies adds an additional layer of independent scrutiny to the due diligence 
process.  
Through their assessments (e.g. certification of management systems or supplier and supply chain audits 
according to Sedex/SMETA, amfori BSCI, SA 8000) conformity assessment bodies help companies to: 
> assure companies themselves that their due diligence systems are sufficient and all-encompassing, 
> assure companies that their due diligence obligations are being upheld on-site, 
> assure their customers (B2B-B2C) that they fully comply with the legislative provisions,  
> help companies to easily demonstrate their compliance with due diligence obligations vis-à-vis  
judicial and administrative bodies,

> help fostering the trust of stakeholders and consumers in their activities. 
Conformity assessment bodies equally reduce the burden on supervisory authorities (Art. 17, 18). Given 
widely diversified global supply chains and the large amount of companies affected, supervisory 
authorities within the member states will on their own not be able to adequately enforce the given 
provisions. It is therefore likely that official controls will mostly be limited to random documentary checks 
leaving out visits of production sites abroad. Independent third parties can support and relieve the 
authorities in their controls and enforcement mechanisms – also owing to their worldwide presence.  
Finally, third party assessments are an important transparency tool for companies to signal to their 
stakeholders – whether in B2B or B2C relationships – a well-functioning due diligence management 
system. Consumers, for instance, can have trust in product claims through a certification mark, be it the 
responsible sourcing of raw materials or a sustainable production. This helps to make informed choices in 
favor of sustainable products. 
Maintain high quality standards through accreditation 
It is also necessary to define standards for conformity assessment, and to set uniform quality standards 
for certification and audit reports. The international standards for conformity assessment and 
accreditation systems (ISO/IEC 17000) should be taken as the basis for this. The European system of 
accreditation ensures a high quality infrastructure. Therefore we recommend amending the definition of 
“independent third-party verification” (Art. 3) to ensure that only accredited bodies, which are 
established as legal persons, would be allowed to conduct verification regarding the implementation of 
the contractual assurances to prevent and mitigate identified adverse effects. These bodies should also 
be external to the companies in question. Internal auditors may lack the necessary distance from the 
company to properly examine and report potential breaches of contractual agreements with business 
partners.  
Accredited independent bodies have consistently to prove that they have both the necessary level of 
technical expertise to carry out such assessments and are independent from manufacturers and users 
alike. In order to further strengthen this quality infrastructure, the European Commission should oblige 
Member States and their competent bodies to accept accreditations as the only possible means for 
demonstrating competence.  
Industry initiatives must prove to meet all the due diligence requirements 
The Commission proposal also foresees the development of industry initiatives to be used to 
demonstrate compliance with the due diligence requirements. This approach is to be welcomed in 
principle.  However, industry schemes should only be recognised if they can prove to meet all the 
requirements established under Articles 4, 5-11, including a mandatory third party verification. This is

4. Accompanying measures (Art. 14) 
Provide guidance to companies and support them in terms of implementation 
According to Article 14, Member States and the Commission are obliged to offer accompanying measures 
to companies that fall within the scope of this Directive and to actors along the global supply chains that 
are indirectly affected. This is highly welcome. In particular, for small and medium-sized enterprises, 
guidance should be available to provide information on how best to comply with due diligence obligations. 
This might include, for example, minimum requirements for the creation of a code of conduct, appropriate 
measures and techniques for risk assessment and information on digital tools for sustainable supplier 
management. 
Integrate and list existing standards and certifications 
Existing industry-specific standards should be taken into account in the design of the due diligence 
legislation to an appropriate extent, but must be sufficiently distinct, suitable to meet the goals of the 
directive and independently assessable. Companies and suppliers certified based on recognised industry 
standards should find it easier to demonstrate compliance with their due diligence obligations. In this 
way, duplication of effort could be avoided and efforts to comply with social and ecological due diligence 
could be recognised accordingly. 
The Commission, in cooperation with the Member States, should issue guidance on the assessment of the 
appropriateness of industry schemes and multi-stakeholder initiatives and should draw up a list of 
suitable standards and certifications.  
5. Combating climate change (Art. 15) 
Article 15 requires Member States to ensure that companies submit a plan whereby the company's 
business model and strategy are consistent with the transition to a sustainable economy and with 
limiting global warming to 1.5 °C (with reference to the Paris Agreement). We welcome the fact that the 
EU Commission, in addition to environmental due diligence requirements, is taking up the limitation of 
climate change and a commitment to the 1.5 °C in its draft. We also welcome, that, in case climate change 
is or should have been identified as a principal risk for, or a principal impact of the company’s operations, 
the company must include emission reduction objectives in its plan.

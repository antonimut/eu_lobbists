Commerce supports European rules for corporate sustainability due diligence – yet a 
number of issues still require resolving 
The Finnish Commerce Federation and its members are focused on advancing corporate 
responsibility in their own activities. In principle, the Finnish Commerce Federation wel-
comes the Commission’s proposal for a Directive on corporate sustainability due dili-
gence. Although the promotion of corporate responsibility is a global challenge, which 
would require primarily global solutions, the Directive should help to ensure level-playing 
field between companies in the Single Market and to avoid the fragmentation of regula-
tion, which would be the inevitable result of purely national legislation. At the same time, 
European rules promote the corporate responsibility of businesses more effectively than 
any unilateral measures by the Member States would. 
1. The regulation must follow the framework formed by international principles and be 
reconcilable with national legal systems 
Given that due diligence is risk-based and situation-specific, the measures required from a 
diligent operator vary from one company and industry to the next. Due diligence does not 
obligate an operator to achieve a defined end result, nor does the materialisation of risks 
automatically imply the neglect of due diligence. This presents its own challenges for leg-
islation, which should be clear and precise, yet flexible at the same time. 
 
European rules should follow the framework set up by the UN’s Guiding Principles on 
Business and Human Rights (hereinafter UNGP) and the OECD Guidelines for Multina-
tional Enterprises as closely as possible. This would provide businesses with adequate 
leeway in fulfilling their duties in terms of due diligence and other obligations applicable to 
sustainable business operations. Even though general principles should be specified 
when put into the shape of statutory obligations, over-regulation must be avoided. While 
the Finnish Commerce Federation is of the opinion that the Commission has, for the most 
part, adhered to the requirement of precise legislation in its proposal for the Directive, it 
could and should be improved. One example of an obligation requiring specifying is the 
obligation of companies to provide support for SMEs. In addition, particularly the require-
ment to seek contractual assurances from both direct and indirect business partners can 
be considered as overreaching.  
 
The Finnish Commerce Federation takes a critical stance towards the proposed provi-
sions concerning directors’ duty of care and liability for damages, which are ill-adaptable 
to the established framework of tort law and corporate law. Although the effective imple-
mentation of the due diligence duty also requires supervision and the addressing of viola-
tions, the focus on stricter liability risks shifting the entire regulation’s focal point too far 
away from the core issue – that of specifying the effective obligations. 
 
The Finnish Commerce Federation has previously adopted the view that the due diligence 
rules should at least initially be limited to human rights. The main reason for this is that the 
establishment and possible limiting of a subcontracting chain’s environmental and climate 
impact departs, in terms of its logic, from the impact on human rights, which always has a 
clear object. The existing international instruments have also been built up from the hu-
man rights perspective. The Finnish Commerce Federation has revised its previous 
stance and is open to extending the regulation to environmental and climate aspects. 
Given that the Directive nevertheless pertains to new types of due diligence duties, it still 
needs specifying in this respect. Authorities are also expected to make substantial contri-
butions in the guidance and counselling of companies.

2. Clear challenges in framing the scope 
According to the Commission’s proposal, the Directive would apply to large European 
companies with more than 500 employees and a net worldwide turnover in excess of EUR 
150 million. In separately defined high-risk industries, the limits would be more than 250 
employees and a net worldwide turnover in excess of EUR 40 million. The Directive would 
apply to non-EU companies if their turnover in the EU exceeds the aforementioned thresh-
old values. Given that the high-risk industries in the proposal include the wholesale trade 
of textiles, clothes and shoes, foodstuffs and agricultural and forest industry products, as 
well as some construction materials and mineral products, the lower threshold values also 
have a direct impact on companies operating in the commerce sector. 
 
The Directive would not place any direct obligations on companies remaining below the 
threshold values. Even so, it is clear that even small operators would in practice have to 
comply with the Directive’s requirements, since large companies are obligated to commit 
their business partners to observe due diligence through contractual terms, for example. 
The limitations of the proposed scope do indeed involve significant challenges.  
 
First, the Finnish Commerce Federation believesthat ensuring the fairness of the rules 
would require the turnover limits applicable to non-EU companies to be adjusted down-
ward. As it is, level-playing field between non-EU companies and European companies, 
especially when they generate only a small portion of their turnover in the single market, is 
not achieved. Given that the EU accounts, depending on the source, for around 15 per 
cent of the entire world trade (import and export), it would be justified for the turnover lim-
its applicable to non-EU companies active in the Single Market to be set proportionally to 
this. 
 
Second, the scope would leave companies that remain below the threshold values in a 
challenging limbo, given that these companies can be bound to the Directive’s obligations 
through agreements, but without a clear possibility to rely on the flexibilities of the rules, 
which the large companies within the scope of the Directive are able to do. Correspond-
ingly, large companies would have to consider, in respect of every small subcontractor, 
whether the cooperation involves excessive risks or costs from the perspective of corpo-
rate responsibility. This could lead to the concentration of supply chains and reduce com-
petition in the market. 
 
According to the earlier position adopted by the Finnish Commerce Federation, the scope 
of the regulation should cover companies of all sizes, but the obligations applied to small 
companies should be lighter than those imposed on larger ones and put in proportion to 
their resources and possibility to influence matters. This would serve all market operators. 
Smaller companies would have a better idea of what is expected of them, while larger 
companies would be able to trust that their business partners operate in accordance with 
the law’s requirements without seeking additional contractual assurances. 
 
So far, the starting point of international instruments concerning corporate responsibility 
has been the due diligence pertaining to human rights. The inclusion of due diligence per-
taining to the environment and climate makes the legislation and the activities of compa-
nies considerably more complex. Simultaneously, it increases, in individual cases, the risk 
of overlapping general or sector-specific environmental rules or these rules being in con-
flict with the requirements of general due diligence.

Given that the Commission has decided to propose that the due diligence be extended to 
the environmental and climate impacts of a company’s activities, it is of paramount im-
portance to ensure that the requirements are based on sufficiently clear, common indica-
tors. For example, any activity with a minor or indirect impact on climate or the environ-
ment should not automatically be seen as falling under the scope of the due diligence 
duty. further, when assessing a company’s emissions and emissions targets, the Directive 
should also specify that only Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions pursuant to the GHG Proto-
col are taken into account. 
3. The due diligence cannot extend beyond a company’s sphere of influence 
The Finnish Commerce Federation notes that assessing the impact of business activities 
and supervising implementation according to the same criteria in all sectors or geograph-
ical areas is not feasible and that, on the other hand, comparable criteria should be ap-
plied to two comparable companies. Because of this, it is more important to define the 
goals than any detailed courses of action in the legislation. 
 
The extent of the due diligence should be limited to the supply chain rather than the value 
chain, as has been done in the proposal for the Directive. Keeping to the supply chain is a 
starting point set by the UNGP and OECD guidelines, ensuring that a company has a rea-
sonable opportunity to account for the impact of its activities. The Commission has stated 
that the aforementioned instruments have been employed as a basis for the proposed Di-
rective, which is why this starting point should also be adhered to in terms of the required 
courses of action. 
 
in principle, the proposal accounts for the need of flexibility by laying down the measures 
required from companies on a general level. On the other hand, it is extremely challeng-
ing, if not impossible, to put some of the proposed measures into practice effectively. It 
can often be unreasonably difficult for companies to comply with due diligence in terms of 
all operators in a value chain with which they have formed an established business rela-
tionship, as is proposed by the Commission. In practice, not even the largest Finnish com-
merce sector operators are in a position to receive information about the more distant op-
erators in their value chains from their direct business partners.  
 
The categorical extension of the due diligence duty to the entire supply chain of busi-
nesses is a heavy measure, even if due diligence often requires the assessment and ad-
dressing of risks further down in the chain. The Finnish Commerce Federation proposes 
that the potential liability for damages should be limited, in accordance with the UNGP, to 
the operations of first-tier business partners, even if the obligation to minimise serious 
risks would be extended to the second level. In this respect, the Commission’s proposal 
does not correspond with the framing of due diligence in the UNGP. Focusing on serious 
risks in lieu of an established supply chain is a better solution from the perspective of all 
operators. 
 
Another clear example of the challenges involved in the Commission’s proposal is the 
duty to commit indirect established business partners to comply with a company’s code of 
conduct through contractual assurances. While this may yet be successful with first-tier 
subcontractors, companies on the second tier and the tiers further down the line remain 
easily in the dark with regard to the content of the agreements. As an alternative, the Finn-
ish Commerce Federation proposes that, as regards indirect business partners, it should 
be adequate to ensure that direct business partners commit to relaying information about

the code of conduct or equivalent requirements to their own subcontractors and to repli-
cating them in their own contracts. 
 
What is also susceptible to criticism is the Commission’s proposal on a company’s duty to 
terminate an existing contractual relationship in the event that a potential or actual ad-
verse impact of an activity cannot be prevented, removed or adequately mitigated. This 
could lead to increasingly detrimental effects if companies were forced to withdraw from a 
business activity, whereas they should primarily be supported in forging long-term, sus-
tainable business relationships. 
 
The Commission also proposes that the duty of care of a company’s directors be provided 
for separately from the general due diligence duty. The Finnish Commerce Federation be-
lieves that this solution is ineffective from the perspective of promoting corporate responsi-
bility and that it could lead to serious challenges when assessing whether the directors of 
a company have acted in the company’s interest. Nor does it correspond with the estab-
lished starting point of company law according to which the duty of directors is to act in the 
best interests of the company. The Commission’s proposal should not be adopted for this 
part. 
 
The Directive would also seem to leave Member States with considerable options for lay-
ing down more detailed rules on the required measures. The Finnish Commerce Federa-
tion is of the opinion that this leeway should be markedly more limited. This would ensure 
level-playing field within the single market and reduce the risks of legislative fragmentation 
and over-regulation. Over-regulation forces companies to use resources ineffectively for 
the fulfilment of overlapping and potentially conflicting national requirements, which is det-
rimental to other corporate responsibility endeavours. 
4. Companies need guidance and counselling, proportional administrative sanctions 
should be prioritised over damages  
Given that the assessment of due diligence is situation-specific, the guidance and coun-
selling provided by Member States is necessary in terms of ensuring the proposed Di-
rective’s effective and consistent enforcement. Although the need for counselling would be 
at its highest when the Directive becomes applicable, it can be considered permanent to 
some extent. This is why it is of primary importance for the Commission to explicitly re-
quire Member States to offer companies information and other support measures so that 
they can meet the Directive’s requirements. 
 
As stated above, the regulation will also have an effect on the activities of micro-enter-
prises and small and medium enterprises as part of international value chains. Although 
the obligations would not concern smaller companies directly, and although they would 
not, therefore, be subject to supervision, even smaller companies will need comprehen-
sive support, particularly when the regulation takes effect in due course. The requirement 
to make the guidance and counselling provided by Member States available to smaller 
companies and the Commission’s proposal to also permit financial support for SMEs are 
welcome. Even though the proposal states that companies falling under the scope of the 
regulation would have an obligation to support SMEs as their established business part-
ners, and this obligation also potentially includes counselling, the principal responsibility 
should be borne by the Commission and the Member States. Otherwise, it runs the risk of 
the SMEs receiving conflicting guidance and the objectivity of the counselling being com-
promised.

Regarding both administrative sanctions and damages, the Finnish Commerce Federation 
notes that their use should be limited to acts or negligence occurring within a company’s 
sphere of influence, which the company had or should have had an opportunity to ad-
dress. In all cases, negligence should have a causal link to the damage or other adverse 
effect. 
 
Violations of the due diligence duty or the reporting obligation associated with it, which will 
inevitably remain highly open to interpretation, should be primarily subject to administra-
tive sanctions alone. The grounds for imposing sanctions must defined precisely and un-
ambiguously and be based, above all, on the severity of the negligence rather than sec-
ondary aspects, such as a company’s turnover. Companies should also be allowed to 
remedy their own conduct when instructed to do so, prior to imposing sanctions on them. 
 
A liability for damages for any issues revealed within supply chains which are not attribut-
able to a company’s own activities and the occurrence of which it could not factually have 
prevented or limited, would be apt to impair companies’ legal security and legal certainty. 
Should this furthermore be coupled with elements of strict liability or merely a reversal of 
the burden of proof, proving the missing causal link would often be nearly impossible for a 
company, given that a simple claim would be enough to create an assumption of liability. 
 
The extent of a liability for damages should be limited, in accordance with the line taken in 
the UNGP, to the first tier of the supply chain and the due diligence duties should be de-
fined unambiguously enough for companies to be able to know what is expected and re-
quired of them. It is the opinion of the Finnish Commerce Federation that the supervision 
of the due diligence duty and the related sanctions should as a whole emphasise effective 
and proportionate administrative consequences over damages and, above all, guidance 
and counselling that supports companies in their endeavours.

commercial finance associations (representing  in the EU  [in alphabetic order] Austria, Belgium, Croatia, the

It is in its role as the representative body of the European factoring and commercial finance industry that the 
EUF wishes to provide feedback to the proposal for a Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive 
(CSDD directive) as adopted by the EU Commission on February 23, 2022 (COM(2022) 71 final). 
 
1. A short overview of factoring and commercial finance in the EU 
 
Factoring and commercial fina nce (FCF) are generic terms for a range of asset based finance services which 
include factoring, invoice discounting, international factoring, supplier finance/reverse factoring and asset based 
lending. Hereinafter, we will generically use the term factoring for ease of reference. 
Due to differences between national laws, especially in civil or contract law, and also because of diverse 
requirements and wishes of  the factoring clients, there are many variations on each of these product sets and 
the precise nomenclature varies from market to market, but all exist to provide working capital funding and 
financing solutions to businesses /clients, particularly SMEs, based upon the debt invoicing or 
receivables created by the client . With a factoring solutio n and based on a contract entered into by the 
factoring company and its client , the factoring company agrees to pay an agreed percentage of approved 
debts/receivables as soon as the receivables are assigned or (in some jurisdictions) pledged to it  by its client. If 
credit protection is part of the factoring agreement, it is referred to as “non -recourse” factoring, while a factoring 
agreement where the credit risk on the debtor remains with the seller is called “with -recourse” factoring. The 
factoring company will often also undertake all credit management and collections work. 
In contrast to the relationships with their clients, factoring companies generally do not have direct 
contractual relationships with the debtors of the purchased receivables and hence only very limited (if at 
all) direct contact with and influence on these debtors. This is a product specificity for factoring  which needs 
to be considered especially with regard to adopting and applying compliance rules and due diligence 
requirements for factoring. 
Factoring is simply a unique blend of services designed to ease the traditional problems of selling on open 
account terms, mainly aimed towards SMEs. 
In 2021, the factoring industry in the EU provided over €274 billion of working capital financing to almost 
265,000 businesses. As repeatedly shown by EUF research and surveys 1, factoring clients are mostly SMEs 
and principally businesses in the manufacturing, services and distribution sectors . 
The amount of working capital provided by the European factoring industry has to be seen in relation to the total 
factoring turnover, which in 20 21 was over € 2 trillion. In relation to  the total GDP of Europe , t he factoring 
industry turnover represented more than 11% of EU GDP in 2021. 
In a number of EU countries (namely Spain, Italy, Austria and France), between 90% and 98% of the total 
factoring turnover  is generated by factoring companies that are banks or part of consolidated banking

In this context, the EUF wishes to point out that sustainability aspects have already been and continue to be 
further incorporated into macro - and microprudential requirements for the financial sector, both on the EU -level 
(e.g. through reports and guidelines from the EBA) as well as on member state level. In contrast to the so -called 
“real economy” and e.g. traditional suppliers, entities in the financial sector do not have a direct connection to the 
production and service processes, but their  financial services reference more generally to the entrepreneurial 
and business actions of their clients or customers. In the interest of having an effective, knowledgeable and 
proportionate regulatory and supervisory system for sustainability issues, the  EUF therefore strongly advises to 
continue to differentiate between the “real economy” and the financial sector, which also includes a clear 
delineation between regulation and supervision of the two sectors. The financial regulatory and supervisory 
authorities such as the ECB, the EBA and the national competent authorities (NCAs) are not only better qualified 
and more experienced with regard to the financial sector, but they also already have wide expertise in the area 
of sustainable finance, also with reg ards to regulation. The CSDD directive should therefore clearly delineate 
between the two sectors and the competent supervisory authorities. 
 
2. Comments on the proposal for a CSDD directive 
 
Should the aforementioned differentiated treatment of the financial sector from the CSDD directive not be  
possible, t he EUF wishes to point out that the current proposal for a CSDD Directive needs to take into 
consideration not only traditional trade and banking, but also other forms of financing such as factoring  
and its aforementioned specificities.  
In some EU member states (e.g.  France and Germany), laws on CSDD have already been adopted, which is 
why the basic concepts behind th e proposal for the CSDD  Directive are not completely unheard of at least in 
these countries . However, judging from the proposal of the CSDD directive, it seems that this proposed EU 
directive will be wider in scope, stricter and also more detailed than any existing national laws in this regard. The 
CSDD directive will be applicable to at least all EU limited liability companies of substantial size and economic 
power, i.e. with 500 or more employees and over EUR 150 million in net turnover worldwide, regardless of which 
sector they belong to. Hence, also factoring companies of a certain size may fall within the scope of application 
of the CSDD directive, which in turn entails the need to comply with an array of due diligence measu res and 
comprehensive mitigation processes for adverse ESG-impacts in their value chains. 
For all companies which are  directly within the scope of application of the CSDD directive, the definition of 
“value chain” is crucial in order to identify which requirements of the CSDD are applicable and to whom . 
In this regard, t he draft CSDD directive contains some clarifications for certain “regulated financial 
undertakings” which limit the value chain relevant for the required due diligence measures under the  CSDD 
directive: In essence, this limited value chain is made up of the activities of the clients of such “regulated 
financial undertakings” as well contract-linked activities of other companies belonging to the same group as such 
a “regulated financial undertakings”, cf. deliberation 19 and art. 3 (d) and (g). 
The EUF welcomes this more narrow understanding of the term “value chain” for regulated financial 
undertakings as it reflects the practical limitations to financiers’ insight into and influence on wh at is ultimately 
only their clients’ value chain . However, the EUF wishes to point out that the CSDD directive’s definition of 
“regulated financial undertaking” in art. 3 (a)(iv) is too limited as it covers mainly CRR credit institutions  
and also e.g. insurance undertakings under the Solvency II -directive but disregards various other forms 
of finance or  financial undertakings  which may not be regulated by any EU prudentiallegislation but 
rather by national laws . It also does not take regulation and supervision on a c onsolidated level of e.g. 
companies within a banking group into account. 
In the EU, there are  a number of factoring companies which for the purpose of the CSDD directive ought to be 
considered as “regulated financial undertakings” in the sense of the CSDD directive  as they either form part of a 
banking group ultimately regulated under the CRR or as they are regulated on a national level but do not fall

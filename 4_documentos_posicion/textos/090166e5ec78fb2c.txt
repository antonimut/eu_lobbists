Proposal for a Directive on Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence  
and amending Directive (EU) 2019/1937 – The IKEA position 
The IKEA brand – represented here by Inter IKEA Group and Ingka Group  – is driven by our vision to create a better 
everyday life for the many people and the IKEA Sustainability Strategy.  
We are committed to respecting and supporting human rights and the environment throughout our value chains, in 
line with the United Nations Guiding Principles of Business and Human Rights  (UNGPs) and the Organisation for 
Economic Co-operation and Development’s (OECD) Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises and Due Diligence Guidance 
for Responsible Business Conduct. With our value chains touching millions of lives globally, we recognise the long-term 
benefits of balancing economic growth with positive social impacts and environmental protection and regeneration. 
IKEA welcomes the proposal of the European Commission  for a Corporate Sustainability  Due Diligence Directive 
(CSDDD) and urges for a harmonised legislation across the EU on human rights and environmental due dili gence, 
which is mandatory and proportionate across the entire value chain and relies on a risk-based approach. We believe 
due diligence can have a significant impact on people and planet by helping businesses understand their impact for 
better governance and decision making.  
To improve the current proposal’s effectiveness and avoid implementation bottlenecks , we would like to share our 
position drawing on our experience in implementing due diligence processes across our value chains globally: 
1. Due diligence requirements should pivot towards managing risks rather than impacts 
To be truly effective, due diligence mechanisms need to proactively identify and manage risks, moving beyond 
simply avoiding adverse impacts . That means companies should align their due diligence efforts  with the UN 
Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights , focusing on identifying and assessing actual or potential  risks 
and impacts, taking appropriate action to avoid/mitigate these (including remediation of impacts when risk 
mitigation is not possible), tracking the effectiveness of measures and meaningful stakeholder engagement 
(including communicating progress on measures to stakeholders). The focus of due diligence legislation should 
be broad-based to ensure companies develop and implement robust, adequate and transparent  due diligence 
processes, rather than limit their approach to managing results and impacts. This would require a pivotal shift in the 
focus of what this legislation intends to achieve and how we reach there. 
2. Shared responsibility of all actors is needed to enforce an effective due diligence 
mechanism 
Avoiding, preventing, mitigating and remediating adverse h uman rights and environmental impacts should be a 
shared responsibility of all actors in a value chain  since the impact on people and planet can occur both up and 
downstream, regardless of size, sector or ownership structure. An inclusive scope of the legislation will help promote 
a level -playing field where all actors  are responsible and accountable while supporting  sustainable investment s. 
Requirements need to be proportionate to the size and nature of the business and should consider the ability to 
impose obligations on suppliers located in other jurisdictions, which could otherwise hinder the consistent 
enforcement of obligations.  
Excluding any actors, specifically Small and Medium sized Enterprises (SMEs), from due diligence obligations would 
provide limited practical benefit since they may still need to meet those obligations as part of the requirements from 
downstream companies  on their  value chains . In this respect, we welcome the European Commission’s 
recommendations for supporting SMEs in meeting these obligations. We call upon EU Institutions to adopt due 
diligence legislation under which all actors, including SMEs, are obligated as part of their shared responsibilities, 
and secure that any assistance provided to them should not be through carving out exceptions but by providing 
additional support (capacity building and/or financial support) in meeting those obligations. 
We also recommend that the scope of this legislation is extended to financial institutions, requiring them to factor in 
the implementation and outcomes of the due diligence process while considering investments in or extending 
financing to companies.

3. A risk-based approach needs to go beyond the limitations of contractual obligations 
Due diligence processes need to encompass a risk-based approach based on severity and likelihood of adverse 
impacts and should not be limited to contractual agreements . In its current form, the proposal could create 
incentives for certain businesses to fulfil requirements only through contractual obligations rather than by assessing 
and addressing actual and most critical risks throughout their value chains.  
Mechanisms like contractual assurances and cascading obligations might prove ineffective  in delivering 
improvements for affected stakeholders by becoming a check -box exercise, while generating significant costs to 
companies and often shifting responsibility from companies to their business partners. In addition, contractual 
agreements will restrict the legal liability for adverse impacts to only those specifically covered in the agreements 
and would not require companies to t ake responsibility for the impacts in the value chains.  Furthermore, it is not 
clear what the scope/content such an agreement would have, what kind of relationships it would create and how this 
would affect the liability of the company or sub-contractors. We would prefer that companies need to follow both 
the letter and spirit of the law.  
4. Clarity on definitions 
We urge EU Institutions to secure more clarity on  the terms ‘established business relationships ,’ ‘stakeholders’ 
and ‘value chains’ and how they interplay with each other. Are ‘value chains’ defined by ‘established business 
relationships’ or do they go beyond them? Further, are all stakeholders considered to be part of the value chain, or 
are they also limited by ‘established b usiness relationships? How do such concepts apply in specific contractual 
arrangements such as a franchise system, labour agencies or on an online platform? 
We also request more clarity on the definition of ‘director.’ Would this legislation cover person(s) with management 
responsibility and/or only include those with authority ? Further, would requirements on ‘directors’  extend to the 
Board of Directors (who take strategic decisions), or would they be restricted to company management? In addition, 
how would these provisions be applied to SMEs or family run operations that may not be publicly listed or subject to 
shareholder/investor structural relationships of authority?  
Both companies and competent authorities would require this clarity to understand the scope of this proposal. 
5. Clarifications of scope 
We urge EU Institutions to provide more clarity on the following aspects of the proposal: 
• How will the CSDDD  proposal apply to group s companies and franchisee  systems? Specifically, how 
would group companies and franchisees be treated in terms of business relationships and liability for action? 
For multinational corporations, would the liability exist at the group level or  on each site/operation in 
separate countries be considered individually liable?  
• How will this proposal apply to online marketplaces? To which extend are online platforms in the scope 
of this legislation? How would due diligence be administered? Securing a level-playing field will require online 
marketplaces to meet similar due diligence requirements as regular brick-and-mortar organisations. 
• How will the Directive  interact with other existing legislations or proposals  covering reporting and 
compliance requirements from upstream suppliers as well as those covering end-use responsibilities such 
as the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive  (CSRD), Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD), the 
Extended Producer Responsibility obligations (Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive, Waste Framework 
Directive), Eco-design requirements, EU Timber Regulation, etc.? 
• How will this proposal secure p roportional due diligence obligations focus ing on affected 
stakeholders? Would the scope of this legislation  exclude shot-term and unsignificant business relations ? 
Would it be applicable even if the business relationship is not accompanied by a written agreement? 
• How will the CSDDD integrate the concepts of corporate governance and due diligence  in the future 
EU framework  for due diligence ? In general terms, corporate governance  (covering the Board’s 
responsibility towards the company/shareholders)  and due diligence (broadly cover ing the company’s 
responsibility towards outside stakeholders) are clearly distinguishable in approach and scope. How would 
these two concepts be married together in this proposal and what would be the  long-term implications for 
companies in the EU?  
• Would elements of the due diligence process be part of required publicly available information under 
corporate governance?

6. Legal certainty of liability regime for homogenous transposition into national law 
The proposal needs to clearly specify the legal liability regime applicable to companies. Clarifying the liability regime 
under this Directive will be essential to secure the homogeneous transposition of its provisions across the EU, 
safeguard the integrity of the Single Market, and set up a consistent liability framework that will support the 
competitiveness of European businesses. From our understanding, currently companies are: 
• Liable for failure to establish and maintain a viable and effective due diligence system, 
• Liable for knowingly making false or misleading statements in their due diligence reports, 
• Not liable for negative impacts (provided they have a viable and effective due diligence system as well 
as are not knowingly making false or misleading statements in their due diligence report).  
We would like to emphasise that liability of companies should be proportional and should result from a breach of 
clearly described, attainable and reasonable obligations. 
We would welcome clarification on whether lapses in Directors’ duty of care would result in a civil or a criminal 
liability, as Member States address this point differently. Liability should be imposed on companies and not on 
individuals, as individuals act on behalf of companies and face internal responsibilities for non-performance. 
7. Harmonisation of requirements and homogeneous transposition 
As a Directive, the proposal leaves the door open for misalignment among Member States during the transposition 
process. We urge the Commission to provide clear, pragmatic guidelines to companies  and Member States , 
including legal obligations and reporting requirements.  
We also call upon the Commission to specify monitoring requirements for competent authorities and sanctions 
regime for violations, which need to be uniform across the EU to secure effective enforcement . Competent 
authorities in Member States should rely upon clearly developed and uniform guidelines for implementation and 
compliance verification. 
8. Multistakeholder initiatives should remain a source of due diligence and verification 
Third-party certifications and multistakeholder initiatives provide an added objective means of monitoring and 
verification which can supplement the company’s own efforts without reducing liability. We welcome the proposal to 
issue guidance for assessing the fit ness of industry schemes and multistakeholder initiatives aimed at due 
diligence verification. This would result in companies having the option to employ a complementary market based 
third party certification scheme to support compliance with the essential requirements based on ground realities.  
9. Securing a future-looking EU framework 
We call upon EU Institutions  to shed light on certain topics currently outside the CSDDD scope to secure a future -
looking EU framework on due diligence: 
• The requirements for remediation  of actual human rights or environmental adverse impacts (e.g., 
specific timelines), as a precondition for companies to continue doing business. 
• How to ensure confidentiality of business sensitive information – including what information needs 
to be shared with competent authorities and what needs to be passed along the value chain. 
10. Supply side measures are needed to avoid adverse effects 
The CSDDD should not result in adverse effects on people employed in or dependent on value c hains, should 
companies be discouraged from being fully transparent or divest their upstream value chains from high-risk sources 
due to fear of penalties  and/or litigation. We urge the  European Commission to put more focus on additional 
measures to provide support (financial, training, capacity buil ding, etc.) in high-risk areas  and sectors  for 
companies to be able to tackle these environmental and human rights risks.  
 
For detailed comments on the proposal and insights on the IKEA approach to human rights and environmental due diligence 
across the value chain, please see the Annexes.

The complaints procedure should be more robust, in terms of inclusiveness and

d) If complaint is processed, then the outcome of the complaint, 
e) Compliance with EU acquis – including GDPR compliance

‘Companies shall adopt a plan to

Greenhouse gas emissions are currently not included within the definition of 
environmental impacts to be prevented via due diligence. Further, climate impacts 
are not included among the impacts that companies must identify, prevent, and

It is not clear how the competent authorities will verify whether a company’s

necessary competence and/or resources to verify these business models, as they 
could vary as per industry, region and nature of value chains. 
 
Further, it is not clear why emission reduction objectives are only required if 
climate change is a principal risk or impact, and why not in all cases. 
 
We request the EU Commission to clarify in more detail what would be expected of 
companies and how the verification of these requirements would be undertaken.

‘Member States shall lay down the

Directive...’ 
Clarity on the types of sanctions under consideration is needed. Are they restricted 
to pecuniary sanctions, or could they extend to restrictions to market access, 
restrictions on operations, etc.? 
 
We urge the European Commission to provide clear guidance on the nature, scope and 
extent of sanctions for violations of the provisions of this Directive. This is essential to 
ensure the homogeneous transposition of the sanctions regime across Member States. 
Article 22: Civil liability 
 
‘The civil liability of a company for 
damages…. shall be without 
prejudice to the civil liability of its 
subsidiaries or of any direct and 
indirect business partners in the 
value chain...’ 
The liability of companies should be proportional and should result from a breach 
of clearly described attainable and reasonable obligations. The Directive should 
also not create a possibility to obtain damages multiple times, i.e., full quantum of 
damages should not be collected multiple times from various entities and damages 
should be paid only once, irrespective of whether different competent authorities 
govern the different entities. The first obliged to compensate the damage shall 
always be the one that caused it 
 
We ask the European Commission to provide clarity on the scope of civil liability. The 
obligation to compensate the damage and liability for non-compliance with the 
requirements of this legislation should always apply to the first entity that caused non-
compliance – the obligation should not travel up or down the value chain. 
Article 25: Directors’ duty of care 
 
‘Member States shall ensure that 
their laws, regulations and 
administrative provisions providing 
for a breach of directors’ duties 
apply also to the provisions of this 
Article.’ 
Greater clarity required on whether lapses in the Directors’ duty of care would 
result in a civil or criminal liability is needed as the implications for breach of 
Director’s duties differ across Member States.  
 
We request the Commission to provide clear guidance on the treatment of any violations 
of the Directors’ duty of care under the scope of this legislation. This is essential to 
ensure uniformity in the implementation of civil/criminal liability across the EU. Liability 
should be imposed on companies and not on individuals since they act on behalf of the 
companies and face internal responsibilities for non-performance.

Annex 2: The IKEA approach to human rights and environmental due diligence  
across the value chain 
Tackling societal challenges, such as climate change and inequality, is fundamental to the IKEA vision. We recognize 
that a long -term approach to meaningful stakeholder engagement and embedded processes to identify and 
minimize risks are imperative to the long -term success of the IKEA business. To ensure we meet our respon sibility, 
the overarching approach by Inter IKEA Group and Ingka Group toward due diligence includes several elements: 
 
Long term strategies: People & Planet Positive , the IKEA sustainability strategy, includes fair and equal treatment 
of people and protection of the environment at its core. The strategy covers the entire IKEA value chain and franchise 
system. Our ambition is that by 2030 the IKEA business will be a leader in creating a positive social impact for everyone 
across the IKEA value chain, helping to create a more fair and equal society.  
 
IWAY Code of Conduct for suppliers:  Globally, the IKEA business is continuously working to ensure the safety and 
rights of the people working in our supply chain through close cooperation and dialogue with our suppliers, using 
IWAY, the IKEA way of responsibly procuring products, services, materials and components, as the foundation. IWAY 
is based on the eight core International Labour Organisation conventions. It also references the guidance of the Ten 
Principles of the UN Global Compact and states its commitment to the fundamental human rights principles as 
recognised in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Our IWAY v erification process relies on both internal and 
external auditors to verify compliance, as well as IWAY development teams to ensure continuous improvement. The 
latest edition (6.0) of IWAY was launched 1 September 2020. 
 
Top level governance:  
For Inter IK EA Group: Sustainability is an integrated part of the IKEA business, and  is led from a holistic perspective 
with an aligned overall direction. The total IKEA sustainability agenda is driven by the Strategic Sustainability Council. 
Key sustainability objectives are monitored and reported on in totality in the IKEA sustainability report. The managing 
directors of the represented IKEA franchisees and Inter IKEA group companies (or core businesses) are invited 
members of the Council. The CEO of Inter IKEA Group is the chair of the Strategic Sustainability Council.  The Inter 
IKEA Group Sustainability Management team includes the sustainability managers from the Inter IKEA Group entities. 
The group is responsible for aligning and developing sustainability ambitions and commitments and reporting on 
performance. 
For Ingka Group:  The Sustainability Committee of the Management Board, chaired by the CFO and Deputy CEO of  
Ingka Group, defines and follows up on the overarching priorities, principles and frameworks for sustainability within 
Ingka Group. Our Chief Sustainability Officer (CSO) oversees our performance against the commitments of the Ingka 
Group People and Planet Positive Strategy. The CSO organises and i s the deputy chairperson of the Sustainability 
Committee, supported by the Sustainability Management Team. The Sustainability Committee (chaired by the Ingka 
Group Chief Financial Officer and with members of Group Management) is responsible for setting sustainability 
requirements. The role of the Sustainability Committee is to establish the IWAY specifications for Ingka Group, to 
monitor and mitigate sustainability risks and follow -up on IWAY compliance. The Sustainability Committee is 
supported by the Ingka Group Global IWAY Forum which supports with alignment, guidance and continuous 
development of the IWAY system and framework in Ingka. On a country level, we have IWAY Country Forums. 
 
Purchasing & Logistics Areas (PLAs):  IKEA Supply AG (part of Inter IKEA Group) has eight Purchasing & Logistics 
Areas (PLAs) spread out around the world, responsible for developing, leading and secur ing an optimal support 
structure for all business units (suppliers) in the area. Among other responsibilities, the PLAs lead and secure risk 
management and compliance to laws and standards and verify quality and sustainability compliance on site at 
suppliers. 
 
IConduct: IConduct is the IKEA franchisee code of conduct. It includes franchise requirements for the IKEA offer, 
health, safety, security, business ethics, information handling and economic, environmental and social development. 
IConduct applies to all IKEA franchisees. IK EA franchisees are responsible for ensuring that the requirements 
described in IConduct are implemented and communicated to all of their co-workers. 
 
External partnerships: A key part of our work is engaging with others to realize our ambitions. We continue to work 
with partnerships and relationships with NGOs and other organisations or institutions – such as the International

The IKEA retail business is operated through a franchise system with franchisees that are authorised to market and

and operate IKEA sales channels under franchise agreements with Inter IKEA Systems B.V..

strategies. Ingka Group owns and operates IKEA sales channels under franchise agreements with Inter IKEA Systems 
B.V. It has three business areas: IKE A Retail, Ingka Investments and Ingka Centres. Ingka Group operates business 
under the IKEA vision – to create a better everyday life for the many people by offering a wide range of well-designed,

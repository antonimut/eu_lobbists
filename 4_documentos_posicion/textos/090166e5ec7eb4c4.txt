The European Hearing Instrument Manufacturers Association (EHIMA) welcomes the opportunity to 
respond to the Public consultation of the European Commission on the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), which 
aims to establish a regulation for digital products and ancillary services on the European market. Our 
specific comments and views are provided below. 
Introduction 
A new European Cyber Resilience Act aims to introduce essential Cybersecurity requirements for 
manufacturers and vendors of tangible wireless and wired digital products (e.g., end devices such as 
sensors and cameras, smart cards, mobile devices, or network devices such as routers and switches), 
intangible digital products (e.g., non-embedded software) and ancillary services. 
• ‘Digital product’ covers both hardware and software products, including software that can be made 
available without hardware (non-embedded software).                      
o A ‘hardware product’ is defined as any device or group of interconnected or related 
devices, one or more of which, using a program, perform automatic processing of digital 
data. 
o A ‘software product’ is defined as an intangible good that processes digital data, stored, 
retrieved or transmitted by a hardware device. 
• ‘Ancillary service’ means a (digital) service, the absence of which would prevent the tangible 
product from performing its functions.

Current state of cybersecurity in the medical device industry  
Medical device manufacturers operate in a highly regulated industry. The importance of cybersecurity in 
the medical device domain has been recognized by regulators, medical device manufacturers, healthcare 
delivery organizations, and other relevant stakeholders across the globe. In order to get their medical 
devices approved by regulators, medical device companies must comply with applicable cybersecurity 
regulations and maintain compliance. A number of EU regulations, standards and guidelines already 
address the cybersecurity of medical devices. For example: 
• Regulations: 
o Medical Device Regulation (MDR) (i.e., Regulation (EU) 2017/745), 
o In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Device Regulation (IVDR), 
• Standards: 
o IEC 81001-5-1:2021 Health software and health IT systems safety, effectiveness and 
security – Part 5-1: Security – Activities in the product life cycle, 
o IEC 80001-1:2021 Application of risk management for IT-networks incorporating medical 
devices – Part 1: Safety, effectiveness and security in the implementation and use of 
connected medical devices or connected health software, 
o IEC 82304-1:2016 Health software – Part 1: General requirements for product safety, 
• Guidelines: 
o MDCG 2019-16 Guidance on Cybersecurity for medical devices, 
o IMDRF Principles and Practices for Medical Device Cybersecurity, 
o Technical Guideline BSI TR-03161 Security requirements for eHealth applications, Trial Use,

There are different cybersecurity standards available, each focusing on different aspects. Unfortunately, 
there is no single standard which a consumer or a medical device manufacturer can rely on. 
In the EU, Cybersecurity of medical devices falls within the scope of the MDR and covers the total device 
lifecycle. The MDR requires medical device manufacturers to develop and manufacture their products in 
accordance with the state of the art considering the principles of risk management, including information 
security, as well as to set out minimum requirements concerning IT security measures, including protection 
against unauthorized access. Cybersecurity requirements listed in Annex I of the MDR, deal with both 
premarket and postmarket aspects. Therefore, our understanding is that medical devices are excluded from 
the Cyber Resilience Act. 
Concern of EHIMA 
As medical device manufacturers, EHIMA members have the following concern with regards of the Cyber 
Resilience Act: 
• It is unclear how medical device manufacturers can demonstrate compliance of their non-medical 
products and services with non-medical regulations such as the Cyber Resilience Act. There is a 
potential risk that a manufacturer will have to follow different processes for medical and non-
medical devices, resulting in increased complexity of the processes without additional value for the 
customer. 
Explanation of the concern 
Besides medical devices (such as hearing aids and software applications), EHIMA members bring into the 
market also non-medical ICT products, software and services. Essential cybersecurity requirements for 
medical devices are covered by Medical Device Regulation (EU) 2017/745 (MDR). Our understanding is that 
EU Commission intends to impose essential cybersecurity requirements on non-medical ICT products 
through the Cyber Resilience Act. That would imply that EHIMA members (and likely many other medical 
device manufactures) would have to demonstrate compliance to both regulations, which may mean for

Proposals 
Medical device manufacturers have established quality management systems to help ensure that their 
medical devices and related services meet applicable requirements and specifications. ISO 13485 standard 
is the basis for quality management systems in the medical device industry. Since cybersecurity is a process 
which is integrated into a quality management system, the quality management system ensures that 
cybersecurity has been addressed for all products and services. An additional compliance imposed by the 
Cyber Resilience Act shall not interfere with the existing MDR legislation.  
IEC 82304-1 standard addresses the safety and security of health software products designed to operate on 
general computing platforms and intended to be placed on the market without dedicated hardware. 
Software as part of a medical device is addressed by IEC 62304 standard. Security of software as part of 
specific health hardware can be addressed by integrating security activities into the product life cycle as 
recommended by IEC 81001-5-1 standard. 
General comments 
We would like to bring to your attention that the cybersecurity industry is facing an ongoing shortage of 
qualified cybersecurity personnel in Europe and beyond. Any initiatives on the European market that 
address these challenges are highly welcome.

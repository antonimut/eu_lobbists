Public Consultation on the Revision of the Directive 2006/126/CE on Driving Licences  
 
20 May 2022 
 
 
 
Current text – Appendix III 
DIABETES MELLITUS  
10. Driving licences may be issued to, or renewed for, applicants or drivers suffering from diabetes 
mellitus, subject to authorised medical opinion and regular medical check-ups appropriate to each 
case. 
 
Context:  
 
More than 32 million people live with diabetes in the EU – one in ten adults. The impact of this life -
long condition on their health and well -being is often underestimated. Diabetes is often 
misunderstood by the general public, healthcare professionals and policymakers  which affects the 
attention the disease is given by national and pan -European decision-makers and fosters stigma and 
discrimination.  
Obtaining a driving licence has important social, economic, and cultural implications for the daily lives 
of Europeans. The autonomy it offers is the basis for essential activities from work to leisure; it is even 
often perceived as a "first step" towards integration into society. The current version of the EU Licence 
Directive is discriminatory towards PwD as it results in PwD hav ing to face a disproportionate 
administrative, logistical, and psychological burden every five years when required to undergo medical 
reviews for the validity of Licences.

Technological Progress Allows for the revision of the Directive (Adaptation to scientific and 
technical progress):   
 
When directive 2006/126/CE was enacted, restrictions on driving licenses for people living with 
diabetes were imposed because of the risk of potential occurrence of hypoglycaemia (low blood 
glucose) while driving.  
However, if this could be justified at the time, the risk must now be put into perspective in view of the 
many technological advances that have recently revolutionised diabetes care. Today, for most people 
with insulin-dependent diabetes, the risk of hypoglycemia is under control.  
Since 2006, when the EU Licence Directive in 2006 was enacted, many technological advances have 
revolutionised diabetes care. For example, continuously and intermittently scanned blood glucose 
monitoring devices (CGMs) now allow PwD to monitor on a real time basis their glycaemic levels. This 
results in improved glycemic control and enables PwD to anticipate hypoglycaemic episodes, which 
could potentially pose a danger when driving. Additionally, newer systems and devices such as insulin 
pumps, sensor augmented pumps, smart pens, and closed or hybrid loop systems are all now offering 
much greater control over insulin delivery and again glycaemic control.  
Continuous Glucose Monitors are devices that have a small sensor placed under the skin into the fatty 
tissue to measure blood sugar levels every few minutes or continuously and sends the result wirelessly 
to another device such as a smartphone, a smartwatch, a pump or a computeri. The latest systems are 
equipped with alarms that are triggered as soon as blood sugar levels start going down in a way that 
could lead to hypoglycaemia, allowing the driver to treat the low blood sugar levels and/or stop 
drivingii. 
Sensor augmented pumps allow for communication between the continuous glucose monitor and the 
insulin pump and adjustments to the basal insulin rate based on real time blood glucose levels thereby 
greatly reducing the risk of hypoglycaemic episodes semi-automaticallyiii.   
Many PwD across Europe are now using new technologies, although inequalities in their use still exist 
between and within Member States. The ability of such systems to regulate blood sugar levels and 
therefore significantly to reduce any potential danger means that the Directive is no longer adapted. 
Additionally, outdated rules greatly add to the burden that PwD are already facing in their daily lives 
both physically and mentally. 
In view of the recent scientific and technological advances and of the rapid pace of innovation 
supporting glycaemic control, IDF Europe calls on the Commission to simplify the rules for PwD and 
extend the validity of the driving licence up to ten years for GROUP 1.  Each Member State should 
then be encouraged to consult with their national diabetes associations to create the ri ght frame 
dependent upon the status of diabetes care in their country. Access to the latest technologies varies 
widely by EU member states; for some, extending the validity of the licence to ten years might be 
appropriate.  
We also call on the Com mission to undertake further analysis of the trends in accidents caused by 
hypoglycemia in European member states over the last decade to further decide on the optimal 
validity.

IDF Europe is the European chapter of the International Diabetes Federation (IDF). We are an umbrella 
organization representing 70 national diabetes organisations in 44 countries across Europe. We are 
a diverse and inclusive multicultural network of national diabetes associations, representing both 
people living with diabetes (PwD) and healthcare professionals.

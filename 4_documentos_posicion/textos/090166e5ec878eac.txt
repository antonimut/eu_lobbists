Joint Position on the Commission Proposal for a Directive on 
Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence 
  
 
COCERAL, FEDIOL, and FEFAC  support the Directive’s objective to combat adverse 
environmental and human rights impacts of business operations  and welcome a proposal 
for mandatory due diligence across all sectors , which builds on existing international 
conventions, United Nations Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights , and on 
OECD guidance for Responsible Agricultural Supply Chains. 
Our preliminary feedback focuses on the need to ensure a high level of harmonisation in 
the implementation of the Directive by Member States, a level playing field for companies, 
and proportionality.  Furthermore, we urge policymakers to clarify certain definitions and 
provisions, namely, to clarify the expectations of the industry. 
Overall, the Directive should  not be too prescriptive on the due diligence approach  and 
should rather allow for flexibility on the means to achieve the goals set out by the Directive. 
Therefore, the Directive should focus on describing what companies should achieve rather 
than how  they should achieve the differ ent elements.  This would allow companies to 
adapt their strategies to  different and changing situations across all the different supply 
chains to attain the desired results in the most efficient and impactful manner. 
Ensuring Harmonised Implementation Across Member States 
To avoid disturbing the EU internal market, the Directive should provide a high level of 
harmonisation for the implementation  and enforcement of rules, including on sanctions. 
Otherwise, the Directive may result in trade flow distortions through changes in the placing 
of goods on the EU market. The lack of consistency  between Member States undermines 
an important purpose of the Directive, fundamental fairness, as well as certainty and 
predictability, which are essential to global commerce. 
Taking Leverage Over Suppliers and Customers into Account 
The due diligence obligations and responsibility need to be based on the leverage of a 
company in exerting an impact on the market. 
• Upstream: as most companies work with numerous suppliers from different 
countries and with different environmental and social challenges , companies’ 
obligations should be proportionate to the risk and allow for prioritisation and a risk-
based approach . As for the leverage that companies can hav e on their  direct 
suppliers (i.e. tier 1), this leverage diminishes once these direct suppliers engage 
further down the supply chain  (i.e. Tier 2, 3 , etc.) Furthermore, responsibility for 
the actions of suppliers beyond tier 1 would create friction as suppliers would have 
to provide transparency about their own suppliers, resulting in exchanging sensitive 
commercial secrets across the supply chain.  Therefore, due diligen ce obligations 
and responsibility should not extend beyond Tier 1 of the suppliers.

• Downstream: since companies cannot influence or control what their customers 
do once they have purchased from them, we oppose holding companies responsible 
for business r elationships downstream, including for how products are used or 
discarded.  
Risk of Unintended Consequences of SMEs Treatment  
While SMEs would not fall under the scope of the legislation, they would de facto be 
impacted by the Commission Proposal since the obligations will impact SMEs with whom 
companies have an established business relationship . To facilitate the involvement of 
SMEs, the  Directive should provide for cooperation among companies, helpdesks, and 
financial assistance. Where measures to verify compliance are carried out in relation to 
SME suppliers, the company shall bear the cost of their independent third-party verification 
according to the Commission’s Proposal, yet this could have the unintended consequence 
of companies reducing business relationships with SMEs which cannot cover such costs  
themselves. 
As for the exclusion of SMEs from the scope of the Proposal, we would recommend including 
SMEs which are first importers in the scope to close loopholes at the point of entry into the 
EU market.   
Complaints Procedure  
Reported breaches  with this Directive should come with evidence ( i.e. pictures, official 
documents, etc.) to prevent the administrative burden of dealing with un substantiated 
claims or consequences thereof, for instance reputational damage, even in cases where a 
court would be in favour of the company concerned. It can also lead to the undesirable 
effect that companies would decide to stop sourcing from certain countries or regions for 
fear of litigation instead of addressing the adverse impact. The claims should therefore be 
legally admissible in court.  
Civil Liability 
Provisions o n civil liability should on ly be applicable if the competent authority has 
established that the company has not complied with it s due diligence obligations as 
described in the Directive.    
Powers of Supervisory Authorities 
Before carrying out investigations related to compliance with the obligations based on their 
own motion or as a result of substantiated concerns , supervisory authorities should first 
consult the operator to give them the opportunity to provide the relevant information to 
show that any alleged breach has not taken place. This is a standard procedure in grievance 
processes, whereby the accused party can present information contradicting any 
allegations before official investigations are launched. 
Annual Reporting on Matters Covered by the Directive 
The annual publishing on companies’ websites of an annual statement covering their due 
diligence activities should be published annually according to their books and not by 30 
April of each year.    
Clarifying Definitions and Provisions 
To ensure the legal cer tainty and clarity of definitions and provisions, we call on the co -
legislators to further specify the following definitions , making more prominent use of the 
OECD/UN guiding principles and definitions:

• Established business relationships (direct and indirect): a clear and narrow 
definition should be provided, taking into account the level of leverage a company 
has over its indirect relationships. It should be clarified how far in the supply chain 
the responsibility stretches in terms of tiers. In line with the OECD guidelines, the 
notions of “cause” and “contribute to” should be incorporated.    It should also be 
made clear what a “negligible” and “ancillary” part of the value chain is. Is the 
production of 1 smallholder farmer considered “negligible” and therefore not in 
scope of the definition? What counts as a “lasting” contract with a supplier in terms 
of timeframe?     
• Guidelines for specific sectors: as the Proposal is cross-sectoral, we support the 
issuance of specific sector guidelines . The Commission should , however,  avoid 
deviations from OECD and FAO guidelines as well as avoid overlaps and/or conflict 
with obligations under other EU laws such as the Regulation on Deforestation. This 
leads to multiple obligations and liability on the same issue, and even potential 
conflict between these multiple attempts at EU rulemaking on the same issue. 
• Adverse human rights and environmental impact: we consider that  further 
guidelines or a narrower definition should be provided . Whereas the  proposed 
directive targets any actual and potential adverse human rights and environmental 
impacts, the UN Guidin g Principles require to look at “salient risk”, which implies 
that the company has influence on addressing these risks. 
• Appropriate measure: clear g uidelines should be provided  for companies to 
understand and anticipate the specific measures that are requi red to mitigate 
actual and potential adverse impacts, depending on the risk level and the need for 
prioritisation. 
• Prevention Action Plan: the elements to be included in the plan should be 
clarified (e.g. which business relationships, whether both investme nts and the 
business model, whether it should focus on own operations or beyond, etc.) 
• Certification schemes:  internationally recognised industry schemes and multi -
stakeholder initiatives should support companies’ compliance with this Directive.  
• Reduction of GHG emissions as part of the Climate Change Plan: does action 
in line with the Paris Agreement extend to activities of business relationships?  
Furthermore, ‘Principal risk’ needs to be defined.  
• Substantiated concerns: can anyone submit substantiated concerns? What is a 
substantiated concern? What counts as a legitimate interest?  The provisions on 
substantiated concerns should be aligned with the same provisions in the 
Regulation on Deforestation-free commodity supply chains.  
• Double Recovery: Article 22 subsection 3 states that civil liability rules be 
“without prejudice to the civil liability of its subsidiaries or of any direct and indirect 
business partners in the value chain”. This implies that there can be multiple 
recoveries for the same damage, and that there can be liability against companies 
even when the suppliers in question have been held accountable in their own 
accord.  This violates fundamental principles of fairness and contribution between 
defendants and furthe r amplifies the tremendous uncertainty on liability for any 
party placing a product on the EU market.   
• Other definitions which require further specification and clarity:  without 
being exhaustive, the due diligence approach , targeted and proportional support 
to SMEs , temporary suspension of commercial relations (i.e. how long?) , the 
complaints procedure , the persons  or communities  affected, and the financial 
compensation. 
We look forward to engaging with the European Parliament and Council of the EU to ensure 
a robust and legally clear Directive which will deliver on the ambition of preventing and 
mitigating human rights and environmental impacts in EU supply chains.

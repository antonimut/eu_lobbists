Comments on the proposal for a Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive 
Background & general remarks of the German Banking Industry Committee (GBIC) 
The European Commission published its proposal for an EU Corporate Sustainability Due 
Diligence Directive (CSDDD) on 22 February 2022. The directive is intended to oblige 
companies to evaluate and respond appropriately to actual and potential adverse impacts on 
human rights and the environment of their own operations, the operations of their subsidiaries 
and the value chain operations carried out by established business partners. Breaches of these 
due diligence obligations are to entail sanctions and civil liability.  
 
The German Banking Industry Committee welcomes the initiative of the European Commission 
to regulate the obligations of companies with regard to aspects of their value chains pertaining 
to human rights and the environment. For financial institutions, both environmental and 
climate protection issues, and social equality, the protection of human rights and sustainable 
corporate governance all represent important steps towards more overall economic 
sustainability.  
 
The explicit mention of “regulated financial undertakings” and “financial services” in the 
proposal underlines its application also to the value chains of the banking industry. We as the 
German Banking Industry Committee are therefore pleased to take the opportunity to 
comment on this proposal. 
 
Scope of application of the directive 
There is already a comprehensive regulatory and supervisory regime in place for financial 
institutions for the identification of ESG risks that includes the observance of due diligence 
obligations pertaining to the environment and human rights in banking. With regard to the 
thresholds defined in Article 2(1) of the proposal concerning its scope of application, we fear 
that an excessive burden will be placed in particular on smaller financial institutions from the 
regulatory requirements entailed by the directive. 
 
Where two or more companies belonging to the same group fall within the directive’s scope of 
application, we believe that the proposal does not answer the question as to whether all such 
companies are equally obliged to maintain due diligence pursuant to the directive. In the 
interests of proportionality and the overall management of financial institutions, these 
obligations should be limited to group level. Based on already existing national regulations such 
as German and French supply chain law, which define a scope of application of more than 
1,000 employees, the European directive also ought not to apply to companies with fewer than 
1,000 staff. 
 
Involvement of the banking industry 
The German Banking Industry Committee supports the general approach of the European 
Commission fundamentally to restrict the obligations for financial undertakings with regard to 
the provision of their services to direct clients (Article 3(g)).  
 
However, according to the definition presented in Article 3(g) of the proposal, not only the 
operations of the direct client or borrower are included in the value chain with respect to the 
provision of financial services, but also those of other companies belonging to the same group 
whose activities are linked to the contract in question. This entails a far-reaching due diligence 
obligation for financial undertakings that goes beyond the direct client or borrower. We believe 
that direct information and due diligence obligations can only be agreed in a direct contractual

relationship with the client or borrower. More far-reaching information and due diligence 
obligations (at the expense of third parties) can only be agreed with great difficulty if at all. 
The information and due diligence obligations ought therefore to be limited to the direct client 
or borrower.   
 
With regard to Article 3(g), a clear definition is also required as to what exactly is meant by the 
terms “loan” and “credit”, as they are not used synonymously in the wording of the directive. 
We furthermore call for clarification of the term “other financial services” in the proposal. We 
fear that on the basis of the currently unclear expression “other financial services” the entire 
banking product portfolio of a financial institution might fall within the scope of application of 
the future legislation. This would on the one hand mean that the scope of business 
relationships to be included in the risk analysis was disproportionately large, while at the same 
time the vast bulk of the financing function in the value chain of European companies is 
attributable to bank loans. We therefore suggest limiting the due diligence requirements to 
specific services such as lending and loan-like products. 
 
As both SMEs within the meaning of Article 3(i) of the proposal and households and natural 
persons are to be excluded from the definition of the supply chain concept for banks, we 
believe that it is not necessary to assess the risks for these client groups. However, the 
exclusion of households and natural persons from the supply chain of banks is only mentioned 
in recital 19 and not in the definition of supply chain pursuant to Article 3(g) of the proposal. 
There is a need here to make clear in the wording of the directive that households and natural 
persons are excluded from the supply chain of banks in the same way as SMEs. With regard to 
financial services for companies that are not SMEs, it should be possible for financial services 
companies to define appropriate thresholds.  
 
Timing of due diligence obligations of financial institutions 
According to Article 6(3) of the proposal, financial institutions are only subject to due diligence 
obligations before providing lending or other financial services. We fundamentally welcome 
this, as due diligence following the payment of loans is largely not enforceable. However, 
Article 7(5) and (6) and Article 8(7) also propose in the event of a breach of certain provisions 
by the client that the business relationship with the client be terminated (in the absence of 
significant reasons precluding this), which implies due diligence throughout the entire term of 
the contract. It should be unequivocally stated here that one-time due diligence prior to 
lending is sufficient. The question also arises as to the impact of these provisions with regard 
to questions of liability and potential financial penalties (see Articles 20 and 22 of the 
proposal).  
 
It must also be made clear that a renewed identification of actual or potential adverse impacts 
with regard to a client is not necessary in the event of a prolongation, novation or comparable 
actions if an already existing contractual relationship is being continued. 
 
Furthermore, an explicit grandfathering clause should be introduced so that only new 
commitments of financial institutions are subject to the due diligence obligations. 
 
Limitation of due diligence obligations  
The “established business relationship” defined in Article 3(f) of the proposal requires further 
explanation stating which aspects are to be taken into account for determining the intensity

and duration. A business relationship is deemed to be “established” as long as it does not 
represent a negligible or merely ancillary part of the value chain. We therefore consider only 
those business relationships to be subject to due diligence obligations whose activities are 
required for provision of the financial service. By contrast, we would classify activities within a 
company’s own business sector and those of suppliers that are not related to the provision of 
the financial service as a “negligible or merely ancillary part of the value chain”.  
 
We furthermore request it to be made clear that refinancing, treasury and derivatives business, 
including business relationships with other market players associated with these activities, is 
not covered by the due diligence obligations within a company’s own business sector.  
 
Avoidance of additional risks through civil liability 
Banks only have a limited view of the production conditions of their clients or their suppliers. It 
is not possible to rule out the establishment of civil liability leading to a shift in liability risks in 
the overall supply chain from the actual causer of a breach of due diligence obligations to the 
financial institutions. Companies should not be held liable for damages in their supply chain if 
they have not caused them directly, could not reasonably know of them or if they have taken 
appropriate due diligence measures to prevent them. In order to avoid additional liability risks 
for companies, the German legislator has opted against including proprietary civil liability in the 
national Act on Corporate Due Diligence Obligations in Supply Chains. However, there are 
extensive sanctions available at the national level. We accordingly also do not consider the 
inclusion of civil liability in the European Commission proposal and the associated additional 
liability risks for companies to be necessary and suggest removing the provision concerning 
civil liability. 
 
Responsible corporate governance also without directors’ duties pertaining to 
sustainability 
The directors’ duties pertaining to sustainability arising from Articles 15 and 25 of the proposal 
should not conflict with the directors’ general duties and their alignment with the interests of 
the company. 
 
At the same time there is no doubt in our view that environmental and social factors in 
particular also belong to the issues to be taken into account for responsible corporate 
governance.   
 
The provisions ought therefore to be worded in a less binding manner. The Regulatory Scrutiny 
Board of the Commission has for the same reasons repeatedly criticised its legislative efforts 
with regard to sustainable corporate governance. 
 
Preventing and bringing to an end adverse impacts 
The European Commission points out that the objective of introducing the directive is not to 
terminate the business relationship in the event of violation of the standards along the supply 
chain, but rather to support the company in implementing environmental and human rights 
standards. Articles 7 and 8 of the proposal make particular reference to this.  
 
These require companies to enforce compliance with the code of conduct to be drawn up for 
each individual company through corresponding contractual obligations. Banks have a large 
number of different clients that likewise fall within the directive’s scope of application and will

accordingly obtain contractual assurance of compliance with their own codes of conduct. This 
will entail wholesale monitoring of all codes of conduct of the companies with which an 
established business relationship exists. There is a compelling need here for a uniform 
standard (e.g. regulatory technical standards) for drawing up a code of conduct that must be 
prescribed by the European Commission. We also consider it necessary to clarify/reword the 
proposal to the effect that an obligation should not follow the (wording of) the code of conduct 
but its contents/principles. 
 
If the measures listed in Articles 7 and 8 of the proposal for preventing and bringing to an end 
adverse impacts are not successful, the business relationship must be terminated. However, it 
is not sufficiently clear from the wording whether a business relationship is to be terminated in 
its entirety or whether such termination pertains to the specific activity giving rise to adverse 
impacts. We call for it to be made clear here that the requisite termination of a business 
relationship pertains to the specific activity resulting in adverse impacts. 
 
It is furthermore necessary to make clear here that ordinary notice of termination is sufficient 
for ending the business relationship and termination with immediate effect is not required. The 
latter could otherwise give rise to substantial conflicts with the requirements for smooth 
business continuation at banks. 
 
We consider the rights, prohibitions and obligations set out in the annex to the directive to be 
generally too broad. It should be borne in mind that these are by their nature addressed to 
states and not companies organised under private law. 
 
Environmental due diligence obligations in accordance with existing provisions  
Climate protection and environmental aspects already play a vital role today in the business 
model of financial institutions. For example, larger financial institutions in particular have 
committed themselves via the voluntary commitment of the German financial sector, their own 
voluntary commitments or their membership of the Net Zero Banking Alliance (NZBA) to act in 
accordance with the Paris climate goals and set themselves their own net zero targets. The 
requirements arising from Article 15 concerning transition plans for companies to comply with 
the Paris climate protection goals must be in line with existing and future obligations from the 
NZBA and CSRD. It is also necessary to ensure that smaller and medium-sized enterprises in 
particular are granted sufficient time to implement their obligations.  
 
Guidance for companies and financial institutions 
We welcome the endeavours of the European Commission to provide the companies affected 
with comprehensive guidance for implementation of the due diligence obligations arising from 
the directive. For example, Article 12 of the proposal provides for the development and 
provision of model contractual clauses by the European Commission. We fundamentally 
welcome this initiative. However, it is necessary here to clarify to what extent it will be ensured 
that the model contractual clauses of the European Commission are recognised under national 
law as sufficient for compliance with national implementation laws. It is important for these 
model contractual clauses to be made available as part of the directive rather than only being 
developed at a later stage, as all changes to financial contracts can only be implemented at 
great financial cost. Furthermore, companies should be deemed CSDDD-compliant if they make 
use of and/or adjust these model contractual clauses in their existing business relationships.

Moreover, the European Commission is to provide guidelines to support companies and 
member states in fulfilling their due diligence obligations (Article 13 of the proposal). We 
consider it urgently necessary for the specific features of the banking industry also to be taken 
into account in the development of such guidelines, especially the existing banking supervisory 
requirements with regard to ESG risks and the fact that direct information and due diligence 
obligations can only be agreed for banks in the direct contractual relationship with the 
borrower, so that the due diligence obligations can only apply to the direct borrower. In 
addition, the guidelines should be issued as soon as possible and before the directive enters 
into force. 
 
The general exemption of SMEs from the directive’s scope of application is a step in the right 
direction. However, all SMEs will be strongly affected by the “trickle-down” effect as many of 
them are in the supply chain of large enterprises. SMEs must receive effective support 
regardless of whether or not they fall within the scope of application of a binding framework. 
Whether the envisaged supporting measures really can provide relief here is something that 
must be questioned. Substantiation of these measures is desirable. 
 
Implementation problematic for project and export financing 
We wish to point out that reviewing the due diligence obligations for export and project 
financing will be challenging. By way of explanation we would like to draw attention to the high 
standards that already have to be upheld in this business segment (cf. OECD Common 
Approaches, Equator Principles), as a result of which financial institutions already meet far-
reaching due diligence obligations.  
 
Financing structures of export financing are geared to different borrowers. The contracting 
partner of an ECA-covered loan of a bank can be the importer itself, a foreign bank in the 
importing country or a foreign finance ministry. These special loan agreement relationships 
place financial institutions before practical difficulties in complying with due diligence 
obligations, as the loan agreement relationship between the financial institution and the 
borrower is concluded abroad. Financial institutions generally do not have any contractual 
agreements with the supplier domiciled at home (export company), although the latter would 
be liable for compliance with the obligations from the proposal. Contractual relationships are 
also largely limited to the project company for non-ECA-covered project financing. With this in 
mind, harmonisation with the already existing level playing field makes sense. 
 
Delineation of scope of application versus other regulatory requirements  
There is also a need for a clear delineation of the respective scopes of application of the SFDR 
and CSDDD for identifying and preventing or reducing adverse impacts. Activities connected 
with the principal adverse impacts of investments in investee companies should be governed 
exclusively by the SFDR, while the CSDDD framework should apply to business relationships 
within a company’s operations. Investment activities on behalf of clients such as portfolio 
management would fall under the SFDR. Such delineation would not compromise the level of 
protection in view of the comparable scope of application for financial market participants 
under both frameworks (application to large financial undertakings with more than 500 
employees and focus on direct clients/investee company in question). We call for recital 19 of 
the proposal on application to regulated financial undertakings to be supplemented by a 
sentence clarifying that adverse impacts arising from investments in investee companies are to 
be dealt with under SFDR rules and are therefore not subject to CSDDD requirements.

Other comments 
As well as the aforementioned issues arising from the proposal, we also wish to address further 
aspects/wordings for which we as the German Banking Industry Committee see an urgent need 
for action and/or clarification. 
 
- As well as EU companies, the scope of application also extends to companies from third 
countries that exceed the thresholds defined in Article 2(2) of the proposal. Clarification 
is necessary in this regard as to which national law is applied to companies from third 
countries and which courts will be responsible. 
 
- The definition of regulated financial undertakings contained in Article 3(a)(iv) of the 
proposal does not include leasing and factoring companies. However, in Article 3(g) of 
the proposal, financial services are explicitly listed as a component of the value chain. 
This contradiction needs to be resolved by including leasing and factoring companies in 
the definition of regulated financial undertakings. 
 
- In addition, the mandatory due diligence obligations for regulated financial services 
should be limited to clients that themselves are not regulated financial service 
providers. Owing to the broad spectrum, large number and complexity of business 
relationships within the financial sector, application of the directive to business relations 
in the interbank market and in the area of securities clearing and settlement, for 
example, would entail a disproportionate amount of time and effort. The added value 
would also be limited as regulated financial undertakings are by definition already 
subject to harmonised regulation and supervision, also with regard to ESG risks. 
 
- Article 3(h) defines the expression “independent third-party verification”. Independent 
third-party verification can – as can recourse to suitable industry initiatives – be an 
appropriate measure for verifying compliance with contractual assurances for 
preventing or bringing to an end adverse impacts. We welcome the fact that the 
proposal does not envisage any further external third-party audit requirements for 
compliance with the due diligence obligations. 
 
- Companies are to be entitled to make use of appropriate resources for investigating 
actual and potential adverse impacts, including independent reports and information 
gathered through the complaints procedure (Article 6(4)). Clarification is needed here 
as to how the “appropriate resources” are to be defined. 
 
- Article 7(2)(d) and Article 8(3)(e) envisage targeted and proportionate support 
measures for SMEs with which an established business relationship exists. It is unclear 
whether support measures are also to be provided for corresponding clients of financial 
services companies.  
 
- Article 7(3) and Article 8(4) of the proposal refer to the option of concluding a contract 
with an indirect business partner with a view to achieving compliance with a code of 
conduct or prevention action plan. It is not clear to what extent the conclusion of a 
contract with indirect service providers is feasible and sensible. 
 
- There is also a need to clarify the relationship between the damages and other financial 
compensation paid by companies to affected persons or communities pursuant to

Article 8(3) of the proposal and potential liability for damages pursuant to Article 22. 
The question arises here as to the extent to which payments resulting from Article 8 of 
the proposal are taken into account as mitigating factors when determining the scope of 
a potential liability for damages. 
 
- Concerning the accompanying measures envisaged in Article 14, the statements about 
which precise information and support measures are to be provided on the websites, 
platforms and portals operated by the member states pursuant to Article 14(1) of the 
proposal remain vague. Here we would like to see further substantiation of the 
information to be published. 
 
- We see an attempt to enshrine in law the provisions of the UN Guiding Principles on 
Business and Human Rights and the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises by 
reference to the annex to the draft directive. However, it should be borne in mind that 
these are not binding and therefore are intended neither to be enforced by the 
authorities, nor to serve as the foundation for judicial damages. It appears that not all 
these limitations inherent in the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights 
and the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises have been sufficiently taken into 
account so far in the legislative procedure for the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence 
Directive.

EU directive on Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence  
A proposal for Directive was proposed by the Commission on February 23rd, 2022 (2022/0051 (COD))     
  
With EU´s Green Deal, the Commission is in the process of revising most of the existing regulatory framework 
underpinning Europe’s environmental and low carbon transition. The Commission has - or will - propose more 
ambitious policies and stricter rules on companies in relation to climate change, energy, and environmental 
policies both in horizontal and sector -specific legislation. EU legislation to promote corporate sustainability 
in the social dimension is also under way e.g. the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive and the Social 
Taxonomy. This flood of regulatory initiatives comes on top of market -driven developments where it has 
become ever clearer that sustainable business conduct is already a competitive factor essential for companies 
to attract customers, partners, investors and employees.  
  
Danish companies are in the forefront of sustainability and spearhead the industry development in areas such 
as renewable energy production and most already have ambitious strategies for corporate sustainability. 
However, the Commiss ion finds that current global voluntary frameworks and guidelines is insufficient to 
ensure the desired level of compliance with international human rights and environmental standards. At the 
same time, national mandatory due diligence regulations have evolved at a rapid pace in recent years in the 
EU Member States. To address this, the Commission has proposed a new set of rules aimed at strengthening 
corporate sustainability due diligence and creating a level-playing field in the EU.   
  
The proposal sets mandatory requirements to conduct due diligence to address adverse impacts on human 
rights, climate and the environment in companies’ own operations and in their global value chains  by 
identifying and preventing risks and mitigating negative impacts.   
  
DI welcomes the objective of strengthening corporate sustainability due diligence and create a level playing 
field but sees several areas where the proposal needs adjustments to make it workable and proportionate. 
The role of states and companies should not  be mixed up as companies do not have the mandate nor the 
ability to solve all problems arising from weakly governed states globally. It is key that the competitiveness 
of European companies in the global market is not compromised.  
  
The proposal also goes further than sustainability due diligence by including controversial elements on 
directors’ duties and executive remuneration interfering with well-functioning corporate governance models 
of the Member States. This despite the Commission’s own Regulatory  Scrutiny Board rejected those 
elements. These elements should be removed to respect the fundamental EU principles of proportionality 
and subsidiarity and to avoid unnecessary EU -regulation with potentially huge negative side -effects. The 
credibility of EU Better Regulation principles is also at risk here.   
  
DI’s key messages on the EU’s proposal for a directive on Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence:  
  
1. Scope should only include the largest companies as proposed . Even without legal requirements, 
SMEs will be affected through trickle-down demands from major customers. Raising SME’s standards 
through market forces is less burdensome and less bureaucratic than trying to invent SME -specific 
legislation which in pract ice could lead to contradictory demands. However, the consequences of 
including financial undertakings in the scope should be reviewed  with a view to avoiding 
unintended, disproportionate spillover effects onto companies outside the financial sector who ar e 
outside the scope of the Directive – such as SME’s.

2. Ensuring legal clarity and avoiding unnecessary administrative burdens is key for EU 
competitiveness. Amendments to this end are needed both as regards the due diligence 
requirements, the definitions, the complaint procedure, the monitoring obligations, and the rules on 
civil liability. The due diligence requirements must be in line with recognised UN/OECD guidelines and 
be manageable for companies based on legal clarity, proportionality, prioriti zation, and severity 
reflecting what companies realistically can/cannot do and adequately capture the complexity of 
multinational value chains.  
  
3. Liability should only apply in relation to direct suppliers with whom the company has a contractual 
relationship and not the whole value chain, which is not within the sphere of control of the company. 
Liability should also more clearly be based on whether the business is causing, contributing, or is 
directly linked to adverse impacts as defined by UNGP/OECD guidelines.   
  
4. More clear and flexible rules are needed for groups of companies to reflect the different ways 
groups can be organized and to avoid unnecessary administrative burdens. Subsidiaries within scope 
should be able to refer to the parent company´s or  another group company´s handling of Directive 
obligations within the group when parent/group company is registered in the EU.  
  
5. Alignment with related EU-legislation should be ensured most notably the Corporate Sustainability 
Reporting Directive, where relevant, to avoid double reporting and disproportionate requirements.   
  
6. Amendments should be made to ensure a level playing field . One of the main objectives of the 
Directive is to ensure a level playing field on due diligence in the EU. However, the proposal sets out 
only minimum harmonization requirements – not total harmonization. Moreover, the requirements 
will be difficult to enforce towards companies from third countries.    
  
7. Elements going beyond due diligence should be deleted . These elements are unnecessary, 
disproportionate and violate the subsidiarity principle, and are more likely to do serious harm than 
good. Current directors’ duties which is part of well-functioning corporate governance models in the 
Member States are obviously not an obstacle to the sustainable transition we all strive for, nor to the 
objectives of this directive. This was also noted by the Regulatory Scrutiny Board who rejected these 
elements by stating a.o. that it ” is not sufficiently clear about the need to regulate  directors’ duties 
on top of due diligence requirements” and that there is ”[not] convincing evidence that EU businesses 
[..] do not already sufficiently reflect sustainability aspects or do not have sufficient incentives to do 
so”. The proposal claims it has "significantly [reduced] directors’ duties by linking them closely to the 
due diligence obligation". However, especially art. 25, 15 and partly 26 clearly go beyond. These rules 
are also impossible to enforce upon companies from third countries.

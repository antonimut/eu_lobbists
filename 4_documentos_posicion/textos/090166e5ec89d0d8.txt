Feedback of Mercedes-Benz Group AG on the draft EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence 
Directive 
 
EU legal framework and level-playing field 
In principle, we welcome the fact that the EU Commission is creating a common basis for national 
legislation in the EU with the proposed directive.  
However, vague definitions and the introduction of the new concept "established business relationships" 
as well as the fact that EU Member States are responsible for the transposition of the directive into 
national law – e.g. they shall lay down rules on sanctions as well as on civil liability – could lead to 
internal market fragmentation. Those provisions need to be clearer defined to ensure a uniform and 
comparable application within the EU.  
We appreciate that the directive will apply to non-EU companies under certain conditions. This is a step 
towards an international level playing field.  
Proportionality and workability 
We support ambitious rules if they serve the actual purpose of improving human rights and 
environmental aspects and we advocate for a sense of proportion. This means above all that rules are 
workable for companies and do not lead to disadvantages in international competitiveness. 
The draft text provides for an obligation of means: Companies are not obliged to guarantee the 
prevention, mitigation or ending of negative impacts under any circumstances and beyond their control 
as long as appropriate due diligence measures are taken. This takes into account the principle of 
proportionality and recognizes the high complexity of supply and value chains. 
On the other hand, the EU draft directive is significantly more ambitious than the German Supply Chain 
Due Diligence Act, e.g. with regard to additional due diligence requirements for environmental aspects 
and the reference to additional human rights conventions. The high level of complexity will present 
companies with major challenges: The implementation of enormous bureaucratic processes will require 
considerable efforts and the need for additional capacities. 
The draft directive further stipulates due diligence across the whole value chain (downstream and 
upstream) and, compared to the conceptual approach of the recently published German Supply Chain 
Act, it does not differentiate between direct and indirect suppliers although the actual influence and 
control of companies differ. For example, the Mercedes-Benz Group only has a contractual relationship 
with its direct suppliers and thus the legal means of enforcement. Furthermore, the attempt to restrict 
the broad scope of the value chain as defined in Article 3 lit. g) of the draft directive by the new concept 
“established business relationship” will not succeed due to the vague definition of that term and a lack 
of risk-based approach regarding the identification of relevant business relationships.

The provision that all established business relationships must be reviewed annually increases the 
administrative burden tremendously and is not feasible in practice. Especially in light of the vague 
definition of established business relationships there is no factual basis that companies could build 
upon trying to identify such established business relationships within all their business relationships in 
their value chains. In our view, this does not lead to significant improvements in human rights or 
environmental aspects. Again, lacking a risk-based approach and/or further guidance on the 
interpretation of the term, it remains unclear how to fulfill the requirement of a periodical reassessment 
of established business relationships. 
Reporting Requirements 
We welcome that reporting requirements are harmonized with the Corporate Sustainability Reporting 
Directive as this will avoid additional reporting obligations for companies - beyond the comprehensive 
provisions expected to be introduced by the CSRD anyway. 
Accompanying Measures 
We support the specified accompanying and support measures such as "model contractual clauses" and 
the recognition of industry and other multi-stakeholder initiatives in support of implementation of due 
diligence obligations.  
Civil Liability 
We scrutinize the requirement for member states to establish a far-reaching civil liability regime that 
applies to the failure to implement due diligence requirements. Transposing the directive into national 
law by Member States, it must be ensured that similar liability regimes apply at national level. For 
example, the burden of proof must be on claimants and must not be reversed to the detriment of 
companies. Furthermore, we would like to add that at least the efforts to mitigate damages should be 
taken into account with respect to the amount of the damages. 
Directors’ Duties 
The inclusion of climate change responsibilities as Director Duties comes out of context and should be 
removed, as climate change responsibilities are covered in other legal frameworks outside the OECD 
and UNGP. The requirement that the business strategy is in line with and takes into account the Paris 
Climate Agreement absent further specification or guidance could also lead to increasing litigation risks 
– without actually adding any value to the ultimate goal of reducing CO2 emissions that companies 
already pursue.  
Transposition 
The draft directive foresees different application dates for due diligence obligations for companies, e.g. 
two years after entry into force of the directive for larger companies and four years after entry into force 
of the directive for smaller companies operating in high-impact sectors. In this context, we need clear 
rules that help larger companies to position themselves vis-à-vis smaller ones in their supply chains 
during the two-year longer transition period. For larger companies, this may result in a dilemma 
situation ("contractual cascading"/indirect obligation of smaller companies through the back door 
versus violation of due diligence obligations).

 
 
Opinion on the European Commission's proposal for a Directive on Corporate 
Sustainability Due Diligence, CSDD. 
 
Unionen wishes to highlight the following overarching subjects: 
Based on its international trade union work with multinational companies, Unionen 
believes that initiatives that facilitate for people in value chains to gain real access 
to influence in order to ensure and promote human and trade union rights are 
needed. As a starting point, we therefore welcome an EU corporate law framework 
on sustainable business in global value chains on how human rights in the world of 
work are respected.   
Unionen believes that a properly drafted directive can accelerate proactive work in 
companies where social sustainability will be seen as a success factor rather than a 
risk factor. Responsible companies operating in the EU can contribute to a more 
responsible and sustainable business environment in the EU and the world. At the 
same time, it will be important to carefully consider the scope of this responsibility 
in order to have an effective and efficient framework.  
Unionen believes that the role of trade unions and respect for the right to organise 
are fundamental to companies' due diligence efforts in global value chains and to 
the ability of trade unions to identify and follow up on value chain failures. 
Without the application of trade union rights, developed partnership models and the 
possibility of collective bargaining are impossible.   
Enforcement of collective agreements should remain the responsibility of the 
labour market parners, in particular trade unions. A due diligence regulatory 
framework should not replace - or be used as a substitute for - compliance with the 
terms of collective agreements and their enforcement. The labour market model 
also risks being undermined if the right to organise and bargaining is not also 
respected in other parts of the world.  
A key aspect to watch is that the responsibilities of employee representatives on 
boards are not so extensive that they will in practice undermine the preconditions 
for employee representation by making it difficult to find employees willing to 
serve on boards. At the same time, there is also a possibility that the role of 
representatives will become more important and more attractive.  
Unionen further believes that it should be ensured that the regulatory framework 
does not lead to a deterioration of our national laws and rules on participation, 
information and consultation, on employee involvement and trade union rights and 
employer obligations or collective agreement regulations in this area. It is also 
important that it does not lead to a deterioration of EU directives on employee 
involvement, for example on European Works Councils (EWCs).

In order to encourage more companies to respect employees' rights and not to gain 
competitive advantages by having indecent employment conditions, Unionen 
believes it is important that breaches of the regulatory framework can be 
sanctioned in an appropriate manner. A major shortcoming of existing instruments 
and guidelines is the lack of proper sanctions.   
The regulatory framework must ensure legal certainty. A sanction for breach of 
obligations must therefore be based on a clear and predictable framework as to 
what the obligations are, when liability arises and what sanction may be applicable.  
The framework should be general and clear in its design, and Unionen therefore 
believes that, as a starting point, all sectors should be covered and the framework 
should be simple and clear, with clear definitions to work in different sectoral and 
country contexts, while taking due account of the size of companies.  
It is important that employee representatives and board members and employees 
receive the necessary training on the new framework. Local trade union 
representatives and members of European Works Councils should also be given the 
tools and opportunities to acquire relevant skills. 
Unionen raises the following issues regarding the content of the proposed directive: 
The proposal's links with other relevant legislation need to be clarified. For 
example with corporate sustainability reporting (CSRD), the whistleblowing 
directive and public procurement, including requirements for tendering and 
contract performance. 
The proposal's unclear and fragmented boundaries on which companies are covered 
need to be clarified and its implications carefully analysed. For example, the list of 
high-risk sectors is incomplete compared to existing standards such as the OECD 
classifications. Furthermore, it is unclear whether public companies are covered.  
A thorough analysis is needed of the selective list of human rights in the annex. 
Firstly, in terms of selection, i.e. why certain rights and prohibitions were selected, 
while others were left out, and secondly, in terms of how the rights and 
prohibitions are intended to be operationalised in the implementation and 
enforcement of corporate due diligence.  
The proposed sanctioning requirements leave a wide margin of discretion to 
Member States as to the form and possible levels of sanctions. The advantages and 
disadvantages of a wide margin of discretion for Member States need to be 
analysed. A possible risk of different sanctioning regimes is that the proposal 
creates an uneven regulatory environment in the internal market, which may 
ultimately lead to "rule shopping" and thus counteract the proposal's objective of 
creating a level playing field for businesses. At the same time, an advantage is that 
Member States can adapt the sanctions to national regimes.  
The proposal limits trade union involvement to internal grievance structures, not in 
the whole decision-making process, which follows from the UN Guiding Principles 
on Business and Human Rights. The impact of such limited trade union

participation on the ability to ensure a workable and effective regulatory 
framework should therefore be clarified and analysed.  
The proposal contains a number of alternative procedures and structures for 
complaints to the courts, such as internal complaints structures, national 
supervisory authorities and the use of contractual clauses. The relationship between 
these procedures, the autonomy and role of trade unions and national complaint 
procedures through the courts needs to be clarified.

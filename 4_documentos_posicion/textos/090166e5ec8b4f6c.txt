Pellervo Coop Center supports the EU-level sustainable corporate governance regulation, and we 
consider it to be very important and sensible for the EU to lead the way in this matter. However, 
the draft directive is a wide-ranging and in some places difficult-to-understand entity, creating a 
partially new type of legislation. 
Pellervo Coop Center is an advocacy organization for Finnish co-operatives, whose task is to promote 
co-operation as an entrepreneurial model and co-operative as a competitive form of enterprise. Our 
members are more than 200 companies and communities, but we represent the entire Finnish 
cooperative field: both small and large companies from almost all industries. 
In general, about the directive 
The Commission's directive proposal for Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence is a very wide-
ranging package with far-reaching implications for both corporate value chains and the society as a 
whole. The proposal recognizes well on the one hand the needs and on the other hand the wide-
ranging challenges of regulating a wide variety of companies in the EU.  
Finnish cooperatives have already done a lot to integrate responsible practices into their business, 
and sustainability is an increasingly important and strong part of their strategies. Human rights and 
environmental issues are important for cooperative businesses and a harmonized EU legislation that 
genuinely helps companies to improve the way they operate in their value chains is very welcome.   
However, there is still a lot of work to be done to clarify the content of the regulation, and we would 
like to express our concern about the proposal being too open to interpretation. There are many 
difficult issues regarding the scope and liability issues at the practical level. The Directive, which is 
currently subject to a lot of interpretation, combined with the fact that the proposal leaves many 
practical issues to be decided at Member State level, also means that, although the aim is to have a 
harmonized European corporate sustainability legislation, the content of corporate due diligence will 
vary from one EU country to another, and the objective of coherency does not appear to be 
sufficiently realized.  
In order to achieve the key objectives of the proposed Directive, regulation should be predictable, 
and the obligations imposed by it sufficiently precise, in particular because the proposal for a 
Directive also includes proposals for sanctions for infringements and for liability for damages. 
Concepts imprecise 
The key definitions of the proposed Directive, such as “business relationship” and “established 
business relationship”, “value chain”, “stakeholder” and “appropriate measure”, are not defined 
with sufficient precision. In addition, the value chain, for example, has been defined to be so broad 
that companies' ability to control its entire value chain is at least challenging and virtually impossible 
to implement. 
In particular, the concept of “established business relationship” is a completely new terminology 
that is not found in the UN Guiding Principles or the OECD Guidelines, which are familiar to 
companies and on which they have been working for years. The concept needs to be defined more 
clearly and should be limited to direct contract suppliers, not the whole value chain. 
In order to realistically achieve the objectives of the Directive, the due diligence requirement should 
distinguish between more comprehensive requirements for direct suppliers and more reasonable 
requirements for indirect suppliers. Mandatory (comprehensive) requirements should be limited to 
those parts of the supply chain with which the company has a direct contractual relationship

As this will be binding legislation, the Directive must be so clear and precise that companies will have 
both the de facto ability to identify the measures required of them and the de facto ability to act 
according to the legal requirements and to ensure that their activities comply with the due diligence 
requirement. 
Impact of the proposal on SMEs 
The impact of the proposal on SMEs should also be assessed. Although they are not subject to the 
due diligence obligation under the proposed Directive, in practice companies that fall under the 
scope will pass on the requirements imposed on them to the SMEs in their value chain. The Directive 
should therefore specify what the maximum requirements could be, taking into account e.g. SMEs 
opportunities to act on those requirements. 
On the other hand, the obligation for large companies to participate in supporting SMEs, as set out 
in the Directive, should also be defined more precisely, and delimit when the aid should be given. 
This is why it is very important that the Commission explicitly requires Member States to offer 
companies information and other support measures so that they can meet the Directive’s 
requirements. 
Climate change mitigation obligations are challenging 
 
Finnish cooperatives fully support the Paris Agreement and the climate neutrality goal, and most 
already have ambitious strategies to reduce their CO2 emissions. However, we find Article 15 of the 
Directive challenging, as the EU already has and is developing climate change and sustainability 
policies such as the Fit-for-55 package, the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) and 
the Sustainable Finance Taxonomy, which guide and will guide companies. As such, we hope that if 
the Directive imposes such direct and binding obligations on companies to limit global warming to 
1.5 degrees or to link the director's variable remunerations to achieving the goals of the company's 
climate change strategies, there will be no unnecessary overlaps in the different legislations and the 
resulting inconsistency shall be avoided at all costs.  
Implementation of the due diligence obligation at the Group level 
The Directive proposal does not provide sufficient clarity on how the due diligence obligation is 
implemented at group level. The current text, and in particular the definition of "company" (Article 
3a), seems to indicate that the requirements of the Directive are set at company level and not at 
group level. This would mean that a company with subsidiaries in other Member States would have 
to comply with the decisions of several different supervisory authorities. Groups should be able to 
organize the work required by the due diligence in accordance with their group's business model. 
This is recognized in the Commission's original version and in the Council's general approach in the 
proposal for a Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), which exempts subsidiaries if 
reporting is done at group reporting. 
Liability for damages 
Article 22 on civil liability is unclear, open to interpretation and exceptionally broad. The Directive 
proposal seeks to expand liability in a way that does not fit into the Finnish legal system and would 
require major changes in legislation in Finland. According to the applicable Finnish law, the 
occurrence of liability for damages presupposes, in addition to negligence, a causal link between the 
damage caused and negligent actions, that the cause of the damage is the negligence of the person 
who caused the damage. Liability for damage caused by another, even in the case of a subsidiary, 
liability that is completely independent of fault, also differs completely from the Finnish principles of 
tort law. Under the provision now proposed, a company could in practice be liable for any adverse 
consequences resulting from the failure of any company in its value chain. All of this poses an 
unreasonable risk to companies that is difficult to predict and difficult to respond to. 
Also, the Directive proposal does not provide provisions for the division of liability between 
perpetrators if several companies are held liable for a due diligence breach, for example when the 
damage is caused by a business partner in the value chain of all of them. Similarly, the proposal lacks 
provisions on the right of recourse if one of the companies liable has to pay compensation for 
damage for which several companies are liable. 
The provision around liability does not make it clear where a company’s liability would start or end. 
The approach to liability should follow UN Guiding Principles to remediation. As in UNGPs, the 
directive should therefore take account the terms of involvement (cause, contribute and directly 
linked) as well as the different levels of responsibility. The causality and liability must be clarified in 
the directive considering the involvement of different parties and the activities that are in their 
influence 
In the first instance, the article on civil liability should be deleted from the Directive. 
Director’s duties 
We have great reservations about Articles 25 and 26, which define the duties of directors with 
regard to corporate sustainability. We do not see the need to define the content of due diligence for 
a single law. The duty of care is a general obligation for the company's management to act 
considering all matters relevant to the company's interests. In the case of Finland, the duty of care of 
the directors and the penalties for violating it are adequately defined in the Community Act, the 
Limited Liability Company Act and the Cooperative Act. The provision cannot be transposed into 
national law without a complete reopening and redefinition of the whole concept and content of the 
established duty of care. 
There is no need to regulate at EU level the details of corporate governance and obligations. In any 
case, the company's management must act diligently and in accordance with the duty of loyalty, 
considering regulatory obligations and sustainability implications, and follow socially acceptable 
practices without being required to do so by law. We do not consider it necessary, in this context 
where we talk about due diligence in the value chains, to include director's duties for corporate 
governance and decision-making that are not necessary to achieve the goal of the Sustainable 
corporate governance Directive. 
In addition, it is important to note that in company law, the duty of care of the directors is 
inextricably linked to the liability for damages. For example, the proposed directive does not specify 
in any way which stakeholder interests and how directors should promote when there is a conflict 
between the interests of stakeholders and not all interests can be pursued at the same time. The 
proposed obligation would therefore expose the directors of the companies to a wide range of 
liability risks. This cannot be considered reasonable for the company's management.

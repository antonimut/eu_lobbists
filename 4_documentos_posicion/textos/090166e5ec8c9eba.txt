POSITION PAPER 
Comments on  Sustainable corporate governance directive 
Answer to the public consultation - 23/05/2022 
 
GENERAL COMMENTS  
EDF is fully aware of the importance of acting in favour of the environment and people and has long 
been committed to integrating human rights and environmental issues into its governance and in 
the frame of the implementation of its activities.  
We strongly support European sustainability initiatives,  in particular the European Commission's 
proposal to introduce a European -wide duty of care. This proposal for a directive, which is part of 
the broader framework of initiatives taken to guide the activity of companies in favour of the green 
and fair trans ition, will enable companies to comply, together and on a equal footing,  with the 
objectives of the Paris Agreement.  
The commitment of companies is crucial to advancing human rights and environmental 
considerations within their own organisations, but also  in their subsidiaries and supply chains. 
However, to ensure the full effectiveness of the mechanism envisaged by the European Commission, 
several adjustments and clarifications are necessary. Thus, in light of the provisions in force in France 
since 2017, EDF wishes to highlight the following three points in particular: 
- Not all environmental and human rights risks posed by an activity are of the same nature or 
intensity and therefore cannot be treated in the same way. It is therefore imperative for 
effective implementation that companies are required to address risks that have a certain 
level of severity. 
 
- The original aim of the scheme is to change business practices by imposing obligations on 
companies, including on their subsidiaries and supply chain. Extending these requirements 
to customers goes beyond the initial framework but, above all, makes the mec hanism 
particularly fragile by fundamentally distorting the contractual relationship. Furthermore, 
and in line with the OECD Guidelines, it is not reasonable to impose requirements beyond 
the rank 1 in the supply chain.  
 
- The proposed Directive places requ irements on ‘directors’, but this concept does not 
distinguish between directors and officers/management of the company. It is therefore 
essential that Member States have the possibility to allocate the different duties relating to 
due diligence to the relevant bodies of the company.

DETAILED COMMENTS 
1) Scope of the companies concerned 
Presentation: 
The Directive defines two categories of undertakings to which due diligence obligations should apply.  
- European companies with more than 500 employees and more than €150 million of turnover. 
- European companies with more than 250 employees and more than €40 million of turnover, 
if at least half of this turnover is generated in one or more of the following sectors: 
o Manufacture and wholesale of textiles. 
o Agriculture, forestry, fishing, agri-food, wholesale of agricultural raw materials, wood, 
food and beverages. 
o Extraction of raw materials ( including crude petroleum, natural gas, coal, lignite, 
metals and metal ores). 
This scope also covers third-country companies if the turnover achieved on European territory is higher 
than the above thresholds. 
Problematic: 
There is no exemption for subsidiaries whose parent companies are subject to the requirements of this 
text. As long as the governance arrangements put in place by the parent company ensure their 
application within the subsidiaries, such an exemption is reasonable and useful to avoid any excessive 
administrative burden. 
Proposal: 
We propose to provide an exemption for subsidiaries whose parent companies are subject to the 
requirements of this text. 
 
2) Scope of risks to be considered 
Presentation: 
The duty of care relates to a range of risks to human rights and the environment. This risk would be 
characterized as an actual or potential adverse impact resulting from the violation of any of the rights, 
prohibitions or obligations listed in the Annex I of the proposal.  
Problematic:  
The proposed Directive seems to require companies to take into account all risks of human rights or 
environmental harm, regardless of their degree of seriousness. Article 4 of the draft Directive uses the 
notion of “potential” or “actual” impact, without further specification of any degree of seriousness of 
the risk of harm that must be caused. The notion of severe adverse impact is only used in Article 7.5.b) 
and justifies the termination of an established commercial relationship. We believe that a more 
proportionate understanding of the scope of the duty of care for companies is essential  to ensure a 
robust approach to identify i) the risks of human rights and environmental abuses and ii) the necessary 
preventive and/or mitigating measures. 
Proposal:

It appears relevant to clarify in Article 4.b) of the draft directive that the adverse impacts that 
companies must identify, prevent, and mitigate are those that have a certain degree of seriousness, as 
required under French law in Article L.225 -104 of the Commercial Code. The concept of “se vere 
adverse impact” used in Articles 7.5 and 8.6 to require undertakings to terminate an established 
business relationship should therefore also be adjusted.  
 
3) Frame of reference to be taken into account 
Presentation: 
The draft directive requires the companies concerned to prevent and mitigate the potential adverse 
impact of its activities on human rights and fundamental freedoms and on the environment. 
Problematic:  
By imposing a diligence duty on undertakings, the directive inevitably gives them a responsibility to 
guarantee human rights and fundamental freedoms and to protect the environment. The reference 
framework referred to in the directive is, however, mainly derived from international instruments 
which have direct effect only for the signatory states. 
Proposal: 
We propose without limiting the role of companies in the protection of human rights and the 
environment, to clarify the wording of the draft directive in order to insert a sentence underlining and 
reaffirming that if companies are subject to a duty of care, it is the States, that are signatories to 
instruments for the protection of human rights and the environment, that are primarily responsible 
for ensuring their implementation and guaranteeing them. The 2011 UN Guiding Principles state that 
“States should make clear that they expect all companies domiciled in their territory and/or under 
their jurisdiction to respect human rights in all their activities.” 
 
4) Contribution to the fight against climate change:  
The directive requires that the company's strategy be compatible with the objective of limiting global 
warming to 1.5°C, going beyond the requirements of the Paris Agreement. 
Proposal: 
We propose to take up  the terms of the Paris Agreement calling for the limitation of global warming 
to 2°C by continuing efforts to reach 1.5°C. 
 
5) Depth of the diligence duty  
Presentation: 
Compagnies would be subject to a duty of vigilance based on 3 axes: identification (art 6), prevention 
(article 7) and the elimination of the adverse impacts of its activity (art 8).

Thus, enterprises must take "appropriate measures" to identify actual or potential human rights and 
environmental impacts arising from the activities of the companies or those of their subsidiaries, as 
well as those of their established business relationships, when linked to their value chains. 
These two concepts are defined as follows: 
- A business relationship, whether direct or indirect, which is, or which is expected to be lasting, in 
view of its intensity or duration and which does not represent a negligible or merely ancillary part 
of the value chain. 
- Activities related to the production of goods or the provision of services by an enterprise, including 
the development of the product or service and the use and disposal of the product, as well as 
activities related to the business relationships of the enterprise esta blished upstream and 
downstream. 
Problematic: 
Unlike French law, the proposal would lead companies subject to the scheme to set up  diligences on 
upstream and downstream activities, moreover going beyond the rank 1. 
However, the difficulty of tracing the availability and use of products and services in the downstream 
value chain appears particularly delicate for reasons relating in particular  to the protection of trade 
secrets and the profound imbalance in customer relations. 
Similarly, beyond rank 1 of th e supply chain , as mentioned by the OECD Guidelines, " that there are 
practical limitations on the ability of enterprises to effect change in the behaviour of their suppliers.  
These are related to product characteristics, the number of suppliers, the structure and complexity of 
the supply chain, the market position of the  enterprise vis-à-vis its suppliers or other entities in the 
supply chain.” 
Proposal: 
EDF therefore proposes to limit the duty of care, on the one hand, to the upstream value chain and, 
on the other hand, to rank 1. 
 
6) Implementation of the obligation with commercial relations 
Presentation: 
In order to transfer the duty of care to the entire value chain, Articles 7.2.b) and 8.3.c) of the proposal 
for a directive provide for a "cascade"  system obliging the company to “endeavor” to obtain 
contractual guarantees from its commercial partners with whom it has a direct commercial 
relationship, and to “endeavor” to obtain corresponding contractual guarantees from other companies 
whose activities are part of the undertaking’s value chain. The purpose of these contractual guarantees 
is to oblige business partners to comply with the company’s code of con duct, particularly regarding 
risk prevention and the implementation of corrective measures. 
However, the company to which the duty of care applies, and which has put in place contractual 
guarantees with its partners remains potentially responsible for its entire value chain. But, even if the 
directive is silent on this point, since it is an obligation of means, there is every reason to believe that 
the company would be exonerated if it could demonstrate that it had deployed all re asonable due 
diligence measures.

The proposed directive further specifies that if it has not been possible to adequately prevent/mitigate 
the adverse impacts of a business relationship, the company should, according to a gradual basis:  
- Seek - with regard to potential or actual adverse impacts of an indirect partner  - to conclude a 
contract in order to enforce its code of conduct or, if necessary, its corrective action plan (Articles 
7.3 and 8.3); 
- Temporarily suspend - with regard to potential or actual adverse impacts of a direc t partner - 
business relationships with the partner and continue efforts to terminate or minimize the extent 
of the adverse impact or, if appropriate, terminate the business relationship in connection with 
the activities concerned, if the adverse impact is considered severe (Articles 7.5 and 8.6). 
Problematic: 
The directive requires companies subject to the duty of care to endeavor to obtain contractual 
guarantees from their partners, including indirect partners (cf. point 5), but obtaining such guarantees 
(contractual cascade) does not make it possible to guarantee the absence of risk of adverse impacts 
and does not exonerate the company from all responsibility for the possible impacts of its entire value 
chain. 
The proposed directive provides for the obligation for the company to suspend or even terminate a 
business relationship without stressing that this measure should only be considered as a last resort, 
which does not respond to the logic that should be the basis for vigilance. Indeed, it should be explicit 
that these measures are the solution of last resort, only after warning or after formal notice measures 
have been implemented.  
Proposal: 
Considering the impossibility for undertaking s to avoid any risk of adverse impact in its value chain 
despite obtaining contractual guarantees with its partners, it would be appropriate to amend the 
wording of Articles 7.5 and 8.6 in order to make it clear that the suspension or termination of a 
contractual relationship should only occur as a last resort. It could be provided that such suspension 
or termination of the contractual relationship should only take place after the defaulting partner has 
been given unsuccessful notice. 
 
7) Place of stakeholders  
The Commission's proposal assumes the involvement of stakeholders (i.e. those whose rights or 
interests are or could be affected by the products, services and activities of that undertaking, its 
subsidiaries and its business relationships) in the identification of risks, the development of the action 
plan and the identification of corrective measures. 
Proposal: 
The choice of stakeholders in the consultation processes and the development of action plans and 
corrective action plans should remain the prerogative of companies. 
 
8) Development of a complaint and reporting mechanism 
Presentation:

Companies need to put in place a mechanism to allow: 
- Persons impacted, or who have valid reason to believe that they could be impacted, by a 
severe impact; 
- Employee representatives of the companies or value chains concerned; 
- Civil society organizations active in areas related to the value chain concerned; 
to be able to complain if they believe (or have good reason to believe) that companies have actual or 
potential adverse impacts on human rights, or adverse impacts on the environment.  
Thus, companies must allow the potential complainant to: 
- Request appropriate follow-up of the complaint from the company involved; 
- Meet with company representatives at an appropriate level to discuss the impacts that are 
the subject of the complaint. 
Directive 2019/1937 on the protection of whistleblower applies to the reporting of any breach of this 
Directive and to the protection of persons reporting such breaches.  
Proposal: 
It is necessary to ensure that this complaint mechanism is linked to the provisions on whistleblowers. 
As the complaint mechanism is close and partially redundant to the internal whistleblowing 
mechanism, this can lead to confusion about the status of the whistl eblower which is reserved for 
natural persons in Directive 2019/1937 unlike the status of the complainant which can be a natural or 
legal person in this Directive. It will be necessary to ensure a better articulation of these two principles. 
 
9) Control, civil liability and sanction 
Presentation: 
Civil liability (Article 22) 
Companies can be held civilly liable if they have failed to comply with mitigation and remediation 
obligations and damage has occurred as a result of these failures. If contractual clauses have been set 
up, civil liability could not be incurred for damages resulting from the activities of an indirect partner.  
Nevertheless, the exception is void if it was unreasonable to expect that the measures taken would be 
sufficient to prevent, mitigate, eliminate or minimize the adverse impact. 
The assessment of the existence and extent of liability should take into account the efforts made by 
the undertaking to comply with the corrective measures imposed by a supervisory authority, but also 
any investment or collaboration with other entities to address the adverse impact in its value chains. 
The civil liability of a company is without prejudice to the civil liability of its subsidiaries or any direct 
and indirect damage of business partners in the value chain. The rules on civil liability of that directive 
should be without prejudice to other applicable national and European rules on civil liability which 
would cover situations not covered or which are more severe. 
Controls (Articles 17, 18, 19 and 21):

Member States should designate one or more national supervisory authorities to monitor the 
implementation and compliance with the obligations under the Directive. Supervisory authorities 
would also be responsible for conducting investigations and could also: 
- To stop infringements and, where appropriate, to require corrective measure s 
proportionate to the infringement and necessary to bring it to an end; 
- Impose administrative sanctions; 
- Impose financial sanctions; 
- Adopt provisional measures to avoid the risk of serious and irreparable harm. 
Supervisory authorities should receive and address "legitimate concerns" from natural or legal persons 
whenever there is reason to believe that an undertaking is not complying with its obligations. An 
assessment of the situation should then be carried out by the authorities, who will inform the authors 
and explain their reasoning. 
The supervisory authority should be subject to an independent and impartial public body that could, if 
requested by the legitimate concerns, review the procedural and substantive legality of the 
supervisory authority's decisions, acts or inactions. 
All national authorities will be coordinated by a European network to ensure that legitimate concerns 
are properly addressed by the right authority. This network should organize itself and share 
information and assistance between  authorities. Any request for assistance should be processed 
within one month of receipt.   
Sanctions (Articles 20 and 24): 
- States should establish sanctions pursuant to this Directive. Sanctions will be published and 
should be effective, proportionate and dissuasive.  
- Sanctions should take into account the efforts of the undertaking  to comply with any 
corrective action required of it by a supervisory authority, any investments made, and any 
targeted support provided pursuant to Articles 7 and 8, as well as  collaboration with other 
entities to address adverse impacts on its value chains. 
- If financial penalties are imposed, they should be based on the company's turnover. 
Supervisory authorities may require an undertaking to take corrective action, without 
prejudice to their civil liability or the imposition of administrative sanctions.  
 
Proposal: 
It seems important to us in view of the multiplicity of sanctions proposed by the directive to properly 
regulate the cumulation of sanctions attributable to an under taking. Such penalties should be 
proportionate to the fault committed. 
It will also be essential to avoid two national agencies sanctioning the same undertaking for the same 
infringement. Similarly, it will be imperative that the sanctioning regime be harmonized between the 
European agencies in order to ensure the functioning of the internal market. 
Furthermore, we consider that the prohibition of a wrongful undertaking from receiving public aid is 
inappropriate. This double penalty would not take into account the efforts made by the company to 
prevent and remedy the adverse impacts of its activity. We therefore propose to delete Article 24.

10) Duty of directors and officers (sections 25 and 26) 
Presentation: 
The directive implies that the directors/officers of the company when fulfilling their obligations to act 
in the best interest of the company, will have to take into account the consequences of their decisions 
on diligence issues. 
They are also responsible for the implementation and supervision of actions related to the duty of care 
and in particular to the compliance policy put in place. A report on  these actions is presented to the 
Board of Directors/ Supervisory Board. 
Officers/directors should also take steps to address the actual and potential adverse impacts identified 
in order to adapt the company's strategy. But also, any measures that would b e taken to prevent 
potential adverse impacts, stop actual adverse impacts, or facilitate the complaint process. 
Problematic: 
"Directors" are defined in the Directive as (Art.2) "any member of the administrative, management or 
supervisory bodies of a compan y and, where they are not members of these bodies, the managing 
director". However, under French law, the powers of the administrative or supervisory bodies on the 
one hand, and the management bodies or the general manager (or the "executive officers" of t he 
company) on the other, are quite different. The term 'director' in the sense of the Directive therefore 
covers quite different concepts in company law. 
Thus, for EDF, and in accordance with the usual powers of the general management and the board of 
directors of a public limited company: 
- The definition of the policy (not specified by the proposed directive) is the responsibility of the 
company’s general management, possibly under the supervision of the board of directors. 
- The implementation of the pol icy (Article 26) is the responsibility of the company’s general 
management. 
- The report on the implementation of the policy (Article 26) is the responsibility of the company’s 
general management, for the benefit of the board of directors. 
- The Board of Directors is responsible for overseeing implementation (Article 26). 
- The consideration of sustainability issues, including, where relevant, human rights, climate change 
and the environment, including in the short, medium and long term, is the responsibility of the 
board of directors.  
- The adaptation of the diligence policy to take account of the actual and potential adverse impacts 
identified is the responsibility of the company’s general management, possibly under the 
supervision of the board of directors. 
Proposal: 
The directive does not distinguish between directors and executive officers/management of the 
company. It seems important to us to recall that the directors determine the general orientations of 
the company's activity, while the executive officer s implement them.  It is therefore essential that 
Member States have the possibility of allocating these different tasks to the relevant bodies of the 
undertaking.

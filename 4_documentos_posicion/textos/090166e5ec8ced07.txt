Subject: EC Due Diligence proposal: more clarity needed to avoid 
excessive legal disputes 
Dear Mrs. Saastamoinen & Mrs. Slotboom,  
The Swedish Academy of Board of Directors (Swe: StyrelseAkademien) hereinafter 
referred to as SABD, is a non-profit non-political membership organization 
representing 8,000 board members in Sweden. Our answer below is based on 
SABD's overall view of the principles that should apply in European corporate 
governance. SABD works to ensure that European companies are internationally 
competitive in the long term, that the shareholders themselves govern the enterprises 
they own, and that rules and regulations of corporate governance shall follow the 
comply- or explain principle to the extent possible. The latter is also a natural 
consequence of the principle of subsidiarity. 
SABD agrees that companies should conduct so-called due diligence (hereinafter 
referred to as “DD”) in relation to their own operations and also include supply 
chains. However, we have the following comments and objections. 
 
IT SHOULD BE CONSIDERED WHETHER THE PROPOSED  REGULATION SHOULD BE MADE IN 
THE FORM OF A DIRECTIVE OR REGULATION. 
The proposed regulations take the form of a directive, enabling the Member States to 
go beyond the requirements set out in the directive. Thus, the proposal runs against 
the Commission's desire to prevent fragmentation and risks being costly for 
companies as well as undermining a level playing field on the internal market.

THE REGULATION SHOULD BE AT CORPORATE GROUP LEVEL AND NOT AT COMPANY 
LEVEL. 
The proposal is designed to cover companies, not corporate groups. SABD believes 
that a regulation should have a corporate group perspective. A regulation with 
requirements for DD with only a company perspective leads to major disadvantages. 
According to the proposal, each company within a corporate group, that meets the 
size requirements, must report separately. This means that if a Swedish corporate 
group has e.g. a German and a French subsidiary, the German subsidiary - provided 
that the subsidiary meets the size requirements - will be subject to the German 
supervisory authority and the French subsidiary, under the same conditions, will be 
subject to the French supervisory authority, while the Swedish supervisory authority 
will monitor the parent company's compliance with the directive. Of course, such an 
arrangement will be both practically difficult for companies to handle and significantly 
more expensive. It also risks counteracting the purpose of the directive - to create a 
level playing field at the EU level, especially when the directive is a minimum 
harmonization directive. 
According to the proposal, the whistle blower function will also be regulated at 
company level. This would mean an increased administrative burden and increased 
costs for the companies, compared with establishing group-wide procedure for 
handling complaints. However, it is even more important that for a complainant, 
group-wide procedures would be better, since similar cases would be treated equally 
regardless of where in the group's operations they have arisen, and, in practice, the 
possibility of being anonymous would be much greater. 
 
THE PROPOSAL IS NOT RISK-BASED, BUT COHERED TO THE COMPANY'S RELATIONSHIPS  
This means that there is a significant risk that efforts when complying with the 
regulations will be a “tick the box” exercise, based on how work in this area was 
carried out more than ten years ago even by companies that met the highest 
standards in this respect. To create a positive development, we believe that long-
term advocacy work would have a significantly better effect compared with legalistic 
formalities, which would risk resulting in long-term legal disputes. 
 
ARTICLES 25 AND 26 REGARDING DUTY OF CARE ARE PROBLEMATIC AND NEED TO BE 
REMOVED. 
It is unfortunate and inadequate that corporate governance issues are regulated in 
this directive regarding DD, and in particular that Articles 25 and 26 which articles do 
not exclusively apply to DD, but also to board members' general duty of care. The 
articles should therefore be removed from the directive. The proposed articles are not 
needed to achieve the stated objectives of the proposed directive. Already today, a 
board and management of a limited liability company must consider the 
consequences for the company and the outside world in all the areas that are 
relevant. In addition, the lack of clarity and precision in Article 26’s is also a reason to 
delete it. If the regulations are to remain in any form, they must not jeopardize the 
board’s right and obligation to balance various factors and interests when acting in 
the best interest of the company.

In addition to Articles 25 and 26, there are other articles related to the previous 
proposal on Directors Duty which, if included in the directive, would as mentioned 
above, harm the systems based on self-regulation regarding corporate governance. 
To oblige companies to consider the fulfilment of the requirements regarding climate 
change strategies when determining variable remuneration to the CEO, deputy CEO 
and board members is to - in a far too high level of detail - regulate issues that are 
best suited for board decisions. Such detailed regulations on variable remuneration 
would be in violation of the principle of subsidiarity. 
 
THE REGULATIONS MUST GIVE COMPANIES INCENTIVES TO INFLUENCE THEIR SUPPLIERS, 
NOT JUST END THE RELATIONSHIP. 
Unlike OECD guidelines, which are based on companies’ efforts to influence the 
supply chain so that the situation for human rights or the environment improves, the 
proposal seems to entail an obligation to end the relationship if the conditions of the 
directive are not met. Companies will risk fines or damages if they fail to prevent or 
stop an infringement. The effect is likely to be that many companies will minimize risk 
by leaving or avoiding trading with players in countries that do not reach applicable 
standards. This would be counterproductive for the development of human rights and 
the environment. Many countries will have problems meeting the requirements set 
out in the proposal, which in the long term could have extensive negative 
consequences for international trade. 
 
THE RULES MUST NOT GO AGAINST OR UNDERMINE THE UN'S RULES ON HUMAN RIGHTS  
The references to what the DD requirement should cover seem to imply that 
individual companies and governmental authorities and courts of law will need to 
interpret and apply various international conventions. However, the conventions are 
created to be applied between states, not between private entities such as 
companies. A parallel system within the EU with interpretations that does not fully 
comply with UN interpretations risks undermining UN rules in this field. 
 
SMES WILL STILL BE COVERED BECAUSE THEY ACT AS SUBCONTRACTORS TO THE 
COMPANIES COVERED BY THE REGULATION 
It should be noted that even if small and medium-sized enterprises are not directly 
covered by the regulations, they will to a large extent still be covered indirectly being 
part of larger supply chains. There is an obvious risk that the larger companies will 
transfer legal risks to the small and medium-sized companies. 
 
SURVEILLANCE/FINES/DAMAGES ARE ISSUES THAT NEED TO BE CLARIFIED  
Monitoring / fines / damages are issues that need to be clarified, among other things 
by deciding whether it is administrative courts or general courts of law that are to 
handle matters concerning how violations are to be sanctioned.

COMPANIES SHOULD ONLY IN EXCEPTIONAL CIRCUMSTANCES BE HELD RESPONSIBLE FOR 
ACTIONS BY THEIR CUSTOMERS  
It is normally not possible or appropriate for a supplier to supervise a customer's (or 
its sub-customers') actions. In situations where the nature of the product is such, for 
example in terms of surveillance equipment or defence equipment, that supervision 
over the use is justified, this, however, is and should be regulated in another form 
and manner.

ABOUT: THE SWEDISH ACADEMY OF BOARD DIRECTORS (STYRELSEAKADEMIEN SVERIGE) 
 
The Swedish Academy of Board Directors, SABD, is a non-profit non-political membership organization of board 
directors, for board directors. SABD is the largest education provider of director’s education in Sweden, 
contributes to the development of Swedish Corporate Governance and represent Sweden in the European 
Confederation of Director’s Associations, ecoDa. We have more than 8000 members organized in 16, regional 
chapters. Our mission is to raise awareness of the importance of the work of boards and directors for profitability 
and growth. We advocate for boardroom professionalism, and offer training, networking opportunities and match-
making for board positions. We are also a very active member of ecoDa, whose member institutes represents 
more than 55,000 board directors across Europe.

French Banking Federation’s position on the proposed  
Corporate Sustainable Due Diligence Directive 
 
The French Banking Federation supports the objectives of the European Commission’s proposal on the 
due diligence to enhance respect for human rights and environmental protection, create a level playing 
field and avoid fragmentation amongst Member States  and non-EU companies in Europe. More 
generally, we believe that the proposal will usefully co mplement the exi sting sustainable finance 
framework by offering guidance and processes t hat will help companies asses s their potential and 
actual negative impacts.  
 
We also support the exemption introduced for financial institutions, with a value chain li mited to the 
“activities of the clients and of other companies belonging to the same group whose activities are 
linked to the contract in question ”. We are also pleased that the value chain of financial institutions 
for financial services does not cover households and natural persons not acting in a professional or 
business capacity and SMEs receiving loan, credit, financing, insurance, or reinsurance.  
Nevertheless, we believe that the proposed directive is too broad and not precise and that there is a 
lack of proportionality. In particular, it should only be applied at the consolidated level of banking 
groups and that, as in the French law, cooperative banks should be exempted,  and the scope of the 
value chain should be limited to 1st tier relationships.  
There should be a recognition of the need for a balanced consideration of the different interests 
involved. We believe it is important to keep the notion of “potential” impacts but companies shouldn’t 
be punished for any “potential” human rights or environmental adverse impacts. The directive should 
be of full harmonisation. 
1. Subject matter   
 
The due diligence obligations apply to “actual and potential human rights adverse impacts ” and to 
actual and environmental adverse impacts”. This would be unduly burdensome and disproportionate. 
They should be limited to “severe” adverse impacts for all companies. Currently such material 
limitation is only foreseen for companies referred to in article 2(1), point (b) and article 2(2) point b)). 
2. Scope  
 
Extraterritoriality  
  
We believe that some  provisions introduced by the Commission are really positive. For example, we 
are in favour of the extraterritoriality mechanism. This tool will help spread European values around

(European and non-European companies that are selling goods and services in the EU should be bound

However, legal requirements to inform the European Commission on the EU turnover shou ld be also

In addition, the text should be more precise on how companies registered in third countries would be

countries. Besides, w hat about sanctions for companies that are subject to the directive but 
established in a third country and with no bank account and no branch in the EU?  
 
Exemption of subsidiaries of certain duties  
 
We understand that t he text does not propose the exemption of subsidiaries from  the obligation to 
define a code of conduct  when the parent entity has set up such  code for the group. We do believe 
that subsidiaries should be exempted from defining a code of conduct when the parent company 
defines one because it would be costly and unnecessarily burdensome and could entail the coherence 
of the commercial policy of the group. The questio n of the responsibility must also be clarified: the 
parent company should not always be held accountable in case of a breach by a subsidiary of its due 
diligence obligations. We believe that the regime of responsibility within the group should be defined 
by the group itself. In this regard, we propose to amend article 22.  
 
Exemption of cooperative banks  
 
As cooperatives are not in the scope of the draft directive (art.3, a, i), we are very much surprised that 
the cooperative banks seem to be in the scope (art 3, a, IV: “a regulated financial undertaking, 
regardless of its legal form, which is a credit institution…”). We believe that cooperative banks 
shouldn’t be in the scope, as it is the case in the French 2017 law regarding the duty of care. 
 
Cooperative bank model is in line with the principle of sustainability and stakeholder focused already:  
 
• Cooperative banks are owned by their customers; member customers participate in the 
governance of the cooperative bank in a democratic way (“one person, one vote”). 
• Cooperative banks are primarily focused on the promotion of the interest of their member 
customers and the region, through regional structures, not on maximizing profit. 
• With more than 60 million members in the EU, the local communities and their well-being are 
very much in the focus of their activities; this implies a long-term approach to banking.  
• They have a strong social focus; solidarity and inclusion are key values.  
• The local governance is attentive to the impacts of the immediate environment i n which the 
members live. 
 
Moreover: 
 
• Cooperatives have limited liability, with some exceptions.  
• Their activities are mostly retail and local banking, w hich therefore present a low risk profile. 
Clients are well known, often for ages. 
• Lots of cooperative have a limited size and would have difficulties to keep up with red tape 
procedures. It should be avoided to have several due diligence plans/codes of conduct in a 
group.

Due to their specific governance and risk profile, and also taking into consideration the size of lots of

not be harmed. 
 
Limit of the value chain 
 
The directive lays down due dil igence obligations  for “companies (…) with respect to their own 
operations, the operations of their subsidiaries, and the value chain operations carried out by entities 
with whom the company has an established business relationship”. We believe that this definition and 
in particular the notion of “business relationship” is vague and would lead to various (diverging) 
interpretations.   
 
Moreover, it is  impossible in practice to require complete coverage of a single company across its 
entire supply chain . In order  to make the proposal clearer and easier to implement , we propose to 
delete the notion of “indirect” relationship s to focus on “direct ” relationships, that are generally 
materialized by a contract. Having a contractual relationship would  indeed ease the implementation 
of the directive’s various requirements (identifying actual and potential adverse impacts , preventing 
potential adverse impacts…).  
 
We understand that for financial institutions the “value chain” is limited to “the activities of the clients 
receiving such loan, credit, and other financial services and of other companies belonging to the same 
group whose activities are linked to the  contract in question” (article 3). In addition, this value chain 
does not cover households and natural persons not acting in a professional or business capacity  and 
SMEs receiving loan, credit, financing, insurance, or reinsurance. 
 
Nevertheless, it is important to keep in mind:  
 
• That banks can be confronted to the specific case of general-purpose loan. In this case we 
don’t know for which activities the financing will be used and which specific subsidiaries 
activities would be involved.  
• That the particular situation and regulation of the financial sector should be further reflected 
as this sector is already he avily regulated, for instance regarding AML or ESG risks.  The wide 
range and large number of business relationships within the financial sector  would make it 
disproportionately burdensome to apply the Directive  to the interbank market and securities 
clearing and settlement which should be excluded from the scope of the directive.  
 
3. Lack of proportionality  
 
We believe that the text lack s of proportionality , for example article s 7 and 8 state that if “  actual 
adverse impacts could not be brought to an end or (…) minimised by the measures (…), the company 
shall refrain from entering into new or extending existing relations with the partner in connection to 
or in the value chain of which the impact has ar isen and shall temporarily suspend commercial 
relationships with the partner in question  (…)” or should “terminate the business relationship with 
respect to the activities concerned, if the adverse impact is considered severe”. 
 
First of all, we warn the legislator that it will be complicated to assess whether the company took 
“adequate” measures to mitigate and stop the negative impact from occurring. Second, company may 
have a negative impact on the environment while having a positive impac t on other aspects (social 
aspect, for example). It is sometimes not sufficient to have a binary analysis.  There should be a 
recognition of the need for a balanced consideration of the different interests involved: our 
commercial interest, the economic and social interest (employment) of the region, and not only the

impacts in terms of human rights and environmental protection. The duty to act in the best interest of 
the company shall prevail over other criteria. Third a company that cannot create new commercial 
relationships may eventually close , which is not the aim of the directive. We believe that adequate 
supporting measures should be considered instead. The legislator must strike a balance between the 
stakeholders’ interest and the company’s interest. In this regard we believe that the gravity of the 
negative impact should be more taken into account, to bring more proportionality.  
 
Concerning p otential impact, we believe it is important to keep the notion of “potential ”, but a 
company shouldn’t be punished for any “potential” human rights or environmental adverse impacts. 
 
As proposed by the EFRAG t he determinant criteria to identify a potential impact should be its 
likelihood and severity.  
 
In fact, we are in favor of this preventive measure; the suppression of the term "potential" would lead 
to the suppression of any preventive aspect of the directive. Therefore, only a rewording is requested 
so that balanced obligations are differentiated for ‘potential’ impacts and ‘severe’ impacts. 
 
Indeed, it is necessary to review the wording of articles 1 and 7 regarding actual or potential negative 
impacts. It is important to limit the article to severe negative impacts and to attenuate the use of the 
term "potential" with at least the deletion of paragraph 5 and 6. 
We propose a suggestion of 2 regimes in which:  
 
- some obligations will be applied for potential risks; and  
- all obligations will be applied for actual and severe risks 
Balanced obligations should be differentiated for “potential” impacts and “severe” impacts. 
 
4. Code of conduct  
 
Article 5 and 7 states that the code of conduct seems to be imposed by a company to its business 
partners with whom it has a direct business relationship. These provisions seem unclear: which code 
of conduct should be respected? The company's or the business partner's (included legal entities) or 
both? How are they imposed to our clients? How clients manage different code of conducts? 
 
We believe that each company should follow its own code of conduct, otherwise creating new business 
relationships may be complicated as companies will have to establish a compliance system with 
respect to hundreds of business partner s. Moreover, in the case of financial institution s, how to 
manage a situation where several par ties require the financial institution to comply with  code of 
conducts that clash with banking rules. Each company should make sure that they have a policy and 
that they follow it. We believe that  the directive should only set general principles  that companies 
should implement their own policies and procedures.  
 
5. Lack of clarity  
 
We think that some parts of the text may lead to legal uncertainty and unlevelled playing field because 
of the lack of clarity of some  concepts. For example, when the text refers to “credit, loan or other 
financial services” (articles 7 and 8) when describing services that financial institutions can provide. It 
would be easier for financial  institutions to have a clear list of the financial services concerned as to 
avoid diverging interpretations. Diverging interpretations may lead to a more fragmented market.  
The same question applies in articles  7 and 8 when it is specified that banks shouldn’t terminate a 
contract if it “can be reasonably expected to cause substantial prejudice to the entity to whom that 
service is being provided”. In our opinion the wording of this sentence may lead to confusion and legal 
uncertainty.

6. Degree of harmonisation  
 
As the proposed directive is not full harmonisation directive, there is a risk of disparate national rules 
among the Member States and no level playing field, in particular, due to gold-plating practices at the 
transposition stage or because of already existing national laws. 
We believe that a full harmonisation is requested. The directive should be both a floor and a celling. 
 
An amendment could be proposed stating that “Member States may not maintain or introduce in their 
national law provisions diverging from those laid down in the directive” for example in Article 1.  
In the case of targeted harmonisation only certain articles should be fully harmonized. We believe that 
among others, sanctions should be fully harmonised. 
 
7. Sanctions  
 
We would like to harmonise sanctions at EU level to avoid divergent treatment from one country to 
another (different sanctions depending on the Member State dealing with the case).  
 
 
8. Director’s duty of care and Director’s obligations as regards due diligence actions  
 
The draft proposal refers to directors without distinguishing between members of the board of 
directors and executive directors.  Worse, the French translation of the proposal only refers to the 
board members, while some obligations are conceivable only for managers. Yet, as regards French law, 
the board of directors shall determine company business strategy and shall ensure the implementation 
thereof. 
 
Articles 25 and 26 would thus benefit from review. 
 
Director’s duty of care goes much too far, as it implies for the board of directors to “take into account” 
the “consequences of its decisions for sustainability matters”, where the board of directors should only 
be asked to “take into consideration sustainability issues of company’s activity”, so as to avoid being in 
a deadlock. 
 
In any case duty of care shall be an obligation of mean in order to be correctly linked to the duty to act 
in the best interest of the company. 
 
Last but not least, so as to avoid jeopardizing the necessary legal certainty, non-compliance with article 
25 shall not lead to the nullity of any act of the company whatsoever. 
 
Article 26 questions in a number of areas. For example, how can stakeholder s’ views be taken into 
account in practice, and what about their possible side effects on companies? Why and how taking 
into account the opinion of civil society organizations , which we understand are not necessarily 
stakeholders? How do we identify them? How is article 26.2 feasible, in practical terms?

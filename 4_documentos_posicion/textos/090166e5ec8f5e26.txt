On 23 February 2022, the European Commission put forward a proposal for a Directive on 
Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence (CSDD) as part of the European Green Deal. Its main 
objective is to require companies and their subsidiaries to identify the negative impacts of 
their value chain on human rights and the environment and take the necessary counter-
measures.  
The German Association of Energy and Water Industries (BDEW) represents over 1,900 
companies along the entire value chain of electricity and heat production and transmission, 
as well as drinking water extraction and wastewater disposal. We and our members are 
committed to enabling the successful implementation of the European Green Deal and the 
achievement of the EU climate targets for 2030 and 2050. The Green Deal should enable the 
EU to transform its economy and put it on a more sustainable pathway. Due Diligence is an 
important pillar for that, as it is a tool to identify risks in value chains and strengthen resili-
ence to sudden changes.  
BDEW supports the EU’s various sustainability initiatives , including the harmonisation of 
human rights  and environmental due diligence obligations in the EU.  We welcome the 
strengthening of social aspects . To ensure that this harmonisation can also be successfully 
implemented in practice, we propose the following amendments: 
- Refining the Scope of Due Diligence  
Currently, it is almost impossible for companies to monitor the entire supply chain according 
to the requirements of the CSDD without legal uncertainties arising. To ensure feasibility and 
the realistic possibility of achieving the objectives of the Directive, the due diligence require-
ments should differentiate between comprehensive requirements for direct suppliers and 
reasonable requirements for indirect suppliers, such as in the German due diligence law. In 
the proposal the scope of the measures which the companies need to comply with, is signifi-
cantly expanded via the definition of "value chain", since it includes the “upstream and 
downstream” business. This means that in addition to the company's own business area and 
suppliers they also have to check their customers including use and disposal of the products. 
Companies cannot control their customers adherence to these requirements. 
- Preventing Additional Civil Liability  
The introduction of extensive civil liability rules for the Directive’s provisions would create 
enormous legal uncertainty and the risk of excessive litigation for companies with complex 
supply chains. Instead, the enforcement mechanism should rely on sanctions and adminis-
trative enforcement.  
- Focusing on Human Rights and Envi ronmental Aspects  
It is of great importance that the EU is moving forward ambitiously on climate mitigation. 
Therefore, BDEW supports an ambitious implementation of the “Fit for 55”-package and an 
extension of sustainability reporting obligations for companies within the Corporate Sustain-
ability Reporting Directive (CSRD). To avoid fragmented and redundant reporting obligations 
and ensure coherent regulations, the present proposal should be limited to human rights 
and environmental due diligence.  
 
3 
- Aligning the CSDD with the CSRD, ESRS, and the EU Taxonomy  
Aligning the reporting requirements in the CSDD with existing or planned requirements for 
sustainability reporting is essential for robust and comparable reporting. This concerns, in 
particular, the ongoing negotiations about the new CSRD and the respective European Sus-
tainability Reporting Standards (ESRS) to be developed by the EFRAG. As reporting require-
ments are increasing extensively through those initiatives limiting the requested disclosures 
based on a materiality assessment is necessary to identify the most important topics.  
- Safeguarding a Coherent Transposition by Unambiguous  Definitions  
Currently, the definitions of “appropriate measures”, “established business relationships”, 
“principal risks” or “principle adverse impacts” are too vague and leave a considerable 
amount of flexibility for the national transposition. Definitions should be clarified to achieve 
greater harmonisation in due diligence and create a level playing field. 
- Minimi sing additional effort s for companies  
In general, the Directive leaves substantial leeway for its implementation by the Member 
States. This risk leading to national legislation across the EU not being aligned, which would 
make the implementation by multinationals in different Member States more complicated 
(as well as hamper a level playing field for compliance). Furthermore, the Directive appears 
to impose a significant administrative burden on both Member States and companies. 
Therefore, we see the need for concretisation of the proposal to avoid a fragmentation of 
due diligence requirements in the EU.

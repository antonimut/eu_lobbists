Position paper on the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive  
 
May 2022  
 
The Economy for the Common Good (ECG) is a social movement advocating for a future-fit economic 
model which is beneficial to all stakeholders of an organisation: employees, suppliers, customers, 
business partners, the local community and society at large, as well as the planet and future 
generations. It is a model that declares the common good, i.e., the well -being of people and respect 
for all life, as its supreme goal and purpose, building on four ethical pillars: Human Dignity, Solidarity 
and Social Justice, Environmental Sustainability, and Transparency and Participation. 
 
The ECG movement is constantly growing and gaining supporters across EU Member States. So far, 
more than 1.000 organisations, mainly companies, but also schools, universities, municipalities and 
cities, have used our Common Good Balance sheet as a means to change their business practices and 
conduct their "non -financial" reporting, including to meet their obligations under the current Non-
financial Reporting Directive. 
 
In this position paper we explain why EU decision-makers should consider the Common Good 
framework1 in the context of the adoption of the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive.  
 
 
In our view, the Commission proposal is a good basis, but must be improved by the Parliament and 
Member States to really make a significant contribution to better sustainability due diligence: 
 
1. Sustainability due diligence should cover the whole value chain, in a risk-based and proportionate 
manner. 
2. Sustainability due diligence should cover all human rights and environmental impacts (including 
climate) as provided for in the international agreements to which the directive proposal refers. 
3. Sustainability due diligence should be extended to all companies which fall under the obligation 
of financial reporting. 
4. Sustainability due diligence should consider the perspective of those affected  based on their 
affectedness and meaningful consultation of stakeholders in all phases of due diligence processes.  
5. The burden of proof must be on the company to demonstrate whether it acted reasonably or not. 
6. Corporate boards should be clearly required to integrate sustainability risks and impacts into the 
corporate strategy, while compensation for board members should be linked to the company's 
sustainability performance.

Recommendations on the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive 
 
1. Positive initiative 
The Economy for the Common Good (ECG) welcomes that the EU-wide harmonized regulation of 
supply chains is underway. In the era of "blind" (unregulated) globalization, global governance was 
developed exclusively on the side of rights and benefits for global companies, but not at all on the 
side of obligations. The "free trade" ideology assumed that free trade and the free flow of investment 
and capital would automatically solve all sorts of problems. This approach proved unsound, as it led 
to a highly asymmetric regulatory landscape on what was supposed to be a "level playing field." 
Global companies were given only freedoms and rights (free trade, global protection of intellectual 
property rights, free movement of capital, investment protection), but no international legal 
obligations. 
 
To rebalance this uneven landscape, a new paradigm based on a balance of rights and obligations for 
global players is needed. In recent years, several attempts to define and introduce legal obligations 
for corporations have failed due to massive lobbying by UN member state governments, e.g., the 
"Norms on the Responsibilities of Transnational Corporations and Other Business Enterprises with 
Regard to Human Rights"2 in 2004. A "binding treaty" is currently being negotiated3, but progress is 
slow and participation and motivation from rich country governments is weak. 
 
2. A holistic and consistent approach towards CSRD, CSDDD and SFDR 
We observe that two guidelines on "corporate sustainability" are being developed and adopted in a 
short period of time: One regulates reporting requirements (CSRD), the other due diligence 
requirements (CSDDD). We raise the question of why two separate guidelines are needed. In 
particular, we question why companies are required to report on a set of standards (currently being 
developed by EFRAG for CSRD) while board members are held accountable for compliance with other 
obligations defined in a different piece of legislation (CSDDD). One could extend this analysis of 
increasing piecemeal regulation to the financial sector: The Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation 
(SFDR) developed a third set of criteria for financial market actors.  
 
We ask whether it would not make more sense to develop a set of relevant sustainability standards 
relevant to all three areas: a) reporting requirements, b) due diligence requirements and c) financial 
market requirements, valuations and ratings. 
 
3. Issues criticized by civil society organizations 
As was the case with the EU Commission’s public consultation on the Sustainable Corporate 
Governance Initiative, countless civil society organizations (CSOs) commented on the proposed 
CSDDD directive after it was published, pointing out a series of shortcomings and proposing changes. 
The most important points of criticism and suggestions for improvement, which ECG fully supports, 
were as follows: 
 
- Restricting due diligence to “established business relationships”. 
This contrasts with the requirement to introduce an effective and robust duty to prevent and 
end harmful human rights and environmental impacts throughout the whole value chain, in a 
risk-based and proportionate manner.

- Limit obligations to selected human rights and environmental impacts. 
Instead, the definitions of adverse human rights and environmental impacts must be 
comprehensive and expanded to cover all human rights and environmental impacts (including 
climate) as also provided for in the international agreements to which the CS3D refers. 
 
- Scope restricted to large, limited liability companies with more than 500 employees and to 
those with more than 250 employees from selected risk sectors. 
What is needed instead, is the inclusion of all companies, including small and medium -sized 
enterprises (SMEs), in the scope of the proposed directive, with a risk-based approach. Impacts 
on people and the environment do not depend exclusively on the size of a company, but rather 
on the industry in which a company operates, its business model and the ethical attitude of its 
management. Moreover, more than 99% of the European economy is made up of SMEs, which 
means that the scope of the CS3D proposal covers less than 1% of all companies. 
 
- Limited stakeholder consultation in due diligence. 
What is needed instead is the consideration of the perspective of those affected based on their 
affectedness and meaningful consultation of stakehol ders in all phases of due diligence 
processes. In addition, the protection of people and communities, as well as human rights 
defenders and environmentalists, from retaliation and adverse consequences should be 
strengthened. The rights of indigenous people s to self -determination and to free, prior and 
informed consent should also be explicitly included in the directive and recognized. 
 
- Vague obligations of the company’s directors regarding sustainability due diligence. 
Instead, corporate boards should be clearly obliged to integrate sustainability risks and 
impacts into their corporate strategy, while the compensation of board members should be 
directly linked to corporate sustainability performance, at least to the same extent as to 
financial targets. 
 
- Limited provisions on civil liability and access to justice. 
The burden of proof must lie on the company to demonstrate whether or not it has acted 
reasonably. This burden must not be placed on the plaintiff, who has limited resources and 
little access to evidence. Companies must also be liable for if they have attempted to achieve 
compliance through contractual means and audit/verification processes. 
In addition, independent non -profit organizations that have a legitimate interest in 
representing victims, must also be given the right to act on their behalf.

Why not strengthen global governance and establishing a World Court of Human Rights? 
The establishment of such a court can help bridging the gap between codified human rights and reality. 
As proposed by Nowak, Scheinin and Koza6, a World Court of Human Rights could be legally based on 
a list of twenty-one international agreements for the protection of human rights (Art. 5). The duty to 
uphold human rights would apply to states and also to business corporations (Art. 4). The authors 
propose that, initially, corporations could voluntarily recognize the competence of the court (Art. 51). 
At a later stage, it would be enough that the state in which they had their headquarters recognized the 
court in order to hold them accountable.  
 
The EU could advocate for the establishment of such a court and give it priority over the establishment 
of a Multilateral Investment Court (MIC), as the protection of human rights should take precedence 
over investors' rights. 
 
Why not claim responsible purchasing practices from all market participants? 
Responsible purchasing practices are an i mportant prerequisite for respecting human rights and 
protecting the environment by companies and their supply chains. The common framework7 published 
by a group of different multi-stakeholder initiatives in early 2022 (based on the recommendations8 of 
the Sustainable Terms of Trade Initiative) could serve as a basis for this. It defines responsible 
purchasing practices and enables an effective and collaborative partnership between purchasing 
companies and suppliers by improving working conditions in  supply chains, paying living wages and 
building resilience in the supply chain. 
 
This, and more, is already inherent in the value -based framework of ECG and could, as proposed by 
the EESC already in 20159 and 201710, already now be transposed into European law. 
 
4. The bigger picture: What is the purpose of companies? 
The ECG advocates a different theory and purpose of business. Instead of primarily seeking financial 
gain, as money and capital are only means for business and other economic activities, every company 
should have a meaningful purpose and the common good should be the overriding goal of all business 
activities.  
 
This view is supported by a number of constitutions of democratic countries. The Bavarian Constitution 
states in Article 151: "Economic activity in its entirety serves the common good." The Spanish 
Constitution states in Article 128: "All property, regardless of its legal title, is subordinate to the 
common good." The German Basic Law says in Article 14: "Property obligates. Its use shall always serve 
the common good." 
 
As two Norwegian professors argue in their paper11, this would mean for companies to evolve from a 
collection of power relationships between shareholders and other stakeholders to meaningful 
organizations with a socially and environmentally sound purpose.

These indications are a strong argument for a general purpose and value orientation of companies. 
Democratic core values should form the basis for a company's strategy, business development, 
controlling and reporting. Board members should be liable for ensuring that these values are not 
violated, and financial market actors should assess how a loan, equity transaction, or other form of 
financing affects this "ethical baseline" of a company. In contrast to the "triple bottom line" concept, 
the common good - democratically defined and comprehensive - as a baseline means that financial 
resources such as capital and money or ROI are only means. 
 
In summary, this would not only make it possible to achieve the goals of the EU Green Deal and the 
Sustainable Finance Action Plan, but at the same time also contribute to a significant simplification of 
legislation in the area of company law.

The Enel Group answer to the Impact Assessment 
consultation on the EU Cyber Resilience Act 
 
 
The Enel Group , a multinational company and a leading operator in the power and renewables markets of 
Europe, welcome the public consultation on the possibilities to build up a holistic European Cyber Resilience Act.   
The Group supp orts the EU’s a mbition to ‘become a cybersecurity leader’ and believes that a n EU holistic 
regulatory action on connected devices and related services, would lead to a high level of trust among users, 
which would help to make Europe f it for the digital age. For this reason, Enel would like to provide legislators 
with some recommendations on how to improve cyber resilience in Europe: 
 
I. ENEL’S CHOICE AMONG THE VARIOUS LEGAL MEASURES PROPOSED 
Enel is of the opinion that the cybersecurity of digital products and services shall be addressed by a mixed 
approach (Option 4) of: 
i. horizontal cybersecurity requirements for tangible digital products and ancillary services, and 
ii. providing non-binding guidelines for the cybersecurity of non-embedded software. 
In Enel’s view, the scope of the Cyber Resilience Act is not yet completely clear and defined in the consultation 
paper, as it is referring to broad categories of devices and services, and to a broad definition of the Market. The 
broad scope raises doubts about possible overlaps with the legislation in force, or doubts about possible gaps in 
the coverage. To enhance clarity on the scope, we would suggest the following actions: 
• Use clearly defined terminology, avoiding ambiguous interpretations (e.g., actually is not clear if in the 
Commission’s opinion embedded software and firmware serve for the same purpose) by providing a 
definition for each term, and an exhaustive list of object s for each category that clarifies the scope 
(categories such as Digital Products, Embedded software, non-Embedded software, etc.). 
• Include in the scope of the Act, both ‘Vendors of digital products with embedded software’ (e.g., video 
cameras, sensors, smart devices, etc.) and ‘Vendors of digital products that are critical components of 
the EU Digital ecosystem’ (e.g., SCADA software, ERP management software, etc.). The responsibility of 
the Cybersecurity requirements’ adoption in those products should be of the Vendors and ICT/OT/IoT 
Manufacturers.  
Generally, the Cyber Risk should be considered a Business Risk for Companies, and the scope of the Cyber 
Resilience posture should  be extended beyond compliance, towards a holistic Risk Management approach  
(considering IT, OT and IoT environments). 
In the Group’s view, the future legal framework on cybersecurity should promote a clear Risk-Based approach 
which shall consider not only the device or related service, but the whole Supply Chain according to different 
levels of risk, and considering the following characteristics: 
• In case the Commission is considering the adoption of mandatory certification schemes, to facilitate 
coherence with previous dossiers and considering the needed experience on the topic, these would 
be better designed by ENISA , and sustainability in their maintenance  should be assured . These 
schemes should be required by the Cyber Resilience Act for critical systems, and be aligned with the 
future legal framework, including requirements on Suppliers and Cybersecure Supply Chain processes 
that are in draft in NIS 2 and Network Code on Cybersecurity.

• A “Security-By-Design and By-Default approach across the whole Value Chain and the whole life-cycle” 
should be implemented for all components with embedded or not-embedded software , to reduce 
vulnerabilities of product and software. 
• A Risk-Based approach should consider the inclusion of Small and Medium Enterprises (e.g., 
ICT/OT/IoT Start-ups, small ICT/OT/IoT manufacturers, etc.) as these can play an important role in the 
EU Digital Ecosystem and in its Cyber Resilience.  SMEs might, in some regions, represent a vast number 
of Vendors providing services to the Supply Chain to the Electricity sector. To avoid having a 
Cybersecurity gap  jeopardizing the security of  the value Chain for essential services, it would be 
paramount that all devices connected to the Electrical Grid will be cybersecure. To achieve so, binding 
minimum cybersecurity requirements should be considered in the EU Cyber Resilience Act or in other 
EU legislation. 
• The upcoming European cyber resilience regulation should apply also to extra-EU Vendors and Service 
Providers to support the EU's efforts on keeping the connected devices secure, and consequently the 
Cybersecurity Ecosystem secure . Hence the cybersecurity requirements shall apply to all digital 
products and services operating in the EU Digital Single Market. 
 
 
II. APPROPRIATENESS OF THE CYBER RESILIENCE ACT IN RELATION TO EU AND NATIONAL LEGISLATIONS 
The future regulation will complement at EU level the existing NIS Directive and the Cybersecurity Act, as well as 
the upcoming legislations on Cybersecurity (NIS2, Network Code on Cybersecurity , and is likely that also the 
Action Plan on Digitalisation of the energy sector  or DoEAP, will bring provisions on cybersecurity ). Besides, it 
shall deal with existing National Legislations on Cybersecurity. For the interplay with European and National 
Legislations, Enel would suggest the following remarks:  
• Regarding the embedded ( or not ) software, it is highly relevant to consider Europe is not starting 
legislation from scratch. Article 52 of the Cybersecurity Act (EU) 881/2019 shall serve as a centerpiece 
in defining the assurance levels that have to be specified by cybersecurity certification schemes on a 
Risk-Based approach for ICT products, services, and processes, which should be valid and incorporated 
to the Cyber Resilience Act. These assurance levels must be guaranteed both for all types of software 
(non-embedded or embedded like in IoT). Indeed, in the same Cybersecurity Act it is worthwhile to 
mention also the Recital 65 which explicitly states that IoT  ‘can thrive only if there is general public trust 
that such products, services and processes provide a certain level of cybersecurity’ and ‘Connected and 
automated cars, electronic medical devices, industrial automation control systems and smart grids are 
only some examples of sectors in which certification is already widely used or is likely to be used in the 
near future. The sectors regulated by Directive (EU) 2016/1148 (NIS) are also sectors in which 
cybersecurity certification is critical’. 
• The Cybersecurity Act shall serve as a centerpiece for EU legislation on software , specifically Article 
51 on security objectives of European Cybersecurity Certification Schemes : (i) designing connected 
devices or non -embedded software without known vulnerabilities , and (ii) provide them with 
mechanisms for secure updates for its whole lifecycle. It is important  to highlight that these security 
objectives are valid also for embedded software.  
In fact, according to recital 96 ‘European cybersecurity certification schemes should take into account  
current software and hardware development methods and, in particular, the impact of frequent 
software or firmware updates on individual European cybersecurity certificates’ , and have to be 
specified ‘the conditions under which an update may require that a n ICT product, ICT service or ICT 
process be recertified’, also embedded software (firmware) must be considered in the cybersecurity

• Cybersecurity requirements applied to scope of  the Cyber Resilience Act should be aligned to

III. ENVIRONMENTAL EFFECTS 
As the impact assessment relates, t he regulation could also have positive environment al impacts "by 
contributing to wider use of the latest digital infrastructures and services, which are more sustainable." 
Enel agrees with the prior statement, and remarks that cybersecurity law shall reflect the lessons learnt at 
sustainable legislation, where every actor bears the responsibility to keep the ecosystem cyber secure . 
Moreover, as previously done in other EU legislations (e.g., the batteries regulation), the EU market shall contain 
certified products not only considering the cybersecurity aspect, but also to support EU’s sustainable targets.

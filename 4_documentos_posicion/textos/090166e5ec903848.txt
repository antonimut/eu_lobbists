 
 
 
 
 
Edison’s response to the public consultation on the European Commission’s proposal for a Corporate 
Sustainability Due Diligence and amending Directive (EU) 2019/1937 
Edison recognizes the need to tackle the issue of due diligence to strengthen sustainable corporate 
practices. Our company is already deeply committed to sustainability and strives to guarantee the highest 
standards. We therefore welcome the European Commission’s proposal for a new directive as well as the 
opportunity to share our remarks on how to strengthen the text in view of negotiations with co-legislators.  
We believe that the objective of the proposal should be focused on human rights and the environment, 
following the framework for minimum safeguards known from the taxonomy of sustainable finance. 
Requesting that EU c ompanies integrate sustainability into their operations  in an encompassing way is 
welcome and needed. It is also important , however, to avoid that those companies are exposed to unfair 
competition from companies outside of the EU (but also companies inside the EU  excluded from the scope 
of the text) that are not subject to this responsibility. Large companies from third countries should therefore 
be held to the same high standards, thereby creating a level playing field. Including third-country companies 
in the scope of the proposal would make it possible to tackle more effectively the layers of complex supply 
chains.  
Furthermore, legal certainty for companies must be ensured and an excessive administrative burden should 
be avoided. A clear indication of the obligations imposed on companies is necessary to avoid grey areas and 
uncertainty. In addition, to avoid high administrative burdens  and costs, consideration should be given to 
limiting the number of actions and steps required from EU companies to investigate complex supply chains 
located outside of the EU, for which only limited data would be available. 
Some clarification on definitions would be helpful . The definition of 'established relationsh ips' does not 
specify the duration and intensity of the relationships, nor what level of indirect partners are affected by the 
provisions. It is unclear what is meant by 'providing targeted and proportionate support to an SME with which 
the enterprise has an established business relationship, where compliance with the code of conduct or the 
preventive action plan would jeopardize the viability of the SME. In particular, it is unclear how to assess the 
targeted and proportionate nature of  the abovementioned  support. The directive itself should give 
appropriate time to SMEs to approve the code of conduct. A gradual approach is necessary, and adequate 
time should be available for SMEs to adapt.  
Furthermore, there are no criteria for enterprises to focus their actions on the most relevant risks. Reference 
is made generically to guidelines to be issued by the Member States, with the risk of an uneven final picture 
and fragmentation across the EU.  
The definitions of harmful effects on the environment and human rights are unclear and the reference to the 
international conventions listed in the Annex, which were not drafted for business, may pose  significant 
challenges. 
We also suggest  replacing 'valu e chain' with 'supply chain' and considering a two -tier model for 
responsibilities, distinguishing between more extensive and controllable requirements for direct suppliers 
and limited requirements to conduct due diligence for indirect suppliers. More clarity is also needed in the 
mechanisms for monitoring and verifying the compliance of indirect suppliers. 
More clarity is needed on measures, websites, portals and joint stakeholder initiatives. We suggest 
establishing public registers in which compliant sup pliers are listed . This would be a win -win because it 
would benefit all players, both suppliers and buyers (notably by avoiding an unnecessary duplication of due 
diligence efforts).

Feedback on the proposed EU Corporate  
Sustainability Due Diligence Directive 
 
BASF is committed to respect internationally recognized human rights and protect the environment in its own 
operations. We embrace the responsibility to foster respect for human rights and environment in relationships 
with our business partners along the value chain. We are open to participate in the discussions on that topic.  
Therefore, we welcome the harmonization of national legal frameworks for due diligence in the EU as foreseen 
by the proposed EU Directive on Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence announced in February 2022. We are 
convinced that respecting human rights and protecting the environment in the supply chain is best regulated at 
the supranational level, at least at European level, since national laws would not sufficiently reflect the complexity 
of global supply chain relationships.  An EU legal framework should create a level playing field, provide legal 
certainty, and contribute to harmonization, even beyond the European Union. However, we see the following 
aspects as being crucial for a proper and successful implementation:  
 
◼ Clear definitions and the precise description of due diligence requirements are of decisive importance for a 
level playing field. Currently, the proposal lacks clarity about the specific due diligence obligations. The large 
number of imprecise terms provide EU Member States with large space for national interpretation. Different 
national approaches are not paying in a harmonization of the frameworks and legal certainty. 
 
◼ Due diligence obligations should be limited to the supply chain and the level of direct suppliers (Tier 1). The 
inclusion of companies’ entire value chain would lead to unmanageable obligations and unforeseeable risks. 
In many cases, companies do not have influence on their entire value chain, especially regarding the down-
stream value chain. Due to the complexity of supply chains , even the identification of Tier 2+ suppliers in 
most cases is impossible. The envisaged liability for suppliers beyond direct suppliers is therefore not real-
istic.  
 
◼ Companies can only be liable for their own activities and not for the activities of their business partners o r 
their suppliers. Civil liability should be limited to cases where the damage can be attributed to or is foresee-
able as a consequence of a company’s actions. A mechanism which foresees liability for the actions of the 
perpetrator would be consistent with the established existing legal system across the EU and bring EU-wide 
harmonization. 
 
◼ The various initiatives on sustainability issues at EU level (EU Taxonomy, Corporate Sustainability Report-
ing Directive, Deforestation & Forest Degradation, Battery Regulation, etc.) must be closely coordinated in 
terms of content and the reasonable interplay between EU level initiatives should be increased. Duplication 
of reporting requirements should be avoided, otherwise the lack of streamlining regulatory reporting frame-
works leads to overlapping and potentially inconsistent or contradictory legislation. 
 
◼ For companies pertaining to the same corporate group, an option to demonstrate compliance in a consoli-
dated manner would enable them to fulfil their obligations more effectively (e.g., by the efficient use of re-
sources) and would avoid multiplications in reporting.

◼ Clearer, more specific and targeting reference to the elements in scope for protection would enable compa-
nies to better understand what their due diligence obligations are. The general reference to many complex 
international agreements is not precise enough and causes the mixing of roles of states and companies.  
Obligations and prohibitions in the annex must be further clarified and the framework of elements eligible 
for protection must be manageable and realistic.  
 
◼ Proportionality of the measures and their consequences need to be fully examined, especially with regard 
to terminating business relationships in case of non-compliance. As this is a highly disruptive step, its ade-
quacy and effectiveness should be considered, especially with regards to the final effect on people whose 
rights are violated. 
 
◼ The due diligence requirements will need to be complemented by sector -specific guidance. Existing busi-
ness initiatives to strengthen sustainability in the supply chain, such as Together for Sustainability (TfS) and 
Responsible Care should be included.

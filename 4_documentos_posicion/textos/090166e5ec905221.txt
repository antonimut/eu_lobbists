Summary 
For the BGA human rights are non-negotiable and must be respected everywhere 
and at all times. Apart from human rights t he protection of our environment and 
climate is also a top priority for us. Nevertheless, we believe that the proposal for 
the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive is too far-reaching in several 
aspects and threatens to massively overburden European companies. The 
application of the proposal  to a company’s entire value chain is not 
comprehensible as it would lead to uncontrollable obligations and risks. 
Furthermore, it is a risk  that companies could consider to shorten their  supply 
chains, to withdraw from regions with highly problematic human rights situations 
and to cease business activities in order to minimise their own risk of breaking 
the law. This would deprive local people of their livelihoods, especially in the 
countries of the Global South. 
In the following text we will elaborate on the weaknesses of the draft and explain 
our position. 
1. Scope of application 
The regulations of the EU Corporate Due Diligence Directive apply to all  
companies with their registered office within the European Union ( EU) and with 
an average of more than 500 employees and an annual turnover of more than 
150 million euros (Article 2). 
Companies with more than 250 employees and a net turnover of more than 40 
million euros also fall within the scope of application, provide d that at least 50% 
of this net turnover was generated in one or more risk sectors. The definition of 
high-impact sectors explicitly mentions wholesale trade in the following 
industries: wholesale trade in textiles, clothing and footwear; wholesale trade i n 
agricultural raw materials, live animals, timber, food and beverages; wholesale 
trade in mineral raw materials, mineral commodities and intermediate products 
(including metals and metal ores, construction materials, fuels, chemicals and 
other intermediate products). 
BGA assessment: The scope of the draft directive is too broad. Small 
and medium-sized companies (small mid-caps with 250-500 employees) 
cannot be required to monitor their entire value chain. Companies of this 
size usually do not have their own compliance department. In its Impact 
Assessment, the European Commission defines the average costs per 
year and per directly affected company as at  least 102,000 euros, plus 
one-off costs of around 30,000 euros. This does not include the costs for 
legal aid or court costs. The Commission does not yet provide any 
information on the costs for indirectly affected companies. The scope of 
application should be limited to companies with at least 3,000 employees 
(1000 as from 2024), pursuant to the German Supply Chain Act, in order 
to relieve the burden on companies. 
It is also problematic that there are no clear criteria for the selection of 
risk sectors. This makes the separation of sectors practically impossible

blacklist of regions that pose a high risk in terms of trade. In the event 
that the definition of the scope is maintained, there is a lack of clear

2. Scope 
The Directive is intended to apply to all companies and their subsidiaries and their 
entire value chain. This means that due diligence requirement s apply both 
upwards to all levels of suppliers and downwards to all levels of customers and 
users. The directive is intended to also apply to companies of the financial sector. 
The due diligence obligations is limited to  so-called "established business 
relationships". According to the directive, this refers to "direct or indirect business 
relationships which, due to their intensity or duration, are or are expected to be 
permanent and which do not represent only an insignificant or subordinate part 
of the value chain". 
BGA assessment: We reject an extension of due diligence beyond the 
immediate business par tners. Due diligence across the entire supply 
chain is not realistic and especially not manageable for SMEs. For 
companies to be able to implement the regulations at all, the depth of the 
supply chain must be clearly defined and demarcated in a legally secure 
manner. Especially against the backdrop of risk mitigation through the 
diversification of supply chains, we demand that due diligence should 
only apply to Tier 1. In principle, companies should furthermore only be 
responsible for their own products, i.e. for products that they produce and 
import into the European market.  No responsibility can be taken for 
products that are produced and placed on the market by third parties. 
We would also like to point out the risk that companies may consider to 
shorten supply chains, withdraw from regions with highly problematic 
human rights situations and cease business activities in order to minimise 
their own risk of violating the law.  
The definition of an "established" business relationship is not sufficient 
and should be defined more clearly.  Import and export trade is 
characterised by a constant adjustment of its supply chain, due to price, 
availability, quality, origin and quanti ty. Of course, there are regular 
customers and suppliers here as well, but this is not always the norm. If 
an "established business relationship" is based on turnover with 
individual customers or suppliers, it should be noted  that the turnover 
might not be determined until the end of the year . We propose to define 
an "established business relationship" as follows: A business relationship 
based on direct framework agreements and thus involving a direct 
supplier or customer, with a minimum level of purchasing volume (5%) 
and joint business activity over a clearly defined period of time (example: 
the supplier was known and part of the supply chain for at least 3 financial 
years within the last 5 financial years).  
According to the draft directive, limitations with regard to the value chain 
are already to apply to certain (under EU law) regulated financial 
undertakings (in particular Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR) credit

Businesses need legal certainty and should be able to clearly identify 
which parts of the supply chain are subject to due diligence obligations. 
This also requires the definition of de minimis thresholds.  Definitions 
should be as precise as possible, also to protect small businesses. 
3. Due diligence 
Article 5 of the draft defines the inclusion of due diligence in corporate policy. 
Thus, companies must develop a strategy to fulfil their due diligence obligations . 
This is a duty of effort, which contains three elements:  
• long-term approach to implementing due diligence,  
• Code of Conduct including rules and principles for employees, including 
employees of subsidiaries, and  
• Procedures for implementing due diligence, including measures to 
review the aforementioned Code of Conduct.  
In addition, the due diligence strategy must be updated annually. According to 
Article 10, the monitoring of this implementation must be carried out by the 
national states. In the evaluation of implementation, "qualitativ e and quantitative 
indicators" are mentioned, but it is not explained in detail what these indicators 
should look like. Companies must establish a complaints procedure that allows 
individuals and organisations (trade unions or NGOs) to lodge complaints wit h 
the company. In addition to the company's internal complaints system, member 
states should also establish national bodies to deal with "substantiated concerns" 
(Art. 19). 
BGA assessment: An almost unrestricted possibility for complaints by 
third parties, including NGOs, represents a risk. We demand that the 
possibilities for complaints and legal action should be limited to those 
directly affected by the violations and to trade unions in the member state 
in order to create legal certainty and to avoid the rise of organisations 
specialising on complaints. In addition, there should be the possibility for 
companies to complain via an external complaints procedure - as 
introduced in the German Supply Chain Act . This could be offered, for 
example, within the framework of an industry initiative. The draft directive 
grants complainants comprehensive rights, including the right to request 
a meeting with company representatives to discuss violations . The 
practical feasibility of this procedure is questionable. Therefore, 
companies should be able to turn to central national supervisory 
authorities to mediate between the conflicting parties.  Furthermore, 
complaints should be centralised at the European  level instead of being 
dealt with in 27 individual instances.

When designing regulatory control and enforcement, it is essential that, 
given the access to sensitive company -specific information, commercial 
confidentiality is maintained at every step and instance of the process. It 
is imperative that the boundaries of data protection and competition law 
are respected. This applies, for example, to the report as well as to state-
appointed third parties. A disclosure of trade secrets could lead to 
sustainable structural changes in the B2B sector, as companies could 
switch to "direct sourcing". In this context, it would also be desirable to 
involve the relevant stakeholders in the discourse on the development of 
criteria for the designation of state-contracted third parties. 
When implementing due diligence, existing certifications such as BSCI, 
Fair Trade, Rainforest Alliance and numerous other, usually sector -
specific standards should be taken into account. In particular, it should 
be clarified that trade with certified products is sufficient to meet the 
requirements of a supply chain law. Furthermore, it needs to be clarified 
whether, for example, self -generated reports of supplier visits are 
sufficient or industry standards that have emerged from private 
multistakeholder processes are recognised. 
In order to make risk analysis more efficient, a white list should be drawn 
up of states and, where possible, also in combination with products that 
pose no or a low risk due to their high legal level or effective law  
enforcement as a country of origin or activity. Another possibility to limit 
the bureaucratic burden for companies would be the creation of negative 
lists ( blacklists). Companies that have demonstrably violated human 
rights could be listed on these lists.  
Avoidance of potential negative impacts 
Article 7 requires Member States to ensure that companies take appropriate 
measures to prevent potential adverse impacts on human rights and the 
environment, or to adequately mitigate such impacts where they cannot be 
avoided or where such measures are incremental. These include:  
• A prevention plan with appropriate and clearly defined timetables for 
action, including qualitative and quantitative indicators to measure 
improvement. 
• A contractual assurance from direct business partners that they will 
comply wi th the due diligence measures and possibly the measures 
defined in the prevention plan. 
• Make the necessary investment, for example, in management or 
production processes and infrastructures 
• Provide targeted and proportionate support to SMEs should the 
implementation of the due diligence and prevention plan jeopardise the 
viability of the SME 
• Work with other companies to improve the company's ability to address 
negative impacts.  
There is also the possibility of concluding a contract with an indirect partner  to 
achieve the implementation of due diligence and compliance with the prevention 
plan. 
BGA assessment : We welcome that the draft already provides clear 
action steps for the companies. When implementing the required 
measures, the proportionality of the company must always be taken into 
account. Smaller companies must not have the same requirements to 
fulfil their due diligence obligations as large ones.

The support for SMEs provided for in the draft is a positive first step in 
the right direction.  
Remedying actual negative impacts 
Should negative impacts on human rights or the environment occur despite all 
previously taken measures, these must be remedie d by the company according 
to Article 8. If they cannot be remedied, the minimisation of the impacts must be 
ensured. This provides, for example, for payments of compensation to the 
affected persons or affected communities. A corrective action plan with cl early 
defined timetables must also be drawn up. Again, it is mentioned that SMEs are 
to be explicitly supported in the implementation.  
If the negative impacts cannot be remedied or minimised by the measures 
described above, the company may not enter into new relationships with the 
partner from which the impacts originate (paragraph 6, Article 8). 
Combating climate change 
Article 15 explicitly addresses the issue of climate change. Companies with more 
than 500 employees and an annual turnover of 150 million euros should prepare 
a plan to ensure that they operate sustainably and make their contribution to 
limiting global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius (Paris climate target) . If climate 
change is identified as a major risk or a major impact on the business, th e 
company should include emission reduction targets in its plan. 
BGA assessment: Companies need legal certainty here as well. They 
should not have to decide for themselves whether the corporate strategy 
is in line with the goals of the Paris Agreement . Appropriate information 
should be provided on the portals and websites mentioned in Article 14. 
These are particularly indispensable when legally binding standards are 
lacking. In addition, we call for a clearer definition of economic sectors 
that are considered by the Commission to be the main risks to climate 
change. However, we point out that wholesale and foreign trade have 
very little ability to influence the climate -friendliness of production sites. 
Instead, we suggest that industry initiatives should be  created and 
promoted to increase transparency in the different economic sectors. 
4. Sanctions 
The draft provides under Article 20 that a breach of the rules implemented by the 
nation state shall be sanctioned in an effective, dissuasive and proportionate 
manner. Furthermore, member states must ensure that sanctions resulting from 
a breach are published.  
Article 22 establishes the mandatory implementation of civil liability. Companies 
are to be held liable for damage incurred due to the neglect of their duty of care. 
Companies are to be liable as soon as the damage arises from the action of a 
direct business partner in the value chain. In the case of damaging acts by indirect 
business partners, companies must be able to demonstrate that they have taken 
sufficient measures to avoid damage (for example, pointing out to the direct 
business partner that they are complying with their own due diligence obligations 
- sincere efforts). Article 25 obliges company directors in particular to comply with 
due diligence. For example, the financial remuneration of directors should be 
dependent on their contribution to the implementation of the due diligence 
strategy (Article 15).

This regulation only applies to established relationships and if the adverse effects 
could have been foreseen, prevented, stopped or mitigated through appropriate 
due diligence measures. 
In additio n, the draft stipulates in Article 24 that companies that have already 
been sanctioned should not receive any further public support measures.  
BGA assessment: We clearly reject civil liability , as it would in many 
cases increase the uncertainty in which companies already operate. 
Companies need legal certainty, which is why we demand that 
indeterminate legal terms be defined. 
We also want to clearly emphasise that the duty of care is a duty of effort, 
not a duty of success. 
5. SMEs 
Within the framework of proportionality, the burden imposed by the Directive is 
based on the size of the company, its resources and its risk profile. Small and 
medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) are not intended to fall within the scope of the 
law due to the disproportionate burden and are exempt from due diligence. In the 
event that a company has an established business relationship with an SME, 
Article 7 provides that the company is required to provide targeted and 
proportionate assistance to the SME where compliance with the Code of Conduct 
or Preventive Action Plan would jeopardise the SME's viability, e.g. through direct 
financing, soft loans, guarantees for continued procurement and assistance in 
securing financing, or technical assistance (in the form of training o r 
modernisation of management systems).  
However, as rightly noted by the EU Commission, SMEs can be indirectly 
affected if the due diligence obligations of companies falling within the scope of 
application are passed on along the value chain. In this case , the Directive 
provides for support measures to help SMEs build operational and financial 
capacity to comply with due diligence obligations. Where a contract is concluded 
with an SME with which the company has an indirect relationship and an SME 
makes contractual representations, the terms and conditions applied shall be fair, 
reasonable and non-discriminatory. Where compliance verification measures are 
applied in relation to SMEs, the enterprise shall bear the costs of the verification 
by independent thir d parties. Article 14 requires Member States and the 
Commission to provide accompanying measures for indirectly covered 
undertakings. Measures mentioned are: the maintenance of dedicated websites, 
portals or platforms, financial support and the promotion o f joint stakeholder 
initiatives. Also, lending to SMEs is to remain unaffected. 
BGA assessment : We welcome the exemption of SMEs from the 
legislation. The draft thus also complies with our wish. At the same time, 
however, we see a great danger that respons ibility will be passed along 
the supply chain. It can be assumed that large companies will impose 
their standards on small companies in order to limit their own sanction 
and liability risks. Examples from dealing with the Dodd-Frank Act, export 
controls, I ran sanctions and many other liability requirements show 
clearly and beyond doubt that these compliance obligations are passed 
on from big to small in the context of civil law requirements.  
The contractual transfer of obligations within the supply chain (according 
to Articles 7 and 8) may lead to indirectly affected SMEs outside the risk 
sectors being confronted with even more comprehensive requirements 
than directly affected companies in the risk sectors, whose due diligence 
obligations are limited to serious impacts within their own sector. We

Unfortunately, there will probably be no alternative for SMEs than to 
follow the specifications of the buyer s. As a consequence, SMEs will 
have to carry out multiple certifications for various customers. The 
problem with these multiple certifications, however, is a considerable 
increase in product costs (examples of multiple certifications: canned fish 
- MSC (Ma rine Stewardship Council) sustainable fishing - IFS - BSCI

As implementation cannot always be guaranteed due to a lack of market 
power, SMEs are at risk of being excluded from the supply chain. The 
directive specifies financial or technical support measures both for SMEs 
with which a company has established business relations and for SMEs 
that are indirectly affected. We welcome this. On the one hand, the 
measures should be further extended and specified, on the other hand, 
care should be taken that the measures do not overburden companies 
with limited personnel capacities. Furthermore, financial support for 
SMEs should be provided exclusively by the Member States or the 
Commission and not, as envisaged in the draft, also by large companies. 
Here, there would be a danger of large companies overburdening 
business relations with SMEs. 
It is urgently necessary to avoid a "reach -through" liability. In addition, 
SMEs should not be forced to give up their economic activities, neither 
by the legally uncertain wording of the directive, nor by the additional 
bureaucratic burden. The policy can support SMEs in the best possible 
way through the following points: 
1) Handouts for SMEs: What requirements does the legislator have for 
the scope of the audit of indirectly affected SMEs? How does this 
differ from directly affected companies? How can SMEs protect 
themselves from liability risks in third countries if a "temporary 
suspension" (e.g. Article 7, paragraph 5a) or "termination" of the  
business relationship (e.g. Article 7, paragraph 5b ) becomes 
necessary as a preventive measure? These and other questions 
would need to be clarified. 
2) Financial support: SMEs have neither the financial nor the human 
capacities to meet the requirements that  the companies directly 
affected by the directive demand of them. It is to be welcomed that 
the draft already provides support for SMEs here.  However, the 
support primarily refers to SMEs that are active in the European 
internal market. SMEs outside the EU  could be disadvantaged by 
this. In addition, companies in countries of origin should be actively 
supported in implementing the requirements of the European Supply 
Chain Act locally. For this, it is necessary that development 
cooperation is provided with sufficient resources. 
3) Joint stakeholder initiatives should be strengthened: Through 
industry, product and networking initiatives, forces can be combined 
and uniform, implementable approaches can be developed to fulfil 
due diligence obligations as well as to verify contractual assurances. 
There is an urgent need to avoid a situation where SMEs have to 
fulfil different due diligence obligations for each of their customers 
and where the legal departments of individual companies are left with

4) "One in, one out" principle: In order not to jeopardise the

6. Harmonisation 
By introducing a s upply chain law at EU level, the EU Commission is creating 
more legal certainty and a "level playing field" for companies. Due diligence and 
accountability obligations will be harmonised across Europe. This prevents 
companies from being disadvantaged by na tional regulations in European 
competition. At the same time, the freedom of establishment of companies is 
guaranteed.  
Uniform EU regulations ensure a level playing field in the internal market, but not 
in other EU countries. For this purpose, the propose d Directive provides for the 
inclusion of companies from third countries that are active in a risk sector and sell 
their products in the internal market.  
BGA assessment: We welcome the harmonisation of due diligence at 
European level in order to create a level playing field within the European 
Union. The coexistence of national regulations and initiatives should be 
prevented as a matter of urgency, as these would entail increased 
complexity for companies as well as additional costs. We also support 
the additional measures taken by the EU to ensure harmonisation. For 
example, the establishment of a European network of supervisory 
authorities (Article 21) and the provision of guidance to assist companies 
or Member State authorities in fulfilling corporate due  diligence 
obligations (Article 13) are mentioned. We call for the German law to be 
merged into a European law. 
7. Context 
The proposal is in the context of a number of other European regulations (e.g. 
Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive, Taxonomy Re gulation, Conflict 
Minerals Regulation, Deforestation Regulation proposal). The aim is to 
complement the EU regulatory environment, which currently lacks an EU -wide 
transparent and predictable framework to help EU companies in all sectors of the 
economy assess and manage sustainability risks and impacts in a number of core 
areas of human rights and environmental risks and impacts and in their value 
chains. 
BGA assessment: We demand clear coherence between the multitude 
of European initiatives and regulations that already exist. There must not 
be an additional burden for companies by having to fulfil double reporting 
obligations towards the Commission. The bureaucratic burden on 
companies must be kept as low as possible.

8. Legal Act 
The proposal is a directive, i.e. a framework law, which leaves the national states 
room for a certain amount of leeway as to the exact rules to be adopted.  
BGA assessment : We call for a regulation instead of a directive to 
ensure the uniform implementation of due diligence within the member 
states of the European Union. A directive leads to unequal conditions and 
an unfair burden on companies within the EU. With different 
implementation laws, each of the 27 Member States will take a different 
approach, fragmenting the internal market. 
9. Differences to German Supply Chain Act 
The EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive  goes further than the 
German Supply Chain Act: the EU draft directive covers companies with 500 or 
more employees and net sales of 150 mi llion euros or more, or 250 or more 
employees and net sales of 40 million euros or more (risk sectors), whereas the 
German law covers companies with at least 3,000 (year 2023) or 1,000 
employees (from 2024). In the EU law, SMEs fall directly within the sco pe of 
application, provided they operate in a risk sector.  
Another difference is the scope of the laws: The EU draft directive covers the 
entire value chain, the German law only the direct business partner in the supply 
chain - indirectly only if there are indications of violations.  
As far as liability is concerned, the EU draft directive provides for fines and civil 
liability for breaches of due diligence obligations, whereas the German law does 
not provide for civil liability, only fines. Furthermore, t he EU draft directive goes 
further than the German law, as due diligence obligations are extended to include 
climate and environmental aspects. The EU draft directive provides for more 
general measures to implement the due diligence obligations, while thos e of the 
German law are formulated more concretely. However, there is a deviation in the 
linking of bonus payments for managers with supply chain compliance, which is 
provided for in the EU draft directive but not in the German law. 
10. The Federation of German Wholesale, Foreign Trade 
and Services (BGA)  
The Federal Association of German Wholesale, Foreign Trade and Services 
(Bundesverband Großhandel, Außenhandel, Dienstleistungen e.V. - BGA) is the 
umbrella organisation of wholesale and foreign trade as well as business-related 
services. It has 69 federal trade associations as well as state and regional 
associations. 
The 23 state and regional associations organised in the BGA act locally as 
employers' and bargaining agent associations and, among other thi ngs, provide 
labour law advice and labour court representation for member companies, 
represent the socio -political interests of wholesale and foreign trade and 
conclude collective agreements for the economic level at regional level. 
The BGA represents the interests of 143,000 trade and service companies in -
Germany with almost 2 million employees and 60,000 apprentices. The 
companies in wholesale and foreign trade generated a turnover of around 1,500 
billion euros in 2021.

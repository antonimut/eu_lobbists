 
 
 
 
 
CCCEU’s view on the Proposal for a Capital Requirements 
Directive and Capital Requirements Regulation 
Brussels, 23 May 2022 
 
On 23 February 2022, the European Commission published a Proposal for a Directive 
on Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence (CSDD). This legislation aims to attain the 
ambitious goal of strengthening human rights and environmental protection through a 
harmonised and uniform legal framework at the European level. 
Following the release of the draft Proposal, the China Chamber of Commerce to the EU 
(CCCEU) would like to present the views of the Chinese business community in Europe. 
Overall remarks 
The CCCEU and its members share the view that companies play a key role in building a 
sustainable economy and society. We recog nise the overall importance of promoting 
sustainable and responsible corporate behaviour throughout global trade and supply 
chains. As such, we are supportive of all international and multilateral  initiatives when 
it comes to the respect for human rights and environmental protection. 
However, even though the CCCEU agrees with the ultimate objectives that the CSDD 
pursues, the proposed framework r aises a number of concerns for our members. In 
essence, we believe that the CSDD Proposal, as it stands now, contains a number of 
shortcomings, which are likely to create unbalanced, disproportionate and ambiguous 
obligations for all business operators, including our members. As such, we fear that 
these obligations will eventually hinder the Commission’s target of creating more legal 
certainty and a level playing field for all businesses in the EU. Therefore, we invite to the 
Commission to reconsider these aspects and to improve the Proposal accordingly. 
Our reasoning originates from the following considerations about legal certainty, the 
level-playing field, and better regulation. The principle of legal certainty requires 
statutory rules to be sufficiently clear, specific and proportionate, and thus predictable 
for the subject of these rules. Likewise, a level playing field entails balanced rights and 
obligations (liabilities), and a fair and non -discriminatory outcome arising from the 
implementation of the rules for all market players. Lastly, better regulati on principles 
demand that EU legislation  is evidence-based and sufficiently justified in terms of the 
need for the proposed EU intervention; that its implications and results are measurable 
and foreseeable ; that the means deployed by the legislation are pr oportionate and 
necessary to achieve the foreseen objectives; and, most importantly, that it conforms to 
the principle of subsidiarity under the EU Treaties and that it is in line with the EU’s 
WTO obligations and international commitments.

Therefore, we regret to observe that the CSDD Proposal does not conform to the above 
cited principles and requirements. It is not surprising that, as it was reported , the 
Regulatory Scrutiny Board (RSB) issued two negative opinions regarding this Proposal.  
In the following sections, we elaborate on these issues, while pointing out that some of 
them are interconnected and go beyond our subdivision in five themes. Accordingly, we 
also provide suggestions for each area. 
Harmonisation and Fragmentation 
As stated in the first pages of the Proposal, one of the main objectives of the CSDD is to 
harmonise the applicable rules and to avoid fragmentation of corporate sustainability 
due diligence standards in the EU. The Commission  also expressed the view that  full 
harmonization would favour the creation of a level playing field in the area of due 
diligence. 
Therefore, the Commission point s out that Article 50 TFEU a cts as the legal basis for 
this Directive. According to the Commission, “ the proposed act is designed to prevent and 
remove such obstacles to free movement and distortions of competition by harmonising the 
requirements for companies to carry out due diligence in their own operation, subsidiaries and 
value chains and related directors’ duties. They wi ll lead to a level playing field where 
companies of similar size and their directors are subject to the same requirements for 
integrating sustainable corporate governance and corporate due diligence measures in their 
internal management systems and thereby  protecting the interests of the company’s 
stakeholders in a similar way.”1 
However, we find that the Proposal adopts a decentralised and fragmented approach in 
terms of enforcement. First ly, the proposed Directive will need to be transposed into 
national law of the Member States to become enforceable. Yet, the Directive only 
provides for high-level principles and requirements without any implementation details, 
and this will give EU Member States large discretion in the transposition process to fit it 
in t heir own - often very different  - national legal system and traditions. This is 
exacerbated by many novel yet ambiguous concepts and definitions provided for in the 
Directive, which give rise to possibly very distinct interpretation s and application 
standards, as we demonstrate below. 
Secondly, Article 17 of the CSDD Proposal allows each Member State to designate one 
or more supervisory authorities to supervise compliance with the obligations laid down 
in the national rules transposing the Directive, and to enforce these rules. The European 
Network of Supervisory Authorities (ENSA) , to be set up in accordance with Article 21 
of the proposed Directive , is mainly designed for information sharing and for 
administrative cooperation. Thus, we find that t his structure does not alleviate the  
abovementioned concerns relating to the inconsistent implementation of the same 
rules amongst different Member States, or even between different supervisory 
authorities within the same Member State.

Thirdly, we expect the situation to worsen and become more controversial when it 
comes to judicial enforcement in relation to the civil liabilities endorsed by the proposed 
Directive. In fact, it can not be excluded that the Member States courts  will apply the 
legal standards prevailing in their respective national legal system for the interpretation 
and application of civil liability.  
As a result of these three considerations, ours members are concerned that uniform and 
harmonised implementation of the proposed rules is unlikely to be ensured. In turn, this 
will lead  to competitive advantages or disadvantages for companies located in the 
different Member States, because the obligations and liabilities will not be consistent 
across the EU, and thus a level playing field cannot be guaranteed. 
Suggestions 
 The Directive shall provide for rules which are as precise as possible, thus leaving 
less room for disparity during the transposition process at national level.   
 Guidelines, model contract clauses, and  delegated acts referred to in the Proposal 
will help the implementation of the proposed rules . They  shall be developed and 
published as soon as possible, before the time limit for the transposition expires. 
 ENSA might be best positioned to ensure consistent implementation among the EU 
Member States where questions relating to critical application standards arise, or 
when enforcement across Member States so requires. 
 Civil liability provisions shall be completely excluded from the Proposal, thus 
leaving the existing national liability rules of each Member State to play the major 
role, in line with its established judicial system. 
Ambiguous concepts, definitions, and rules 
The CSDD Proposal introduces vague concepts, definitions and rules in several essential 
aspects. This creates difficulties a nd ambiguities for the CCCEU members in their 
preparation for compliance , and it increases legal uncertainties in disfavour of the 
businesses. The legal uncertainty is particular problematic considering the enhanced 
public enforcement, sanctions, and potential civil liabilities associated with the breach 
of the due diligence obligations laid down in the Directive.  
Such ambiguity is related, but not limited, to the following aspects: 
o Established business relationship 
The new concept of ‘established business relationship’ will be introduced by the 
Proposal. It is associated with overarching due diligence obligations that subject 
companies will need to comply with, fault of which would trigger sanctions and liabilities. 
Therefore, it is critical that this concept is well defined and that its scope is ascertainable. 
According to Article 3(f) of the proposed Directive, an “established business relationship” 
is defined as “a direct or indirect business relationship that is or should be permanent in view 
of its intensity or duration and that does not represent an insignificant or merely subordinate 
 
   
4 
part of the value chain. ”2 Whether business relationships are considered “established” 
shall be checked regularly, and at least every twelve months, by companies, pursuant to 
Article 1(1) of the proposed Directive. 
We believe that t he criteria used in this definition are too vague and leave too much 
room for interpretation , eventually leading to legal uncertainty for a c ompany that 
needs to identify its business partners with which it has ‘ established business 
relationship’. Likewise, the related intensity and duration standards are not defined, nor 
is the insignificant or subordinate role the partner might have in the supply chain. In 
addition, n o reference is made to quantifiable criteria, such as share of cost (for 
upstream business partners) or turnover (for downstream business partners). As such, 
companies cannot obtain precise indications to identify said  business relationship s, 
which seriously undermines their abilities to conduct the due diligence process. 
o High-impact sectors 
As regards the scope of companies subject to CSDD, i n addition  to the em ployee and 
turnover thresholds, Article 2(1)(b) lists  certain high -impact sectors where lower 
thresholds apply to determine which companies are subject to the Proposal. In this 
regard, we find that t he list of high -impact sectors is quite broad and not sufficiently 
refined, because it includes almost the pro duction and distribution of any products in 
the area of textile and footwear, agriculture and food, mineral and metal products 
(excluding machinery and equipment). 
This very general description , which does not correlate  the products to any sort of 
nomenclature, for instance the WCO HS system, or the activities to any sector 
classification like NACE, creates confusion about which products and sector s are 
covered by the proposed rules . Additionally, the  term “wholesale trade” is nowhere 
defined in the proposed text, leaving room for divergent interpretations. 
o Contractual assurances 
The term ‘contractual assurances ’ is referred  to several times in the Proposal (e.g. 
Articles 7(2), 7(4), 8(3) , 8(5)), and it is described as an effective mechan ism to ensure 
compliance with companies’ codes of conduct, and prevention or corrective action plans 
by their partners, with which they have an established business relationship. Again, 
details on what needs to be covered in such “contractual assurances” a re missing in the 
Proposal, and the guidance about voluntary model contractual clauses are yet to be 
adopted by the Commission. 
The CCCEU members voiced strong concerns about the practicality of such contractual 
assurances and about the negative consequences of such contractual cascading in the 
supply chains, in particular towards SMEs and non-EU companies. These will eventually 
become the ultimate obligation performers and will assume most of the burdens and 
costs, given their comparatively lower, or even nil, bargaining power in the business 
relationship, even though  the Proposal suggests that fair, reasonable , and non -
discriminatory contractual terms ought to be used in such contracts.

o Emission reduction target 
In Article 15, t he CSDD Proposal lays down requirements for companies to link their 
business model and strategy to the goal of combatting climate change and emission 
reduction, with an explicit r eference to the Paris Agreement’s target of limiting global 
warming to 1.5 degree  Celsius. In this regard, we find that it is  unclear how this can be 
implemented in practice. Additionally, t his becomes more problematic when the 
fulfilment of these obligations by a company is tied with the variable remuneration of its 
directors. In the a bsence of any practical and objective assessment mechanism, these 
obligations seem hardly implementable. 
Above all, we would like to stress that this target is binding for signatory countries to 
the Paris Agreement, rather than for the private companies. It follows that n either the 
EU nor the national governments should carry their own obligations over to companies. 
o Directors’ duty of care 
Article 25 of the CSDD Proposal only introduces the duty of care of company Directors 
without further details. Conseque ntly, this leaves unanswered how this rule  shall be 
implemented in practice by the Directors, or under what conditions the Directors’ duty 
of care can be deemed fulfilled. This is particularly relevant due to the potential personal 
administrative or civil liability of a company’s management board for breach of such 
duty under national laws, as prescribed by Article 25(2). 
o Essential legal terms in determining obligations and liabilities 
Under the CSDD Proposal, a  ‘reasonability assessment ’ is key for  determining the 
obligations and liabilities. See, for instance, “reasonable expectation” under Articles 7(5), 
7(6), and 8(7);  “reasonable grounds to believe” or “reasons to believe” under Articles 
9(2), 10 and 19(1); and Article 22 on civil liability. 
Yet, i n the absence of clear and indicative standards for the assessment, these terms  
may produce very divergent results , especially when being interpreted and applied by 
Member States supervisory authorities , or by national courts in accordance with local 
legal traditions and procedures. 
Additionally, when pecuniary sanctions are imposed as a result of a breach, they will be 
based on the company’s turnover  ex Article 20 (3). However, no further clarification is 
given with regard to whether it refers to the turnover of the individual subject company 
committing the breach, or to that of the company group on a consolidated basis. 
Likewise, when it comes to non -EU companies, it is unclear whether it will relate to the 
turnover generated in the EU or to the company’s worldwide turnover. 
The ambiguous concepts, definitions or rules identified above are only some illustrative 
examples. In brief, we find the proposed Directive to be too vague in many aspects, and 
we are worried that this will make its implementation extremely difficult for companies, 
thus jeopardising its consistent enforcement across the EU. With these definitions, legal 
certainty and the level-playing field are not ensured.

Suggestions 
 We call on  the Commission to adopt guidelines on practical issues, as well as 
guidance on model contract clauses as soon as possible, so as to provide the 
necessary clarifications to the above mentioned issues. 
 All key concepts or standards shall be defined as unambiguously as possible. Where 
possible, the definition should rely on quantifiable standards, for example costs or 
turnover, or it should correlate to HS nomenclature or existing classification 
systems. 
 The climate change and emission reduction target shall be removed, since it is not 
practically enforceable through the due diligence procedure. 
 Standards for the duty of care and “reasonability” assessment are to be elaborated 
further, with a view to ensure a coherent implementation. 
 Pecuniary sanctions shall be limited to and based  solely on the turnover of the 
individual company which committed the due diligence breaches. 
Broad scope of application 
In our view,  the scope of the CSDD Proposal  with regard to the identification of  the 
subject companies is broad and very difficult to implement.  
Ex Article 2, two basic factors are used as threshold criteria in the determination of the 
companies subject to the Directive: the number of employees, and the company’s annual 
turnover. The thresholds applicable to EU and non-EU companies are slightly different, 
but in both cases the thresholds for companies in certain high-impact sectors (Group 2) 
are lower than those for other companies (Group 1). Based on the Commission’s 
estimation, the Directive will cover about 13,000 EU companies and about 4,000 non -
EU companies. Nonetheless, the CCCEU believes that this is an underestimation. 
Firstly, the thresho lds prescribed by the CSDD Proposal are lower than those in the 
existing due diligence law in EU Member States like Germany and France, and thus they 
catch far more companies across and beyond the EU.  
Secondly, transnational corporations have footprints and operations globally or within 
the EU. This is the case, for instance, of many financial institutions. As a result, it is much 
easier for companies to meet the thresholds laid down in the CSDD Proposal, contrary 
to the estimation of the Commission.  
Thirdly, although not directly falling within the scope the CSDD, companies along the 
entire supply chain of the subject company will be also subject to the due diligence 
obligations and will become liable for any breach, through the contract cascading. As 
such, the companies eventually covered by the CSDD are multiplied by hundreds or 
even thousands. This evidently deviates from the original objective of harmonizing the 
EU legal framework and from the principle of subsidiarity, but it will result in the 
creation of new standards which go far beyond those provided by existing national rules.

Suggestions 
 The applicable thresholds of the CSDD should be considerably raised, in particular 
with regard to the number employees, and at least aligned with the national 
thresholds in EU Member States like Germany and France. 
Excessive administrative burdens and disproportionate liabilities  
We believe that a good regulatory tool shall maintain a balanced and reasonable 
approach with regard to rights and obligations, and potential liabilities, for the subjects 
of the regulation. In particular, it shall not impose overly burdensome, unnecessary, or 
overlapping obligations. 
In the field of sustainability and corporate due diligence, there are already best practices 
and several initiat ives of regulatory and self -regulatory nature at international, 
European, and national level. In the EU, this includes for instance the Corporate 
Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) and the Social Taxonomy. Therefore, it makes 
sense from a better regulation perspective to consider it reasonable that any new EU 
initiative in this field shall be aligned from the outset with prevailing practices and 
existing initiatives.  
The CCCEU believes that this is not the case with regard to the CSDD Proposal. We 
understand that , under the Proposal, companies will assume general obligations to 
identify, prevent or mitigate potential human rights and environmental adverse impact, 
and to end or minimise their actual ones. The Proposal lays down a list of specific 
measures to fulfil those due diligence obligations. However, the latter go much f arther 
than existing practices and national supply chain laws in some EU member states, not 
least by introducing a civil liability regime, and by imposing disproportionate, 
administratively burdensome and cost -intensive obligations on companies, with the  
consequent liabilities.  
This can be observed in three aspects : in  the much wider scope of subject companies 
along the entire value chain as opposed to direct suppliers; in the much severe sanction 
and liability regime; and in the trickle-down effect created by the contract cascading. 
o Unlimited tracing up and down in the supply chain 
The most problematic aspect of the CSDD Proposal is that it requires subject companies 
to exercise due diligence along the entire value chain, both directly and indirectly. This 
requirement inevitably leads to the customers and suppliers having to check each other 
as part of their duty of care, at a level which goes beyond their control.  
According to the consideration in paragraph (18) of the preamble to the CSDD Proposal, 
the value chain should be considered as encompassing both upstream and downstream 
business relationships, both inside and outside th e EU. It should include direct and 
indirect business relationships, and it should involve all products and services to the 
company that are necessary to carry out the company’s activities. It is th erefore not 
difficult to imagine that , for a big corporatio n, direct suppliers alone could amount to 
thousands, without mentioning direct customers and the layers beyond the direct 
business relationships in the supply chain s. This  reasoning will be even truer when 
 
   
8 
extended to service providers for transport and product storage, product dismantling, 
recycling, composting or landfilling, and so on. 
We find that the unlimited tracing up and down the supply chain completely disregards 
the limited resources that subject companies could dispose  of for monitoring and 
supervision. In a nutshell, it makes the due diligence practically impossible. The resulting 
compliance costs would also be extremely disproportionate and excessive. 
Companies need clear, foreseeable and practically executable measures.  These 
measures shall also be limited to the activities within the control of a company, namely 
those of its own company, its subsidiaries and its direct suppliers. Very few companies 
can exercise influence over their direct business partners in the supply  chain, even less 
over their indirect ones.  
In light of the above, the CSDD Proposal results impractical in light of its implementation 
requirements. It imposes overly burdensome compliance obligations on companies, it 
entails excessive administrative costs for them, and it breaks the balance between rights 
and obligations. Consequently, companies will have to limit and severe ties with most of 
their supplying sources to limit their exposure to the due diligence, which in turn puts at 
risk the security of supply and the already vulnerable supply chains in the post-COVID-
19 recovery. 
o Parallel and overlapping reporting 
Article 11 of the Proposal requires companies to communicate their due diligence 
compliance through reporting or by publishing on their website an annual statement. 
Such reporting requirement is feasible and not burdensome for the companies. At the 
same time , we argue that it  shall avoid creating parallel, differentiated or even 
contradictory reporting contents and standards.  
To this end, we remind that the Commission has already tabled a  CSRD Proposal in 
parallel, and there also exist sector specific reporting or disclosure requirements, as well 
as established practices. Companies should not be asked to make multiple reporting for 
the same objective , which creates unnecessary administrative burden and cost s. We 
also argue that , if a subject company belongs to a company group, its reporting 
obligations shall be deemed fulfilled where  the reporting or communication is already 
carried out at group level. 
o Compliance with local law standards in third countries 
Companies in non-EU countries which are active in the EU market might also be subject 
to the CSDD , as long as the y meet the  applicable turn over and employee thresholds . 
However, these companies established under the law of third countries are first bound 
by statutory requirements of these countries with respect to human rights protection 
and environmental standards. In this regard we would like to stress that, provided that 
the third country local standards and requirements are in line with the international 
conventions and best practice, the CSDD shall not have such extra-territorial effect of 
imposing additional or even contradictory obligations on non-EU companies, which will 
make it impossible for these companies to comply with the local regulatory framework.

o Alternative implementation options 
The proposed Directive provides for contractual assurance to be accompanied by 
appropriate measure to verify compliance, including suitable industry initiatives or 
independent third -party verification. If global standards and third -party certification 
are considered to be an acceptable implementation strategy of the Directive, it will be 
possible to avoid overlapp ing and cross -audits, thus considerably reducing the 
administrative cost on companies. Certification by authorized independent agents will 
also improve the coherent interpretation of standards, promote internationally 
recognized best practices, and help establish a global and common playing field. 
o Unpredictable and disproportionate liability risk 
We believe that t he CSDD Proposal also entails considerable and disproportionate 
liability risks for companies, which can be attributed principally to the extensive scope 
of the duty of care along the supply chains.  
The Proposal introduces both public and private enforcement mechanism s aiming at 
sanctioning breaching companies. On the one hand, it provides for a robust system of 
public enforcement, combing the full -fledged investigation power s of national 
supervisory authorities and corresponding sancti ons in case of breach findings, ex 
Articles 18 and 20. On the other hand, provisions are also introduced to allow for private 
liability claims for damages caused by an adverse impact arising from a company’s 
failure to comply with the due diligence obligations, including those caused by an  
adverse impact arising from the activities of an indirect partner with whom a subject 
company has an established business relationship ( Article 22). This liability comes on 
top of the claims against the subsidiaries or business partners causing the damage s at 
stake, and it overrides any equivalent national law applicable to claims to that effect. 
The CCCEU considers the above civil liability mechanism  to be too far-fetched. Firstly, 
we believe that a company shall only be responsible for its own wrongdoings. Hence, its 
liability shall be limited to the activities that it can control and of which the 
consequences are reasonably foreseeable.  That shall only include activities of the 
subject company and its subsidiaries. Beyond that, companies would end up being held 
accountable for excessive responsibilities,  in a way which is  disproportionate to the 
company’s due obligations and legitimate expectations. Considering that a subject 
company has to identify, prevent and mitigate not only actual human rights and 
environmental adverse impacts, but “potential” impacts as well, it is virtually exposed to 
unmanageable risks and unlimited liabilities, against any established legal principles. In 
the same manner, ex Article 25 directors of a company subject to the duty o f care and 
related liability shall not concern themselves with the management personnel who does 
not have a say on the management issues relating to sustainability or who was not 
attributed the power to do so. 
At the same time , there is a high likelihood for a subject company of being penalised 
twice for the same behaviour, i.e., first for the damages caused by an adverse impact 
when it arises, and then for the failure to identify or prevent such adverse impact even 
if it was only remotely potential before it arose.  
 
   
10 
Moreover, we think that the Proposal risks triggering a wave of enforcement 
investigations combined with multiple civil actions for damages against subject 
companies and their business partners. Regardless of any wrongdoings having been 
confirmed through public enforcement or court conviction, media attention will likely 
increase due to  such enforcement actions ; and  this could lead to a preliminary 
conviction by the pubic and to inevitable reputational damages to the companies before 
any definitive outcome of the proceedings is released.  This is without mentioning the 
vast resources that these companies have to dedicate in order to handle these judicial 
proceedings. 
Last, the overriding mandatory application of the civil liability provisions in the CSDD 
directive, which would be prevailing over the Member States national legal orders, is 
above all questionable. It arguably goes beyond the competence of the EU and it is in any 
event against the principle of subsidiarity of EU law. 
Suggestions 
 The due diligence compliance obligations shall apply only to the scope over which a 
subject company can effectively exercise control. That is either within the company 
group, or in only one direction in the supply chain, i.e. upstream (with suppliers only); 
and limited to the direct business relationships. 
 Likewise, a subject company shall only be held liable for its own wrongdoing s, 
potentially including its subsidiaries, but not for those of its business partners. Any 
“potential” adverse impact shall be reasonably foreseeable. A company shall not be 
responsible for any adverse impacts as long as it acts with sufficient care to identify 
and prevent them, or as long as it takes sufficient remedial actions. 
 The duty of care and liability of directors of a company  shall not be imposed on the 
management personnel who does not exercise an executive role in the board, or 
who is not attributed any functional power relating to sustainability. 
 Non-EU companies shall be exempted from certain due diligence obligations under 
the CSDD Directive if fulfilling these obligations will make the companies violate 
the local laws of the countries where they are established, as long as these local laws 
do not contradict the international conventions to which these countries adhere, or 
if these countries have made express reservation relating to the relevant provisions 
of the conventions as the latter so permit. 
 Provisions of civil liability for damages shall be removed from the CSDD Directive. 
If any such mechanism were judged necessar y, it should be based on existing 
Member States national laws to that effect. Such claim shall only be admissible only 
after a pre -requisite complaint has been brought to the company by stakeholders 
having a legitimate interest and direct concern, allowing  it to take remedial 
measures and , if the foregoing measures are unsatisfactory, after affirmative 
findings of adverse impacts causing that damages at issue are established by the 
competent supervisory authorities or through the conviction by a national co urt. 
Companies shall be exclusively liable for the actual damages directly caused by 
 
   
11 
themselves. In no circumstance shall they be held accountable for the damages 
caused by their business partners. 
 We advise the Commission to streamline reporting and discl osure requirements at 
both EU and national levels to avoid the overlapping and duplicating of such 
obligations on to the subject companies.  Any such disclosure or communication 
obligations shall be closely aligned to existing sectorial initiatives and prac tices, 
including the CSRD, and combined with it to the extent possible. Reporting should 
be preferably allowed to be made at company group consolidated level. 
 The Commission should also accept the implementation strategy of third -party 
certification on the  basis of globally accepted standards. Detailed rules and 
guidance shall be developed and adopted accordingly for the practical 
implementation of third-party certification mechanisms. Once audited and certified 
by authorized organizations, a company shall be deemed having fulfilled its due 
diligence obligations. 
 The CSDD Directive shall provide for equal access to accompanying measures, such 
as financial and technical support, available to all companies in the supply chains, 
including non-EU companies. 
Insufficient transition period for businesses  
The CSDD Proposal provides for a two -year transposition period and it requires 
immediate application of the due diligence compliance for Group 1 companies at the 
expiry of the transposition period ; whereas Group 2 companies active in high -impact 
sectors are granted a grace period of two more years (Article 30).  
However, we believe that the Proposal ignores the fact that the Directive only provides 
for minimum and high -level harmonization , and that the Member States have a 
considerable discretion in selecting the means for its transposition , which will 
eventually lay down the details regarding  the implementation and related procedures. 
As such, companies will still need sufficient time to take in and implement these detailed 
rules for due diligence. The immediate application of these rules after their adoption at 
national level is impracticable, if not impossible. 
Suggestions 
 A sufficient transition period needs to be included in the Proposal, in ord er to 
allow companies to prepare for the due diligence process  they will meet at 
Member State level. Preferably, this transaction period shall be at least two years, 
starting after the adoption of the rules transposed at national level. 
Conclusion 
For the CCCEU members, the Proposal raises a number of concerns with regard to 
unbalanced, disproportionate and ambiguous obligations imposed on  business 
operators. In particular, we would like to voice their concerns about the legal certainty  
and harmonisation which the CSDD Proposal aims to bring about . We find that the 
current Proposals ultimately fails to do so, due to issues in the definition of key legal 
 
   
12 
concepts, in the competence repartition between the EU and its Members states, and in 
the creation of disproportionate liabilities on companies for matters and actors which 
fall out of the companies’ scope and capacity to act. 
Nonetheless, we share the European Commission’s view that companies play a key role 
in building a sustainable economy and society. We recognises the overall importance of 
promoting sustainable and responsible corporate behaviour throughout global trade 
and supply chains. As such, we are supportive of all international and multilateral 
initiatives when it comes to respect for human rights and environmental protection. 
In conclusion, we hope that our comments and constructive suggestions made above 
can help the Commission assess the content of the Proposal and improve it accordingly, 
in order to support the companies in complying with the due diligence requirements. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
About the CCCEU 
The China Chamber of Commerce to the EU (CCCEU) is a non -governmental 
organisation founded in Brussels in 2018. The CCCE U now represents over 70 
companies and chambers in EU Member States, and speaks on behalf of about 1,000 
Chinese enterprises in the EU.  
Its mission is to promote China -EU economic cooperation, to increase the mutual 
understanding and dialogue between China and the EU, and to bridge the existing gaps 
and explore ways to enhance collaboration of Chinese businesses in Europe. Its 
overarching aim is to contribute to common prosperity while respecting the diversity in 
the EU.

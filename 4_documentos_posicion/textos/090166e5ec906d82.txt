Introduction  
On 23 February 2022, the European Commission published its long-awaited proposal for a Directive on Corporate 
Sustainability Due Diligence (CSDDD).  
As a business association  whose mission is to support its members with improving the social and environmental 
performance of their supply chains through common tools and collaborative approaches, amfori has been a long-standing 
advocate of the benefits of well-designed EU-wide due diligence legislation1. Importantly, we see legislation as one element 
of a broader comprehensive policy approach  to due diligence  which should also include cooperation with non -EU 
countries. 
We therefore welcome the EU Commission’s move and the fact that the proposal aims to elevate the topic of due diligence 
by also addressing companies’ governance aspects. The tone from the top matters and we believe it will be essential to 
generate greater positive impact.  
We were also encouraged to see a positive acknowledgment of the role that industry initiatives can play in supporting 
companies with their due diligence, something that amfori has been doing for the past 20 years.  
Industry collaborative due diligence initiatives play a key enabling role in the implementation of due diligence. They can 
offer efficiencies, economies of scale and impact. They are not the solution to due diligence, but an important piece of the 
puzzle. Effective use of these tools is key to ensuring that the high policy ambition can be implemented on the ground  – 
and it is important that accountability for compliance with legal responsi bilities remains fully with individual companies. 
Conversations around how to make them an effective part of the due diligence ecosystem is where amfori is eager to play 
a constructive role.  
While we applaud the EU Commission’s level of ambition, we believe the proposal in its current form falls short of its policy 
objectives and needs further strengthening, in terms of enhanced harmonisation, greater legal certainty, full level playing 
field and stronger alignment with international standards.  
In this paper we offer a set of recommendations to improve the proposal so that it delivers on its intended impact, whilst 
remaining pragmatic in its implementation. We are currently assessing both the intended and unintended consequences 
of the proposal with our members, and as such we may elaborate further on additional aspects as we move through the 
legislative process.

What the EU CSDDD proposal should aim to achieve 
1. Harmonisation 
Since the United Nations Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs) were endorsed in 2011, there has 
been a growing body of national legislation mandating that companies act responsibly. Those laws, despite being largely 
inspired by international norms,  have been framed differently. The due diligence regulatory landscape that businesses 
need to navigate is therefore quite patchy. 
The EU CSDDD proposal  has the potential to move away from this fragmented approach by setting a n ambitious yet 
pragmatic standard that could potentially be replicated in other jurisdictions  outside of the EU , or become the de facto 
global standard if taken up by internationally-operating companies.  
Unfortunately, the way in which the proposal is currently drafted leaves room for  Member States to depart from the letter 
of the EU text. This is likely to result in a fragmentation of the EU internal market – with key elements being implemented 
in different ways, including at enforcement level. It would therefore mean missing out on one important objective that the 
Directive aims to achieve: harmonisation of the due diligence requirements across sectors and supply chains.  
→ We urge the EU Institutions to work towards achieving full harmonisation.  
From amfori’s perspective , it is important  to avoid that businesses are confronted with differing and inconsistent 
expectations depending on the markets they operate in. Such a scenario could result in important resources being diverted 
into compliance. It also bears the risk of a multitude of differing requests being cascaded to smaller players in the supply 
chains of larger companies. We have many SMEs among our members in this situation and already today they are under 
considerable pressure by their customers  to meet certain sustainability criteria.  A fragmented approach risks 
sharpening the existing burden.  
2. Legal certainty & policy coherence  
Definitions and provisions ought to be clear -cut to avoid ambiguity and the triggering of wrong corporate behavio ur. The 
definition of established business relationships, for instance, leaves room for interpretation and could potentially offer 
companies who do not want to act responsibly an incentive to adopt a strategy based on producer hopping (i.e., not having 
any “established business relationships”) . Similarly, the provision around civil liability does not make it clear where a 
company’s liability would start or end. The article on combatting climate change, for instance, refers to principal risk or 
impact but fails to define what “principal” means.  
→ From amfori’s members’ perspective, there is a need to ensure that the EU Directive provides for greater legal certainty. 
→ Furthermore, w e encourage the EU Institutions to ensure greater coherence across the broader regulatory 
ecosystem.  
Building a coherent policy architecture is needed both for legal certainty and policy effectiveness. We see  for instance 
important synergies between the objectives of the EU CSDDD legislation - which will require companies to conduct due 
diligence - and the EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive ( CSRD) proposal, which is meant to improve

3. Level playing field 
International standards leave no doubt: due diligence is a business responsibility regardless of company size or position 
in the supply chain. While all businesses bear this responsibility, however, those standards make it clear that expectations 
need to be reasonable and commensurate to the capacity, resources and leverage of a given business.  Proportionality 
therefore becomes key alongside flexibility.  
→ We still believe the ultimate ambition of the EU law  should be that operating in the EU internal market  is conditional 
upon conducting due diligence, regardless of sector, size and supply chain.  
This could be achieved by means of a progressive expansion of the scope of the Directive for instance, and by factoring 
in clear time-bound transitional periods for that expansion. Both proportionality and flexibility should be applied so that the 
due diligence duty does not become too heavy  a burden, especially for smaller players  who might lack resources , 
knowledge or time to take up those practices.  
It is positive that the proposal acknowledges the need to support , including financially, those SMEs that will be impacted 
by the supply chain knock-on effect of the law, and that accompanying measures will be put in place to that effect. Indeed, 
at amfori, we understand the value that capacity building, training programmes and tools can have in supporting businesses 
with operationalising their due diligence. Our approach to supporting companies with their due diligence ranges from early 
detection, to monitoring, building capacity, tracking and remediating.  
→ We therefore encourage the EU Institutions to ensure that the package of accompanying measures is as comprehensive 
as possible, and to further specify when and how those support mechanisms would be made available.   
4. Alignment with international standards 
The EU proposal broadly mirrors international responsible business conduct due diligence frameworks such as the UNGPs 
and the OECD MNE Guidelines. Those standards represent the foundation of industry voluntary initiatives and 
commitments such as amfori , and have also inspired much of the regulatory and societal expectations surrounding 
responsible business conduct.  
Alignment with international norms is the best way to drive convergence towards a standard that is universally recognised, 
that businesses are familiar with and that has received the backing and support by governments and civil society 
organisations alike.  
→ We therefore support the EU Commission’s ambition to build on those international norms.  
There are nevertheless important points of departure from international due diligence standards that need fixing.

a. The due diligence duty should be risk -based and designed in a way that allows 
businesses to prioritise action   
When conducting responsible business conduct due diligence  according to international standards like the UNGPs and 
the OECD MNE Guidelines, businesses are expected to have processes in place to understand the risks  and impacts, to 
people and the environment, they might be connected to.  
Once this assessment is completed and a vulnerability lens applied throughout the process, businesses can prioritise their 
actions on the basis of the severity and likelihood of the risk materialising.   
The current proposal moves away from this spirit and asks that companies look at the nature of their business relationships 
instead of focusing on where risks might be the most severe. The proposal further remains silent on the need to account 
for the specific risks and differentiated  impacts of business activities on vulnerable groups such as women, migrant 
workers and children. 
→ We encourage the EU Institutions to reconsider this point by organising the HREDD logic in alignment with the UNGPs 
and OECD Guidelines.  This would mean first and foremost embedding a risk-based approach and the related logic of 
prioritisation within the proposed due diligence duty. It would also mean expecting businesses to apply a vulnerability lens 
throughout the due diligence process.  
→ We suggest that greater emphasis be placed on the role of the CSRD in helping to build a company’s narrative around 
its prioritisation strategy.  
b. Companies should be encouraged to drive continuous improvement  
Due diligence is intended to be ongoing. When gaps or shortcomings are identified, companies are expected to have 
processes in place to manage them and provide for remedy where appropriate.  
Legislation should encourage companies to disclose the steps they are taking to manage risks and, if applicable, remediate 
adverse impacts that might have occurred.  This would mean  recognising that due diligence is not about perfection but 
rather about engaging and aiming at continuous improvement.  Disengagement should be done responsibly and be 
regarded as an option of last resort.  
The above could be achieved e.g. by giving greater prominence to the need not only to monitor impacts but also to disclose 
efforts to continuously improve - for instance via a greater emphasis on the proposed CSRD.  
The Directive could also make it clearer that capacity building, engagement with stakeholders and collaboration with 
business partners are equally important tools to be leveraged when undertaking due diligence and when working on 
adverse impacts. 
→ We suggest exploring additional ways to  ensure companies are encouraged to continuously improve , including by 
means of a greater emphasis on the CSRD.

c. The approach to due diligence should be more inclusive and designed in a way that it 
becomes a supply chain endeavour 
In the spirit of international norms, due diligence requires companies to engage to understand how they relate to potential 
or actual risks and to take ownership by managing risks and impacts to which they might be connected.  
For due diligence to effect positive impact on the ground , it should ideally result in every actor along the supply chain 
taking its share of responsibility and owning its due diligence. Due diligence would therefore become a real supply chain 
endeavour.  
By singling out contractual cascading,  however, the current proposal would seem to encourage a top-down approach to 
due diligence and could be misinterpreted as incentivising the shifting of responsibility further up the supply chain , 
instead of fostering a more inclusive approach. 
→ We suggest that the  CSDDD proposal make it clearer that contractual cascading and third-party verification systems 
are just tools within a broader HREDD toolbox.  
This means that, depending on the maturity of a company’s due diligence processes and programmes and/or that of their 
supplier base, or the nature of the risk in question, other complementary strategies could be equally deployed.  
Conclusions 
Our member companies have been working to improve the social and environmental performance of their supply chains 
for the past two decades. They have done so through voluntary commitments and standards, and by working collaboratively 
to address supply chain challenges.  
With our members, 70% of which are SMEs, we look forward to working with the EU Institutions and other stakeholders 
on the next stages of the CSDDD development, to offer our expertise with supply chain due diligence and to ensure the 
final text delivers on its intended objectives while remaining pragmatic in its implementation.

European Commission 
Sustainable Corporate Due Diligence Directive Proposal- Open consultation 
Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) Response: Cover letter and Appendix  
 
GRI would like to commend the EU for taking forward this initiative towards increased transparency, and 
for offering an opportunity for public feedback to this extremely important proposal. In our capacity as 
providers of the world’s most widely used Sustainability Reporting Standards, we welcome being able to 
provide our insights and expertise.  
As co-constructors of the European Sustainability Reporting Standards alongside the European Financial 
Reporting Advisory Group (EFRAG), we would like to emphasize our commitment towards this process. 
Furthermore, GRI recently entered into a MoU with the IFRS Foundation, to ensure our respective 
sustainability-related standards are aligned. To effectively achieve corporate transparency, duplication 
and unnecessary reporting burden must be minimized. This is something that GRI, given our bridging role 
between the IFRS’ International Sustainability Standards Board and EFRAG, is uniquely placed to help 
achieve. 
Whilst GRI welcomes this proposal and the opportunity to provide our feedback, we have a number of 
concerns with the proposal, which can be found summarized in this letter, and also provided in the form of 
proposed amendments in the Annex of this document.  
 
Scope 
Currently, the scope of the draft legislation would apply to approximately less than 0.2% of European 
companies, therefore excluding a significant amount of harmful business operations and impacts, 
particularly given that the amount of annual turnover and staff size do not correlate with the impact of a 
company on human rights and the environment. Notably, Principle 14 of the United Nations Guiding 
Principles on Business and Human Rights, is clear that the “responsibility of business enterprises to 
respect human rights applies to all enterprises regardless of their size, sector, operational context, 
ownership and structure. Findings from the Business & Human Rights Resource Centre indicate they 
have approached 600 European companies since 2020 about alleged human rights abuses, of which the 
vast majority occur outside the EU.1  
Given the significant difference in scope between this proposal and the Corporate Sustainability 
Reporting Directive (CSRD), and in order to create a level playing field, GRI would strongly recommend a 
higher level of ambition in terms of scope, and aligning it as far as possible with the CSRD.

Ensuring a robust system of transparency, traceability and liability across the whole supply and 
value chain 
GRI is greatly concerned with a number of potential loopholes inside the Directive, particularly the 
insinuation that companies can fulfil obligations by including contractual clauses in their contracts with 
suppliers, effectively offloading the verification processes onto third parties. Responsibilities on adverse 
impacts to the environment and to human rights should not be shifted onto suppliers, and creating a 
system based on contractual clauses could potentially encourage companies to partake in ‘check box 
exercises. For robust due diligence, it is critical that civil liability goes beyond direct suppliers and that 
there is supply chain transparency and traceability down the entire supply chain, with assessments of 
impacts and risks being carried out down to the bottom tier of suppliers.  
As pointed out by Human Rights Watch, in the mining industry, the failure of companies to conduct 
assessments and respond to the human rights harms and risks at the lowest tier of their supply chains – 
the mines themselves – has meant that gold and other minerals mined by children enter supply chains, 
and that conflict-related violence and environmental harm continue unchecked. 2  
Furthermore, we are also concerned with the specification that business relationships falling under the 
scope of this Directive must be ‘established’ business relationships, that are ‘expected to be lasting’. We 
believe that this could greatly increase the risk of supplier and subcontractor ‘hopping’, whereby informal 
relationships are sought out to avoid liability under the Directive. It is vital that this Directive does not 
inadvertently create a ‘race to the bottom’.  
 
Stakeholder engagement 
GRI would strongly recommend ensuring that the Directive effectively protects stakeholder engagement, 
and in its definition does not exclude reference to the categories of stakeholders that are or could be 
affected by an organization’s activities, such as business partners, civil society organizations, consumers, 
customers, employees and other workers, governments, local communities, non-governmental 
organizations, shareholders and other investors, suppliers, trade unions, and vulnerable groups .  
Human rights have a particular status as an entitlement of all people under international law. The most 
acute impacts the organization can have on people are those that negatively affect their human rights. 
The term ‘rightsholders’ refers to stakeholders whose individual human rights or collective rights (held by 
groups such as indigenous peoples) are or could be affected. Stakeholder interests can be negatively or 
positively affected by the organization’s activities. Due diligence focuses on identifying stakeholder 
interests that are or could be negatively affected by the organization’s activities. Stakeholders may not 
always have a direct relationship with the organization. For example, the workers in the organization’s 
supply chain can also be its stakeholders, or there can be individuals or groups living at a distance from 
the organization’s operations who can be affected or potentially affected by these operations. They may 
not be aware that they are stakeholders of that particular organization, especially if they have not yet 
been affected by its activities. The organization should identify the interests of these and other 
stakeholders who are unable to articulate their views (e.g., future generations). 3 
Robust stakeholder engagement is crucial in establishment and implementation of due diligence, and 
engaging with stakeholders helps the organization identify and manage its negative and positive impacts. 
Not all stakeholders will be affected by all activities of the organization. The organization should identify

the stakeholders whose interests have to be taken into account in connection with a specific activity (i.e.,

Sometimes it is necessary to distinguish between stakeholders whose interests have been affected (i.e., 
‘affected stakeholders’), and those whose interests have not yet been affected but could potentially be 
affected (i.e., ‘potentially affected stakeholders’). This distinction is important in due diligence. For 
example, if an organization’s activity leads to a safety hazard, workers who are injured because of the 
hazard are affected stakeholders, and workers who have not yet been injured but who are exposed to the 
hazard and could be injured are potentially affected stakeholders. The distinction between affected and 
potentially affected stakeholders helps identify which workers should receive remedy.4 
Therefore with the above in mind, we would also strongly suggest amending Article 5 to ensure that rights 
are guaranteed for workers representatives and trade unions for involvement in the establishment and 
implementation of the due diligence strategy.

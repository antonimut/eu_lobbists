Contribution to the public consultation  
EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive  
 
 
INTRODUCTION 
The International Cocoa Initiative (ICI) welcomes the publication, on February 23 rd 2022, of the European 
Commission’s proposal for a Directive on Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence, which objective is to 
“improve corporate governance practices and increase corporate accountability in relation to human 
rights and environmental risks, improving access to remedy for those affected by adverse impacts as well 
as avoiding fragmentation of due diligence requirements in the single market”.  
Human rights due diligence (HRDD) legislations, when well designed, can be a positive game-changer as 
a means to accelerate responsible business practices, good governance and accountability. With such a 
proposal, the European Union positions itself as leader and good practice example on the endeavor 
towards a just and sustainable economy.  
This is all the more welcome as the EU Directive proposal allows for a harmonization of approaches 
across the EU instead of a multiplication of national laws, even though its specific application may vary 
from one country to another. As such, it has the potential to create legal certainty and the desired level 
playing field. 
In the cocoa sector, child labour and forced labour have been identified as some of the most salient 
human rights issues. ICI’s theory of change, articulated in our 2021-2026 Strategy, is that together with 
members and stakeholders we will, by 2025, have significantly contributed to the achievement of SDG 8.7 
(to eliminate all forms of child labour and to take effective measures to eradicate forced labour) and the 
advancement of the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGP) in the cocoa sector 
through the full upscaling of effective child protection systems and forced labour prevention and 
remediation measures by governments, businesses, development actors and communities.  The EU’s 
legislative proposal will certainly contribute to intensify the need for companies to implement such 
systems and measures at scale, thus increasing transparency and accountability.  
Our experience over the past decade has allowed us to learn from what is working, where barriers to 
scale are and under what conditions effective approaches can make a difference. Inspired by the 
evidence derived from our operational experience in both Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire, this paper presents 
ICI’s assessment of the Directive proposal, outlining opportunities for improvement .  
 
ICI’S ASSESSMENT OF THE DIRECTIVE PROPOSAL 
ICI appreciates that the Directive proposal aims to build on existing voluntary frameworks, especially the 
UNGPs, the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises and the OECD Due Diligence Guidance for 
Responsible Business Conduct. However, to be fully effective and avoid un-intended consequences, we 
believe that certain areas should be clarified or modified. Our main comments and suggestions for 
adaptation concern the scope and the reach of the Directive, the due diligence obligations, the sanctions 
and the accompanying measures mentioned.

Scope and reach 
We welcome the intention to cover not only EU, but also third-party country companies under the scope of 
application of the Directive proposal. We consider this a way to  raise standards worldwide . At the same 
time, we regret the exclusion of Small and Medium Sized Enterprises (SMEs) from the direct scope of 
application. Indeed, we are concerned that by doing so, the European Commission takes the risk of creating 
a distortion among players across the value chain,  whereas we see that in reality, more and more SMEs 
are increasingly asked by their clients and business partners to implement HRDD measures without 
however necessary receiving the support for doing so. Whereas we believe that, in line with the UNGP, all 
companies, regardless of their size, should be responsible for respecting human rights, the HRDD process 
will vary in complexity with the size of the business enterpris e - a distinction which could be introduced in 
the legal liability regime. 
Another area of concern relates to the notion of “established business relationships” . Not only because 
the concept is vague and so far unknown, but especially because it brings the risk of having companies 
avoiding the engagement in long-lasting business relationships or switching the sourcing to indirect 
suppliers. Instead, the Directive should encourage the establishment of long-term supplier contracts, with 
a need for support and capacity building, especially to SMEs. In the cocoa sector, we know that child 
labour and forced labour, which are known as the most salient human rights issues, as indicated above, 
exist mainly at the up-stream of the supply chain, at farming level. Extending the due diligence obligation 
to the entire supply chain is therefore crucial to create the intended level playing field and the needed 
legal certainty. 
The due diligence obligations 
Although the Directive proposal is said to be based on existing voluntary standards, it however misses a 
full alignment with some of the principles which are central to the effectiveness of the UNGP and the 
OECD Due Diligence Guidance, especially: 
- Prioritisation based on the saliency of the risk: central to any realistic HRDD process, this 
principle is missing, or is insufficiently reflected, in the current Directive proposal. In the cocoa 
sector, we know that 1.56 million children are child labourers in both Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire and 
that, due to the nature of the cocoa harvesting, most of them are involved in hazardous activities. 
Addressing this issue and ensuring that these children and their families are provided with 
sustainable alternatives implies to be able to identify them, and to provide adapted support . To do 
so in a way that is both manageable and successful, those children, families and communities 
that are most at risk need to be supported first.   
- Notion of leverage and relationship to the harm (cause/contribute/linked to) is missing: In line with 
the UNGP, the Directive should expect companies to take appropriate action to identify, prevent, 
mitigate and account for their adverse human rights and environmental impacts depending on 
their involvement and the extent of their leverage on their business partners.   
o If a company has caused or may cause an adverse impact, it should cease, prevent or 
mitigate the impact and remediate any harm if the impact has occurred.  
o If a company has contributed or may contribute to an adverse impact, it should cease, 
prevent or mitigate its own contribution to the impact, and use or increase its leverage 
with other parties to prevent or mitigate it. It should also contribute to remediating the 
harm if the impact has occurred, to the extent of its contribution.

o If a company has not caused or contributed to an adverse impact, but its operations, 
products or services may be linked to an impact through a business relationship, it should 
use or increase its leverage with other parties, including suppliers, to seek to prevent or 
mitigate the impact. 
 
In addition, and in line with a joint position paper ICI has signed together with other members of the 
Cocoa Coalition, the respect for the right to an adequate standard of living as a fundamental human right 
and a ‘living income’ as a precondition to access other human rights, should be explicitly mentioned in the 
legislative proposal.  
Finally, while companies are expected to communicate on the due diligence measures developed and 
implemented, the European Commission should ensure that this can be done in a way that encourages 
transparency and accountability, instead of abusive litigation – another aspect which is key for continuous 
improvement.  
Liability regime and sanctions 
ICI welcomes the proposed introduction of effective, proportionate and dissuasive sanctions in case of an 
infringement of the due diligence obligations. We believe however that, in line with the UNGPs, sanctions 
should take into account whether the company has directly caused, contributed to or has been linked to 
the harm, as well as the extent of a company’s involvement and leverage over entities that cause a harm. 
In that logic, civil liability, if applied, should be reserved to harms directly caused  by the company’s own 
activities or those of its controlled entities. Administrative sanctions, such as fines, could be prescribed for 
weak HRDD processes, for example.  
While national authorities will be tasked to define how companies should fulfil their due diligence 
obligation and decide on the companies’ compliance with these obligations, it is crucial to avoid limiting 
this compliance merely to contractual clauses and audit verification processes. 
Accompanying measures 
ICI welcomes the fact that the Directive proposal foresees the possibility for issuing guidelines to support 
companies and Member States authorities on how companies should fulfil their due diligence obligations.  
Indeed, supporting and defining the extent to which due diligence systems put in place by companies are 
comprehensive and effective enough will be a key aspect of the Directive’s operational success. In that 
context, building on the work done by Multistakeholder Initiatives to support the implemen tation of 
company’s due diligence, is another aspect foreseen by the Directive proposal and apricated by ICI.  
 
ABOUT ICI 
ICI a multi-stakeholder initiative which mission is to prevent and address child labour and forced labour in 
the cocoa sector. Members include companies and civil society organisations (incl. certification bodies, 
unions, farmer organisations), as well as the ILO, UNICEF and ILO which act as advisors to the ICI 
board. ICI also works very closely with governments in both cocoa producing c ountries (mainly Ghana 
and Côte d’Ivoire) and cocoa consuming countries.

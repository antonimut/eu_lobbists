  
  
Bologna, 23rd of May 2022 
 
EU COMMISSION “HAVE YOUR SAY INITIATIVE”: Proposal for a DIRECTIVE OF THE EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT 
AND OF THE COUNCIL on Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence and amending Directive (EU) 2019/1937 
 
Unipol Group S.p.A. (Unipol Group) has examined the Commission proposal of 23 February 2022 for a directive 
on Company Sustainability Due Diligence (CSDD)  and welcomes the opportunity to provide input  for the 
upcoming discussions between the European institutions.  
Unipol Group is a leading insurance group in Italy in the Non -Life business  and undertakes to contribute to 
sustainable development, as defined by the UN  Sustainable Development Goals - SDGs, by integrating this 
commitment into its business model. 
Unipol Group strongly supports the approach of having special rules for regulated financial undertakings, which 
are already subject to significant reporting requirements, including on non -financial information, and to a 
number of obligations on clients’ and counterparties’ due diligence. However, Unipol Group is of the opinion 
that more discussion and clarity is needed on the proposal, in particular on the following main issues:  
 The application at consolidated group level of the CSDD , at least with respect to financial institutions 
only;  
 The definition of  “clients” as part of the value chains of financial institutions and of “partners” as 
part of the  established business relationship s as well as of  the scope of the financial  
products/services/solutions covered.  
Due diligence expectations on regulated financial undertakings should take into account  the differences in 
terms of business model between financial institutions  and companies of the “real economy” a nd provide for 
requirements that are appropriate in this context. 
 
Consistency with existing regulatory framework 
As a preliminary remark, Unipol Group would like to stress the importance to ensure consistency and better 
alignment of CSDD with other EU legislations. With specific reference to regulated financial undertakings, i t is 
unclear at this stage how the proposal will fit with other legal requirements as set out by other EU regulations 
(e.g. Solvency II, SFDR) on  sustainability issues. Policymakers should ensure that due diligence obligations do 
not duplicate or contradict the existing rules for the financial sector; otherwise, difficulties in practical 
implementation of the CSDD would lead to a fragmented network and to inefficiencie s.  
This could happen in particular because the CSDD does not allow for consolidation. In the financial sector, a

holding company structure is prevalent and  due diligence plans and codes of conduct are  usually decided at 
group level  and then applied by subsidiaries. In addition, the parent company is usually assigned with the 
responsibility of corresponding with the lead regulator for the purpose of monitoring and supe rvision of the 
financial group. Different instead is the approach chosen by the Commission in the CSDD proposal  as 
highlighted below. 
In the explanatory memorandum that accompanies the proposal, the European Commission underlines how 
the CSDD will complement the current NFRD  (Non-Financial Reporting Directive, 2014/95/UE)  and its proposed 
amendments (proposal for the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive, CSRD ) by adding a substantive 
corporate duty for  some companies to perform due diligence to identify, prevent, miti gate and account for  
external harm resulting from adverse human rights and environmental impacts in the  company’s own 
operations, its subsid iaries and in the value chain. The two initiatives are closely interrelated and will lead to 
synergies, the Commission emphasizes.  
Currently, the NFRD applies to large public -interest entities with an average number of employees in excess of 
500, and to public -interest entities that are parent companies of a large group  with an average number of 
employees in excess of 500 on a consolidated  basis. The NFRD exempts subsidiaries  from its reporting 
obligations if their parent company does the reporting for the whole group, including the subsidiaries . 
The CSRD proposal rightly keeps the exemption  and requires exempted subsid iary companies to publish the 
consolidated management report of the parent company reporting at group level a s well as to include a 
reference in its legal -entity (individual) management report to the fact that the company in question is 
exempted from the requirements of the Directive. 
The CSDD, which is strictly linked to the above-mentioned legislative initiatives, under Article 11 establishes the 
obligation for Member States to ensure that companies that are not subject to reporting require ments 
(“exempted companies”) under Directive 2013/34/EU  (the Accounting Directive  as amended by the NFRD ) 
report on the matters covered by CSDD and publish an annual statement on their website .  
Unipol Group does not fully agree with this approach  on due diligence communication, since it does not take 
into account the group companies’ structures and the way company law – including reporting requirements 
– applies with this respect . Hence, Unipol Group is concerned about the potential consequences o n the 
financial sector and the impact in terms of costs and additional administrative burdens.  
Indeed, w hile the exemption set forth on non-financial reporting obligations recognizes certain duties of  the 
parent company concerning subsidiaries based on control through group policies and  corporate ownership, the 
application of Article 11 of the CSDD would create discrepancy and would risk to duplicate reporting obligations 
between parent companies (which fall for example into the scope of NFRD and, once a dopted, of CSRD) and 
the relevant subsidiaries (which instead will fall only into the scope of CSDD).   
Against this background,  Unipol Group would like first to call on the co -legislators to amend the proposal in 
order make the due diligence obligations to apply at the consolidated group level only , which is where due 
 
diligence policies and processes are set up. This means that subsidiaries (and their value chains) should be 
expressly exempted . Conversely, each entity of an insurance group would be subject to high administrative 
burdens and costs, even though it is not subject directly to the CSDD according to the relevant threshold.  
Should this not be the case, Unipol Group proposes to allow t he subsidiaries that are exempted from the 
obligation to report non -financial information under NFRD (and once adopted, the CSRD) to include the 
communication on due diligence obligations under CSDD in the consolidated management report of their 
parent company. Considering that, as proposed by the CSRD, those subsidiaries will be required then to publish 
the consolidated management report of their parent undertaking,  such a proposal would increase clarity and 
consistency among EU legislation and would be nefit both prepares and user s of sustainability information  by 
ensuring that this kind information is easily accessible. 
 
Definitions of “ Business relationship ”, “commercial agreement ”, “partner”, “value chain”  and their 
application with reference to the insurance sector  
Under the CSDD, companies will have to comply with due diligence obligations also with respect to the value 
chain operations carried out by  entities with whom the company has an established business relationship . 
Considering that the proposal aims to harmo nize rules on liability for violations of these obligations, clarity on 
scope of application is crucial. 
The proposal defines  “business relationship ” as a relationship with a contractor, subcontractor or any  other 
legal entities (“partner”) (i) with whom the company has a commercial agreement  (which is not further defined 
in the text ) or to whom the company provides financing, insurance or reinsuranc e, or  (ii) that performs 
business operations related to the products or services of the  company for or on behalf of the company. The 
proposal further defines what an “established business relationship” is but it does not clarify what is a direct or 
indirect business relationship, despite the fact that the civil liability regime would  apply differently in case harm 
occurs in a  company’s own operation, at the level of its subsidiaries and at the level of direct and indirect 
business relations in the value chain.  
In addition, the CSDD indirectly defines “partner” within the definition of “business relationship” but it s 
meaning is not always clear considering also that the directive uses  the term in different ways such as  
“business partner” (Article 7, paragraph 2, letter b)), “direct partner” (Article 8, paragraph 3, letter c)), “indirect 
partner” (Article 22, paragraph 2). When it comes to regulated financial undertakings, some confusion is 
further added by the definition of the value chain, which uses the term “clients” and does not include the same 
financial services as set out in the definition of “business relationship”  (“As regards companies  within the 
meaning of point (a)(iv), ‘value chain’ with respect to the provision of  these specific services shall only include 
the activities of the clients receiving such loan,  credit, and other financial services  [insurance and reinsurance 
are not explicitly mention ed in this context]  and of other companies belonging to the same group  whose 
activities are linked to the contract in question]-

Against this background, Unipol would like to see further clarification in the recitals and legal text of the CSDD:  
 With respect to the definition of “value chain” in relation to r egulated financial undertakings, which 
needs to be clarified to fully reflect the insurance sector’s specificities.  To this end, the definition of a 
“client” in the financial sector value chain should be included  in the CSDD so to avoid any legal 
uncertainty on the legal entities  included in the definition and, consequently,  in the scope of 
mandatory due diligence obligations; 
 On how and to what extent the CSDD definition of “business relationship” and “established business 
relationship” apply to the activities of r egulated financial undertakings , in particular insurers . D o 
insurance companies have to apply the notion with respect to their “clients” (e.g. policyholders) 
and/or their “business partner s” (e.g. the legal entities – including other regulated financial 
undertakings, such as banks , that may be subject as well to the CSDD ) with w hom they have  
agreements in place ? It is also unclear what  the reference to a  “commercial agreement” within the 
definition of business relationship exactly means, as it could be interpreted by way of example as a 
form of distribution and/or co-marketing and/or sponsorship and/or even business-commercial 
collaboration. Moreover, w hat is the difference, if any, between “client”, “business partner” and 
“direct/indirect partner” when the CSDD apply to insurance companies ? Finally, co-legislators should 
also clarify what the criteria are about e.g. the duration or value  of the contract in order for the latter 
to be considered  as an “established business relationship” , with our starting point being that an 
“established business relatio nship” should be limited to established direct business partners only. 
The potential application to distribution agreements set up by financial i nstitutions would have indeed 
significant implications with the risk to jeopardize business models innovation and the position of 
insurance companies in the market. To compete with Big Tech and start -ups, for example, which rely 
on different – more agile – distribution business models, insurance companies tend to establish 
partnerships and to improve their distribution channels to enlarge their customers’ base; the 
application of CSDD in this context would make this process harder and commercial alliances h arsher. 
At the same time, the implementation of due diligence obligations with respect to the contractual 
relationship between an insurer or reinsurer and its policyholder would have to consider important 
consequences related respectively to the applicatio n of another layer of legislation that may conflict 
with insurance contract law and to the risk that companies may refuse to cover certain policyholders.  
In light of the above , l egal certainty in this context is key to understand the correct  practical 
application of Articles 7 and 8 and the due diligence measures indicated therein (e.g. code of 
conduct; the seek of contractual assurances  from direct business partners ; etc.)  as well as the 
interpretation and application of civil liability rules  (how do they apply to companies’ subsidiaries?) , 
which create an unaccountable and uncertain legal risk for companies ; 
 Regarding the  notion of “ other financial services“ , which is not  defined in the CSDD. I n order to 
understand whether the proposal has to be read as requiring that  the due diligence duties refer to all 
 
services that financial institutions provide, clarification is necessary also to better assess implications 
for potential intermediary and advisory services provided in the distribution chann els.  
As mentioned before, i n order to ensure clarity and legal certainty, it is of essential importance to pursue 
consistency not only intra  CSDD but also vis -à-vis other relevant rules applicable to regulated financial 
undertakings with the aim to make the application of  the CSDD with reference to the latter more effective and 
efficient.

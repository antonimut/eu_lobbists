FFE comments on the European Commission’s proposal for a 
Directive on Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence 
European Commission published on 23th February proposal for a Directive 
on Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence. The proposal aims to create ob-
ligations for companies regarding to actual and potential human rights and 
environmental impact, linked to their own operations, those of their subsidi-
aries and limited to established business relationships in upstream and 
downstream operations. The proposal has also objective to define a liability 
regime for violations of these obligations and to harmonise the duty of care 
of company directors. The objective is also to prevent the potential frag-
mentation of the internal market caused by recent national initiatives on the 
same matter which often diverge significantly. 
General remarks 
Federation of Finnish Enterprises welcomes the Commission's proposal for 
corporate sustainability due diligence directive. We consider the key objec-
tive of the proposal to promote corporate responsibility and the protection of 
human and environmental rights to be very important. EU-level legislative 
framework would harmonize policies for all companies operating in the EU 
and would also ensure a level playing field for European companies. These 
objectives are important for European companies that would be subject to 
the obligations. 
The achievement of the objectives requires that the regulation is carried out 
at least at EU level. Therefore, EU level due diligence obligations should be 
prioritized over national initiatives. It is likely that without EU-level legislative 
framework, many Member States would take steps regarding their own na-
tional due diligence initiatives. This would lead to harmful regulatory frag-
mentation and hamper cross border activities of companies. A fragmented 
regulatory environment would also hamper the effective promotion of due 
diligence.  
However, we emphasize that still lot remains to be done to achieve practi-
cal enforceable legislative framework. Particularly the clarity and precision 
of the proposal requires improvement to achieve a workable and coherent 
regulatory framework. 
The proposal seems to leave Member States with considerable options for 
laying down more detailed rules on the required measures. It is important

that the proposal does not leave too much national discretion to the imple-
mentation of the directive. Member States' discretion on key issues could 
adversely lead to significant differences in national requirements. It is im-
portant that the companies’ obligations are defined exhaustively and with 
precision so that the requirements will not differ in the national laws. Insuffi-
cient coordination of the requirements could lead to duplication and addi-
tional costs for companies. 
Obligations  
It is important that directive provides a clear operating environment for com-
panies. The objectives of the directive can be most effectively achieved 
through obligations that realistic for the companies. As breaches of the obli-
gations would lead to sanctions, it is important for legal certainty of compa-
nies, that the directive clearly sets out what measures company must take 
to fulfil its obligations.  
In order to ensure that companies have a real possibility of complying with 
their obligations, critical attention must be paid to the broad concept of 
value chains and to the ability of companies to fulfill its obligations through-
out their value chains. 
We find it problematic that some obligations have only been concretised by 
referring to international human rights and environmental agreements. The 
obligations should be set out clearly and in detail in the directive. Obliga-
tions subject to interpretation could lead to divergent interpretations in dif-
ferent Member States. 
Scope 
The obligations would apply to companies with more than 500 employees 
and a worldwide turnover of more than EUR 150 million and to companies 
operating in high-risk industries with more than 250 employees and a 
worldwide turnover of more than EUR 40 million. The scope would also 
cover companies whose activities are based on the legislation of a third 
country which exceeds the above-mentioned turnover thresholds. 
Even if the obligations only apply to large companies, the defined scope of 
the directive is not entirely unproblematic and does not mean that the ef-
fects would not extend to companies outside the actual scope. When obli-
gations are imposed to large companies, the obligations will also be passed 
on, at least to some extent, to SMEs due to subcontracting relationships. 
However, it is appropriate to exclude SMEs from the actual scope, as some

obligations such as the establishment of a complaint handling procedure 
would be unduly burdensome for SMEs. 
SMEs, as subcontractors to larger companies, would be subject to various 
obligations imposed by the companies covered by the directive. If an SME 
has a business relationship with more than one company covered by the 
directive, it could be subject to varying obligations that are not compatible. 
This could lead to a significant increase of the burden passed on SMEs. 
There is also a risk that obligations that SMEs cannot fulfil would be passed 
to them.  
Regarding the obligations imposed on SMEs, the only limitation is that the 
company covered by the directive bears the costs of the independent 
third-party verification. This limitation is not sufficient to prevent the uncon-
trolled transfer of obligations to SMEs. 
The possibilities for SMEs to exercise their influence in subcontracting 
chains or to effectively enforce certain due diligence conditions on to their 
own business partners are limited. It is important that SMEs are only sub-
ject to obligations that they can actually meet. 
Due to the challenges mentioned above, there is need to manage that reg-
ulatory burden is not transferred to SMEs. The burden on SMEs can be 
managed by defining what obligations a company covered by the directive 
can impose to business partner that is a SME. 
 
Measures to support SMEs 
It is very important that the proposal includes measures to support SMEs. 
Supporting SMEs is essential to effectively integrate the requirements into 
companies' value chains that includes SMEs. However, we would like to 
point out that it is questionable whether the measures to support SMEs are 
sufficient. 
Legal certainty for SMEs can be increased through guidance and advice. It 
should be ensured that Member States provide adequate support, advice, 
and guidance to SMEs. We consider the requirement for Member States to 
set up websites to provide guidance to SMEs to be important in this regard. 
The directive would oblige that when relevant companies should seek con-
tractual assurances from its business partners to ensure compliance with 
the the company’s code of conduct and prevention action plan (Article 
7(2) point (b) and Article 8(3) point (c)).

There is a risk that larger companies will transfer their own obligations and 
burden to SMEs. Therefore, it is important that the company seeking con-
tractual assurances has an obligation to provide targeted and proportionate 
support to the SME with which it has an established business relationship 
(Article 7(2) point (d) and Article 8(3) point (e)). Also, we consider that the 
requirements of fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms to be im-
portant when contractual assurances are obtained from SME (Article 7(4) 
and Article 8(5). 
Although the above measures are important, as such they are too vague. 
The measures do not indicate how the support to SMEs should be pro-
vided, nor do they indicate what conditions are reasonable for SMEs. Fur-
thermore, there is no way for SMEs to interfere when they are imposed on 
disproportionate obligations. 
Termination of business relationship 
The obligation to terminate the business relationship would be problematic, 
as in the fear of sanctions, companies may feel compelled to terminate 
business relationship, even though they may not consider it a necessary or 
best option in the situation. The obligation of termination of business rela-
tionship should be carefully considered in the light of SMEs. There is a risk 
that the business relationship will be terminated with an SME which is not 
able to fulfil the obligations. It would be more sensible that the proposal 
would rather encourage into long-term business relationships and also en-
courage to give support to SMEs when shortcomings emerge.  
Civil liability 
We consider that the provision regarding civil liability (Article 22) should not 
be included in the directive. The provision of civil liability that goes beyond 
the direct influence of the company is not feasible. The proposed provision 
on civil liability is also too vague to ensure a uniform application in different 
Member States. The ambiguity of the civil liability provision is underlined by 
the broad definition of value chains which makes the liability unreasonably 
broad. The enforcement mechanism should rely only on sanctions and ad-
ministrative enforcement. 
Directors’ duty of care 
We consider that the provision regarding directors’ duty of care (Article 25) 
should not be included in the directive. The provision is unclear and unnec-
essary for achieving the regulatory objectives. The provision is also com-
pletely separate from the rest of the directive. The objectives set out in the

regulation of directors’ duty of care can be achieved more effectively and 
appropriately through specific due diligence obligations than by interfering 
with fundamental company law structures.  
The details of corporate governance should not be laid down at EU level. 
Establishing mandatory and detailed regulation at EU level to determine the 
general responsibilities of a company's management and “directors’ duty of 
care” is not possible, as Member States' regulatory systems differ signifi-
cantly. In this respect, the effort to harmonize the regulation may lead to 
significant changes in company law structures and also lead to legal uncer-
tainty.

MedTech Europe’s response to the Public Consultation on 
the European Commission’s proposal for a Directive on 
Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence 
 
23 May 2022 
  
Introduction  
We welcome the European Commission ’s proposal  addressing human rights and environmental due 
diligence in global supply chains. Th is proposal for a Directive on Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence  
(CSDD) is an important step forward in reaching the sustainability-related goals of the EU Green Deal.  
 
In our response to this consultation, we highlight some of our sector’s specificities, which call for a pragmatic 
approach, and make a number of recommendations for the consideration of EU institutions in the upcoming 
legislative process. 
 
The medical technology  industry is characterised by long and complex value chains and a large variety of 
medical devices. The sector has an obvious strategic dimension, and the continuity of supply chains bears a 
crucial role at all times, so as to avoid unintended disruptions that could affect patients  and health care 
systems. We estimate that  over 75% of our member companies fall within the scope of th is Directive as 
currently drafted. Although small and medium-sized companies (SMEs*) make up almost 95% of the medical 
technology industry, the majority of them employ less than 50 people (small and micro-sized companies). 
 
This is why we make four key recommendations, namely:  
1. Consider due diligence obligations for the supply chain and for Tier 1  suppliers only 
(established business relationships), excluding obligations for customers  
Requiring companies to conduct due diligence along the value chain  would de facto mean conducting due 
diligence amongst customers. For medical technology companies, this would translate in having to conduct 
due diligence in, for instance, hospitals and clinics across the world, including where these are public entities. 
In addition, the requirement laid down in the proposal foresees that a company should terminate a business 
relationship with a customer – in this case a hospital - if this one is not compliant with the CSDD legislation. 
This measure would run against one of the most basic ethical and humanitarian obligati ons of healthcare 
companies towards continued provision of care.1   
This is why we suggest the approach taken by Germany’s Supply Chain Due Diligence Act, whereby the 
obligation to conduct due diligence for Tier 2 suppliers (indirect business  relationships) can be considered

2. Ensure harmonisation wit h existing international guidelines, principles and standards 2 ; 
consistency with other European Union policies addressing supply chain due diligence;3  and 
alignment of auditing and sanctions requirements with relevant existing frameworks.4   
Whilst the CSDD proposal aims to achieve greater harmonisation, a Directive does not provide the same 
level of harmonisation as a Regulation, and leaves room for in tra-EU fragmentation  in terms of legal 
requirements and standards-setting. Requirements of the CSDD Directive may go beyond those foreseen by 
national laws, and vice-versa, leading to an uneven level playing field, possibly distort competition, and make 
it challenging for global companies to comply with different national requirements. A proportion of MedTech 
Europe’s member companies do business in multiple Member States within the EU and would therefore be 
adversely impacted by th is lack of harmonisation . It is  also worth noting how the CSDD proposal impacts/ 
incorporates EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive requirements  and other international ly 
recognised frameworks. 
 
3. Clarify a number of definitions – such as : ‘value chain’, ‘indirect business relationships’, 
‘adverse impacts ’, ‘stakeholders’, ‘established business relationship ’, ‘substantiated 
concerns’.  
For example, we advocate for a clearer definition of ‘substantiated concerns’  to establish clear evidence 
thresholds. A prerequisite of lodging a complaint to the Supervisory Authority should be related to the degree 
to which the evidence or information on which the complaint is based is sufficiently compelling to merit further 
investigation and potentially remediation. Furthermore, the range of actors who can lodge a complaint should 
be more narrowly defined, and limited to natural or legal persons directly concerned/affected by the adverse 
impact, and that have a legitimate interest in the matter. This would keep the number of complaints received 
by companies to a manageable level ( including the associated resources to investigate and resolve), while  
achieving the objectives of justice and redress.  
 
4. Provide requirements for well-defined, proportionate support from large companies  to 
small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), and define the role of the European Commission 
and implementing/supervisory authorities in supporting SMEs. We propose that the Directive 
excludes the liability of large companies on behalf of SMEs. 
In Article 7, requirements of large companies towards SMEs should be clarified. Providing financial and 
compliance support to SMEs could amount to a significant burden for large companies. Additionally, it would 
also be an administrative burden and a human  resources challenge for SMEs to comply with  differing

supplying information to a variety of different tools , since every company will use different tools to conduct 
due diligence. In addition, if we consider civil liability, the Directive proposal provides that companies are

issues when SME have several customers which may categorise as large companies.  
It is worth highlighting that, despite the intent to exclude SMEs from the Direc tive’s scope, SMEs remain 
indirectly impacted, for instance when they are part of the value chain of larger companies . This is because  
the Directive’s obligations will be cascaded down to them by larger companies.  
 
On the other hand, the requirement for large companies to provide support to SMEs may bear unintended 
economic and competition consequences for SMEs. Indeed, the proposed Directive introduces such 
complexity of working with SMEs that it may force larger companies to choose entering in  a business  
relationship with another large company. 
 
 
In addition to the points elaborated upon in the above, we would like to draw the attention of the European 
Commission to our upcoming position paper, which will touch in more detail on the following points: 
• Transition and implementation time to allow for consistent implementation of the Directive across 
Member States 
• Minimum standards regarding inspection criteria (e.g. inspection intervals) and clearly defined 
sanctions when the Directive’s provisions are violated 
• Auditing standards so that 3rd party auditors apply identical requirements 
• Alignment with the EU Taxonomy Regulation and other similar regulations/directives  
• Definitions of all various impacts and appropriate measures 
• Definitions and metrics related to adverse human rights and adverse environmental impacts  
• Requirement for complainants to meet with company representatives 
• Monitoring based on risk assessment, introduction of a risk-based approach 
• Combating climate change 
• Civil liability 
• Directors’ duties. 
 
We will send this position paper to the Commission as a complement to our input to this public consultation 
as soon as it is finalised, and upload it on our website.

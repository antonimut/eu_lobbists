Preliminary notes 
 
For the German Association of the Automotive Industry (VDA) and its member companies, 
respecting and protecting human rights along automotive supply chains is both mission and 
obligation. The VDA is actively involved in the "Industry Dialogue for the Automotive Industry 
(Branchendialog Automobilindustrie)" of the Federal Ministry of Labor and Social Affairs to 
implement the National Action Plan for Business and Human Rights (NAP), has founded the 
Responsible Supply Chain Initiative e.V. (RSCI) together with manufacturers and suppliers 
and discusses issues to a sustainable procurement of raw materials. German automobile 
manufacturers and suppliers focus on safe and good working conditions and the protection of 
human rights at all locations worldwide. Any regulation – whether at national level, in the EU 
or within the framework of international agreements – should be designed in such a way that 
it does not lead to companies withdrawing from difficult production countries or to the 
termination of trade relations, but rather focuses on improving local conditions. 
This short statement represents an initial assessment of the draft directive. 
We agree: 
• In principle, the VDA welcomes the fact that the proposal for a “Directive of the 
European Parliament and of the Council on Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence and 
amending Directive (EU) 2019/1937" has laid the foundations for a uniform 
implementation of sustainability requirements in the member states of the EU. Together 
with the regulation t hat under certain conditions the directive should also apply to 
companies not based in the EU, the first steps towards equal conditions of competition 
are being taken. 
 
• The recognition of industry initiatives when assessing companies' commitment to 
sustainability is also to be welcomed, even though more precise details are still 
required here as legislation progresses. The same applies to the announced model 
clauses, which can contriute to a common understanding of human rights due diligence 
by all those who are part of the supply chain. 
 
• Finally, it must be recognized that the directive obliges companies to make every effort, 
but not obliges companies to succeed in those, and thus fundamentally recognizes the 
actual and, in individual cases , different impact companies have on o ther companies 
along the supply chain. 
Critical notes: 
• The threshold of 500 or 250 employees, above which companies sh all be subject to 
regulation, contradict the declared aim of ex cepting medium -sized companies from 
regulation. With good reason, the legislator in Germany has set the thresholds in the 
Supply Chain Due Diligence Act at 3,000 or 1,000 employees, since the resulting effort 
can hardly be managed for companies below this size and represents a significant 
disadvantage in global competition. With the obligation for larger companies to support

small and medium -sized companies, for example with auditing costs, the directive 
confirms the disproportionately high financial effort that small and medium -sized 
companies incur. To ensure that small and medium -sized companies do not suffer a 
competitive disadvantage because large customers have to reward the efforts of 
European suppliers to fulfill their duty of care, the support should not be provided by 
the companies but by national or European institutions. 
 
• The wide range of due diligence obligations beyond direct suppliers to "established 
business partners" or "indirect" suppliers is critical: companies only have legal claims 
against their contractual business partners, which th ey can enforce in a binding 
manner. 
 
• The same applies to the intention of the draft directive not only to focus regulation on 
the origin of materials and the manufacture of products – i.e. the supply chain – but 
also on the use phase and subsequent repro cessing or disposal, i.e. the entire value 
chain. Responsibility for all stages and elements of the value chain is a joint 
responsibility of all those involved and cannot be assumed by one company alone on 
behalf of the entire value chain. Here, cooperativ e approaches of all those involved 
along the value chain are to be pursued. 
 
• Numerous recurring terms in the proposed directive remain unspecific and vague: this 
applies above all to the definition of "appropriate" and "suitable" in relation to measures 
and of "established" and "indirect" in relation to business relationships. 
 
• The civil law liability provided for in the draft directive in the event of failure to exercise 
due diligence corresponds neither to the general proportionality of the project, to which 
the Commission is committed, nor to the principle of prioritizing remedies and 
improvements before sanctions and withdrawal. With good reason, the German Supply 
Chain Due Diligence Act and the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human 
Rights do not provide for civil liability for companies. 
 
• The inclusion of climate targets in the catalog of due diligence obligations overburdens 
the core idea of protecting human rights. The compatibility of the business model and 
corporate strategy with the cli mate target of limiting global warming to a maximum of 
1.5 degrees Celsius according to the Paris Agreement can be assessed differently from 
different perspectives. Differing views on this entail a great risk of legal disputes that 
would tie up company resources. 
 
 
Final note: 
 
The companies in the Association of the Automotive Industry are pursuing ambitious goals to 
protect the climate, the environment and, last but not least, human rights worldwide. They make 
significant efforts to achieve significant and continuous improvements along the entire supply 
and value chain, even beyond their direct sphere of influence. Regulation is effective when it 
supports the commitment of companies through appropriate state and political action where 
companies and NGOs alone only have a limited effect . The Association of the Automotive 
Industry will continue to play an active role in the deliberations on the draft directive.

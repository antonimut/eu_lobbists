Executive Summary 
Software, connected hardware, and network-connected services are integral to society, creating a wide 
spectrum of benefits and risks. Increasing connectivity, more critical use scenarios, and escalating cyber 
threats underline a significant and growing need to develop multi-faceted and coordinated strategies to 
address substantial risks. Along with initiatives to uphold norms of responsible state behavior, strengthen 
technology users’ operational cybersecurity risk management,  and invest in skills and infrastructure,  
efforts to enhance the cybersecurity of information and communications technology (ICT) products and 
services are essential to an overall strategy to improve the security and resilience of our ecosystem. 
Microsoft is keen to partner with the European Commission and other governments pursuing these 
important efforts, and we are grateful for the opportunity to share our views in response to the European 
Commission’s Call for Evidence on the EU Cyber Resilience Act (CRA). We support effective horizontal 
approaches to enhancing the cybersecurity of ICT products and services, promoting greater cross -
ecosystem consistency and coherence in the EU’s single market. But we also recognize the complexity of 
such horizontal, holistic efforts and the importance of a phased approach to implementation. 
This paper provides foundational context on the ICT environment . It also describes interconnected 
challenges and opportunities related to determining which digital  products and services should be in 
scope for regulatory measures, defining effective security practices, and verifying use of those practices. 
Given anticipated complexity and the value of an iterative approach to ensuring meaningful enhancement 
of cybersecurity, this paper supports a model that moves from the Commission’s suggested policy option 
two to policy options four or five, implementing more stringent rule making based on evidence and 
experience gained over time . It also  offers the following  recommendations for effective, scalable 
approaches to driving horizontal cybersecurity improvements: 
• Take a holistic, phased approach. A holistic approach addresses cybersecurity risks associated with 
ICT products and services as well as the operational behavior of users. It also considers scope (i.e., 
products, services, or organizations targeted by measures),  security practices (i.e., guidance or 
requirements to be implemented for products or services or by organizations) , and verification 
of conformance (i.e., methods of assessing whether practices have been implemented) in concert. 
Taking a holistic approach is complex but necessary to achieve positive security outcomes. When

Introduction 
Our modern technology ecosystem is vast and complex, generating enormous value and risk. S oftware, 
connected hardware, and network-connected services underpin the operations of organizations and are 
relied upon by people around the world. The breadth of their reach is compounded by the variability in 
functions they enable; everything from online games to health research to energy innovation and far 
beyond is powered by digital products and services. Yet security compromises of individual products or 
services or their components can also have broad effects, rippling across interconnected supply chains. 
Compromises can lead to disclosure of confidential information, disrupt the integrity or availability of data 
or functions on which people or organizations rely, and impact safety in the physical world. 
Effective cybersecurity policies are needed to reduce the risk of security compromises and enhance trust 
in technology and the opportunities it affords, including in at least four complementary areas:  
1. First, policies that drive responsible state behavior are needed to reduce threat activities that 
societies widely consider intolerable, such as attacks on critical civilian services. 
2. Second, policies that help enhance the cybersecurity of digital products and services are 
needed to increase resilience against attacks. 
3. Third, policies that foster operational risk management by technology users are needed to help 
prevent, detect, and improve recovery from attacks. 
4. Fourth, policies that strengthen investments in people and infrastructure are needed to advance 
the skills and organizational readiness to drive continuous risk management improvements as 
the technology ecosystem and threat environment evolve. 
While many governments are pursuing important policy initiatives across each of these areas, some are 
bringing new focus to the second: efforts to enhance the cybersecurity of digital products and services. 
Recent attacks that pose cascading risk across information and communication technology (ICT) supply 
chains have helped bring attention and urgency to such policy activities. 1 Governments are leveraging 
incentives, such as labeling programs or procurement requirements, as well as regulatory proposals.  
These efforts may be “horizontal” in nature, applying widely to on-prem software, services, and/or 
connected devices, or more targeted, focusing, for example, on certain types of devices. 
Efforts to enhance the cybersecurity of digital products and services  have enormous potential but are 
riddled with complexity.  There are benefits and drawbacks associated with  both horizontal and more 
targeted approaches. There are also challenges with defining sufficiently robust but flexible cybersecurity 
practices and hard questions in determining whether to incentivize or require use of th ose practices as 
well as how to verify conformance with required practices. Moreover, to achieve desired outcomes, efforts 
to enhance the cybersecurity of ICT products and services often depend upon complementary 
investments, such as in improving operational risk management practices of technology users.

This paper is Microsoft’s contribution to the European Commission’s consultation on the Cyber Resilience 
Act (CRA), offering insights and recommendations in support of government  efforts to enhance the 
cybersecurity of digital products and services. It first provides foundational context on the dynamic and 
interconnected nature of the ICT environment. Change is constant in the ICT ecosystem – among ICT 
providers, technology itself, and threat actors and criminals – and policies and requirements that account 
for this reality and the need for agility in operations and technology capabilities are more like ly to 
ultimately enhance security. Second, the paper  considers challenges  and opportunities related to 
determining which digital  products and services should be in scope for regulatory measures, defining 
effective security practices, and verifying conformance. Given the interconnectedness of these challenges, 
Microsoft recommends a holistic, phased approach to enhancing the cybersecurity of ICT products and 
services. Amidst enormous complexity in the hardware, software, services, and user ecosystems, 
developing and implementing effective cybersecurity policies requires iterative learning and adjustment. 
A dynamic and interconnected ICT environment 
There are many driver s of constant change for ICT providers and their products and services.  Evolving 
technology and infrastructure, including improvements in hardware platforms, architectures, services, 
security capabilities, and standards, unlock new opportunities and add value. Customer expectations or 
market dynamics can also shift. New or escalating security risks prompt new threat models , which are 
most effective if built in during the design phase of new products or services. Organizations also must 
constantly maintain products or services to address new security vulnerabilities as well as changes from 
suppliers. As a result, updates might include everything from minimal security patches to major or minor 
changes in functionality or to entirely new versions of products or services. 
New ICT solutions build on  or adapt preexisting general -purpose elements available within the ICT 
ecosystem when the solution was built. Solutions leverage preexisting general-purpose elements – such 
as hardware chipsets, software libraries, development tools, online services, and communication protocols 
– because no economically viable organization can build all of these from scratc h. General-purpose 
elements also create their own economic value through reuse in a variety of applications and accelerate 
innovation by allowing ICT providers to focus on the unique value of new solutions. 
Frequently, ICT design, development, production, and maintenance activities are globally distributed and 
involve a complex web of upstream and downstream supplier interconnections, necessitating supply 
chain risk management . Technology providers need to decide which portions of their solution s to 
implement themselves, which to source from external suppliers, and how to align the prioritization and 
mitigation of risk between a supplier and an organization using a solution.  The supplier’s governance 
practices ( e.g., risk treatment, adherence to security best practices, vulnerability coordination, 
transparency) and the product characteristics ( e.g., security, performance, integrity, confidentiality, 
availability, cost, and support lifecycle) should match the requirements of the consuming organization.  
Supply chain risk management efforts also extend beyond aligning with  direct suppliers. Technology 
developers and operators  seek consistency further along a supply chain , though vendors and their 
products and services may be dynamic, adding complexity. As an organization maintains a solution over 
time, dependencies implemented by a chain of suppliers may become incompatible with the 
organization’s governance practices, a solution’s product characteristic metrics, or other risk management

priorities. While an organization might be able to adapt to changes or mitigate risks associated with direct 
suppliers, their third-party vendors, or components, it may also need to pursue new solutions. 
Ultimately, layers of dynamism and interconnectedness – at the level of individual solutions and  across 
the ecosystem – make clear, consistent ways to communicate adherence to security practices across ICT 
product and service supply chains  especially valuable. Because most products and services are built

communicate (1) what must be done and (2) how to prove it  – as well as evolving  expectations. Policy

including as a result of  decisions about the scope of products and services  that will be impacted , the 
security practices that will be required or incentivized, and the ways conformance will be verified. 
Defining scope, cybersecurity practices, and verification  
Efforts to enhance the cybersecurity of digital products and services must concurrently consider:  
1. what products and services are in scope;  
2. what cybersecurity practices will be required or incentivized; and  
3. what ways of verifying use of those practices will be required or incentivized.  
Policy initiatives may broadly scope in many or all products and services – i.e., taking a “horizontal” 
approach – or more narrowly target certain types or classes of products or services . Likewise, policy 
initiatives may define many or a few prioritized security practices , more or less flexible practices, and 
required and/or incentivized practices.  To verify use of those practices , self-, third-party, or other  
assessment methods may be mandated or encouraged. 
This section first considers each of these areas – scope, cybersecurity practices, and verification – in turn, 
weighing benefits and drawbacks of different potential approaches. It then describes why a holistic view 
– one in which each of these interrelated areas is considered concurrently rather than just in isolation – is 
critical to effective, practical policies. Finally, it describes why a holistic view leads to recognition of the 
need for a phased approach – one in which implementation is rolled out over time, allowing for iterative 
learning and adjustments throughout the process . Ultimately, this paper recommends initiating and 
expanding a holistic, phased approach to enhancing the cybersecurity of ICT products and services. 
Defining scope: challenges, opportunities, and recommendations 
In developing policies to enhance the cybersecurity of digital products and services, a first consideration 
is whether and how to include software, services, and physical devices, including integrated software 
and/or dedicated services leveraged by connected devices. Policies can focus on one or two categories 
or scope in all three in parallel; in addressing two or more categories, policies might treat them similarly 
or differently – for instance, incentivizing security practices for one categor y and requiring them for 
another. Within categories, policies might likewise take an ecosystem-wide approach or more narrowly 
address subsets or classes of products or services based on higher or lower risk – for instance, requiring 
elevated assurance for devices in safety-critical use scenarios.

Determining the right approach is complex because the scope of ICT products and services of potential 
concern is matched only by the breadth of their use scenarios, resulting in benefits and drawbacks for 
both horizontal and targeted efforts.  Horizontal efforts drive consistent improvements and promote 
interoperability and efficiency across an interconnected ecosystem , but they pose challenges not only 
with scale but also in calibrating requirements and conformance verification processes to system criticality 
or risk severity. Alternatively, more narrowly targeted efforts can address more specific scenarios, but they 
also risk redundancy – or worse, inconsistency – in requirements or verification methods, and they miss 
an opportunity to drive change across an interconnected ecosystem. 
This section first briefly recounts how software, services, and connected devices often intersect, resulting 
in interplay across policies that address all three categories, whether holistically or separately. It then 
compares ecosystem-wide and high risk -focused approaches, which may be applied to one or more 
categories of products and services. Finally, it discusses differe nt ways of scoping  high risk -focused 
approaches, such as by focusing on context of use or ICT product or service attributes or functions. 
Accounting for intersections across connected devices, software, and services 
Hardware, software, and services are distinct categories but may have integrated development processes 
or shared dependencies. For example, b uilding on what is sourced from suppliers  and the larger ICT 
ecosystem, a device manufacturer has strong control over its own incremental contributions to a finished 
product. However, to support a product throughout its lifecycle, the manufacturer must continuously 
adapt the product to how the ecosystem elements it relies on (or supports) evolve (or move out of 
production or support). These elements often include software and network-connected services, which 
might be impacted by security practice  and conformity assessment expectations associated with 
connected devices as well as developed and implemented separately (for software or services). 
The extent to which broader ecosystem elements impact the functionality and security of a device varies. 
Stable protocols and standards can help support interoperability and insulate boundaries between a 
manufacturer’s product and ecosystem elements provided by third parties. For example, a product that 
retrieves and displays e-mail could rely on established authentication, networking, and e-mail protocols 
for its communications over a network and its ability to retrieve e -mail from different e-mail servers. In 
such a case, the product scope could consist of the hardware and software on the physical device. 
In contrast, another product may require proprietary communications to connect an Internet of Things 
(IoT) device that collects data with cloud services-based, product-specific analytics of that data. In such a 
case, the product scope could implicate not only the hardware and software on the physical device but 
also the communication protocols between the device and the cloud service and analytics. As a result, 
policy approaches focused on device requirements may not achieve desired security outcomes for the 
overall product if implicated protocols or services do not likewise meet appropriate security standards. 
Weighing ecosystem-wide and high risk-focused approaches 
In addition to considering whether and how to scope in connected devices, software, and services, efforts 
to enhance cybersecurity must determine whether and how to address all and/or some products and 
services within those three categories. Taking an “ecosystem-wide” approach means broadly addressing 
all or most types of products or services within each category . Alternatively, policies might focus on  a 
narrower subset of products and services, often starting with those associated with higher risk.

As with other scoping considerations, both approaches offer benefits and drawbacks. The interconnected 
nature of supply chains and potential risk escalations associated with compromise of otherwise relatively 
low-risk devices (e.g., being leveraged for botnets that pose broader security risks) might argue in favor 
of ecosystem-wide approaches. However, the daunting breadth of the ICT environment and need to 
prioritize – a foundational principle of effective risk management – might argue in favor of high risk-
focused approaches. Such approaches may especially be of interest if they can achieve wider impacts – 
for example, as directly implicated ICT providers cascade requirements through their supplier s (even if 
realizing this intention may take more time, and progress may be less measurable). 
Government may also find value in pursuing both  approaches. If developed  and implemented  in a 
coordinated manner – through a phased, iterative approach – both lower and higher security baselines 
and conformity assessment expectations might be relevant for both ecosystem-wide and high -risk 
scenarios. By first establishing basic core tenets of security practices and implementing them across a 
broad swath of products  and services , governments can create a regulatory foundation that 
accommodates industry-wide standards while also preparing for layered tiers of elevated standards to be 
phased in over time. They can also survey impacts, address concerns, predict future advancements and 
risks, and effectively tailor solutions using the evidence and foresight provided by an initial phase. 
Scoping high risk-focused approaches 
If high risk-focused approaches are pursued, governments must determine how to differentiate between 
what’s higher or lower risk. At least two approaches to defining such boundaries are used today, each 
with varying implications:  
1. Context of use; and 
2. Attributes or functions of an ICT product or service.  
Scoping by context of use 
Context of use-based approaches recognize that people and organizations may use the same types or 
categories of ICT products and services for everything from entertainment to productivity, livelihood,  
national security, and safety. What matters is not the type or category of product or service but rather 
the level of risk associated with how it’s used . Operating systems and cloud infrastructure are examples 
of products that are widely used across scenarios with very different risk impacts. 
Existing and in-development security requirements and conformance verification methods take a context 
of use-based approach to addressing scope. Across numerous governments, security programs authorize 
that cloud services can be used by government organization s, and in some cases, those programs 
incorporate various levels of authorization. For the U.S. Government, the Federal Risk and Authorization 
Management Program (FedRAMP) includes low, moderate, and high  security baselines with different 
levels of requirements . C loud services providers (CSPs) can receive authorization that they meet the 
requirements of any of the baselines , with higher baselines involving a more extensive process of 
reviewing cloud service readiness against a broader set of controls  or control parameters . Then, U.S. 
agencies can choose to procure a cloud service at an authorization level that meets their risk needs  (as 
determined by applying the Standards for Security Categorization of Federal Information and Information 
Systems of low, moderate, and high risk to their workloads). Likewise, the EU Cybersecurity Act established 
a framework for the development of cybersecurity certifications at basic, substantial, and high levels for

ICT products, services, and processes. As basic, substantial, and high certifications for cloud services and 
other technologies are available, organizations can choose products certified at a level that likewise meets 
their risk needs, recognizing that higher assurance levels are often associated with higher costs. 
Scoping by attributes or functions of a product or service 
Approaches to risk differentiation based on ICT products or services themselves alternatively recognize 
that, irrespective of use scenario, there are also ways to distinguish between risks associated with product 
attributes or functions. For example, unlike word processing applications, identity, credential, and access 
management products are associated with privileged access, meaning that their compromise could pose 
higher risks (i.e., security exploits, phishing attempts, or other methods by which unauthorized users could 
gain access are associated with the potential for relatively greater damage). 
The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) focused on the implications of how 
software functions or what it enables in defining “critical software” as required by U.S. Executive Order 
(EO) 14028. “EO-critical software” has, or has direct dependencies upon, one or more components with 
at least one of the attributes defined in the EO (e.g., is designed to run with elevated privileges or manage 
privileges, has direct or privileged access to networking or computing resources, is designed to control 
access to data or operational technology, or performs a function critical to trust). 
Scoping by context of use and attributes of a product or service 
Approaches to defining high risk could also consider both attributes of a product or service and context 
of use. While implementation of U.S. EO 14028 is ongoing, it s provisions on EO-critical software could 
ultimately function in this manner. NIST defined categories of products or services with attributes 
associated with higher risk as a first step; next, as directed by the U.S. Office of Management and Budget 
(OMB), agencies are applying the EO’s definition and NIST’s initial phase of categories within their own 
environments (e.g., based on context of use) to identify their EO-critical software. 
For U.S. agencies, this multi -step process is particularly important because  the definition of EO-critical 
software is being used to help them prioritize  where to apply the  operational cybersecurity risk 
management practices detailed in Security Measures for EO-Critical Software Use. The definition is not 
being used to determine a scope of products that should be subject to security requirements; rather, all 
software2 procured by U.S. agencies is subject to guidelines defined in the Secure Software Development 
Framework (SSDF) Version 1.1: Recommendations for Mitigating the Risk of Software Vulnerabilities. With 
that “horizontal” baseline established for software vendors, the EO-critical software definition then directs 
more targeted operational risk management efforts by U.S. agency users of such software. As highlighted 
above, to achieve desired security outcomes, efforts to enhance the cybersecurity of ICT products and 
services often depend upon complementary investments in improving practices of technology users.

EU processes and authorities for defining high risk-focused approaches 
Leveraging a process to assess and describe contexts of use and/or products and services that pose 
elevated risk  will be important to ensuring clarity for technology providers , technology users, and 
regulatory authorities. Currently, at the EU  level, there is no  process by which to  collect input from 
appropriate stakeholders,  assess risk s, make higher or lower risk determinations , and adjust such 
determinations as risks evolve—nor is there an agency or institution responsible for that process. Through 
the Cyber Resilience Act, Microsoft recommends that the European Commission consider defining such 
responsibilities at the EU level and describing appropriate mechanisms for cooperation and coordination 
with existing, comparable national authorities across EU Member States. 
Defining cybersecurity practices: challenges, opportunities, and recommendations 
In addition to making decisions about scope, policies to enhance the cybersecurity of digital products 
and services must consider the cybersecurity practices that will be incentivized or required. While the 
details of such practices should be determined through a  public-private collaboration process and 
adjustable over time, a clear, principled approach helps address anticipated challenges from the outset. 
Such an approach is particularly important if practices are ultimately captured as requirements (i.e., where 
use of practices is mandated for market access or to enable procurement); however, where practices are 
incentivized, it can also help facilitate adoption and drive positive impact. 
Anticipated challenges range from defining practices to ensuring broader coherence and interoperability. 
Defending against ever-evolving threats requires constant innovation, so practices should establish clear 
baselines while fostering agility. Required or incentivized practices also need to recognize overlaps and 
integration across hardware, software, and services; drive consistency and continuity among dynamic and 
interconnected supply chains; and ensure coherence with other policy domains, including privacy and  
sustainability. Effective sets of practices will also promote global interoperability to the maximum extent 
practicable, driving investments in security (versus compliance), facilitating access to best-in-class security 
technology, and enabling small businesses to integrate into global supply chains. 
This section first describes the public -private collaboration process  that is critical to defining effective 
cybersecurity practices. It then discusses the principles that should guide the development and evolution

of such practices. These include fostering ongoing security innovation ; driving consistency, continuity,

Developing practices through inclusive and iterative processes 
Many technology developers, providers, and operators  have been investing in efforts to improve the 
cybersecurity of ICT products and services and engaging in  collaborative efforts to define effective 
practices or standards for years, resulting in useful context and lessons learned. Managing a public-private 
process to de fine practice s enables governments to benefit from industry experience , understand 
potential implementation challenges, and promote clarity from the outset. Such processes should be  
transparent and inclusive, open to all stakeholders or at least those that will be impacted by required or 
incentivized practices. They should also be iterative, creating multiple opportunities for exchange so that 
desired outcomes and input can be better understood and refined. For example, in developing the SSDF 
and other guidance documents to implement U.S. EO 14028, NIST managed multiple public consultations, 
calls for papers, and open public workshops.3 In its efforts to develop the CRA, the European Commission 
is likewise undertaking similar consultations that can help improve outcomes and industry readiness. 
Encouraging ongoing security innovation through outcomes-focused practices 
Leveraging outcomes-focused (i.e., implementation agnostic rather than overly prescriptive and inflexible) 
approaches to security requirements is critical to allowing technology providers to have sufficient agility 
to respond to a dynamic ICT ecosystem and threat environment. Pairing outcomes-focused approaches 
with more prescriptive, optional guidance also helps to ensure value to organizations at different levels 
of risk management maturity and to address anticipated potential challenges with conformity assessment.  
Outcomes-focused approaches define desired security outcomes or processes and how best to measure 
whether those have been achieved rather than defining specific implementation activities. For example, 
in starting with a desired outcome: “Sensitive personal data is protected from unauthorized access,” more 
specific, but still implementation agnostic, requirements can be derived, such as: “Sensitive personal data 
is protected from unauthorized access when at rest or in transit.” Alternatively, the requirement “Data at 
rest should be AES encrypted with a key length of at least 128 bits” is implementation specific. Similarly, 
describing a required process, such as use of secure software development practices  like reviewing or 
analyzing code for vulnerabilities, allows providers to share evidence of various potential practices for 
doing so. However, requiring use of specific static analysis tools to check code is implementation specific, 
limiting how software providers can improve tools and practices over time based on continuous learning. 
Defining requirements in terms of outcomes  or processes  rather than implementations allow s ICT 
providers to choose the standards and practices that best align with their business processes and existing 
and ongoing security investments. Outcomes-focused approaches also encourage innovation and help 
ensure that security practices do not become stale over time  as threat actors continue to evolve their 
tactics and as investments in security improvements are ongoing. Alternatively, requirements stating that 
a certain implementation should be used risk constraining providers’ ability to evolve and enhance  
practices and disincentivize research into and development of better implementations.

Outcomes-focused approaches can also be when paired with implementation guidance, such as relevant 
standards, best practices, and controls, ensuring that more prescriptive explanations of security practices 
that could help organizations meet security outcomes are available to those who need them . However, 
integrating implementation guidance within outcomes-focused approaches could impact high assurance 
conformity assessment schemes, which are often geared towards specific implementations. If assessors 
lack deep subject matter expertise, example implementations might help them evaluate whether desired 
outcomes have been met – but they might still struggle to assess novel implementation s. These 
assessment challenges can be countered by restricting the scenarios where high assurance assessments 
are used (e.g., to narrowly scoped high-risk scenarios, allowing those assessments to be more detailed) 
or by establishing fast-track and alternate paths (e.g., for pre-vetted versus new implementations).

including relevant standards and best practice frameworks.4 The U.S. National Telecommunications and

of Materials (SBOM). Rather than defining a new standard or choosing an existing standard, NTIA instead 
defined use cases they want ed to achieve ( desired outcomes) and what information an SBOM must 
contain to enable those use cases. This resulted in NTIA’s Minimum Elements for a Software Bill of 
Materials, which identifies seven required data fields and additional optional data fields. Existing standards 
(e.g., SPDX, CycloneDX) were then mapped to those minimum elements, facilitating interchange between 
the formats, allowing software producers and consumers of SBOM information choice in SBOM format 
and tools, and supporting further innovation by not prescribing specific implementations. 
By focusing on cybersecurity processes and outcomes, the future EU Cyber Resilience Act would similarly 
help ensure that  industry maintains critical operational agility in a  complex and quickly evolving  ICT 
security environment. Secure software development practices, vulnerability management processes, and 
transparent lifecycle policies are important areas to include. Providing of SBOM information could foster 
software supply chain transparency and help prepare the ecosystem for additional use cases to enhance 
security and integrity. Transparent, inclusive, and iterative processes for further defining such practices 
and implementation examples are necessary to help ensure agility and interoperability.

Fostering alignment across regions, sectors, and policy domains 
Just as consistent requirements across ICT environments (e.g., hardware, software, and services) reduce

domains, such as privacy, sustainability, and cybersecurity.  
Leveraging standards and best practices for global and cross-sector alignment 
Recognizing that governments, industry, and other stakeholders worldwide share a common goal of using 
ICT products and services with enhanced security, the extent to whic h governments can synchronize 
cybersecurity practices across jurisdictions and sectors will have profound effects on all stakeholders.5 For 
governments, the process of building out security baselines from scratch and verifying conformance  is 
resource intensive and time consuming. For large organizations that operate across borders, having to 
comply with fragmented or redundant requirements diverts resources from security functions. For local 
businesses, fragmented requirements limit market access and opportunities to integrate with global 
and/or cross-sector supply chains. For all stakeholders, fragmentation prevents access to best -in-class 
security technology and reverses the partnerships that have enabled access to advanced technologies, 
such as smart phones, that support new business models and operations across geographies.  
Even with an intention to foster alignment, policies may inadvertently contribute to fragmentation. 
Building and enforcing conformity assessment schemes associated with outcomes -focused security 
requirements often formalizes implementation of best practices and technologies popular at the time the 
conformance scheme was developed. Effective requirements are frequently replicated in other regions 
with modifications intended to improve them. This can lead to very similar but fragmented ICT

Ensuring coherence across policy domains 
Increasing technology regulation across domains , including privacy, sustainability, and cyb ersecurity, 
often has complementary goals of helping to protect consumers and the ecosystem. However, optimizing 
for one domain may (often unintentionally) negatively impact another. For instance, sustainability and 
privacy policies may impact how ICT products are maintained and to what extent providers can integrate 
security learnings into product improvements, unintentionally reducing or impeding security.  
Approaches to and impacts of efforts to enhance security often differ for new versus existing ICT products. 
New products create an opportunity to address current security risks in design and development phases 
(using new suppliers, technology, standards, innovation, or hardware req uirements) and to balance 
security and user experience requirement s. However, security maintenance of released products , 
especially physical devices, often involves “bolting on” security mitigations and features to respond to 
previously unanticipated threa ts and ecosystem changes. User experience is sometimes unavoidably 
degraded by such security mitigations due to decreased performance (e.g., transitioning to stronger 
cryptography requiring more computation) or extra steps required by users (e.g., using mu lti-factor 
authentication). Security improvements are often intertwined with functionality changes in ways that are 
complicated for manufacturers to explain to end users ; for example, feature updates invariably include 
security improvements as code is refactored and revised. Moreover, manufacturers are not only focused

on fixing vulnerabilities but also making sure customers deploy updates.  Cybersecurity can be greatly 
improved when users are on the same version , simplifying threat monitoring, minimizing the testing 
necessary for future security enhancements, and simplifying integration with other stakeholders. 
Sustainability and consumer protection policies increasingly intersect with such efforts to enhance security 
and user experience . Regulations intended to extend the longevity of devices or that impact how 
providers maintain  software and services , especially on hardware products , can inadvertently block 
security best practices or degrade their effectiveness. For example, while users should be able to configure 
whether updates are installed automatically or require consent prior to installation,  some regulatory 
approaches could result in delays to automatic deployment of security updates. Likewise, requiring that 
users must be able to persistently rollback updates to any incremental software version (versus a more 
limited set of older versions for a narrower time frame after an update is installed) would significantly 
complicate security patch testing, which would then need to occur against every possible software 
version. As a result, the creation and rollout of security patches could be delayed. Additionally, schemes 
to enable use of devices after a manufacturer’s support period ends might compromise the security of all 
devices by allowing an untrusted party to make updates. 
Likewise, privacy policies with broad data localization mandates risk preventing global access to security 
telemetry and advanced threat intelligence. ICT providers leverage global security telemetry to investigate 
incidents, understand threat actors’ evolving techniques, improve threat modeling and product security 
by design, and incorporate automated threat detection and response into current products.   
Pursuing important objectives in  ICT regulation across domains  is a complex endeavor that will likely 
require ongoing reflection and adjustment to effectively address unintended consequences. Providing a 
legislative mechanism to account for this complexity would help future proof in -development 
requirements, especially when domain experts are not engaged in all cross-domain regulatory activities. 
For example, manufacturers could be allowed to identify and explain why outcomes  from one domain 
justify adjustments or waivers of other regulatory requirements , and a cross-domain process could be 
established by which regulatory authorities can prioritize outcomes as appropriate.

Defining verification methods: challenges, opportunities, and recommendations  
Conformance verification is the final pillar of efforts to enhance the cybersecurity of digital products and 
services. Defining effective, practical approaches for verifying that in -scope products and services  
conform with cybersecurity practices requires an understanding of the benefits and drawbacks of various 
existing methods as well as investment in new methods that offer greater scale and impact . For the EU 
CRA, allowing for calibration of conformance verification methods based on needs and challenges today 
as well as future opportunities can help maximize cybersecurity impact while managing market impact. 
This section first describes various existing methods for conformance verification, including first - and 
third-party assessments. It provides more detailed context on, and examples of, labeling and certification 
programs as global interest in both is increasing. It then introduces new potential methods for conformity 
assessment, recognizing that challenges that exist today with verifying use of cybersecurity practices are 
exacerbated by skilling and infrastructure gaps that require long-term investment. 
Evaluating approaches to security conformance verification 
Various methods for verifying or proving that security requirements have been met may be appropriate 
to leverage as part of a holistic effort to advance the basic security of ecosystem-wide ICT products and 
services and/or elevated security for higher risk scenarios. Each of these methods can be described using 
different terms, though ISO/IEC 17000 offers consistent definitions for the terms “conformity assessment” 
and “attestation” (including first-, second-, and third-party attestation).6 Methods include: 
• First-party or self-attestation (i.e., supplier declaration of conformity): ICT providers can attest that 
they conform to security practices (i.e., meet requirements), including through declarations or in 
the context of contractual commitments. In doing so, they may share information about security 
practices publicly or upon private request as evidence to support their claims. Declarations and 
commitments could later be subject to regulatory reviews or legal disputes. 
• Second-party attestation: a technology user  (i.e., often a government) determines whether and 
attests that an ICT product or service conforms to required security practices. 
• Third-party attestation: an independent third party assesses and attests to whether an ICT product 
or service conforms with security practices . The independent third -party assessor may also be 
subject to evaluation before being considered an approved or trusted assessor. 
Labeling programs and certifications are two types of second- and/or third-party attestation, depending 
upon whether the assessor and issuer of a label or certification is the technology user (i.e., often a 
government) or an independent third party. They might also involve a mix of second- and/or third party 
attestation; for example, governments might  rely on a mix of independent and government pe rsonnel 
assessor or issue labels or certifications they rely on themselves while providing transparency to inform 
purchasing decisions in the broader ecosystem. While similar and in some cases potentially overlapping, 
labeling programs and certifications are often considered distinctly today. They may be associated with 
different ICT products and services (e.g., labeling programs with IoT and certifications with cloud services). 
In addition, while both labels and certifications generally depend on assessment by  a government

Notably, across any method, conformity assessments might focus on organizational processes, products

also be resulting delays in access to products or services with enhanced cybersecurity. 
Self-attestation 
Self-attestation offers numerous benefits as well as potential limitations. Benefits include scale, speed, 
and relatively low costs – and as the ecosystem is vast, the pace of technology deployment continues to 
accelerate, and high costs impact market access, especially for small and medium-sized businesses, these 
benefits are significant . Third-party attestation depends on existing assessor infrastructure, including 
skilled, available assessors and processes to establish trust in assessment quality – challenges with which 
could reduce the security value of assessments, slow delivery (which sometimes results in users leveraging 
unassessed technology), or drive-up costs, especially as certification demands grow. Alternatively, self-
attestation has fewer dependencies and costs; suppliers may still need to invest in processes to implement 
requirements and produce evidence  to support their claims, but they can share such evidence directly 
with technology users rather than paying a third party to review it. However, where more detailed reviews 
of ICT providers’ security practices are required  (e.g., in higher risk scenarios where there’s a need for 
higher assurance), technology users may prefer confirmation by third-party assessors. 
While implementation of U.S. EO 14028 software supply chain security requirements is ongoing, NIST has 
recommended that U.S. agencies “accept first-party attestation of conformity with SSDF practices” unless 
the need for third -party attestation is determined using a risk -based approach.7 In doing so, NIST has 
further recommended that agencies request from software vendors high-level “artifacts of conformance,” 
such as documentation “describing the methodology, procedures, and processes a software producer 
uses for its secure practices for software development.”8 NIST cautioned agencies against requesting low-
level artifacts, such as threat models or source code vulnerability scan reports, as such artifacts only 
represent a snapshot in time,  may be difficult to analyze appropriately, and may contain sensitive 
information that attackers could use for hostile purposes ,9 increasing overall ecosystem risk if such 
information is exposed as well as potentially motivating attackers to target agencies.

up to date on emerging product information. A well-designed label would focus on communicating 
security outcomes, rather than implementations, to the customer, minimizing technical terminology that

high costs and slow speeds. Certifications are often resource intensive, in terms of both financial costs 
and personnel resources.  For ICT provider s, such costs  decrease resources available for operational 
security, product security enhancements, and continued security innovation and disproportionately affect 
small businesses, impacting the potential for  innovation, competition, and diversity.  Governments may 
also expend significant resources investing in the expertise to employ assessors and/or establishing and 
maintaining the infrastructure and processes to evaluate independent third-party assessors. For example, 
in establishing the U.S. Department of Defense Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC), third-

Investing in new methods for conformity assessment 
As ICT products and services continue to proliferate and the importance of understanding and reducing 
risk continues to escalate, ecosystem needs to attain higher levels of assurance across a broader scope 
of products and services will also  continue to increase. Technological solutions that provide verifiable 
assurances can be effective at meeting this demand  for increased scale while managing costs. For 
example, hardware (such as Trusted Platform Modules) that automatically verify the integrity of low-level 
software (such as the operating system) when a computing system starts can provide continuous 
assurance that software foundational to trusting a device has not been subject to tampering. 
Microsoft has been investigating using technologies to store and exchange supply chain security 
compliance artifacts and attestations, such as software bills of materials  (SBOM), so they can be 
independently verified and to enable automated and continuous assurance based on those attestations. 
Supply Chain Integrity, Transparency and Trust (SCITT) offers one path forward for storing and exchanging 
such meaningful artifacts and attestation. It could enable users of technology to evaluate artifacts of 
conformance with requirements not just from direct vendors but also from their suppliers. 
Microsoft’s goal is to use technologies like SCITT, in combination with open international standards , to 
continuously exchange trusted supply chain information, including from the open source community . 
SCITT could ultimately be used to verify the integrity of supply chains, share threat intelligence, and detect 
supply chain attacks in a more automated and scalable way. We welcome coordination and collaboration 
from European government, EU Member State, and other partners on SCITT and related efforts, helping 
us continue to build such technologies in a way that is responsive to government interests and concerns. 
Taking a holistic, phased approach 
Policy decisions about scope, cybersecurity practices, and methods for verifying conformance are highly 
interdependent. As each of these areas interrelates, a holistic approach to considering options will yield 
the best outcome. For instance, a horizontal approach to broadly scoping in products and services might

21 
 
argue in favor of a narrower set of required, flexible security practices, ensuring agility in implementation 
and reducing conformity assessment demands. Such an approach might also argue in favor of allowing 
for a mix of conformance verification methods, enabling calibration to a wider set of anticipated use cases 
with varying assurance needs. When new requirements are formulated, it is also valuable to consider how 
conformance with those requirements might need to be demonstrated at the same time, enabling ICT 
developers and operators to engage with governments on potential challenges and expectations. 
A holistic view also allows governments to simultaneously con sider how to address interests in both  
ecosystem-wide and high risk-focused approaches as well as in enhancing not only the cybersecurity of 
ICT products and services but also the cybersecurity risk management practices of users.  Horizontal, 
ecosystem-wide approaches respond to cybersecurity risks resulting from interconnectedness across the 
dynamic ICT ecosystem, including many different ICT products and services, components, and suppliers. 
Narrower, high risk-focused approaches help focus attention on ensuring adequate security for safety-
critical or other sensitive technology use  scenarios. Attention to risk management practices of users 
reduces the risk of most cyber threat actor inroads today; attackers often take advantage of poor cyber 
hygiene, compromising credentials (e.g., through phishing attacks) or leveraging unpatched systems.14 
A holistic approach – that enhances the cybersecurity of ICT products and services  used across the 
ecosystem and in high-risk scenarios as well as improv es user cybersecurity risk management – also 
requires a phased approach.  Principles of effective risk management  and the reality of resource 
constraints mean that risk prioritization and responsible and intentional tradeoffs are necessary in the 
near term. However, as part of a longer-term plan, more holistic improvement can be pursued. Moreover, 
when linked as part of an iterative plan, the effectiveness of earlier policy efforts can be  assessed and 
improved upon as they’re expanded, allowing implementation to become more rigorous. 
If developed and implemented in a coordinated manner – through a phased, iterative approach – both 
lower security baselines and conformity assessment expectations for ecosystem-wide approaches and 
higher security baselines and conformity assessment expectations for high-risk scenarios  might be 
relevant. By first establishing basic core tenets of security practices and implementing them across a broad 
swath of products  and services, governments can create a regulatory foundation that accommodates 
industry-wide standards while also preparing for layered tiers of elevated standards to be phased in over 
time. They can prioritize security practices widely applicable across ICT products and  services and use 
scenarios, such as secure software development practices, patching, and implementation of multi-factor 
authentication. They can also survey impacts, address concerns, predict future advancements and risks, 
and effectively tailor solutions using the evidence and foresight provided by an initial phase. 
Many governments are taking phased approaches to implementing cybersecurity policies. In efforts to 
advance the cybersecurity of critical infrastructure and essential services, governments  in the EU, UK, 
Australia, and other regions  are expanding the scope of sectors impacted by associated requirements 
over time.15 The EU has likewise phased in the development of cybersecurity certifications resulting from

Conclusion and summary of recommendations 
ICT products and services are relied upon by people and organizations around the world, and enhancing 
their cybersecurity is foundational to efforts  to improve ecosystem resilience and trust. Microsoft is 
supportive of government efforts to take a horizontal approach to enhancing the cybersecurity of ICT 
products and services, promoting greater cross-ecosystem consistency. We also recognize the complexity 
of such an effort and offer the following summary of our recommendations: 
• Take a holistic, phased approach. A holistic approach addresses cybersecurity risks associated with 
ICT products and services as well as the operational behavior of users. It also considers scope (i.e., 
products, services, or organizations targeted by measures), security practices (i.e., guidance or 
requirements to be implemented for products or services or by organizations), and verification 
of conformance (i.e., methods of assessing whether practices have been implemented) in concert. 
Taking a holistic approach is complex but necessary to achieve positive security outcomes. When 
paired with a phased approach, which allows for iterative learning and improving, complexity is

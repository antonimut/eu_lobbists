 
 
 
Confederation of Danish Industries (DI) position regarding  
a future Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) in response to EC public consultation 
Background 
Over the last couple of years cybersecurity requirements have been included in at least four 
different pieces of EU legislation1. Furthermore, requirements on cybersecurity will be imposed on 
most production facilities following the revision of the framework for the security of network and 
information systems (NIS2). On top, a call for evidence and a public consultation have been 
launched in preparation for a proposal for a Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) to be launched in third 
quarter of this year. 
As an attachment to the public consultation DI hereby shares our perspectives on the proposal 
under development, including specific considerations that we believe would ease the compliance 
of cybersecurity requirements for manufactures and other economic operators along the supply 
chain. In the end of the paper, we conclude by describing our preferred policy option among those 
described by the EC in their call for evidence. 
 
Horizontal approach is key 
DI supports the introduction of horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products in the EU. We 
believe there is a need to provide for basic requirements for all products on top of which 
additional requirements can be added, based on the products’ risk profile i.e., their application, 
and the threat landscape throughout the products’ lifecycles. 
One set of requirements covering cybersecurity for each product. 
With the fragmented approach to regulating cybersecurity the same product will soon be subject 
to multiple requirements at the same time. As the requirements are defined in different ways in 
different pieces of legislation, legal uncertainty is introduced. Therefore, it is important that the 
proposal for a CRA cleans up the regulatory environment around cybersecurity and doesn’t add an 
extra layer of complexity. To help solve the challenges the CRA should become a lex generalis 
concerning baseline requirements for cybersecurity for connected products, regardless of whether 
they are connected directly or indirectly to the internet. Furthermore, it should explicitly relate to 
existing requirements and state which rules that prevails for different products. One product given 
a specific application should only be covered by one set of cybersecurity requirements. At the 
same time the delegated acts under the radio equipment directive should be repealed. In DI’s 
opinion it does not make sense to have different pieces of legislation to cover wired and non-
wired products respectively and cybersecurity measures for different purposes i.e., fraud, privacy,

The call for evidence and the public consultation suggests the scope of the future regulation would 
include both tangible and intangible products i.e. software, and ancillary services. DI is of the 
opinion that it is much too ambitious and most likely would result in legislation of poor quality. It is 
our sincere hope the EC will reconsider and instead focus on improving the framework for 
connected products including embedded software or software intended to be embedded into 
specific products.  Soft law would be a more natural approach to stand alone software and 
ancillary services for now. 
Application of well-established principles for EU-product legislation. 
To facilitate ease of compliance, well-known principles from EU product legislation should be 
applied i.e., New Legislative Framework (NLF). The principles that define NLF can be extracted 
from four texts: Decision 768/2008/EC, Regulation 765/2008/EC, Regulation (EU)2019/1020 and 
the Blue Guide providing guidance on their interpretation2. In DI our members have identified the 
following principles from NLF which they believe should be applied in CRA. See below. 
1. Product compliance, declaration of conformity and CE-marking 
Product compliance is the manufacturer’s responsibility. The conformity assessment to ensure 
compliance is done prior to placing the product on the market given the ‘Intended purpose’ of 
the product as defined by the manufacturer and ‘the conditions of use which can reasonably be 
foreseen’ (EF 765/2008). It is based on a risk assessment of the product. Following the 
conformity assessment (as required by the legislation) the declaration of conformity (DoC) is 
completed and the product is CE marked. That should also be the case for the CRA. As the supply 
chain of connected products is complex, shared responsibility should be made possible (See also 
5). 
2. Application of essential health and safety requirements and harmonised standards 
Essential Health and Safety Requirements (EHSR) are stated in the legislation in a manner that is 
technological neutral and general in nature. The technical specifications are developed in line with 
the standardisation regulation, (EU)1025/2012, and the voluntary application of harmonised 
standards provides for presumption of conformity with the EHSR. This allows for the product to 
move freely within the EU. The harmonised standards should be aligned with international 
standards to allow for global market access. Otherwise, the manufactures that act globally will 
have to document compliance with different sets of requirements and/or await application of the 
latest state-of-the-art solutions introduced in international standards3.

EHSR most often relate to products, not processes and systems - that also have an impact on 
cybersecurity. None-the-less, NLF does not prevent the regulation from including requirements in 
this regard. When it comes to processes the aim of them should be included in the EHSR, in the 
body of the regulation. The technical specifications related to the processes should be developed 
as part of harmonised standards (as is the case for other EHSR). When establishing the 
requirements, already existing international standards in the field should be considered as should 
alignment with NIS2 requirements to ease compliance. 
Although systems do indeed have an impact on cybersecurity, the inclusion of systems to the CRA 
would add too much complexity. Thus, DI believes it should be left out. Furthermore, the new 
requirements under NIS2 most likely would close the gap, at least partially.    
Ensuring the cybersecurity of services falls outside the scope of NLF. If included in the CRA it needs 
its own framework for compliance, but preferably rely on the same set of definitions etc.   
If the European Commission is to be delegated powers to develop technical specifications outside 
the established standardisation system, the delegation to do so should follow very strict criteria 
aligned across sector specific legislation4. Based on an assessment of their product, within NLF, the 
manufacturer decides which of the EHSR that is relevant to his product. 
In NLF there is a clear distinction between EHSR which relate to the product and obligations of 
economic operators. DI believes this should also be the case in the CRA  
3. Legislators decide which modules of conformity assessment the manufacturer can choose 
from when performing the assessment  
When designing the legislation, the legislators choose between well-established modules of 
conformity assessment described in Decision 768/2008/EC. The modules specify who should carry 
out the assessment and the scope of the assessment (product and/or processes). The application 
of module A (self-assessment by the manufacturer) should be allowed for most products. The 
manufacturers have greater knowledge of their products than third party notified bodies. 
Furthermore, it underlines the manufacturers responsibility. Allowing for application of self-
assessment also ensures better access to qualified cybersecurity experts for smaller companies 
who can’t afford to employ such experts themselves. Furthermore, the application of self-
assessment allows for faster speed to market, which is important in an ever evolving and 
innovative technology that can influence cybersecurity. There is no reason to believe self-
assessment is less secure than third-party assessment, provided the standard used is effective. It 
has worked very well for decades in safety under the low voltage directive (LVD).  
According to NLF, the legislators should apply a risk-based approach to conformity assessment 
modules. Therefore, the ability to apply self-assessment could be made depended on the existence 
of harmonised standards and/or the economic operators maturity level when it comes to 
 
Member States. They call for the identification of proper solutions, including justification of means and 
proportionality.

Current NLF requires of manufacturers to react after the placing on the market of their products if 
they find they present a risk. New proposals from the European Commission call for conformity 
assessment after the placing on the market (AI regulation) or product sampling and testing 
explicitly (GPSR). Especially alignment with the AI regulation is important, and the later alignment 
with NLF 2.05.  
4. Legislators should ensure sufficient competences of other parties engaged with conformity 
assessment of the product 
Decision 768/2008/EC and Regulation 765/2008/EC establish requirements for accreditation 
bodies and notified bodies that carry out third-party conformity assessment (among others). DI 
believes the CRA should relay on the existing framework for those actors. However, there is a need 
to ensure the competences of the notified bodies better. Mandatory accreditation could serve that 
purpose as well as a requirement to participate in peer reviews and round robin testing.  
5. Aligned definitions and obligations of economic operations 
Decision 768/2008/EC and Regulation 765/2008/EC introduce definitions and obligations for 
economic operators that is applied across NLF legislations. The CRA should apply the same 
definitions and obligations. Given the nature of cybersecurity the lifecycle of the product needs to 
be considered when establishing the EHSR. It may also have implications for the conformity 
assessment modules as described above (point 3). Furthermore, it will be needed to define new 
actors with new obligations to ensure compliance and fairness between the different actors. For 
instance, the user should be obliged to apply for software updates and the manufacturer (or 
provider) to supply them (under specific circumstances). The obligations most likely would need to 
differentiate between B-t-B and B-t-C products and applications as the two markets work 
differently, as do the expected behaviour of the two segments.  In the proposal for an AI regulation 
the two actors “provider” and “user” are introduced. The CRA seems to plan on applying the 
term “vendor”. Emphasis in the CRA should be on alignment with existing terms (later to be 
included in NLF2.0). Furthermore, special attention should be given to the fact that the vendor (as 
the provider) in NLF terms combine different roles in the supply chain i.e., the manufacturer, the 
importer, and the distributor, with different degrees of obligations when it comes to ensuring 
compliance. Finally, also the role of the fulfilment centre service provider from the market 
surveillance regulation, (EU) 1020/2019, should be included.

6. Alignment of other important definitions and regulatory techniques 
A product that undergoes a substantial modification needs to go through a new conformity 
assessment. Such modifications may be introduced by a change in the design to improve 
functionality, for instance through a software update or use of an advanced AI system. If the CRA 
introduces definitions of that nature, they should be aligned with existing definitions in legislation 
which the product is already (or will be) subject to. That should also be the case if the regulation 
introduces definitions of relevance to the green transition i.e., refurbishing, remanufacturing. In 
the ideal world all those definitions were already defined under the NLF framework. 
Components of connected products, such as a processor or a sensor, can be viewed as partly 
completed machinery to be integrated into a machine. Therefore, DI suggests a similar legislative 
technique is applied in CRA. It will ease compliance to build on well-known principles. 
Furthermore, many connected products already fall under the machinery regulation.  That would 
mean, only sensors and processors that can function independently of the device it is  integrated 
into will need the CE-marking and DOC. 
7. Allow for digital labelling to support sustainability 
Decision 768/2008/EC and Regulation 765/2008/EC introduce information obligations for the 
different economic operators. For instance, the obligation to mark the product with the 
manufacturers physical address, the CE-marking and instructions in a language easily understood 
by the consumer (official language of the country where the product is being sold) or end user. The 
CRA should follow the same framework for information obligations but allow for the information 
obligations to be complied with digitally. An e-mail or webpage allow the consumer or the market 
surveillance authority to communicate with the manufacturer (or importer). Instructions and other 
information obligations can easily be approached digitally, requiring less use of paper and less 
need for freight than if only print versions were allowed. All such information could also be 
accessed by use of an QR code or similar. If not immediately, the CRA should allow for digital 
compliance of information obligations after a given period. 
8. Sufficient and effective market surveillance 
Sufficient and effective market surveillance is needed to ensure compliance and a level playing 
field. Therefore, the CRA must take on board the requirements of the Market Surveillance 
Regulation (EU) 2019/1020, and the European Commission follow-up on the reporting from the 
Member States as to what extent they fulfil their obligations. Also, the necessary competences of 
the competent authorities must be ensured. Participation in standardisation should be encouraged 
as well as peer review and best practices, and support during the implementation of the new 
regulation. 
 
Relationship to the Cyber Security Act(CSA) 
Among other things the CSA establishes a framework for voluntary certification in EU, including an 
ENISA mandate to develop certification schemes given certain procedural requirements are met.  
It is important there is an alignment of requirements in the voluntary certification schemes under 
ENISA and the harmonised standards to grant presumption of conformity under a future CRA. Even 
more important is it that they are aligned with international standards for global market access.  
 
 
DI strongly supports the application of harmonised standards and believe they should be the core 
of CRA compliance work. We are concerned about the limited role of industry in the development 
of schemes, which increases the risk of misalignment with international standards and market 
uptake. Furthermore, harmonised standards are developed in line with European democratic 
values, the schemes are not. In that sense they are comparable to the technical specifications the 
EC is introducing to new product regulation (point 2 above). In DI’s opinion CRA should rely on 
harmonised standards to grant presumption of conformity. At the same time it should allow for 
businesses that have invested in certification amble time to comply with the requirements of CRA, 
should the certification schemes differ from the requirements in CRA. 
 
Preferred Policy Option in the Call for evidence 
DI is in favor of policy option 4 with the modification that we do not believe ancillary services 
should be included. From Option 5 we would support the inclusion of embedded software and also 
non-embedded (standalone software) with an intended use to be integrated into specific products, 
preferably only for critical software. 
Regarding conformity assessment we believe self-assessment should be the preferred option for 
most cases. However, we do acknowledge that 3rd party involvement of notified bodies could be 
necessary for the products that involves the highest level of risk. Choice of conformity assessment 
modules may depend on “cybersecurity”-maturity of the organisation responsible for the 
assessment and the existence of harmonised standards.

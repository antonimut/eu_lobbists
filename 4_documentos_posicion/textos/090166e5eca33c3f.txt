Comments to public consultation on Cyber Resilience Act  24th May 2022  We would like to thank the Commission for giving us the opportunity to provide comments to this important initiative. We share the objectives of the Commission in improving cybersecurity and cyber-resilience. Cybersecurity benefits from the increased awareness of policy makers and we can attest to the positive effect EU legislation like GDPR and NIS1 had to the overall cybersecurity landscape in Europe.  Whereas the available information on the considered proposal from the Ares document are fairly limited our response is going to focus on the current market environment and our understanding of how it is likely to evolve should options 4 or 5 materialise. In the light of the current market realities, we would consider option 4 as more appropriate, provided that some of the questions outlined below are sufficiently addressed. A fundamental question that needs to be considered is at what stage of the technology will the intended CRA cybersecurity requirements be introduced. The security of a particular technology can be very effective by design, but the user may decide to modify or ignore certain properties out of the box. The operational stage, i.e. the stage when the technology is deployed and used is the stage where the technology is exposed to different risks but this area is already heavily regulated. Nevertheless, it is also the area that users have most discretion in increasing and managing security effectively for their unique circumstances. Interplay between the CRA scope and other EU instruments The operational cybersecurity landscape in the EU is regulated by NIS1 (and soon NIS2) at the level of provider/operator of critical infrastructure. Our understanding of the recently announced NIS2 agreement suggests that, the definition of critical infrastructure is likely to cover a broad range of cloud computing services including the delivery of Software as a Service (SaaS). GDPR regulates security requirements on all personal data in Articles 32 and 33. In addition it creates design obligations (privacy by design and default Article 25), while RED introduces privacy and security requirements for wirelessly connected devices at design level. CSA regulates how certification can happen in the EU for cybersecurity purposes. Thus, service provision (NIS1, NIS2), infrastructure (NIS1, NIS2), data (GDPR) and wirelessly connected devices (RED) are currently fully regulated in the European Union.  To frame it differently, a smart wearable is simultaneously regulated by GDPR and RED if it is wirelessly connectable. If it is using an ancillary cloud service that cloud service will also be regulated by GDPR and NIS2. If the activity it engages specifically relates to another regulated sector e.g. a financial transaction by covering point of sale (POS) devices it is possible that provisions under PSD2 and/or DORA (also close to official adoption) will apply.  In the light of those observations, although one is not against the principle of a horizontal instrument to address cybersecurity requirements one needs to ask what is out of scope already that needs to be covered and what would be the overlap if any? • Would this initiative address non-connected tangible digital products that escape from the application of RED? If that is the case, then what is the real cybersecurity risk for a non-connected product (that is likely to be old e.g. a second generation iPod) and how much of that is likely to be phased out already or soon?

• Would this initiative address physically connected (not wirelessly) tangible digital products? If that is the case, then what is the likelihood that a device will have only wired connectivity thus escaping the application of RED? • If ancillary services are covered, does ancillary services mean software on premise in the light of the fact that SaaS is covered by NIS2? • If the intention is to cover both SaaS services delivered on hardware and the security of the device itself how will these requirements not overlap NIS2 and RED from the perspective of the organisation delivering this service to its customers? Neither NIS nor RED make a distinction between B2B and B2C markets therefore it is difficult to understand whether the proposal under consideration intends to address these requirements from a consumer protection perspective (B2C) or to apply them also in B2B relationships. In practice a B2C relationship can be the smart meter at a consumer’s house. A B2B relationship on a similar device can be a smart meter (IoT/tangible digital product) as part of the infrastructure of an energy producer but in the environment of an energy distributor supported by a SaaS service for detailed control, configuration, diagnostics etc. Such a relationship would be covered from a cybersecurity perspective by NIS2 and RED but not GDPR if there is only M2M communication and no personal data involved. • Would the proposal cover on premise software? One can see the appeal of this approach, but in reality, the security properties and security decisions around on-premise software are determined by the operators/users of the software and not the providers. Decisions on patching, default security settings, user permissions etc are not the decisions of the manufacturer. In that case the only aspects that are left relate to vulnerability management. The Ares document seems to suggest that: “the current framework fails to cover a variety of widely used hardware (e.g. hardware not falling under the Radio Equipment Directive or the Medical Devices Regulation)” and that: “non-embedded software products are not addressed in the current framework, even though vulnerabilities in software products are increasingly serving as a channel for cybersecurity attacks”.  The examples outlined above seem to indicate that the limited (assuming RED remains in force) gap justifying regulatory intervention that policy makers need to consider is more likely around IoT devices and especially in the consumer space. Alternatively, if the security and privacy requirements of RED would not remain in force and would be replaced by CRA the focus on consumer IoT would still be justified, while reducing the overlap. We would strongly recommend the Commission to be specific about the devices/technologies that will be covered by the proposed framework to understand the true scope and impact of the proposal. Given the multitude of requirements and the existence of sector rules (namely in the field of payments and finance) it is critical that any horizontal proposal in this area has a clear scope and a well-defined set of objectives. For instance, RED is excluding medical devices and motor vehicles from its scope. Equally the security of non-embedded software if delivered as a service via the cloud (SaaS) is addressed by NIS2. On premise software is out of scope of NIS2. If the intention is to cover on premise software, then its security and vulnerability management is dependent upon the use done by its operator and the only issue at hand would be the frequency of available patches and longevity of the support.   User aspects, lifecycle timelines and security requirements 
To the degree that the end goal would be to address the complete life cycle of products with a focus on updates and end-of-life arrangements it should be noted that the needs and commercial practices vary considerably per product/service category and per industry and market segment. For instance, consumer software life cycle has an average period of 3-5 years with notable exemptions for certain product categories while enterprise software may have a longer lifespan although for instance on cybersecurity due to the evolving threat landscape there are shorter timelines.  In addition, on the question of patching and updating it should be noted that existing EU legislation like the proposed ePrivacy Regulation under discussion explicitly allows users (natural and legal persons) to refuse updates. Therefore, any provisions of CRA need to take into account the role of the user in modifying the security controls. This is not a consumer only issue but rather an issue also for industry that will often make risk-based decisions to update or manage patching in a complex technological environment. The question of CRA scope is also linked with the applicability in the context of B2B or B2C. The security capabilities and needs in the B2B context vary dramatically from those in B2C. The average consumer does not have the knowledge or expertise around cybersecurity, while the opposite is the case for medium and larger organisations that understand business risk and increasingly include cybersecurity in that risk, taking steps to manage it themselves or alternatively choosing to seek outside expertise by outsourcing their security arrangements. It is therefore recommended that CRA focuses on B2C which is also the area of largest risk when it comes to IoT.  An additional reason why a particular segment like B2C is more appropriate is because the security requirements in B2B cannot easily fit “protection profiles” without a detailed understanding of the specific use case and without limiting organisations from making risk-based or use-case decisions. It will become extremely complex to develop protection profiles for each device category if it is not targeted at a specific segment e.g. B2C and even more complex if one has to consider every possible use-case. For example, none can disagree on the importance of strong authentication in the context of cybersecurity. Requiring however of all devices to enable always strong authentication irrespectively of the context may be unnecessary or even counter-productive in certain use-cases. For instance, strong authentication in a home router is useful for router administration purposes but it is not useful when enabling guest WiFi or in the case of an airport allowing free WiFi access to travellers. Requirements at the design level, conformity self-assessment and “free” software If the objective of the proposal is to create a horizontal obligation at the design level to “manufacture and ship secure products” then apart from addressing the RED and GDPR overlaps, it would need to consider that there no one single way of developing a product in a secure manner. In fact, different methodologies exist that are also dependent of a multitude of factors including the size of the company. CRA should not impose a specific methodology for secure development but rather be principles based and outcome focused. In doing that the CRA should acknowledge that does not exist error-free software and that any obligation to deliver a high standard of security is met by abiding to a certain development methodology and by making timely available updates/patches for a reasonable period. CRA should consider that requiring a particular methodology or a particular standard is likely to have distortionary effects in the market. Large enterprises can adherence to a particular 
standard at a cost, smaller enterprises and start-ups will be seriously challenged to carry the cost of meeting such standard, as well as to find a market if their products are presumed to be less secure. A mechanism along those lines described earlier that is principles based and outcomes focused is best served through a conformity self-assessment procedure. Manufacturers and importers would self-certify the security features and methodologies followed and market surveillance authorities could take action if there were complaints identifying failures in the products. Third party conformity assessments should only be required in very few cases and for devices for which there is already a specific technical standard and demonstrated high risk such as medical devices or automobiles.  The idea of requiring security properties of products to be disclosed to enable users to select such products against their needs is appealing in the consumer market and is likely to have a positive effect on security awareness. It is something that is less relevant for the enterprise market whereby the selection of products is done by professionals and subjected to intense scrutiny from departments responsible for cybersecurity. Open source versus free or proprietary software is another area of consideration and what should be included in the CRA. The model through which organisations capture value and create revenue for their offerings should not determine cybersecurity properties. Moreover, the existence of open-source software does not mean that open-source components are not included in proprietary software, nor is free or open-source software always “free of charge”. Often organisations commercialise open-source software through alternative offerings, e.g. services and equally often software that is made available as “free” is collecting large amounts of personal data that is then utilised for online behavioural advertising. All these aspects suggest that there is no obvious justification why any software, irrespectively of its licensing model should escape cybersecurity requirements. Conclusions We would like to thank again the Commission for giving us the opportunity to comment to this important initiative. We would invite the Commission to reflect on the interplay of existing and upcoming EU law with the CRA to ensure that any obligations created by CRA will be easily explainable, enforceable and do not create confusion, overlap and unnecessary burdens to economic operators of all sizes that are active in the software and hardware digital products market.  We believe a that a proposal that is B2C and IoT focused with a well-targeted scope that is principles and outcome based can have a positive impact in the EU cybersecurity eco-system. It is important to ensure that CRA does not create a rigid technical regime or a one-size-fits-all set of requirements. The technology market is extremely diverse and a proposal that takes into consideration this diversity can result in the EU demonstrating leadership in this space.

PREFACE  
The European Union has to regulate the cybersecurity  of products for the European Market. We see 
this as common understanding of politics and industry. The regulation needs to be drafted with great 
care because it will have a massive impact on the European Economic Area and its competitiveness.  
Digitalization changes nearly every sector. IT is part of a rapidly increasing number of products. And 
digital developments are progressing extremely fast. Moreover, IT is the basis of important innovative 
developments shaping our future  (like Industry 4.0, smart cit ies or green mobility). All this makes it 
necessary for the regulation to allow innovation and  a wide variety of different technical solutions 
when setting cybersecurity requirements.  
The New Legislative Framework (NLF) was designed to address exactly these needs: Under the NLF,  
essential requirements are set in a high-level and technology-neutral manner, resulting in a sustainable 
and stable regulation, while the evolution of the state of the art is reflected in harmonized standards  
developed by the relevant stakeholders incl. governmental authorities.  
On this basis, the position paper “EU-wide Cybersecurity Requirements” of the Federation of German 
Industries (BDI), German Institute for Standardization ( DIN) and German Commission for Electrical, 
Electronic & Information Technologies of DIN and VDE (DKE) published on February 1st 2021 calls for 
a horizontal regulation adhering to the NLF. The NLF in Decision 768/2008/EC stipulate so-called 
reference provisions, which have to be used when drafting EU product regulations. The essential 
requirements, typically the Annex I, are the core of such a regulation and need to be drafted 
individually for each regulation. With the following paper we want to contribute to the development 
of this centerpiece.    
Between August 2020 and May 2021 in an open working group initiated by the Presidial Committee 
FOCUS.ICT of DIN, over 30 experts in particular from the cybersecurity addressing working groups of 
BITKOM, ZVEI und VDMA  developed this contribution. The work of the group was characterized by 
humbleness concerning the complexity and conscientiousness considering the assumed expectations 
from the different parties.  
The paper is structured in three parts: 
A) PRELIMINARY REMARKS AND GUIDING PRINCIPLES 
which we see necessary to consider when developing a cybersecurity regulation and to 
understand the approach taken in part B) 
 
B) ESSENTIAL REQUIREMENTS 
which are the central proposal of this document: a draft for an Annex 1 in a horizontal 
cybersecurity regulation under the NLF  
 
C) ADDITIONAL CONSIDERATIONS 
which need to be considered in the NLF cybersecurity regulation, but, due to their character, 
would not fit well into the essential requirements section of that regulation 
 
This paper does not claim to be a closing position  or concluded consensus of all involved players in 
every detail. Rather, it shall help to find consensus in the European discussion . We believe that the 
contribution would be a consistent and robust starting point.

A) PRELIMINARY REMARKS AND GUIDING PRINCIPLES 
Essential requirements for a regulation under the NLF 
The requirements in section B) are formulated as essential requirements for a horizontal EU regulation 
on cybersecurity under the NLF. Thus, they stipulate the "resilience -objective" without any reference 
to certain technologies and it is left to harmonize d European standards to elaborate more detailed 
technical requirements  and security measures reflecting the state of the art.  The essential 
requirements will focus on the objective to be attained and therefore on purpose do  not provide a 
security engineering layout. This way the regulation does not have to be revised repeatedly as the state 
of the art evolves. The regulation remains technology neutral, is future proof and innovation friendly. 
With the essential requirements covering all key aspects of cybersecurity, the EU Commission can issue 
open standardization requests, which specify only strategic objectives based on the essential product 
requirements. Based on such open standardization request s, the European Standardisation 
Organisations (ESOs) should, in a timely manner,  develop the necessary standards which specify the 
details, e.g. security measures, to substantiate the essential requirements. A modular standardization 
system that consists of basic standards (phenomenon-related) as well as product and/or product group 
standards (product -related, refer to basic standards) supports the uniformity of the phenomenon -
related requirements. In order to foster international marketability as well as the availability of 
products and components from outside the EU, all harmonized EN standards (hENs) should largely be 
based on or align with international standards. 
Focus on resilience 
The product requirements are formulated with a focus on cyber -resilience. Resilience is the ability to 
withstand attacks and recover into a defined mode of operation after an attack. It consists of technical 
(e.g. in design) as well as organizational (e.g. in operations) measures.  
Adequate protection shall be provided for a defined and/or agreed time. A product must provide 
adequate resilience against cyber-attacks. Adequate resilience means that the measures implemented 
or foreseen correspond to the state of the art for the given product and its intended use as well as the 
resulting “t hreat environment”.  Then a product can be considered as compliant to  the product 
regulation even if its protection is overcome by an illegally acting attacker utilizing tremendous efforts 
or when new vulnerabilities are discovered.  
The ability to achieve a higher level of security, or the availab ility of other products that present a 
lower risk, is not a sufficient reason to consider a product to be insecure or that its resilience is 
insufficient to meet essential requirements.

Focus on manufacturers’ measures 
Cybersecurity cannot be achieved only by setting and verifying product requirements. They must be 
complemented by measures taken during  development and operation as well as  organizational 
measures like clear responsibilities. Therefore, the essential requi rements contain not only product 
requirements but also requirements with respect to measures (actions) taken by manufacturers. 
The manufacturers’ measures include measures in the manufacturing process to ensure compliance of 
the manufactured products with the essential requirements. However, since manufacturing is already 
sufficiently addressed in the NLF conformity assessment procedures ( e.g. internal production control 
in Annex II of NLF Decision No 768/2008/EC), there is no need for additional manufacturing provisions 
in the essential requirements.  
When prescribing the manufacturers’ measures, the objective is that the manufacturer really 
implement the relevant measures for a given product rather than merely prescribing that a process for 
the measures exist s. Consequently, we emphasize in section B) 1 that for the given product, the 
manufacturer documents the results of the measures  carried out. The requirement to document the 
outcome of the se measures in the technical documentation of a given product will also facilitate 
market surveillance of products. 
Same as the product requirements, the requirements for these measures are described on a high level 
rather than in detail. Any details, where necessary, could be elaborated in harmonized standards which 
are based on existing standards like the EN IEC 62443 “Industrial communication networks – Network 
and system security ” series of standards, the EN ISO/IEC 2700X “ Information Security Management 
Systems” family of standards or the upcoming standard for automotive ISO/SAE 21434. 
The measures which we address in the essential requirements are furthermore limited to those 
measures, that are to be taken by the manufacturer. We understand that cybersecurity can at last only 
be achieved if users and operators of products also act responsibly, e.g. by using products as intended 
and making use of updates and user information provided by manufacturers. However, for the sake of 
clarity of the regulation and for consistency in the NLF, operator obligations should not be regulated 
in a horizontal NLF regulation. Where necessary, such operator obligations could for sake of 
consistency be addressed on EU level in existing regulations focusing on op erators, like  the 
occupational health and safety regulations.

B) ESSENTIAL REQUIREMENTS (Annex I) 
1. General principles and measures 
(1) The manufacturer or his authorised representative must ensure that a security risk 
assessment is carried out in order to determine product requirements referred to in section 
2. which apply to the product. The requirements determined in this way are binding for the 
product. The requirement to provide instructions and information referred to in section 2.3 
applies in any case. The product must be designed and provided with security measures 
taking into account the results of the risk assessment.  
 
This comprises the following elements: 
a. Threat and risk analysis 
where the manufacturer  
i. determines the limits of the product, which include its intended use, its scope and 
its environment, the assets to be protected, over the whole lifecycle of the 
product (development, production, installation, operation, maintenance and 
decommissioning), 
ii. performs a threat modelling (e.g. attacker capabilities, local or remote attacker, 
exposure, potential value for attackers etc.), 
iii. assesses the identified risks, 
iv. determines based on above i.-iii. which of the product requirements referred to in 
section 2. apply to the product. 
 
b. Definition of specific security measures  
where the manufacturer, based on the threat and risk analysis, defines specific security 
measures according to the state of the art for the product and its environment in order 
to fulfill the applicable product requirements referred to in section 2. 
c. Security implementation 
where the manufacturer implements the specific security measures and ensures that no 
new risks are introduced through the implementation. 
d. Verification and validation of the specific security measures 
where the manufacturer verifies and validates that the secure implementation fulfills 
the specific security measures by using best practices like security guidelines, design 
review, unit testing, static code analysis, vulnerability scans and pen testing, etc. 
The results of the elements above (a. - d.) shall be documented. 
 
(2) The manufacturer shall observe the following order of priority when selecting security 
measures:

a. eliminating or minimizing risks by the way the product is designed, i.e., integrating

b. implementing the necessary security measures against risks that cannot be eliminated

(3) The security measures taken by the manufacturer to meet the requirements identified in

2. Product requirements 
2.1 Essential product requirements 
Products must be designed and manufactured in consideration of the risk and the state of the art such 
that, when used and maintained as intended in accordance with the instructions, they: 
(1) provide adequate resilience to anticipated cybersecurity threats (e.g. attacks affecting the 
integrity, availability and confidentiality) without significant impairment of the essential 
functions of the products; 
(2) do not significantly compromise or impair the cybersecurity of other connectable products; 
(3) do not pose a threat to networks or other communications infrastructure. 
 
2.2 Specific product requirements  
These product requirements can be broken down into more specific requirements . This means that 
products shall be designed and manufactured as such that: 
(1) unauthorized access is denied (e.g. through access control);  
(2) resilience against denial of service attacks is provided (e.g. through validation and recovery 
design);  
(3) confidentiality of data, commands and programs is protected (e.g. through encryption, data 
and communication security);  
(4) external interfaces are protected (e.g. through input validation and access control); 
(5) they are protected against physical attacks (e.g. through physical protection or hardening 
and hardware protection); 
(6) integrity of data, commands or programs is protected (e.g. through integrity protection);

2.3 Instructions and user information 
The product must be provided with instructions and information in an electronic or physical format 
which allow the relevant stakeholders (end users or manufacturers of products or solutions into which 
the product in question will be integrated ) a secure installation, operation and usage of the product. 
This includes information about: 
(1) the correct identification of the version of the product and the corresponding instruction 
and user information, 
(2) the intended use including the security environment foreseen by the manufacturer as well 
as the product’s essential functionalities,  
(3) the necessary measures during initial commissioning, secure configuration, 
(4) the installation of security-relevant updates, 
(5) the type of support offered by the manufacturer and how long it will be provided  
(6) the necessary product maintenance for secure operation,  
(7) the point of contact where information about cybersecurity-vulnerabilities of the product 
can be reported and received, 
(8) the secure decommissioning of the product, e.g. secure removal of user data and 
credentials

C) ADDITIONAL CONSIDERATIONS 
When developing the above draft for essential requirements we found aspects, which due to their 
character, would not fit into the essential requirements section of the NLF cybersecurity regulation but 
should be further discussed within the course of developing the regulation.  
These aspects are: 
- Interface to other EU (NLF) product legislation: This interface must be designed to ensure 
coherence, legal certainty, appropriate conformity assessment procedures and efficient 
implementation on manufacturer and enforcement side.  
Whenever the NLF cybersecurity regulation makes cybersecurity requirements in other 
product legislation obsolete (like the delegated acts under the RED), those cybersecurity 
requirements should be withdrawn.  
- Acceptable level of security:  Security is not safety.  Safety focusses on accident avoidance 
and security  mainly on crime prevention. Safety  primarily addresses the danger of the 
product itself for the user and the environment. Therefore it is a generic task for the 
manufacturer. Security risk instead originate from a 3rd person. It usually addresses an attack 
on the product , through which the product might be dangerous for the user or the 
environment. The practice shows that influences cannot be foreseen, due to the creativity of 
attackers or mathematicians finding efficient ways to overcome security measure s or do 
cryptanalysis. Taking this into account, security must be considered in a different way  than 
safety. It is obvious that it is less the task of the manufacturer than safety. Moreover, it is in 
the nature of this issue, that criminal energy can be higher than the state-of-the art 
prevention measures at any time.  
Nevertheless, and that is the common sense behind this paper , the manufacturing of 
connected products should anticipate typical environment al risks. For example, there are  
viruses in the physical world and viruses in the virtual/connected world. When designing this 
legislation it has to be considered, that the level of security, with which the manufacturer at 
least participates in “crime prevention”, has to be chose n wisely. There is never absolute 
security.   
- Successful attacks on compliant products does not lead to non -compliance: Even state-of-
the-art security measures cannot ensure 100% security. Therefore, a successful attack on  a 
product does not  necessarily imply non-compliance with the NLF cybersecurity regulation 
(see also “focus on resilience” described in part A) of this paper). 
- Security support is part of the manufacturer’s obligations: Cybersecurity is a moving target. 
Therefore, manufacturers have to provide security support for their products e.g. in form of 
updates. The essential requirements drafted above already set the obligation to inform about 
the type and period of this support. However, the actual obligation to provide support would 
better fit into the section “manufacturer obligations” which  is foreseen in reference 
provisions of the NLF Decision No 768/2008/EC.

In light of above considerations, we suggest three risk‐based  c lasses for the assignment of the NLF 
Conformity Assessment Procedures.  
 
  
Figure 2: Proposal for assignment of NLF Conformity Assessment Procedures to products under the CRA 
If a product fulfills the criteria of two classes, it is to be  classified according to the highest of the two 
classes. 
Class III – the top of the product pyramid 
Class III comprises internet‐connectable products that are intended for use in critical applications. Both 
criteria  (internet‐connectable  and  intended  for  use  in  critical   applications)  must  be  fulfilled  for 
classification as Class III. A product is considered “internet‐connectable” if it is capable to communicate 
using a protocol of the Internet Protocol suite. A critical app lication is understood as an application 
within a critical infrastructure which has substantial effects for the critical infrastructure. 
Since critical application by definition involves a higher security risk, we consider it justified to foresee 
a mandatory involvement of a 3rd party (Notified Bodies) in the conformity assessment. 
It should be noted that not every product used in a critical infrastructure is necessarily used in a critical 
application. For example, using a firewall to protect a nuclear power plant is likely a critical application, 
while the vending machine used in the same power plant is hardly a critical application. 
The  classification  as  “intended  for  use  in  critical  application s”  would  need  to  be  made  and 
communicated by the manufacturer. This will facilitate market surveillance and also help operators of 
critical applications or their system designers to select appropriate products for these applications.

Class II – the middle of the product pyramid 
Class II comprises products intended for direct connection to t he internet. This class in general has a 
higher exposure to cyber threats than products of Class I, but  the risk associated to these products is 
significantly lower than for Class III.  
For the products intended for di rect connection to the internet , the higher exposure to cyber threats 
is caused by their direct connection to the internet. A typical  example for this group would be a DSL 
router which is used in the households of many consumers. It sh ould be noted that for this class the 
reasonably foreseeable use is relevant besides the intended use. If a manufacturer labels a typical DSL 
router for home use as “not for connection to the internet” thi s would be a false declaration and the 
DSL router would still be classified as Class II. Another example for Class II would be a smartphone. The 
smartphone is intended for direct connection to the internet vi a public mobile networks, e.g. for free 
surfing on the internet.

C) ADDITIONAL CONSIDERATIONS 
When drafting the above proposal, we found the following aspects need to be considered when 
adopting NLF Conformity Assessment Procedures and when further developing and implementing 
the Cyber Resilience Act: 
 
‐ We suggest the three risk‐based classes, as a starting point. Their distinction is not clear cut 
yet, and needs further clarification. Especially the distinction between „direct and indirect 
connection“ (based on the RED Delegated Act) needs further consideration (e.g. what exactly 
constitutes a direct or an indirect connection to the internet, definition of “internet”). 
‐ Harmonized standards are mentioned in the above description of Class II. It should be noted 
that such harmonized standards are important not only for Class II but for all classes. They 
are important because a) they concretize the essential requirements, which intentionally do 
not specify all details, b) they can be adapted relatively quickly when the state of the art 
evolves, c) the manufacturers applying the harmonized standards enjoy the presumption of 
conformity with the essential requirements, and d) they set a benchmark in case harmonized 
standards are not applied. In order to ensure international compatibility beyond the EU, 
these harmonized standards for the CRA should, whenever possible, be based on already 
existing cybersecurity standards such as the EN IEC 62443 “Industrial communication 
networks – Network and system security” series of standards. Such international 
compatibility is a key for European manufacturers to efficiently develop products for the 
international markets.

ZVEI - Electro and Digital Industry Association promotes the industry’s joint economic, technological and environmental policy interests on a national, 
European and global level. The sector has round about 873,000 e mployees in Germany plus 790,000 employees all over the world. In 2020 the turnover 
was Euro 180 billion. The electrical and electronics industry is the most innovative industry sector in Germany. One-third of the industries sales are based 
on new products. Every third innovation in Germany´s manufactur ing sector stems from solutions of this sector. More than 20 pe rcent of all industrial 
R+D spending comes from this industry. Every year, the industry  spends 20 billion euros on R+D, more than 6 billion euros on i nvestments and two 
billion euros on training and further education.

FOCUS.ICT - Presidial Committee of DIN, the Strategy Committee on Standardization in Information and Communication Technology, is one of the five 
Presidial Committees of the German Institute for Standardizatio n, and supports the Presidial Board in its various activities. High-profile experts from 
business, politics, research and society analyze relevant ICT d evelopments and give advice on s tandardization needs and redund ances. They initiate 
necessary measures such as coordinating and corrective actions - at national level as well as via the relevant partners CEN/CE NELEC/ETSI, ISO/IEC/ITU 
and industry consortia at European and international level. FOC US.ICT focuses on complex projects with a cross-body character and pursues the goal of 
using the potential of standardization in ICT even more effectively for the development of Germany as a business location. The Chairs of the Committee 
are Prof. Dr. Dieter Wegener (SIEMENS) and Dr. Jochen Friedrich (IBM).

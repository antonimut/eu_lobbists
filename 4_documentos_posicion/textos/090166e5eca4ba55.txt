 
 
 
 
Towards a stronger and more resilient Union  
Huawei’s response to the Call for Evidence on the EU Cyber Resilience Act 
 
 
 
 
 
 
May 2022 
 
 
Ref. Ares(2022)3933404 - 25/05/2022
Why cyber resilience is key to digital transformation  
 
Digital transformation is not just a buzzword –there has been significant growth of the Internet of 
Things (IoT) and other connected devices. As per Huawei’s Global Industry Vision (GIV) by 
2025, 100 billion digital connections will be achieved globally, covering 77 percent of the 
population; while 85 percent of enterprise applications will be deployed on the cloud; and smart 
homes will comprise 12 % of all households, with a billion-dollar plus market. Most of this 
transformation will happen in Europe, with citizens able to access much faster and more 
innovative technology at a lower price and carbon footprint. According to ETNO’s State of Digital 
Communications 2022, active IoT connections are expected to reach 352mn in 2023, up from 
180mn in 2020 in Europe. It is clear that Europe is well poised to benefit, as per Huawei’s latest 
Global Connectivity Index, 13 out of the top 20 “front-runner” countries are already in Europe. 
 
With such a wide range of digital products and services about to be put on the European 
market, cyber resilience becomes an essential priority, both for hardware and software 
providers, but also for regulators and citizens. A large number of new technologies, systems, 
and applications will be used in industries such as transportation, energy, finance, 
manufacturing, digital government, healthcare, and education. As these sectors undergo digital 
transformation, these new technologies, systems, and applications impose higher requirements 
on the infrastructure of these industries and bring more challenges. In the telecommunication 
sector, the shifts to 5G and to a virtualised, software-defined infrastructure means that the 
networks and end-to-end services of tomorrow will be delivered by an ecosystem of operators, 
vendors and providers, where important functions and control points will move closer to the end-
user and will be outsourced from telecom providers to other actors in the value chain. A certain 
sense of urgency is reflected in the European Union’s focus on developing harmonized 
cybersecurity standards – first with the Cybersecurity strategy in 2020, then with the review of 
the NIS directive, and now with the Cyber Resilience Act proposal (henceforth, CRA). 
 
While it is clear why cyber resilience is critical for the digital transformation, it is easy for a sense 
of urgency to drive regulatory overlap on cybersecurity, with requirements being introduced in a 
piecemeal fashion, resulting in an ever growing number of existing or proposed legal acts that 
seek to regulate products, organisations, or simply update requirements in specific sectors. 
Cybersecurity requirements should not be a labyrinth or puzzle to decode for companies, but 
rather serve as a compass for industry – it should aim to simplify, direct and guide while raising 
the horizontal level of harmonized cybersecurity standards across verticals and sectors. 
 
Decoding the EU’s approach to cybersecurity legislation 
 
There have been multiple relevant regulations in the digital domain have been proposed or 
already adopted, and so rather than leaving it up to industry to decode, the European 
Commission should ensure an increased coherence between these. To improve legal clarity 
and business certainty, the CRA should clarify the relationship with the European Cybersecurity 
Act (CSA), Radio Equipment Directive (RED), General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), 
General Product Safety Directive/ proposed regulation (GPSD/R), Machinery Directive/ 
proposed Regulation, Medical Device Regulation (MDR), eIDAS Regulation (EU Digital ID), 
Sales of Goods Regulations (Art. 7(3)), proposed Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA), 
proposed Network and Information Security 2 (NIS2) directive, as well as the proposed 
Regulation establishing the European Cybersecurity Industrial, Technology and Research 
Competence Centre and the Network of National Coordination Centers.  
 
Therefore, in order to achieve the objective of ensuring a consistent high level of security for 
digital products and ancillary services and given the horizontal scope of the CRA, it will be of 
key importance to define the context in which the CRA will operate, especially with regard to 
vertical legislation like the RED, or potentially mandatory certification of IoT or critical 
infrastructure under NIS2. To resolve the issue of overlapping requirements, it is critical to 
improve cyber resilience across the common market and to avoid creating redundancies in 
reporting, and instead to acknowledge sectoral needs. 
 
One example is the delegated act (2022/30) under the Radio Equipment Directive (RED) with 
requirements relating to the protection of data and network resources, and against fraud for 
wireless product. Considering that under the RED Delegated Act (henceforth, RED DA), where 
the scope is limited to certain Radio Equipment, a one-size-fits-all approach was considered 
difficult to achieve by European Standards Organisations (ESOs). This seems even more 
ambitious under the CRA.  
 
The CRA aims to cover all ICT products, regardless of whether and how they may be 
connected. IoT products are only a subset of ICT products, yet though IoT equipment all share 
certain common characteristics, they include a wide diversity of use cases with very different 
implications for appropriately estimating their risks. 
 
Beyond covering all tangible ICT products, the CRA aims to cover stand-alone software as well 
as ancillary services. It is unclear to Huawei that a common legislative tool can properly address 
these different categories of products, and in any case, they differ sufficiently to deserve being 
approached independently at least in an initial assessment phase. 
 
Therefore we recommend that the CRA scope be limited to connected products while not 
addressing software and ancillary services. This is one way to navigate the labyrinth – through a 
“strategic compass” of complementary, and not overlapping regulation. Moreover, we would 
recommend to set out general legal requirements that are detailed at technical level through 
harmonised standards – to leave enough room for the required customization for distinct 
products and scenarios, while maintaining flexibility on how to ensure cybersecurity. 
 
While the RED DA only addresses early stages of a product lifecycle, the CRA aims to cover all 
phases. It has to be emphasized that each phase of a product’s lifecycle, corresponds to 
different expected behavior and is subject to different risks. Each phase therefore deserves a 
separate and standalone risk assessment, resulting in a specific (and often differing) set of 
security requirements compared to other phases. In addition, the legislation should not ignore 
the fact that the proper definition of lifecycle phases is use case dependent, nor assume that 
product manufacturer bears primary liability for all of them.  
 
For example, most connected scenarios require an initial onboarding, provisioning or 
configuration phase before an endpoint can connect to a serving infrastructure (which may need 
to be repeated in case of transfer of ownership or change of economic operators), and most 
industrial use cases require the possibility to switch back and forth between operational and 
maintenance phases. This is why we recommend that at least in an initial phase, the CRA 
approach should limit its ambition to addressing only simple B2C scenarios, where simplifying 
assumptions can be made, before assessing whether and how it could be generalized to 
sophisticated B2B ecosystems where many stakeholders are typically involved.   
 
This is also helpful given that harmonised standards need to be developed to support the new 
legislation, and a realistic estimation of the number of standards needed should drive the 
issuance of multiple standardization requests, each associated with a realistic time frame.  
 
When considering multiple legislations, it is wise to avoid duplication of efforts in procedure and 
to reuse documentation and evidence available in the context of related legislations. Therefore, 
when introducing general cybersecurity requirements within the context of the New Legislative 
Framework (NLF), it will make sense to acknowledge cybersecurity certification under the CSA 
as one (but not the only) way to prove conformity. 
 
Policy recommendations on the Cyber Resilience Act 
 
1. Scope of the CRA 
The aim of the CRA is to define streamlined cybersecurity requirements covering a wide range 
of digital products and their ancillary services. This would include tangible digital products 
(wireless and wired) and non-embedded software. 
 
In order to quickly increase the cybersecurity level of the digital products within the European 
market the CRA has to define a baseline of horizontal technical security requirements broadly 
applicable to all the digital products and it has to focus mainly on Hardware and the Embedded 
Software, where the lifecycle is directly controlled. 
 
For standalone software products, it may be difficult to define common security requirements 
due to a lot of diversity between existing software products. Furthermore, given the B2C focus 
of standalone software, and considering the relative cost for smaller app developers, it may 
increase the commercial barriers to entry for SMEs and, thus, it may not seem proportionate to 
implement cybersecurity requirements for such software products.  
 
Huawei believes ancillary services are not well defined in the CRA Call for Evidence and would 
like to have more details and examples before providing feedback. For example, in electricity 
systems, ancillary services are commonly referred to as auxiliary functions from grid operators 
with regards to maintaining the resilience and reliability of the system, which is contradictory to 
the holistic and horizontal definition of the initial scope for CRA. 
In short, on scope, we recommend that the CRA should only:  
i. focus on connected products and respectively embedded software; and 
ii. consider standalone software (i.e. non-embedded software) and ancillary services only 
at a later stage or at least define the scope more narrowly with detailed examples based 
on relevant concrete risks. 
 
2. Overlap with other cybersecurity related legislation 
 
As written in the CRA call for evidence, the current EU framework applicable to digital products 
includes several pieces of legislation covering cybersecurity aspects of digital products. For this 
reason the CRA has to: 
i. Provide general cybersecurity requirements within the NLF framework to be detailed at 
technical level by harmonized standards. 
ii. These technical requirements under the CRA should avoid overlaps or conflicts with the 
existing legislation that include cybersecurity requirements for the digital products, or 
data protection regulation (GDPR, specifically for articles 24, 28 and 32). In particular 
Huawei welcomes the intention to consider CRA a complement of the RED Delegated 
Act 2022/30 (RED DA) to cover the digital products not in the scope of the RED DA. If 
other aspects such as lifecycle, design and vulnerability management are to be included 
for all the digital products, the CRA will need to rely on more robust legislative 
instruments, and complement the above mentioned RED DA to avoid an overlap of 
regulations applying to Radio Equipment. 
iii. Define mechanisms to reuse digital product's documentation such as, for example, 
assessment results from other legislation such as but not limited to RED and CSA. 
iv. Generate coherence between legislation addressing only the intended use and 
legislation addressing the challenge related to cybersecurity also taking non-intended 
use into account. This is because adversarial attacks are usually not focused on the 
intended use of digital products alone. 
 
3. Impact of addressing the entire product lifecycle 
 
Cybersecurity is a chain that is never stronger than its weakest link, therefore we welcome the 
intention of the CRA to address the entire lifecycle of products under scope. Proper definition 
and management of the phases of a product’s lifecycle is a critical step to enable secure 
systems, but requires proper communication and governance across all stakeholders in an 
ecosystem, as it needs to take into account their operating constraints and translate them into 
appropriate requirements. As such, there is an opportunity for the CRA to assist the industry in 
better understanding and addressing this foundation of product security. However, the impact of 
this endeavor on the legislation should be clearly recognized and acknowledged from the start, 
rather than being implicit or hidden behind the other dimensions of the legislation. 
 
Even in basic B2C scenarios where a product is manufactured and sold directly to a final user, 
the product lifecycle can be more complex than just “Design / Manufacturing / Distribution / 
Operation / End of life”. Indeed, as soon as an ICT product requires connection to a serving 
network to properly operate, an initial phase of onboarding prior to operation is required to 
establish security credentials and to configure the product to operate safely. This is a critical 
phase in terms of vulnerability, which in itself requires proper risk assessment, resulting in 
specific security requirements compared to normal operation. A clear establishment of the 
respective responsibilities between the manufacturer, the network operator and the end user is 
needed to address their potential liabilities in case a security credential is leaked or spoofed 
during this phase. A risk assessment performed in isolation by a manufacturer prior to market 
placement may fail to capture requirements that depend on the environment and policies 
specific to the operator or user of the serving network.  
 
Also, transfer of ownership deserves to be considered, which may require switching back from 
the operating phase to a new onboarding phase. This illustrates that proper management of a 
device lifecycle requires not only a clear definition of its expected behavior expected at each 
phase of its life, but also of the triggers that control its transition between different phases. 
 
Industrial scenarios involving additional economic operators such as integrators, installers, 
multiple tenants in customer premises, and maintenance operators may result in more complex 
lifecycle management, involving multiple steps of security provisioning to address different 
levels of stakeholders, as well as different types of maintenance phases. In conclusion, it is of 
utmost importance that the European Commission recognizes the existence of the following 
dimensions that the CRA will need to address: 
i. Definition and management of the lifecycle phases of a product is a fundamental 
step for security, which needs proper involvement of all economic operators and will 
depend on specific ecosystems. The legislation should refrain to assume or mandate 
a “one size fits all” lifecycle. 
ii. Each step of a product lifecycle corresponds to different operating conditions, hence 
each deserves their own governance and definition of roles and responsibility. This 
cannot be predicted independently from considerations on the ecosystem within 
which a product will be operating. 
iii. Phases of a product lifecycle after purchase by a consumer may be unknown to 
manufacturers, hence liability for such phases should not solely rest with them.  
iv. The CRA should consider compliance with technical requirements at all stages of the 
product life cycle. In addition, technical conformity assessment methodology of the 
product life cycle should be considered. 
 
4. Risk based approach 
 
We recommend for the CRA to provide general cybersecurity requirements within the NLF 
framework to be detailed at technical level by harmonized standards. The scope and technical 
requirements should then reflect the concrete cybersecurity risks of respective products and 
embedded software in the specific environment where they are deployed so that the addressees 
are required to take such measures that are “appropriate to the risk”. In addition, at this 
legislative stage, the scope and technical requirements of the CRA should also be determined 
based on the results of such an assessment of risks. 
 
To implement the risk based approach, we recommend that in general, cybersecurity 
requirements properly reflect the risk profile of the digital products. A clear, transparent, 
inclusive, and standards-driven process should identify the risk profile of digital products. In the 
context of the CRA, a restricted approach to assessing this risk is possible assuming simple 
scenarios where mainly a manufacturer and a user or customer is involved. However, in 
complex B2B scenarios where entire ecosystems of actors are involved, proper assessment of 
risk requires involvement of all economic operators and should not rely solely on manufacturer’s 
assumptions. In addition, the CRA should create a suitable and traceable way to derive security 
requirements for each operator. 
 
5. Conformity with the New Legislative Framework 
 
The aim of the CRA is to improve the internal market's functioning and would complement the 
RED DA. This means the CRA has to reflect the objectives of conformity with the NLF, 
specifically, by being primarily based on NLF legislation (768/2008/EC) that allows the 
presumption of conformity and the possibility for the vendor to choose the self-assessment 
procedure. This also means that the CRA should ensure that all the options defined under the 
NLF as regards to conformity assessment should be available for all digital products, regardless 
of their risk profile. Finally, the CRA should preserve the voluntary nature of third party 
conformity assessment for vendors, should they choose to do this. 
 
It is also important is to link the CRA with the European Commission’s Better Regulation 
Toolbox (November 2021). This solid repository of best practices must be rigorously applied, 
and assessment criteria and methodologies described here, including “co-regulatory” 
approaches for higher risk products should be explored. The role of industry to define 
harmonized standards (typical of NLF regulation) cannot be overstated here. It is our hope that 
harmonized standards are developed through an inclusive and proportionate process that 
involves industry stakeholders and through European Standardization Organisations (ESOs). 
 
6. Liability 
 
In most industrial scenarios, good security governance requires the involvement of all suppliers 
in the value chain (including third parties contracted by the final customer, e.g. integrators or 
installers) through an overall Risk Assessment specific to the deployment, so that a restrictive 
legislation putting responsibility only on manufacturers/developers would be counterproductive 
to the overall ecosystem. Some lifecycle stages are not under the control of manufacturers, so 
applying very strong constraints to manufacturers/developers may not bring any security 
improvement in the end if other links in the value chain are left unaddressed by the legislation. 
 
7. Vulnerability Management and Disclosure 
 
Vulnerability management is a cornerstone of security throughout the product lifecycle, and 
depends both on technical capabilities of products, such as secure updates and security 
controls, as well as development and operational processes of vendors, their suppliers, and 
other actors of the value chain. It makes a difference for vulnerability management if products 
are already deployed in a system context to be operated, or yet on the shelf. Here again, 
cognizance of deployment scenarios and coherence of the CRA with the existing EU legislation 
will be paramount. We highlight in particular that, while vendors provide for vulnerability fixes in 
their products, the responsibilities to distribute fixes, identify vulnerable assets, apply fixes or 
otherwise mitigate vulnerable states, usually lie with other actors in the value chain and are 
highly dependent on the deployment scenario. For example, off-the-shelf consumer products 
cannot be placed under the same regime than products that are deployed by system integrators 
or operated by service providers. 
 
Vulnerability disclosure is an important component of vulnerability management as it enables 
vendors to become aware of new vulnerabilities affecting their products. Vendors should remain 
the primary receivers and handlers of vulnerability reports, and it should be ensured that 
vendors can receive vulnerability reports in a timely fashion. In this regard, some aspects of 
coordinated vulnerability disclosure, such as the availability of a disclosure policy along with 
contact information, could be promoted as part of the product legislation. In addition, an overall 
legislative approach to cybersecurity should not hamper legitimate vulnerability research, but 
rather encourage it, as cooperation between vendors and security researchers in the academic 
and ethical hacking communities is essential. We recognize that there might be cases where 
vulnerabilities are not directly reported to vendors. For such cases, diligence to follow-up and 
hand the report over to the vendor should be the focus. 
 
Depending on the position of a vulnerability in the affected technology stack and supply chain, 
coordinated vulnerability disclosure may involve a large number of vendors and other 
stakeholders. The responsibility of each stakeholder (vendor, customer, member state, etc.) 
should be clarified in the process of vulnerability disclosure and vulnerability management as in 
some particular cases mostly in B2B (where essential and important services associated to 
critical infrastructures), this process has a different impact than in B2C contexts. Further, this 
can significantly increase the time necessary to develop a fix, test it, and ensure distribution, as 
illustrated many times with vulnerabilities affecting hardware or core software libraries. For this 
reason, we strongly advise against setting hard deadlines for the resolution of vulnerabilities in 
the product legislation, as these may put market actors in unresolvable situations and be the 
cause of mistakes with catastrophic consequences on the security of end-users. Rather, an 
overall legislative approach should facilitate efficient multi-vendor coordination and should not 
limit innovation on the vulnerability handling methods nor on the techniques used in vulnerability 
research and vulnerability discovery. 
 
8. Impact on innovation 
 
Cybersecurity requirements should serve as a strategic compass for industry and simplify, direct 
and guide companies while providing for a higher level of harmonized cybersecurity standards 
across verticals and sectors. Facilitating interoperability and integration of security in different 
products can encourage innovation as long as the regulation focuses on the objectives to 
achieve, the “what” rather than the “how”. 
 
This is important also because increasingly more complex and disruptive technologies (AI, edge 
computing, etc.) are becoming more essential in the digital transformation. Requirements under 
CRA should facilitate the use of these technologies, rather than discouraging vendors and 
smaller developers. Further, requirements should not need lead to delays or encourage 
discrimination of vendors based on politicized requirements. In sum, the CRA should encourage 
innovation, not create an unwitting “innovation penalty”.  
 
Harmonised standards should be used as the means to allow manufacturers to prove the 
conformity of their digital products. They will be key to reduce the cost of conformity and 
improve legal and business certainty. At the same time, ESOs should define cybersecurity 
requirements independently of technical implementation in order to avoid the creation of barriers 
for innovation, especially in the case of software. 
 
9. Non-discrimination 
 
Huawei is encouraged with the European Commission’s commitment to create a level playing 
field for vendors and providers of digital products, regardless of where they are from. It should 
be emphasized that the CRA should focus on technical requirements and ensure compliance 
with WTO agreements and principles of good governance and EU law. 
 
Given the new provisions to be introduced on conformity assessment, on the notification of 
conformity assessment bodies, and on market surveillance, it should be guaranteed that these 
assessments and surveillance aspects are done in an independent and transparent way, thus 
giving the possibility for the vendor to properly assess, refute, respond and acknowledge the 
final decision from the assessment body. 
 
The CRA should also ensure cybersecurity requirements and the conformity assessment 
procedure shall be the same for all similar digital products regardless of the size of the vendor. 
The security level of the digital products placed in the European market shall not be jeopardized 
by different treatments depending on vendor characteristics not related to cybersecurity, and the 
“risk based approach” should continue to uphold technology neutrality and non-discrimination.  
 
10. Response to policy options 
 
Regarding the proposed policy options in the Call for Evidence, Huawei believes that: 
i. Option 4 is the best one to start with. It would be good for the European Commission to 
first focus on hardware digital products and their embedded software, as this will already 
improve the security level dramatically in the European market, while also providing 
room to define achievable milestones in the meantime. 
ii. Option 5 is more comprehensive but it may be a risk to handle hardware and software 
digital products at the same time. This is because of myriad differences in types of 
software, and the complexity and relatively lower risk profile of the bulk of software. 
CVSS scores are going down, so there is time to do this in the future. 
iii. Option 3 is not suitable to fulfill the ambitious scope of the CRA. The amendment of 
existing legislation will introduce fragmentation in the market and also increase the cost 
and complexity on the vendor side due to the different numbers of legislations to be 
considered for the product assessment. 
iv. Option 2 includes voluntary measures that vendors may choose, and our focus on 
cybersecurity means we support these where possible. Soft-law initiatives should focus 
on the creation of harmonized standards, and comply with good governance guidelines 
while avoiding unjustified politicization.

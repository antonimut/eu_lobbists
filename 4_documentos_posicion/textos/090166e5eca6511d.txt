Introduction 
 
SMEunited explicitly supports the proposal's underlying objective of a sustainable circular 
economy. However, when achieving this goal, various factors must be considered. 
Amendments on the Unfair Commercial Practices 
Directive 
 
We welcome the proposal to amend Directive 2005/29/EC (“Unfair Commercial Practices 
Directive”) as the right approach to protect  against unfair commercial practices related to 
greenwashing and misleading environmental claims in general. The proposed changes to the 
Unfair Commercial Practices Directive are suitable for strengthening both consumer protection 
and the competitiveness of the sector in this area. 
The amendments to the Directive are appropriate as they ensure that dishonest operators do 
not gain a competitive advantage through greenwashing and misleading environmental 
claims. This will also increase consumer confidence for  environmental claims in general. 
Consumers could make sustainable purchasing decisions based on more reliable information. 
As a result, those who have invested in environmentally friendly business models will gain more 
benefit from their investments. 
However, the timing of the proposal is premature. This concerns in particular the fact that this 
proposal is intended to create information obligations and sanctions due to legal requirements 
in other matters (e.g. , the reform of the Ecodesign Directive 2009/1 25/EC and the repair 
indicator). As a consequence of this early stage, it is not clear in many cases how new terms 
are to be understood, because their context is missing. The proposal will create a level of legal 
uncertainty that significantly exceeds the usual level of legal uncertainty associated with any 
new regulation. This would be negative for all market participants concerned. For the subjected 
entrepreneurs, this would also be additionally associated with - for them - unforeseeable 
sanctions.

In addition  several terms are unclear.  For instance, the terms "environmental and social 
impacts". The examples given in recital 3 "working conditions, charity contributions or animal 
welfare " are no substitute for this. In the absence of a more specific definition of the "impacts" 
covered by this definition, it becomes almost impossible for entrepreneurs to assess in advance 
whether relevant statements meet the requirements of the provision. This circumstance is also 
made clear by recital 3, which states that the business' practices - with regard to, among other 
things, the "environmental and social impacts (...) may be considered misleading on the basis 
of a case -by-case assessment " - without clarifying which criteria are to be used for this 
assessment. Therefore, concretisation is necessary on this point as well.  
It is regrettable that t he requirements of this directive do not  take into account that most 
entrepreneurs in the EU  run micro and small enterprises . Their possibilit ies to deal with 
environmental and social impacts of their activities are limited. Certification and monitoring 
systems are too costly and not affordable for m ost micro and small enterprises. The 
requirements of this directive must therefore be designed in  such a way that the activities of 
micro and small enterprises are not constantly threatened with constituting an unfair business 
practice. 
The requirement of verifying environmental claims is understandable. However, the 
requirements for the verification of environmental claims should be defined more precisely so 
that it is clear to companies when the verification is sufficient. The methods of verifying 
environmental claims should be such that they can also be used by SMEs without too much of 
additional administrative burdens. This presupposes also that the cost of verification methods 
remains reasonable. However, the verification of environmental declarations cannot be carried 
out by parties other than the producer (art.2,  point 4 - directive 2019/7711, 20.05.2019).  
Furthermore, the relationship between the different terms of the environmental claims should 
as well be clarified as their relationship to the sustainability label. In particular, the distinction 
between a "general environmental claim" and a sustainability label in the form of a "voluntary 
private trust mark" is essential.  
In the proposal a distinction is made between "certification scheme" (i.a. art 2(s)) and 
"independent monitoring system " ( art 6(2)(d)). The difference  should be clarified and the 
essential criteria for both should be specified which they have to fulfil. 
Concerning the amendment of art 6 paragraph 2 (e) : Advertising with self-evident facts is by 
no means new and has so far been well delt with. From our point of view the proposed ban is 
unnecessary excessive. Although something is considered ‘common practice’ in the market, it 
could still be beneficial to inform the consumer, as not every consumer knows the common 
practice. Only its labelling as a unique selling proposition is misleading.

Concerning Annex I 23d: The criterion " negatively impact" seems to be  too broad. Even the 
subsequent need for updates of other applications could be considered a "negative" impact.  
Therefore, the unrestricted term "negative" should be replaced by e.g., "to a considerable extent 
negative”. 
Concerning Annex I 23f: The indication of an actual shelf life of a concrete product would be a 
prediction which is per se impossible. Apart from imponderables such as storage, weather, 
maintenance intensity, product care and type of use, such a statement can only refer to an 
average shelf life for a product that is mass produced . This point should therefore refer to the 
average durability of the product. 
Concerning Annex I 23g: If a product is presented as repairable although this is not the case, 
this is indisputable a misleading act and thus already an unfair business practice. Since the 
offence is based on the "presentation of goods", an examination of the individual case is 
necessary in any case. In addition to purely technical asp ects, economic aspects must also be 
examined (e.g. , the relationship between the current value of the goods and the costs of 
possible repair). Thus, this new provision does not change the legal situation at all. 
Concerning Annex I  23h: Even if the replacem ent of operating materials is not absolutely 
necessary for technical reasons, an earlier replacement may be useful for other reasons (e.g. to 
achieve a longer shelf life of the product). If, due to these facts, warnings were only given at the 
last possible moment, this could - depending on the type of product - have negative effects on 
the consumer, e.g., if the warning light of a car to change the engine oil only lights up at the last 
possible moment. Since several aspects have to be considered, this point  is not suitable for a 
per se ban but should be left to a comprehensive examination by courts (and authorities). 
Amendments on the Consumer Rights Directive 
 
On the other hand, the European Commission's proposal to amend Directive 2011/83/EU  
(“Consumer Rights Directive”) misses the relevant points. It is stated that the Consumer Rights 
Directive does not currently provide sufficient incentives for producers to provide consumers 
with commercial guarantees and information on the repairability of goods. To address this, the 
European Commission proposes to not oblige the manufacturers themselves, but the sellers to 
pass on relevant manufacturer information to consum ers. This passing on of manufacturer -
related information obligations on commercial guarantees and repair information to companies 
that conclude consumer sales contracts will lead to an unnecessary and disproportionate 
burden on small and medium -sized enterprises. In fact, SMEs which conclude such contracts 
will be burdened with new information obligations even though the current consumer law with 
its regulatory complexity and its numerous information obligations already represents a major 
challenge, especially for SMEs.

The amount of pre-contractual information provided to consumers is already considerable. The 
proposed changes would further increase the risk of information overload for consumers. It is 
therefore necessary to critically assess whether the new information obligations would produce 
added value to the consumer when their making a purchase decision. The constant increase in 
information obligations can lead to situation where consumers will no longer be able to 
distinguish key information from th e information provided to them. Instead of increasing the 
information obligations, it should be considered whether the information obligations can be 
reduced. 
The European Commission should rather focus on the actual needs of consumers and introduce 
further simplifications of consumer law to make it more efficient and practicable. What is really 
needed is that EU consumer law becomes more user -friendly, more legally secure, and 
appropriate. A higher level of consumer protection regarding the provision of co mmercial 
guarantees and repair information could also be achieved by means of a mandatory and easily 
accessible provision of information by the manufacturers themselves. 
The consumer should be informed of the existence of a commercial guarantee if the commercial 
guarantee is valid for more than two years. Disclosing this information to the consumer would 
be misleading in those Member States,  e.g. Finland, the Netherlands, Spain…, where the legal 
guarantee period is longer than two years.  
In addition, according to the proposal, in case of energy-using goods, the consumer should also 
be informed that the manufacturer has not provided any information on the existence of a 
commercial guarantee of more than two years. In particular, the approach of having to provide 
such negative information should definitely be dropped.  
It is a fact that also a e.g. one-year durability guarantee of the manufacturer is positive for the 
consumer, especially because he gets an additional liable party besides the seller.  The 
consumer would be informed about this  one-year-guarantee in any case, but in the future he 
would also have to receive the following "negative information" for the same product at the 
same time: " The manufacturer has not provided any information about the existe nce of a 
commercial guarantee of more than two years .” This will definitely not lead to the 
empowerment of consumers for sustainable decisions, but rather inevitably to  their complete 
confusion. 
Regardless of the fact that negative information is generally not appropriate, we want to point 
out that the definition of energy -using goods would mean that even small and very small 
devices (e.g. battery -powered flashlights) would have to be provided wit h such negative 
information. This would certainly be perceived by c itizens as pure overregulation by the EU 
legislator. 
Moreover, it should be considered that the information requirements on commercial guarantees 
and on reparability will have to be based on information that the retailer receives from a third 
party (manufacturer) and he passes them on.  However, if th is information is not correct, the

seller would infringe the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive. He could be sued for cease and 
desist, because it is not relevant for this that he is not at fault.  This is not appropriate. 
Furthermore, the obligation to pass on information from third parties (the manufacturer) gives 
rise to the following problem: according to the current Art 6 (5) CRD, all inf ormation provided 
forms an integral part of the contract with the dealer.  Thus, it is absolutely not a proper result 
that the seller would ultimately be held liable for the content of third party information by the 
obligation to pass it on. 
Therefore, we do not support the proposed changes to the information obligations.

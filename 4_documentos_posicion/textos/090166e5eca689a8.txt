Introduction 
 
Digital products and ancillary services create significant opportunities for EU economies and societies, along with new challenges that 
need to be addressed. In a connected environment, a cybersecurity incident in one product can affect an entire organisation o r a whole 
supply-chain. It can lead to severe disruption of economic and social activities or even become life threatening. The lack of appropriate 
security in digital products and services constitutes one of the main avenues for successful attacks. 
In her State of the Union 2021 address, President von der Leyen underlined that the EU should not merely settle to ad dress the cyber 
threats, but also strive to become a leader in cybersecurity. This could be achieved through legislation on horizontal requirements under 
a new European Cyber Resilience Act, included in the Commission Work Programme for 2022 under the headline ambition “A Europe 
Fit for the Digital Age”. This comes against the background of a growing number of high -profile cyberattacks with a global footprint, 
the annual cost of cybercrime to the global economy in 2020 was estimated to be EUR  5.5 trillion, double that of 2015 1. This is also 
partly a result of suboptimal cybersecurity measures for digital products and ancillary services. The new European Cyber Resilience Act 
will also contribute to the EU’s continuous effort for an effective and genuine Security Union in the digital era. 
This public consultation aims at informing the Commission’s upcoming Cyber Resilience Act init iative. Your answers will help the 
Commission analyse cybersecurity-related problems associated with the digital products markets, explore possible ways forward and 
assess the impact of different types of interventions. 
 
Please note that the translations of this consultation in the other EU languages will follow. This consultation will remain open until 25 
May 2022 
 
Definitions 
For the purposes of this public consultation, the notion of digital product covers both hardware and software products. A hardware 
product is defined as any device or group of interconnected or related devices, one or more of which, pursuant to a program, perform 
automatic processing of digital data. A software product is defined as an intangible good that processes digital data, stored, retrieved 
or transmitted by a hardware device, by executing encoded instructions. Software products include for example operating systems, user

How to respond to the questionnaire  
 
The questionnaire and all its questions are open to everyone, from ordinary consumers to cybersecurity experts and other potentially 
affected stakeholders. In addition, to facilitate responding to the questionnaire, the questions have been grouped into different 
categories requiring different types of expertise: 
 
Section 1 contains questions on the state of cybersecurity of digital products and users’ ability to choose secure products and use them 
in a secure manner. 
Section 2 explores various options to improve the cybersecurity of digital products. This includes also questions on the types of 
products to be covered by an intervention, on other relevant legislation, on security requirements, on risk, as well as ways to assess 
the conformity of vendors. 
Section 3 focuses on the EU added value and the estimated impacts of potential measures on stakeholders. 
Section 4 focuses on cybersecurity challenges beyond those related to digital products.  
Whenever the questionnaire refers to ‘users’, the term encompasses both consumers using digital products as well as businesses, public 
authorities and other types of organisations deploying digital products.  Whenever the questionnaire refers to ‘ vendors’, the term 
encompasses hardware manufacturers, software developers as well as distributors and importers of digital products. 
Please note that you can also upload a document (e.g. position paper) at the end of the questionnaire.

Assigning one specific digit as a representative answer across a vast span of different digital products, consumer, enterpris e, and 
critical infrastructure providers, as well as for different use cases and different conditions regarding the users’ abilities and capacities 
to make informed relevant cyber security relevant decisions is a monumental task. Furthermore, different products may have different 
levels of security based on their intended use and user, balancing the requirements and willingness to pay. 
 
Moreover, focusing on product level security alone is insufficient. If the intended policy objective is to protect the ultimate end users 
of a service that is rendered by a system/network rather than predominantly by an individual digital product.  From the perspective of 
complex IT systems and telecom network systems, that provide end users with digital services, the overall security posture of  these 
systems impacts end -users’ de facto security experience. Before,  specifically addressing the security of a ” digital product” in this 
context, it is essential to also consider that end-users’ security experience is significantly co-dependent on the security of the standards, 
defining the base -line security requirements , configuration and hardening of “digital products” comprising the functional complex 
IT-systems/telecom networks and the daily operational security of those complex IT-systems/telecom networks. 
 
Given these critical security dependencies created through t he configuration and the operation of a complex IT -system/telecom 
network, impacting end-user security, the security posture of these products building up the complex IT -system/telecom network, is 
also dependent on the security standards embedded in these products and the vendor specific development process activities. 
International security standards, for mobile systems such as 5G are set in working group SA3 in 3GPP 
https://www.3gpp.org/specifications-groups/sa-plenary/sa3-security In the EU, 3GPP securit y standards are realized through ETSI 
standards. 
 
5 
In the mobile industry, the 3GPP security standards are either mandatory or optional for the operator of a system/network to procure 
(not necessarily as frequently optional for a vendor to support). To this end, product security will also depend on service providers of 
complex IT systems/telecom networks procurement strategies, e.g., to which extent the buyer excludes optional (but mandatory for 
vendor to support) security features in the procurement decisio ns. Lastly, security requirements defined in standards, such as 3GPP 
and others may also be optional for vendors to support. 
 
Product security is also impacted by a vendor’s maturity and adherence to a security by design development process and supply chain 
risk-mitigation framework in place. Most vendors are also dependent on third party hardware and software providers and open-source 
software as well as their own inhouse development. These different aspects together impact the product security posture. This means 
that vendors also need to manage the security requirements in their sourcing strategies with third-party suppliers. 
 
Lastly, as a buyer of vendors’ digital products, building up the complex system/telecom network, the operator may have a procurement 
strategy that does not rely on the latest available software version provided by the vendor, and this means that there may be  more 
secure product versions in the market than there are actually in use in specific complex IT-systems/telecom networks. 
 
Lastly a vendor’s digital product security posture may be impacted by implementation requirements set by the buyer in the 
procurement phase, dictating specific use of certain sub-suppliers’ hardware, software including open-source software. 
 
This said and repeating that it is virtually impossible to provide a reliable answer to question 1 by assigning one specific digit that is 
representative across the vast span of different digital product, but limit to 5G, then our view is that it could be set a digit of 4.

The answer is limited to complex IT-system and telecom network systems, one can elaborate as follows. 
 
Firstly, there is no way to entirely remove threats and vulnerabilities in software, hardware, configurations, operations of complex IT

6 
detecting vulnerabilities (3) estimating the probability of an event occurring and (4) estimating the resulting impact, to understanding 
the risk. 
 
Vulnerabilities can exist at system or product level and can be of different degrees of severity, in this sense not all vulnerabilities have 
the same impact which means that priority should be given to critical and severe vulnerabilities. Lastly, not all vulnerabili ties are 
equally exploi table and hence this aspect needs to be considered as well when setting priorities. This means that severity and 
exploitability should be prioritized for any policy intervention, while avoiding one size fits all approach that strives to a ddress all 
known vulnerabilities.  
 
In sum: risk is a relationship between, threat actor capability performing actions using the exploitable vulnerability or wea kness at 
operational, configuration or product level (including third-party sub-components), and the probability of this occurring and the impact 
of the occurrence.  
 
For an attacker to be able to reach target asset and act on the objective, the attacker would normally have to successfully e xecute all 
the stages of the Intrusion Kill Chain, from Reconnaissance to Command and Control, see example here 
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kill_chain.  
 
Achieving this on a more conventional target like a consumer device or an enterprise office network is not trivial but become s much 
more difficult in case of a telecom network or complex IT-system. This is because a software vulnerability to be exploited in a network 
function or node inside telecom network requires multitude of security controls and processes to fail. Due to how a telecom n etwork 
is defined, designed, developed, and deployed – all this makes it rather difficult to exploit a software vulnerability in the critical – the 
more interesting from attacker point of view – parts of the network. Only if all these processes fail to catch the attacker, an attack can 
be successful. 
 
The layered defences in telecom networks, protect the network and provide resilience against trivial attacks. And in the worst case, if 
an attacker has been able to manoeuvre inside a network and avoid detection, it most likely means that the attacker has established a 
level of access to the network allowing the attacker to conduct the mission without the need to exploit a software vulnerability in the 
network equipment. This means that attentions should be also placed on system level vulnerabilities at operational and configrurational 
level. 
 
7 
Understanding the complexity of exploiting a software vulnerability in telecom networks in comparison to same vulnerability i n 
internet facing, consumer-grade or otherwise widely deployed and practically un-managed system affirm the importance of deploying 
different, fit-for-purpose security frameworks and requirements needed to keep these very different types of systems secure 
Given this, the overall risk of cyber -security incidents have increased for a number of reasons, the growing trend of threat actors  
engaging in cyber-attacks is a function of the pay-out ratio (risk/reward). In principle the risks of getting caught and prosecuted with 
severe penalties is too low while the rewards have increased due to increased degree of digitization. To change the pay-out ratio, it is 
necessary to increase the cost e.g., risk for criminal actors to engage in illicit cyber activities. The proliferation of off ensive cyber 
capabilities (OCC) and tools, and the facilitation of such tools by various intermediaries for prof it has also decreased the technical 
barriers for individuals to engage in illicit cyber activities. Hence, facilitation should also be addressed. Lastly, increas ed activity of 
state actors or state affiliated actors to attack national critical infrastructure has increased both due to changing geopolitical landscape 
but also due to lack of progress at international level e.g., adherence, implementation and enforcement of norms.  
In sum, a primary policy objective should be about decreasing the growing threat  exposure to criminal and state actors that the civil 
society including business is facing today.  
 
Vulnerabilities (people, processes, and technologies) have also increased with technological developments by adding complexit y. 
Criminal threat actors ratio nally pursue low risk/high reward strategies that target individuals, organizational processes and 
technologies.  From a product/system/network perspective the evolution has been heterogenous, while vulnerabilities are virtu ally 
impossible to remove, incre ased attention has been given to develop risk management frameworks and vulnerability management 
frameworks. Increased awareness of vulnerabilities with open -source software has also led to increased understanding of associated 
risks example log4j incident. While open-source software is valuable for the entire ICT industry, increased focus on securing open -
source software is welcomed including though public support. 
 
Limiting the answer to complex IT -systems and telecom network systems, an increasing risk l evel over the last years  has been a 
general trend.

Furthermore, some additional vital principles regarding product security regulation to consider:  
• Rules and requirements should be proportionate and relevant to the purpose, fact based, ideally globally harmonized, 
technology neutral, avoiding technical barriers to trade, and ensure that responsibilities are laid on actors that can be held 
accountable for the outcome. 
• The stringency should be sufficient to meet the public interests but performance beyond this should be left for competition in 
the marketplace. 
• Furthermore, rules and regulation should be balanced and based on a holistic view, between different policy areas such as 
environment, safety, security and over the product life cycle. 
• Avoiding overlapping and contradictory requirements. There is a tendency to extend the scope of legal acts and include 
requirement from other areas. The obvious risk is contradictory or overlapping obligations and conformity assessment 
procedures which all must be fulfilled when a product is placed on the market or put into service. 
• Decisions related to technical regulation and product compliance should be made by the most appropriate body with access to 
relevant competence.  
• Essential requirements, i.e., the political goals, should be defined in the political arena. Technical requirements should 
preferably be defined by recognized standardization bodies, following a well-defined process where all parties can participate.

• The technical security requirements should be prepared by standardization bodies (preferably international) with the 
participation of all relevant stakeholders to ensure transparency and relevance.

The level of security understanding among the users and stakeholders varies in different sectors. The horizontal security baseline for 
all stakeholders may not work optimally since the need of applied security measures and required competence depends on how and 
where the ICT products are used. The better security awareness and competence among stakeholders, the better security solution can 
be applied and maintained. 
As security is always context dependent, a common baseline cannot meet the requirements in all different categories and sectors. For 
example, a common mandatory baseline can be too large for the end device category in case of constrained IoT devices and too small 
for the mission critical Servers and systems. Also, as security can only be accurately defined in a specific context, requiring similar 
security controls for the same product used in different contexts may lead to a false sense of security. 
The horizontal approach with minimum set of security requirements combined with sector specific additional requirements would 
potentially be even more effective as that would allow defining minimum security baseline horizontally applicable to all ICT products 
while requiring stricter sector security controls for higher risk deployments of the ICT products. 
 
If the policy objective is to protect the end users of complex IT systems and networks, product security focus alone is an insufficient 
perspective and dependencies described in earlier answers regarding standards, products, configurations and operations are critical to 
consider. Any product level security assessment should be considered in the broader context including standards, products, 
configuration and operations.

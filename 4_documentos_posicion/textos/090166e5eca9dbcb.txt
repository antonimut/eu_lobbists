GENERAL REMARKS 
The European Automobile Manufacturers' Association (ACEA) supports the Commission’s 
objective of enhancing and ensuring a high level of cybersecurity for digital products and 
related services and of setting up a level playing field for vendors. This will help increase 
resilience against a continuously evolving and more complex cyber threat landscape. It may 
also help build trust with consumers, which will have a positive effect on the economy, 
provided the measures are appropriate, risk-based, and flexible enough not to hinder 
innovation. 
ACEA understands the Commission's rationale in considering establishing horizontal rules on 
cybersecurity, which would apply to a broad range of sectors and products. However, ACEA 
would like to stress the need for the Commission to take into account existing vertical 
legislation for specific industries and/or product groups, and the importance of avoiding 
duplication or contradiction between the different sets of rules.  
A detailed review and gap analysis of existing regulation should be undertaken – a 
consideration that we believe was not reflected in either the call for evidence or the public 
consultation. 
SPECIFICITY OF THE AUTOMOBILE 
SECTOR 
In the automobile sector, vehicle manufacturers and motor vehicles are subject to extensive 
cybersecurity requirements.  
CURRENT LEGISLATION ON CYBERSECURITY 
In the EU, motor vehicles must undergo a homologation process (type-approval) before they 
can be placed on the market, to ensure their safety and security. In this context, the type-
approval framework1 includes two United Nations Regulations that relevant in the context of 
cybersecurity: 
• UN Regulation 155 - Uniform provisions concerning the approval of vehicles with 
regards to cyber security and cyber security management system

• UN Regulation 156 - Uniform provisions concerning the approval of vehicles with

Together, they set technical requirements encompassing the entire vehicle’s cybersecurity, 
throughout its entire life cycle. The Commission (DG GROW) actively participated in the 
elaboration of these international rules at UN-ECE. 
In addition, automotive products are also covered by the Radio Equipment Directive2 and 
the delegated act supplementing the Directive3, for the communications of connected 
vehicles.  
Finally, the upcoming NIS2 Directive4 includes technical and organisational obligations for 
companies to ensure the security of their networks and information systems, which apply to 
automotive manufacturers and their suppliers as “important entities”. 
SECTORAL CYBERSECURITY REQUIREMENTS 
Cybersecurity requirements under UN Regulation 155 notably include: 
• The setup of a Cyber Security Management System (CSMS) 
• Risk management across the entire company and the entire life cycle of each 
individual vehicle 
• The management of dependencies with the supply chain 
• Risk assessments for each type of vehicle 
• Cyber security audits for every type of vehicle 
• The analysis of weak points during the entire development and production process 
• Cyber security monitoring and incident response to existing vehicle types 
• The documentation of cyber security management

RECOMMENDATIONS 
As explain above, ACEA believes that a detailed review and gap analysis of existing 
regulation should be undertaken, which should pay particular attention to sectoral legislation 
applicable to specific sectors. 
In the case of the automotive sector, should the gap analysis highlight any missing aspects 
that would not be covered under existing sectoral regulation, we believe that such gap would 
be best addressed by amending existing sectoral rules, rather than including motor vehicles 
in the scope of the CRA. 
The Cyber Resilience Act should take into account the fact that these regulations already 
provide a comprehensive cybersecurity framework for the automotive sector and its products. 
This new legislation should only complement existing texts where gaps, if any, are identified, 
in order to avoid regulatory duplication. This would otherwise lead to additional and 
unnecessary administrative burdens for companies in the automobile industry, not to mention 
the risk of inconsistencies or contradictions arising between the various pieces of legislation. 
We therefore submit that, while the Cyber Resilience Act may act as lex generalis regarding 
the cybersecurity of digital products, the Commission should ensure that it explicitly clarify 
the relationship of this horizontal legislation with sectoral legislation providing for similar or 
even stronger requirements. It should also specify which legislation shall therefore take 
precedence.  
ACEA therefore believes that the type-approval regulation should be given precedence as a 
lex specialis. This would otherwise lead to a risk of inconsistencies or contradictions arising 
between the applicable legislations, thus creating legal uncertainty and generate additional 
and unnecessary administrative and financial burden for the automobile industry. 
For vehicle manufacturers, the security, including cybersecurity, of motor vehicles and the 
related safety of these vehicles, of the people and the goods that they transport, and that of

In this context, ACEA believes that the overall level of cybersecurity of motor vehicles within

motor vehicles in the scope of the Cyber Resilience Act.  
We therefore believe that, in order to avoid any duplication, or any conflict or contradiction 
between existing text, the Commission should provide for the exclusion of motor vehicles 
from the scope of the upcoming Cyber Resilience Act. 
 
 
ABOUT THE EU AUTOMOBILE INDUSTRY 
• 12.7 million Europeans work in the auto industry (directly and 
indirectly), accounting for 6.6% of all EU jobs  
• 11.5% of EU manufacturing jobs – some 3.5 million – are in the 
automotive sector  
• Motor vehicles are responsible for €398.4 billion of tax revenue for 
governments across key European markets  
• The automobile industry generates a trade surplus of €76.3 billion 
for the European Union  
• The turnover generated by the auto industry represents more than 
8% of the EU’s GDP  
• Investing €58.8 billion in R&D per year, automotive is Europe's 
largest private contributor to innovation, accounting for 32% of the 
EU total

ACT | The App Association’s response to the European Commission’s call for evidence for an impact assessment on the Cyber Resilience Act  I/ Introduction and statement of interest ACT | The App Association is submitting feedback to the European Commission in response to its proposal for a regulation of the European Parliament and the Council of the European Union on horizontal cybersecurity requirements for digital products and ancillary services. The App Association appreciates the Commission’s efforts to make Europe fit for the digital age and its commitment to a modern, stable, and secure cyberspace across the Union.  The App Association represents thousands of small software application development companies and technology firms. Our members create the software apps used on mobile devices and in enterprise systems around the globe. The ecosystem the App Association represents—called the app economy—provides more than €187 billion in revenue in the European Union and supports up to 1.7 million jobs across the European economy, adding 0.4 per cent to the EU’s annual GDP. Alongside the world’s rapid embrace of mobile technology, our members create innovative solutions that power the internet of things (IoT) across all modalities and segments of the economy. Our members are small technology and app development companies, which makes them vulnerable to security threats in cyberspace. The consequences of malicious actors gaining access to our members’ technology and/or customer data could be disastrous for them.   Consequently, small businesses have strong incentives to implement stringent cybersecurity measures and increase their resilience. A cyber-attack on a small business can cause irreparable damage and the loss of reputation, constituting an existential threat. Our members’ continued ability to mitigate serious and dynamic cyber threats is, therefore, crucial to their livelihood. The Cyber Resilience regulation must address the issue of harmful actors by preserving the current security environment, especially on software distribution platforms, to ensure the continued success of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) as well as startups.   The App Association, thus, welcomes the Commission’s efforts to further harmonise and improve cybersecurity in Europe exploring the feasibility of a dedicated Cyber Resilience Act. We also acknowledge the success of the current framework, including the NIS Directive and Cybersecurity Act, and encourage the Commission to build on that success when considering the Cyber Resilience Act. Adding to the existing baseline cybersecurity framework could be a useful and flexible approach to enhance cybersecurity across Europe and advance the Digital Single Market (DSM).  However, the App Association notes that current legislative proposals risk lowering the current level of privacy and security protection through mandating practices such as sideloading or the introduction of third-party app stores which may increase the likelihood of invasive trafficking, fraud, and piracy. This will increase the costs for app developers in maintaining a cyber-resilient environment for the benefit of consumers. The Cyber Resilience Act could be a tool to address these issues. Nonetheless, the App Association urges the Commission to carefully consider the consequences of further regulating cybersecurity provisions in a way that will likely entail excessive compliance costs and have disproportionate effect on small app developers.

II/ Feedback on the call for evidence for an impact assessment a. Political context, problem definition, and subsidiarity check The App Association appreciates the Commission’s commitment to make Europe a leader in cybersecurity, as announced in President von der Leyen’s 2021 State of the Union speech. EU cybersecurity policy should not neglect the technological advances and the resulting increased attack surface for malicious actors, and it must assess and consider risks across the entire attack surface to develop policies that ensure effective risk mitigation. We welcome the notion that the Commission’s problem identification recognises this situation, and we agree with the statement that cybersecurity incidents in one product can easily affect entire organisations or supply chains. While the rise of IoT and mobile apps holds great promise, it also raises security threats due to the increase in networked devices in a wide range of industries. Consequently, we believe companies using IoT devices will require more evolved and dynamic risk management practices. The App Association believes that additional guidance and the promotion of voluntary measures in the industry would allow companies to achieve this objective, while preserving innovation and not overburdening smaller actors.  The Commission states that vendors (e.g. hardware manufacturers, software developers, distributors and importers) often do not put in place adequate cybersecurity safeguards because (i) they want to benefit from being the first to put a product or service on the market, due to network effects, (ii) they lack qualified security professionals, and (iii) they do not want to incur additional costs, all combined with a lack of economic incentives. We disagree with this statement and urge the Commission to likewise consider the incentives that vendors do have in implementing adequate security safeguards.  SMEs especially, including members of our association, largely depend on consumer trust to succeed, and cybersecurity is a crucial aspect in building and retaining consumer trust.   Consumer trust, security, and privacy are among the most essential features for small app developers. While consumers are likely to trust global brands with name recognition, small companies seeking to build their brand often rely on software distribution platforms to provide a trusted marketplace to their users, notably by updating the security of the device’s operating system and by ensuring that the proposed apps are not malware. For these small businesses and startups, software platforms ensure high levels of security and trust in the interest of consumers and businesses. The current levels of cybersecurity and privacy that large software platforms guarantee are fundamental in supporting small app developers who rely on consumer trust to compete with global brands on a level playing field.   In addition, malicious actors steal legitimate applications, remove their copyright protections, and place the apps in illicit stores for downloading, thereby misappropriating revenue from the original developer(s). Pirated apps and ransomware are not only harmful to consumers; a seemingly trustworthy app that turns out to be a pirated version or a Trojan horse that downloads spyware and malware onto consumers’ phones can also destroy a developer’s reputation. Simply stating that vendors’ responses to vulnerabilities throughout the lifecycle of their products are often inadequate and that they do not provide information on product security is inaccurate and lacks nuance. For example, software updates are a crucial form of action in achieving higher levels of cybersecurity and neutralising vulnerabilities; therefore, vendors release software updates regularly.   The Commission further states that it is difficult for consumers to inform themselves and assess the security of products and services. Indeed, small businesses can find themselves in a similar situation. Small businesses represent 99 per cent of all EU firms, and they require assistance in addressing dynamic cybersecurity threats. Our small business members simply do not have the luxury to hire

dedicated cybersecurity teams or to dedicate large budgets to hiring consultants, which makes these threats an even more substantial issue for them. For many of our members, the role of chief security officer may be one of five roles a single employee holds. We urge the Commission to take a more holistic view of the vendors it is considering in its policy development process of the Cyber Resilience Act and take into account the different resources industry participants may have. Additionally, many of our members operate within complex ecosystems, e.g. the platform economy, and we need to ensure that the whole ecosystem remains secure and trustworthy. The Commission must think about cyber resilience more broadly and distinguish between the measures device manufacturers can take and those that software developers and distribution platforms can, and do, take.  As the Commission states, the current EU framework that applies to digital products comprises several pieces of legislation. We believe this can cause some confusion for businesses that try to comply, especially as some of these regulations do not have specific cybersecurity provisions but may cover certain aspects linked to cybersecurity (e.g. the General Product Safety Directive or the Machinery Directive). Furthermore, the Commission rightly states that not all digital products are currently covered by all legislative texts, such as hardware, which the Radio Equipment Directive or Medical Devices Regulation do not cover. Non-embedded software products likewise do not currently fall under any existing framework. This gap risks creating legal uncertainty for businesses, and we welcome the Commission’s recognition of these issues. Additionally, the Commission could use the Cyber Resilience Act to address the potential cybersecurity threats that the implementation of the Digital Markets Act will likely cause, such as the obligation to enable sideloading. This would allow for apps to enter the market without a thorough investigation into their inner workings, making it easier for malicious actors to compromise the level of cybersecurity of the app ecosystem. As a result, consumers could stop trusting apps, especially if they are not well known, with fatal implications for small app developers.  It is, therefore, important that the Commission sets proportionate cybersecurity standards for app developers and manufacturers, although more crucially also for software platforms, to ensure that malicious apps can be rapidly identified and rendered inaccessible for end users. In terms of the legal basis, we agree that using Art. 114 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU) for this Regulation is appropriate. Harmonising cyber resilience measures would enhance the functioning of the single market by reducing fragmentation and cross-border spill overs across the Member States. We agree with the Commission that joint EU-level action would be more useful than individual Member State actions due to the inherent cross-border risks of cybersecurity incidents.   b. Objectives and policy options The App Association welcomes the Commission’s objectives in launching this initiative. Enhancing and ensuring a consistently high level of cybersecurity of digital products and ancillary services is a worthwhile goal in completing the Digital Single Market as well as securing a broad range of products and associated services throughout their whole lifecycle, proportional to the risks they present. We also support enabling users to match the security properties of products against their needs, enhancing the transparency of cybersecurity features, and protecting users from non-secure digital products and ancillary services. In particular, we appreciate the Commission’s intention to incent vendors to offer more secure products, increase trust in the Digital Single Market, and improve the functioning of the internal market by levelling the playing field for vendors of digital products and ancillary services. We note that the emphasis on the ‘whole lifecycle’ of a product should consider the software updates that vendors do provide and emphasise that this objective is disjointed from other

Commission initiatives that would force vendors to enable users to roll back software updates if users chose to do so.  While we welcome the Commission’s objectives, we have concerns regarding the proposed policy options. In addition to essential cybersecurity requirements, the initiative would put additional obligations on economic operators, and introduce provisions on conformity assessment, the notification of conformity assessment bodies, as well as market surveillance. The essential cybersecurity requirements would then translate into harmonised standards specific to the different categories of products. We believe that instead of ex-ante requirements and obligations, the Commission should maintain a light-touch regulatory approach.   Cybersecurity and the market environment wherein digital players operate are uniquely complex and dynamic. Light-touch regulation is, therefore, best suited to maintain flexibility and be able to address the continuously changing dynamics in cybersecurity instead of horizontal cybersecurity requirements. Digital service providers can self-regulate effectively, and their business models rely heavily on consumer trust; thus, they have high incentives to adhere to strong cybersecurity standards. Additionally, ex-ante rules as opposed to an ex-post approach severely increase the compliance burden for businesses, which particularly hurts the SMEs the Cyber Resilience Act would cover. As such, small businesses may be locked out of the market by the introduction of rigid requirements. The development of voluntary codes, guidelines, or best practices through an industry-led effort would much more effectively benefit the entire security landscape.  As such, we strongly oppose policy options four and five, meant to develop a horizontal regulation introducing cybersecurity requirements for a broad scope of tangible and intangible digital products and associated ancillary services, including non-embedded software. Overly prescriptive requirements may induce the risk that companies comply in ways that are not the most efficient or effective for their individual circumstances. With more general risk management obligations, companies have the flexibility to find the tools and policies that are best suited for them to manage cyber risks most efficiently and effectively. Additionally, technological progress might outdate any prescriptive requirements rapidly, rendering them impractical and futile in a short time. Levels of risk management should be as harmonised as possible across different national markets so that the compliance burden is decreased for companies that operate across the EU.   Policy option one could be feasible, in maintaining the status quo through existing legislation that partially addresses the cybersecurity of tangible products (e.g. the Delegated Regulation under the Radio Equipment Directive, legislation on medical devices, motor vehicles, machinery, or product safety, amongst others). The App Association endorses measures taken to maintain and preserve the current system to guarantee proper cybersecurity protection to consumers without causing unbearable burdens on SMEs in the identification of such threats. The App Association would welcome additional guidance on the application of existing legislation   Similarly, policy option two—introducing voluntary measures by further developing and applying voluntary certification schemes under the Cybersecurity Act—is satisfactory. Introducing mandatory certification to demonstrate conformity may be particularly cumbersome for small companies, therefore, a voluntary approach to certification and conformity would be favourable. Soft law measures such as guidelines or recommendations could also be considered, in particular on the cybersecurity of non-embedded software.

We most prefer policy option three, which would introduce ‘ad hoc’ regulatory interventions for the cybersecurity of digital products and ancillary services and would be limited to adding and/or amending the cybersecurity requirements in existing legislation and regulating new risks as they emerge, including potentially on non-embedded software. This could be a flexible and nuanced tool that assesses risks on a case-by-case basis, giving companies the option to address incidents as they best see fit. When developing the Cyber Resilience Act further, the Commission should likewise consider the importance of central policy management as well as flexible and technology-neutral rules to ensure swift security control and quick incident response. Under policy option three, the EU could also provide additional guidance to companies on implementing best practices, such as how to remove complexity from internal policy management, how to identify and recognise vulnerabilities and network segmentation, in addition to conducting analytics such as security configuration assessments, risk scores, and traffic flow.   III/ Conclusion The App Association supports the Commission’s efforts to further harmonise and improve cybersecurity in Europe. A thoughtful design of the Cyber Resilience Act could enhance cybersecurity across Europe and advance the Digital Single Market overall.   A Cyber Resilience Act based on existing processes and structures could be a solid regulatory framework that offers flexibility to the Member States while emphasising the importance of harmonisation. The Cyber Resilience Act could also advance the Digital Single Market by institutionalising cybersecurity at the European level to decrease existing fragmentation. We urge the Commission to carefully consider whether its proposed policy options contribute to the complexity of existing structures and if they create regulatory overlap. In addition, we advise the Commission to consider the impact on SMEs and whether the revised legislation creates a proportionate regulatory burden that will enhance cyber resilience in the EU Digital Single Market.  The App Association thanks the European Commission for considering our views, and we remain at your disposal should you have any further questions.  Sincerely,

• Number of vulnerabilities/threats increase with the increasing number of connected products: 
more assets = more corresponding threats. As systems and services become more integrated, the 
risk of cyber-attack/infiltration has increased. 
• ENISA’s threat landscape reports: increasing number of incidents for many threats.  
• The combination of increased internet exposure and lack of user awareness to secure their 
equipment (i.e. using strong, unique passwords for each account) means that if a password is 
cracked, it can probably be used to access multiple devices/accounts, gives rise to new 
opportunities for attack.  
• Subjective risk seems increased: ransomware as a service, cloud being leveraged for attacks at 
scale, phishing, hybrid work, etc.  
• Yet, the CVSS score has decreased from 2016 to 2021 indicating the threats are less effective. 
• Definition of “level of risk” appreciated (e.g.: combination of possible impact/CVSS score and 
number of vulnerabilities). 
• From 2020 to 2021 the average attacks per company over the year went up 31% to 270 attacks. 
(source: State of Cybersecurity Report 2021 | 4th Annual Report | Accenture). 
• NB: The number of identified vulnerabilities found annually (CVE ID’s at www.cvedetails.com), is 
rising. But it can also mean that the increase of CVE counts is due to organizations now having 
more bug bounty and other offensive research programs, encouraging more vulnerability 
research. 
• It can be assumed that identified vulnerabilities are/will be exploited -> connection between CVEs 
and incidents. Yet, mitigation becomes more sophisticated, too.

It is important that the “full” life cycle is not well defined. We note that manufacturer alone cannot 
ensure security along the life cycle (for instance a proper treatment at end of life). 
In our view, the manufacturer needs to have the possibility to declare for how long which security 
support can/will be provided along the life cycle. Stronger transparency obligations to disclose the 
duration of available support (while maintaining flexibility to determine that duration and adjust it) 
strike the correct balance. In when the customer is known to (registered) an obligation for 
information on of service could be an additional option 
For more details se attachment with comments for this question as well as position paper 
prepared in relation to related proposed requirements in eco-design regulation Lot X 
(https://www.digitaleurope.org/resources/technical-annex-on-operating-system-update-
requirements-in-proposed-lot-x-regulation/).  
Cyber threats can change over the time, new vulnerabilities can be discovered; one obvious 
solution to this are software updates but there are other systemic solutions, too. Concerning 
software updates, there is no optimum period applicable to all products, all situation and all 
customer demands. Obligatory provision for a too long time must cover lots of scenarios and can 
easily be overdesigned and expensive. The best way to address this is by transparency: as long as 
there is the legal obligation to be transparent as to the period during which software updates are 
guaranteed, the market will offer the best solution for all customers.  
Moreover, in most industrial scenarios good security governance requires the involvement of all 
stakeholders in the value chain through an overall Risk Assessment specific to the deployment, so 
that a restrictive legislation putting responsibility only on manufacturers/developers would be 
counterproductive to the overall ecosystem.  
Lastly some lifecycle stages are not under the control of manufacturers, so applying very strong 
constraints to manufacturers/developers may not bring any security improvement in the end if 
other links in the value chain are left unaddressed by the legislation.In fact, each affected 
stakeholder (or actor) should secure life cycle stages over which he has control. For instance, apart 
from vendors and or manufacturers, operators and other service providers of complex IT systems 
and telecom networks are in charge for operational security of a deployed networks/systems 
where they need to continuously assess the performance of the network to meet the defined 
security threshold levels.

Sub-section 3.c. – Regulatory burden and costs for small and medium-sized companies 
 
 
 
Horizontal requirements will raise the security level of products and may make them interesting 
for some "military" but non-operational use (such as connected appliances in military barracks). 
However, usually mil specs are higher than civil requirements due to a different risk profile. 
Generally, we’re not in the position to estimate whether there are other benefits. 
Looking at the threat landscape, threat actors appear to target both the civil and defense 
sectors. For example, nation state sponsored groups and advanced persistent threats often 
attack both sectors. Therefore, for dual use devices, a good level of cyber security protection 
should have standards that are applicable for both civil and defense sectors. 
However, civilian users especially in B2C context will not use products which are difficult to 
install or use – even if it means the device is more secure.  
These are challenging requirements especially when it comes to dual use devices. 
 
 
 
Any additional requirement costs money. The relevant question is however, if the investment is 
worth it. Amending existing legislation leads to overlap and thus increased excessively cost due to 
double testing double documentation possibly solutions for conflicting requirements when more 
than one law applies. 
On Guidelines: It would be good guidance for the IT Security team. If there is a priority list for the 
guidance, then easier to implement. However, Guidelines have the risk to be taken up by some 
players only thus effectively creating a market fragmentation 
Quantifying and identifying cost implications is extremely challenging as it varies based on the 
design processes of the organization, legislative requirements, and business sector. Even a minor 
security change could have great impact if redesigns were required. Arguably, good security 
practices (e.g., secure development life cycle) could reduce the likelihood of a major rework to 
mitigate these redesign costs. Nevertheless, legislative, or other security process requirements may 
increase costs, e.g., testing/scanning costs.

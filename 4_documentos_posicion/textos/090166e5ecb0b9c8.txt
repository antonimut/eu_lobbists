UNDERSTANDING THAT CYBERSECURITY  IS AN ONGOING  PROCESS  
 
Today’s cybercrime ecosystem has become increasingly complex and brings new, 
significant challenges to security experts. Businesses and governments must grapple with 
the ongoing digitalisation of supply chains with new sectors being targeted by 
cyberattacks1. They must also manage shifts to work-from-home and back-to-office caused 
by the pandemic, navigate growing security challenges due to geopolitical changes and 
conflicts, and improve resiliency as cybercriminals become more specialised and 
professionalised, running illegal business models such as “Cybercrime-as-a-Service”. 
 
This means that cybersecurity should be seen as a continuous process and not an end 
state: the security of a product changes as its deployment environment changes, as 
different technology develops, and as attacks evolve, therefore security cannot be 
measured simply by assessing the product against some abstract concept of “secure”.

However, what can be assessed are the efforts (policies, investments, etc.) a company has

This threat landscape requires the CRA’s regulatory framework to remain flexible enough 
and focused on risk-based security practices to allow businesses to evolve products 
quickly to address ever-changing cyberattacks.   
 
PROVIDING CLARITY IN SCOPE AND DEFINITIONS  
 
The CRA should clearly outline what type of digital products are in scope and include clear 
definitions of such products. While it is important the CRA covers all digital products, it 
should take into account differences in the development, functionality and use of these 
digital products, and apply proportionate rules following a risk-based approach (for 
example, B2B vs. B2C products, embedded vs. non-embedded software). The EU New 
Legislative Framework (NLF), which typically includes modules for determining the 
respective risk, is a mechanism that could serve as a basis for this approach.   
 
“High-risk” products should also be clearly defined, and such definition should consider the 
environment in which a product is ultimately used, rather than defining entire sectors as 
“high-risk”. Such an approach would also ensure that the CRA rules can be future-proof and 
that the legislative framework can remain effective and allow for product innovation, 
particularly in emerging technologies. 
 
 
ENSURING CONSISTENCY WITH EXISTING – AND UPCOMING – LEGISLATION  
 
The current EU regulatory framework already covers many digital products: for example, the 
Machinery Directive, the Radio Equipment Directive, and the General Product Safety 
Directive under the NLF. They are complemented by the Cybersecurity Act (CSA), the 
Network and Information Security (NIS) Directive, and the General Data Protection 
Regulation (GDPR). Consistency between these pieces of legislation, as well as 
upcoming regulation (such as NIS 2) must be ensured. 
 
Overall, the CRA should be ‘lex generalis’, outlining baseline cybersecurity 
requirements for connected products, while avoiding the introduction of mandatory 
cybersecurity certification schemes replacing existing harmonised standards, as these 
can overlap or conflict with other EU cyber legislation. Self-assessment against these well-
known European standards should always be the default option to show evidence of 
compliance in the area of cybersecurity and lead to the ‘presumption of conformity’ as 
defined by the NLF and its open and inclusive processes.

FOCUSING ON HARMONISED AND EFFECTIVE RULES  
 
Fragmentation can persist across certain domains due to the number of existing legislations 
affecting digital products. Individual Member States adopting different national laws also 
mean that companies could be more subject to conflicting requirements. The CRA should 
then focus on reducing fragmentation by introducing horizontal, risk-based rules, which 
could, if aligned with international standards and consistent with existing legislation, help 
simplify improvements in security. 
 
Such requirements should enable self-assessment as the default conformity 
assessment procedure (including the possibility to have third party assessments for a 
certain category of high-risk products). We would caution against developing ad-hoc 
regulatory rules which may lead to further fragmentation.  
We also believe that a mandatory introduction of Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) 
requires thorough consideration, as it might not achieve the intended objectives:  if 
SBOM includes overly prescriptive transparency requirements, for all kinds of products, and 
is not aligned with international standards, it will not be effective due to the complexity of 
today’s software and cloud environments, where application components constantly go 
through automated updates. In this context, the feasibility and effectiveness of SBOM 
would soon reach its limits. Moreover, caution should be taken with regards to transparency 
requirements and the information that should be made available to the public vs. what 
information should be provided to market surveillance authorities.  
 
ALIGNING WITH INTERNATIONAL STANDARDS  
 
European standards should be aligned with international standards. International 
standards provide widely vetted, consensus-based information and guidance for defining 
and implementing effective security methodologies, and facilitate common approaches to 
common challenges, thus enabling collaboration and interoperability. There are already 
good examples showcasing close alignment with international standardisation (e.g., product 
safety, electromagnetic compatibility). 
 
Additionally, ICT standardisation is by nature global, and involvement of all stakeholders 
is important. The EU has an effective standardisation infrastructure (with CEN-CLC/JTC 13; 
ETSI TC CYBER, etc.) and it will be critical to continue to closely engage industry in strategic 
committees for the development of standards. Common (technical) specifications should 
only be developed following clearly establish criteria, as a last resort.

DIFFERENTIATING B2B AND B2C PRODUCTS  
 
Products, designed for B2C and B2B markets, have different lifecycles, potential risks and 
intended purposes: in a B2B environment, products are integrated into highly complex 
systems.  
 
In B2B software, the supplier typically has little control over what their business clients 
do with the software and might be limited to intervene by contractual agreements. For 
example, modern cloud-enabled applications are based on software running as composable 
micro-services, which are provided and consumable as SaaS (Software-as-a-Service) 
components. In addition, almost all cloud solutions are built on open-source software 
stacks and leveraging these commonly available software building blocks to form hundreds 
of thousands of different applications.  
 
Therefore, the author of a software component could design a software for use in one 
environment, but it might end up being deployed in a completely different environment for 
which it is ill-suited – and unbeknownst to the author – for example a similar SaaS 
component in a smart coffee machine vs. usage of the same software building block in a 
medical device. The CRA must then consider such specificities (and the context, in 
which a piece of software is developed, integrated, and utilised) when defining 
cybersecurity requirements.

Position paper: Input consultation on a 
Cyber Resilience Act 
 
Summary 
The Belgian, Dutch, French and German CIO-associations represent business users of digital 
technologies in their countries. As representatives of business users, we firmly support the 
need for mandatory horizontal cybersecurity requirements for all digital products and 
services as is envisioned by the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA). We fully support the goals that 
the CRA envisages and we gladly support the Commission in shaping the text of the CRA. 
Specifically, we urge the following points to be taken into account: 
1. The CRA should regulate the cybersecurity of digital technologies throughout their 
life cycle, from design to integration, administration, operation and configuration in a 
mandatory, EU wide, approach. 
2. The CRA should cover digital technologies in products, services and processes, 
irrespective of use by individual consumers or business users, and during the whole 
economic life cycle from a user perspective. Vulnerabilities in the current software 
are increasingly impacting business users, leading to high direct costs and indirect 
opportunity costs as the security specialist’s skills are turned towards addressing the 
vulnerabilities.  
It is important that the cybersecurity risks of non-embedded software products are 
clearly addressed.  
3. The CRA must be complementary to regulations in specific sectors  
4. Compliance with CRA should be monitored and enforced in such a way that the 
uniform and effective application of cybersecurity requirements is ensured at EU 
level . 
5. Enforcement of CRA should be gradual, starting with providers of critical components 
in enterprise-level information systems and large digital solution providers.  
 
Business users of digital technology welcome the opportunity to provide input on the 
process to come to a Cyber Resilience Act. We consider that essential ingredients for cyber 
resilience allow for a flourishing European economy and society; ingredients such as clear 
norms and standards, assurance of compliance by public authorities, fair competition and 
equitable liability rules.  
 
Who we are and why our input matters 
We are the Belgian, Dutch, French and German CIO-associations; the communities of Chief 
Information Officers (CIO’s) and other senior leaders that are responsible for digital 
technologies and digital transformations within private or public organisations. All our

Business users of digital technology are a key link in the digital transformation of society and 
economy: they rely on the digital technologies they use, either hardware or software, and 
need these to be secure and safe to use and compliant with EU laws and regulations. 
Business users also play an influential role in keeping digital technologies used in their

through their combined purchasing power. To reap the benefits of digital technologies in 
Europe, the position of business users - companies and government organisations - of digital 
technology must be taken into account.  
 
Context: The position of business users in the markets of digital technologies 
and software, specifically relating to cybersecurity 
Before commenting on what business users of digital technology see as essential for the 
CRA, we set out the context of the digital market and why the European Commission’s 
proposal for the CRA is such a welcome and necessary legislative proposal. We expand on 
several elements of the digital market, such as power imbalances and a lack of incentives for 
vendors to provide secure software.   
  
At the foundation of the Cyber Resilience Act, should lie the assumption that producers of 
digital technology products should have a strong incentive to deliver safe and secure 
products, services and processes and that consumers and business users should be 
protected from, or compensated for, costs and other damages caused by non-secure 
products, services and processes.  
 
Strong dependency on software and suppliers 
Defective, unsafe or unsecure software and other digital technologies can quickly and easily 
cause harm or damage to users, both private persons as well as organisations. Hereafter we 
shall mostly talk about software, as software, either embedded in hardware, stand alone, or 
provided as a service, is the most versatile, complex and security-relevant part of digital 
technology. This does not mean we disregard other manifestations of digital technologies. 
Software is so deeply embedded in almost all products, services and processes nowadays, 
that defects can easily cause these products, services and processes to become inoperable, 
insecure or unreliable. This is especially the case where software is used by business users 
and integrated into their production-, logistical-, security- and other processes.  
 
Furthermore, business users are often in a dependent position with regard to the suppliers 
of the software they use. This is due to the lock-in effect of software; because of the limited 
level of standardization, interoperability and modularity on the one hand, and the number of 
connections software creates with many other systems, processes and users within the 
organisation, it is very difficult and extremely costly to change from one software product to 
another. It should also be considered that often, there are not many reasonable alternatives

to be found. These effects lead to a distinctive unbalance in negotiating power between 
software provider and business user. 
 
This unbalance also leads to having to accept unfavourable terms and conditions, for 
instance with respect to liability of the producer/vendor of the digital technology or 
software product, service or process. As there is freedom of contract, the argument will go 
that business users are fully aware that they waive liability. The reality is that the business 
user does not have the negotiating power to demand that liability for faulty or insecure 
software lies (partially) with the software vendor when (re-)contracting the use of the digital 
technology.  
 
Restraints on finding and repairing flaws in software 
Yet another aspect of the digital market is the complexity and other restraints that make it 
impossible for the business user to adequately inspect or adapt software if it is found not to 
be secure enough. The source code of software is usually available only to the vendor, 
making it impossible for entities other than the vendor to scrutinize it. Furthermore, 
proprietary software code is protected by intellectual property rights. So even if someone 
was to find flaws or security leaks, they may not alter the code to repair the situation and 
can only refer the discovery to the vendor, who then has to decide what to do about this. 
These issues do not always get the attention they deserve.1  
 
Transferal of costs of security flaws to business users 
Even if the vendor steps in and provides patches for cybersecurity risks in its software, it is 
then up to the business user to organize testing of the patch to see if it does not impair 
connections with, or operation of, other software or processes within the organization. As all 
software vendors make use of such patching practices, business users must employ 
significant numbers of employees to check and deploy patches to cover mistakes made by 
the software vendor.  
 
For example, a company with 150,000 employees worldwide and the equivalent in 
Office 365 licences, mobilizes an average of fifteen FTEs/year to carry out the routine 
operations of correcting security flaws in Microsoft software and control console. 
This is equivalent to an average expenditure of more than 1 million euros/year, at the 
business user’ s expense, to compensate for the by -design security flaws in 
Microsoft’s products. More recently, the management of the so -called 
« PrintNightmare » flaw alone repres ented 300 man -days for the company’s IT 
department.2

found that: ‘At present, government and other organizations have few possibilities for 
requiring software manufacturers to safeguard cybersecurity in their products. As a

In well-functioning markets, such situations would cause business users to migrate to other 
suppliers. We have indicated above why that is not usually possible in the case of digital 
technologies, especially software. The dependency of the business user on the digital 
technologies and software they use, also inhibit their possibilities for addressing the 
situation in court. Confronting a supplier in court with situations a business user deems 
unlawful, not in accordance with contracts, or with general business practice, could well 
(and in practise does) result in unfavourable conditions or price raises when the next 
renewal of contracts is needed. 
 
More efficient use of expertise to prevent security flaws 
The indicated situation not only impacts costs for business users of digital technologies, it 
also puts additional strains on the talent pool of cybersecurity specialists. With business 
users needing to employ relatively large staffs of (security) personnel to cover the 
vulnerabilities and flaws in digital technologies and software, they need to compete with 
each other and with the producers/vendors for scarcely available experts. It would be 
beneficial for all concerned if producers/vendors would be incentivised to ensure higher 
quality and better security of their products, services and processes. A concentration of 
security expertise at the core of a system, where the design and production are located, is 
more efficient than distributed across all endpoints. Examples are found and instituted in 
many other industries, for instance drinking water, medicines, car manufacturing, etc. 
 
Make use of international initiatives that promote security by design of digital 
products and services throughout their life cycle 
Despite the absence of a common legal framework, various international organisations have 
worked on securing digital products and services by design and throughout their life cycle. 
We would mention the OECD report, "Enhancing the digital security of products”4, published 
in February 2021 and whose work should be incorporated in CRA initiative. Similarly, the 
work of Working Group 6 of the Paris Call on securing the ICT supply chain5 should be taken 
into account in the CRA as it makes recommendations to implement international 
frameworks on securing the ICT supply chain.

Cyber resilient product as reasonable expectation of the customer 
Given the importance of digital technologies in our economy and society, it would be 
desirable to promote the emergence of a situation where cyber resilience/security of a 
product or service is regarded as a common practice. This would mean that the customer, 
either an individual consumer or a business user, can have the reasonable expectation to 
have a cyber secure product, that complies with the applicable EU and national legislations 
and regulations. This is the same principle as in car manufacturing industry. The car 
manufacturer is responsible and liable for the good functioning of the car. When there is 
(negative) impact or risk, then the car manufacturer needs to solve the issue. This results for 
instance in recall of products, and their being fixed without cost to the user. At the moment, 
this is not the situation with digital technologies. 
 
New, mandatory legal framework only way to really improve security 
The context described above provides ample indications of the need to ensure that software 
is developed secure, right from the start and that security is maintained throughout the 
employment of the software by business users. It would liberate much needed resources, 
save costs and increase productivity of security specialists. As indicated above, and 
underscored by the Dutch Safety Board, users often have no way of incentivising the 
producer to incorporate their requirements into the product, service or process. This means 
the only way to make sure vendors do the utmost to provide secure software products and 
services, is to lay down mandatory legal requirements and enforce compliance. 
 
No current legal framework addresses this adequately at this moment. As stated by the 
Netherlands government in its non-paper of January 2022:  
 
‘While the RED delegated act is key to set mandatory security requirements in the 
short term for wirelessly connect devices (including many IoT devices), it only 
addresses wireless products when they enter the market and cannot encompass the 
broader scope of ICT products, processes and services. Also, at the time the RED was 
not created with cybersecurity in mind. The CSA on the other hand offers the benefits 
of a broader scope for ICT products, processes and services, but is a voluntary system. 
From this perspective, horizontal legislation can contribute to a desired horizontal and 
mandatory level of security and ensure consistency and legal certainty for both 
manufacturers and consumers (citizens and businesses).’6  
 
Furthermore, guidelines, where available based on work already undertaken by other 
international bodies as indicated in the previous paragraph, are needed in order to help both 
developers, vendors and business users to comply with the legal framework as soon as 
possible and with the least amount of uncertainty as to what the legal requirements mean in 
practice.

What does this mean for the Cyber Resilience Act? Our four associations urge the European 
Commission to ensure four aspects in its proposal for a Cyber Resilience Act, as set out 
below. 
 
1. The CRA should regulate the cybersecurity of digital technologies throughout 
their life cycle, from design to integration, administration, operation and 
configuration in a mandatory approach. 
In the previous chapter, we have explained the need for legislation to make sure vendors of 
software and other digital technologies have the incentives (and obligation) and the 
guidelines to provide products, services and processes driven by software and other digital 
technologies that are secure throughout their life cycle. This means from their design and 
development to integration, administration, operation and configuration. It is important that 
the legislation is mandatory. Due to the interconnectedness, by design or in practice, of 
digital technologies and software, flaws in one product or service may impact others.7 This 
means that producers of digital technologies and software should adhere to security 
requirements. As set out above, it is apparent that software vendors are not sufficiently 
incentivised to provide software that is safe and secure by design. Hence, we call for a 
mandatory, EU wide approach to the security requirements. 
 
2. The CRA should cover digital technologies in products, services and processes, 
irrespective of use by individual consumers or business users, and during the 
whole economic life cycle from a user perspective. 
‘If everything is connected, everything can be hacked’, as Commission President Ursula von 
der Leyen stated in her State of the Union speech in 2021. .8 This, together with the 
previously mentioned practical evidence that systems can be affected even if they are not 
specifically targeted, can only lead to the following conclusions about security requirements. 
These requirements should apply to all: 
a. Digital technologies and software in products, services and processes that are of can 
be connected, 
b. Categories of use, irrespective of the user being a consumer, SME or large 
organization (private or public), 
c. Digital technologies and software that is brought to the market and/or used within 
the EU. 
 
Therefore, producers/vendors should be obliged to keep providing necessary updates and 
patches throughout the economic life cycle, to keep ensuring the security of the software, 
and by extension all connected systems and users. This applies to all connected products,

It is important to define the life cycle independently from the producer/vendor. Leaving it up

force users to invest more than necessary from the user’s perspective. It would create the

On the other hand, if the end of life is declared by an independent party and support for 
security is subsequently stopped by the producer/vendor, it will be the (business) user’s 
responsibility to migrate to other digital technology or software, or to make sure the old 
technology is no longer able to connect to other systems. 
 
Furthermore, producers/vendors should be obliged to inform their customers and act within 
a reasonable time to correct flaws in their digital products and services throughout their life 
cycle.  
 
3. The CRA must be complementary to regulations in specific sectors  
The CRA should implement a basic level of security for all digital technologies and software, 
especially those that are connected. In some industries, processes, and situations additional 
measures are, or could be, required, either by law or sectoral regulations. The CRA should be 
complementary to such regulations and make compliance easier as producers/vendors are 
incentivised to adhere to higher levels of security. If other regulations already apply and are 
more stringent, that regulation/framework applies. If not, CRA applies. It should in any case 
be prevented that CRA creates ambiguity or conflicts in requirements with other legislation. 
 
4. Compliance with CRA should be monitored and enforced in such a 
way that the uniform and effective application of cybersecurity 
requirements is ensured at EU level  
Compliance with CRA should be monitored and enforced in such a way that the uniform and 
effective application of cybersecurity requirements is ensured at EU level, otherwise the CRA 
will fail in attaining its objectives. Time and again it was shown by i.a. extensive testing of 
GDPR-compliance10 that the existence of legal requirements does not mean digital 
technologies, or the terms and conditions imposed by vendors, are compliant. There needs

To make sure the requirements are uniformly interpreted and enforced across the EU, there 
should be a central authority responsible for this. This would also ensure that the cost to 
producers/vendors of digital technologies and software is not higher than need be, and that 
the required expertise can be most effectively brought together. This will require a great 
effort, a lot of expertise and time to set up, but it is essential to start working at this, in order 
to be able to make sure we can make the EU more secure in the future. Should, 
alternatively, national authorities be appointed to enforce the CRA, it is of critical 
importance that coordination between these national regulators is set up in a way that 
avoids the issues that make enforcing the GDPR less efficient than necessary. For instance, 
differences in interpretation and application of the regulatory framework creates 
uncertainty for users. And the differences in size and expertise of the regulators make it 
relevant which authority is in the lead to pick up cases etc. This could lead to many cases not 
being enforced when the lead-authority is understaffed or doesn’t have the required 
expertise at hand. 
 
Similar situations where requirements have to be met for admittance to the EU market are 
manifold, for instance in the aircraft industry, food, pharma and many other industries. In 
those situations too, as with digital technologies, the impact and costs of faulty products on 
individuals, society and economy is large and liability of producers is hard to put into 
practice. In these cases (centralized) regulations and enforcement thereof have over the 
years been put into place. This should also be the case for digital technologies, especially for 
software.  
 
5. Enforcement of CRA should be gradual, starting with providers of critical 
components in enterprise-level information systems and large digital solution 
providers. 
We can imagine that the implementation of a regulatory framework such as the Cyber 
Resilience Act, and the enforcement thereof, will take some time. And that choices have to 
be made in what areas to focus enforcement on first. In the paragraphs on the context of 
digital technologies, business users and cybersecurity above, we explained the strong 
dependence of business users on software products, services and products, the extensive 
interconnectedness of some software suites with other processes and systems within an 
organization and the difficulties and costs related to switching to another provider or 
software suite, if one is even available. We also indicated the practices and incentives 
relating to cyber security related flaws and repair policies, as we encounter them. Following 
from these observations, we would suggest as a first order of priority, to make sure that 
enterprise level information systems, cybersecurity software and services, and software 
products and services that are most widely used in business environments, are the first to be 
made compliant with the requirements of the CRA. These are most critical to business

continuity and security. This compliance should be assured by a third party, either an 
independent auditor or a regulatory body.  
 
This suggestion for prioritization does not mean that other digital technologies should not 
need to comply with CRA requirements, but the focus should in our opinion be on the 
previously mentioned products, services and processes first. 
 
It will be necessary to work with different ‘levels’, you cannot expect the same level for all 
applications and vendor size. It is not the intention to block innovation by upfront 
certification. For less critical industries, software, etc, a light regime with self-assessment 
could be put in place.  
Final remarks 
This comment on the Cyber Resilience Act initiative focusses mainly on the role of vendors, 
which is indeed an important role that has not received the necessary level of attention in 
the past. So as a way of correcting this lack of attention, the Cyber Resilience Act is a 
necessary step. On the other hand, cyber resilience needs more than just secure hard- and 
software and services. It also needs good implementation and operation, monitoring and 
well thought out and tested recovery plans, frequent exercises of how to handle incidents 
etc. Most of all, what is necessary, is that owners/boards of directors of all organizations and 
companies, both vendors and users of digital technologies, should be made more aware and 
responsible for the cybersecurity of their organization. In this respect, the name of the Cyber 
Resilience Act may imply a broader approach than seems to be proposed, if the proposed 
Act follows the reasoning of the questionnaire. We would like to call attention of the 
European Commission to also take steps to encourage those responsible for organisations, 
to take action to ensure the security of both their organisation and its operation on one side, 
as well as the (digital) security of the products, services and processes it provides on the 
other side. 
 
Our four CIO-associations are readily available to assist shaping the text of the CRA. We are 
more than happy to provide more insights into the business user’s perspective on this topic.

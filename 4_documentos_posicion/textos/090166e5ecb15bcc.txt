  
 
BSA | The Software Alliance 
Position Paper Supporting the EU Commission’s Public Consultation On The 
Cybersecurity of Digital Products and Ancillary Services 
 
25 May 2022 
 
BSA | The Software Alliance (“BSA”) 1 welcomes the opportunity to provide input to the European 
Commission’s public consultation on the cybersecurity of digital products and ancillary services (“the 
consultation”).  
 
BSA is the leading advocate for the global software industry. BSA members are at the forefront of 
software-enabled innovation that is fueling global economic growth and digital transformation by 
helping enterprises in every sector of the economy operate more efficiently, securely and in a privacy-
protective way. BSA’s members are enterprise software companies that offer technology services that 
other organizations use —such as cloud storage services, customer relationship management 
software, and workplace collaboration software —to make their own operations more efficient, 
innovative, and successful.  
 
The public consultation raises the issue, in question 16, of the “responsib[ility]” of the “software 
developer”, “for the full life cycle of a digital product (such as by being required to provide updates)”. 
BSA and its members support the EU Commission’s efforts to enhance  the cyber security in digital 
products and mitigate the risk of incidents or attacks that can affect an entire organization or a whole 
supply-chain. Managing security risks involves shared responsibility among all businesses and 
customers in the ecosystem. We  strongly believe that  responsibility should take into account the 
various places in which negligence can arise. Certainly software developers have an important 
obligation to employ practices designed to account for foreseeable risk . But even with securely 
developed software, risks will remain, which is why a fact -specific inquiry is needed for assigning 
liability is the appropriate framework. 
 
In BSA’s view, high standards and due diligence in software development, deployment, and use, 
throughout its lifecycle, is best adapted to ensure cybersecurity in digital products. Such standards 
and due diligence are developed through the software community’s best practices that help software 
developers address important aspects of software security.

I. A Strict Liability Regime for Software Will Undermine EU Cybersecurity   
 
The rules governing product liability at EU level assume that the manufacturer of a product is best 
positioned to mitigate harms that might arise before it is placed on the market. - This is indeed mainly 
the case for “tangible products” (i.e. as cars, TV, etc.) which are designed for a specific use. Unlike the 
consumer goods that typically are subject to product liability rules, software is deployed into complex 
operating environments over which vendors or developers have little control or foresight once it 
reaches the end-users, and in ways that create unpredictable potential risks. Software is typically 
installed by end- users into general -purpose computing environments with diverse hardware 
configurations and numerous other software titles. Unpredictable compatibility issues arising from 
unique combinations of software and hardware can lead to system failures.  
 
When a software is licensed to a customer (business or consumer), vendors or developers have limited 
control on how their software are deployed, used, maintained by end-users as well as the environment 
in which they operate. Certainly, software developers have an important obligation to employ 
reasonable coding practices that are designed to account for the foreseeable risks that end-users 
might encounter. However, securely coded software is no guarantee against system failure s or 
security breaches, which often arise due to  “improper program installation or maintenance by 
corporate IT staff, careless password protected by end users, and improper security procedures by 
internet access providers. Even if the software supplier provides a secure program, any one of the 
other involved parties can open a program or system to attack.”
2 Similarly, software companies have 
no control over the workforce that uses its software and whether or how they are skilled or trained 
on managing cybersecurity risks. In a corporate setting, even if vendors  or developers ship software 
with enabled security features or provide patches, administrators may not follow through.  
 
Proposals for strict liability on software developers also lose sight of the fact that the vast majority of 
damages arising from security incidents results from malicious activity by criminal actors. Botnets, 
spear phishing and ransomware are just a few of the many methods of software compromise that can 
adversely affect enterprises and, ultimately, end-users. While developers have an obligation to protect 
their software against foreseeable threats, a strict liability framework is ill- equipped to allocate the 
costs that derive largely from criminal interventions.  
Moreover, a strict liability regime could create perverse incentives that could actually undermine 
cybersecurity. Even the most robust and secure information systems are only as secure as their use . 
Most security breaches are not a result of vulnerable software or questionable coding, but rather 
because of an incorrect use of the software (e.g. user unawareness, incorrect installation or non -
application of security updates, the use of insecure passw ords, opening unsolicited e-mails, etc .). 
These can all result in cybersecurity breaches of information systems. A strict liability regime that 
places responsibility solely on software developers could have the unintended effect of discouraging 
end-users from mitigating risks that they are best positioned to address.  
Moreover, e xposing developers to liability without regard to fault for harms that are largely 
unforeseeable, often beyond their control, and frequently the result of criminal intervention will 
imperil their ability to bring cutting -edge software to market. The softwa re development process is 
strongly democratized. Today’s software is the result of a collaborative process that relies on the 
sharing of tools, technologies, and components. Indeed, the internet has given rise to a software 
development ecosystem that enables companies to share both open source and proprietary “software 
libraries” on agreed upon terms. These software libraries are core components that enable developers

II. Strict liability for software developers is inconsistent with EU harmonization 
 
Currently, the liability of a software (stand -alone or embedded) developer derives from the general 
EU framework, namely: 
1. Directive 85/374/EEC of 25 July 1985 on the approximation of the laws, regulations and  
administrative provisions of the Member States concerning liability for defective products;  
2. Directive (EU) 2019/770 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 20 May 2019 on certain 
aspects concerning contracts for the supply of digital content and digital services; a s well as  
3.  Directive (EU) 2019/2161 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 27 November 2019 
amending Council Directive 93/13/EEC and Directives 98/6/EC, 2005/29/EC and 2011/83/EU of 
the European Parliament and of the Council as regards the better enforcement and modernisation 
of Union consumer protection rules. 
 
These legislations, and the principles of liability they entail, are well established: they have been in 
place (some revised, in the meantime) for a long time and offer an efficient, predictable, safe and 
entrusted legal framework to EU businesses, consumers and citizens. Indeed, the current negligence-
based liability regime for software defects is working, and there is no evidence that EU consumers are 
being injured by defective software (stand-alone or embedded) for which they cannot recover or seek 
redress for. Moreover, i mposing heightened liability standards on software suppliers will chill 
innovation into software offerings that have the potential to improve consumer welfare considerably. 
 
Moreover, several existing EU sectorial legislations address software (either stand- alone or 
embedded), in particular: 
1. Regulation (EU) 2017/745 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 5 April 2017 on 
medical devices, amending Directive 2001/83/EC, Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 and R egulation 
(EC) No 1223/2009 and repealing Council Directives 90/385/EEC and 93/42/EEC  i ncludes 
“software” in the definition of a medical device (embedded software) . Moreover, s oftware, “or 
software that are devices in themselves” , should apply “ minimum requirements concerning 
hardware, IT networks characteristics and IT security measures, including protection against 
unauthorised access, necessary to run the software as intended”. 
2. Directive 2014/53/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council of 16 April 2014 on the 
harmonisation of the laws of the Member States relating to the making available on the market 
of radio equipment and repealing Dir ective 1999/5/EC w hich regulate, via delegated acts, stand -
alone software uploaded in connected products that communicate via certain radio modules. This 
Directive requires that specific classes or categories of radio equipment support features ensuring 
that the compliance of that equipment is not compromised when software is uploaded. 
3. Directive 2006/42/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 17 May 2006 on 
machinery, and amending Directive 95/16/EC (recast)  re quires that a fault in the software of the 
control system does not lead to hazardous situations. 
 
Finally, recently, a number of ongoing EU initiatives also include references to software, and liability 
in particular, namely: 
4 
 
1. The 2021 EU public consultation on “Civil liability – adapting liability rules to the digital age and 
artificial intelligence” which could lead, in particular, to potential revisions to extend strict liability 
rules to cover intangible products that cause physical or material damage (e.g. software and digital 
content), address defects resulting from changes to products after they have been put into 
circulation (e.g. software updates or circular economy activities like product refurbishments), 
defects resulting from interactions with other products and services (e.g. IoT). 
2. The current review of the EU General Product Safety Directive  where several political groups of 
the EU Parliament have tabled amendments to include software ( stand-alone or embedded) in 
the definition of “product”, thereby including software in the scope of the Directive and its 
provisions. 
3. The current review of the Directive on security of networks and information systems  (NIS 2) 
introduces “technical, operational and organizational” risk management measures for high -risk 
software. This obligation will apply equally to  the software operated by a regulated customer, or 
by the product manufacturer (as the latter also qualifies as an “ essential service” operator under 
the inclusion of Software as a Service - SaaS- in Annex I) , and also covers embedded software. 
Moreover, s ome cybersecurity services, like managed detection and response (externalized 
security operations), also fall under the scope of NIS 2. 
 
Software liability is included in a broad range of different legislations , the potential future legislation 
on cybersecurity requirement for digital products initiated by this public consultation being the last 
one to date. The multiplication of legislations, and the various scopes and understandings of software 
(stand-alone or embedded) as well as the liability covered create legal uncertainty and run essentially 
against the EU efforts towards harmonization. Such discrepancies create burdens not only to 
businesses, in particular SMEs, to ensure that they abide by the different requirements of the different 
legislations but also to EU consumers and citizens who will be face with different redress mechanisms 
depending on the type of legislation applying to their specific case, creating financial burdens and 
lengthy resolution periods. 
 
Therefore, and in order to ensure legal clarity and certainty, both for businesses and consumers , we 
would urge the EU Commission not to contribute to further  fragmentation of the rules and work 
towards its existing goal of harmonization. As stated above, it has been shown that the current Union 
product safety legislations efficiently take into account the safety risks stemming from software, either 
stand-alone or embedded, and that there is no evidence that EU consumers are being injured by 
defective software (stand -alone or embedded) for which they cannot recover or seek redress for.  
Therefore, we believe such existing legal framework should be retained. 
 
 
III. Software Community Best Practices & BSA Framework for Secure Software 
 
However, from within the software community, best practices are emerging that help software 
developers address important aspects of software security, including security -by-design principles, 
secure development lifecycle processes, and internationally recognized standards for key security 
elements such as identity management, encryption, and secure coding. Although attention to each 
specific security consideration can achieve marginal security gains, effective security requires a 
comprehensive and risk -informed approach that combines individual considerations into a holistic, 
lifecycle-long framework. Moreover, a comprehensive approach must be tailored to address the 
nuanced, diverse, and evolving challenges associated with different types of software and connected 
devices. 
 
Building on best practices pioneered by many of its  members, BSA | The Software Alliance has 
developed a software security framework to bring consistency to these  complex challenges. The BSA 
5 
 
Framework for Secure Software3 is intended to establish an approach to software  security that is 
flexible, adaptable, outcome-focused, risk based, cost-effective, and repeatable. Avoiding a one size-
fits-all solution, this voluntary framework will provide a common organization and structure to capture 
multiple approaches to software security by identifying standards,  guidelines, and practices that can 
help software development organizations achieve desired security outcomes while accounting for the 
wide spectrum of intended uses, risk p rofiles, and technological solutions  among software products 
and services. 
 
Designing a horizontal cybersecurity policy framework like the Cyber-Resilience Act is a complex task 
and will greatly benefit from active and structural consultation and collabora tion across th e 
stakeholders involved in building up the cybersecurity ecosystem. BSA strongly believes that structural 
cooperation between the public and private sectors is key to ensure the cybersecurity of digital 
products and ancillary services. Therefore, we would recommend setting up a dedicated framework 
in the upcoming Cyber -Resilience Act to ensure such public/private cooperation so that public 
authorities can rely on the private sector to provide industrial and practical expertise in policy 
developments in the matter. As cybersecurity policy is growing and becoming more complex, we also 
welcome more cross -institutional collaboration in the E U Commission to ensure as more holistic 
approach to policymaking. We especially believe that , in the process of designing security -related 
horizontal legislation, it is important to conduct cross-consultation and take into account the aspects 
of other policies, such as sustainability, to ensure their overall alignment. 
 
We would therefore encourag e the European Commission to focus on an outcome-focused, 
standards-based risk management tool to help stakeholders in the software industry – developers, 
vendors, customers, policymakers, and others – communicate and evaluate security outcomes 
associated with specific software products and services.

V. Promote the development and use of internationally recognized standards 
 
Another key issue with regards cybersecurity requirements for digital products is the need to 
harmonize such cybersecurity requirements with international standards.  
 
The public consultation’s question 22 alludes to common standards but merely for the specific case of 
“dual-use items”. We strongly believe that such harmonized cybersecurity requirements based 
international standards should apply to all digital products. These standards are developed in open, 
transparent, consensus-based processes, and are widely adopted in the international marketplace. 
Internationally recognized standards leverage global security expertise from governments, industry, 
and academia. For example, ISO 27001 “specifies the requirements for establishing, implementing, 
maintaining and continually improving an information security management system within the 
context of an organization” and ISO 27017 provides “guidelines for information security controls 
applicable to the provision and use of cloud services.” 
 
Regional, national, or local standards fragment this landscape and increase the costs to customers 
(including government customers) and decrease both the ability to provide innovative solutions and 
the number of players competing for business thereby ultimately not only limiting consumer choice 
but harming the entire cybersecurity ecosystem.  
 
In contrast to regional , national, and local standards, laws and policies based on internationally 
recognized standards enable international interoperability, allow governments and businesses to 
better communicate at the technical level, have a track record of being developed and updated more 
efficiently than laws, increase competitiveness, incentivize innovation, and account for how 
technology is evolving. Ultimately, internationally recognized standards result in digital products that 
are more effective, efficient, safe and innovative, while also being less expensive. This would 
ultimately provide a more secure cybersecurity environment for digital products. By participating in, 
and adopting, international standards, the EU could raise and address the cybersecurity concerns with 
regards digital products without passing costs to consumers, hampering innovation, or limiting 
competition. 
 
This is particularly relevant when it comes to the issue, absent from the public consultation, of 
enforcement. Indeed, future rules including harmonized European standards that are based on 
requirements such as conformity self-assessment by default (including the possibility to have third 
party assessments for a certain category of high-risk products) would be an appropriate policy option. 
However, the public consultation does not mention which type of information is necessary to assess 
whether digital products and ancillary services fall under the category of “high-risk”. Moreover, there 
is also no mention of which entity will ultimately make the as sessment that a digital product falls or 
not under the “high-risk” category nor based upon which information (e.g. threat/risk assessment). 
We would welcome additional clarity on the EU Commission’s part on this point.

VI. Recognizing Software Bills of Materials (SBOMs) as a promising but so far limited tool 
 
The public consultation’s question 17 on approaches to contribute to the cybersecurity of digital 
products notably mentions, as one of the options, the software bills of materials (SBOMs). 
 
We should explicitly recognize the limitations of SBOMs as an emerging technique to manage supply 
chain and cybersecurity risk. An SBOM can provide useful information to a customer but, without 
additional context, can also be misleading and result in suboptimal action. For instance, an SBOM may 
identify a vulnerable component that cannot be exploited as used but may nonetheless lead an 
organization to compel a software developer to addresses that unexploitable vulnerability. 
Alternatively, even if an SBOM identifies a vulnerability that is exploitable, an SBOM may 
inappropriately prioritize the use of cybersecurity resources as there may be more effective ways to 
address the vulnerability or otherwise improve the security of the product or service. Moreover, while 
an SBOM is valuable to a developer during the development process and can provide point -in-time 
information to a customer, an SBOM can also provide a roadmap for a criminal  actor, and like any 
tool, an SBOM can and will be used for both noble and nefarious ends. 
 
We believe  an SBOM is one of the many  pieces of a broader cybersecurity risk management 
ecosystem. Because SBOMs are an emerging tool, with strong limitations as exposed above, the EU 
Commission should be explicit that they are intended to provide supplemental information and do not 
themselves provide sufficient information on which an organization can measure either the 
cybersecurity risk or quality of the software they describe, nor do they identify the most effective use 
of cybersecurity resources.

BSA and its members support the EU Commission’s efforts to ensure the cyber  security in digital 
products and to prevent cybersecurity incidents or attacks that can affect an entire organization or a 
whole supply -chain. However, we strongly believe that such efforts would not be met by the 
introduction of a strict software vendor or developer responsibility for the full life cycle of the digital 
product but that responsibility should take into account the various places in which negligence can 
arise. Certainly software developers have an important obligation to employ practices designed to 
account for foreseeable risk. But even with securely developed software, risks will remain, which is 
why a fact-specific inquiry is needed for assigning liability is the appropriate framework. 
 
Rather, BSA strongly believes that high standards and due diligence in software development, 
deployment, and use, throughout its lifecycle, is best adapted to ensure cybersecurity in digital 
products. Such standards and due diligence are developed through the software community’s best 
practices that help software developers address important aspects of software security. 
 
We would therefore encourage the European Commission to focus on an outcome-focused, 
standards-based risk management tool to help stakeholders in the software industry – developers, 
vendors, customer s, policymakers, and others – communicate and evaluate security outcomes 
associated with specific software products and services.

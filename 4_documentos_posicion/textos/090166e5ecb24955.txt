EBF VIEWS ON THE REVIEW OF THE CENTRAL SECURITIES 
DEPOSITORIES REGULATION (CSDR) 
 
The European Banking Federation (EBF)  welcomes the initiative of the European 
Commission to propose changes to the Central Securities Deposit ories Regulation 
(CSDR) with a view to enhance the efficiency of EU’s settlement markets , in accordance 
with the 2020 Capital Markets Union Action Plan (CMU). 
European banks are strongly committed to the development and integration of financial 
markets in the EU, and fully share the objective of making securities settlement safer and 
more efficient, thereby improving the attractiveness of EU's capital markets, and 
ultimately contributing to the financing of our economy. 
This commitment is exemplified by the Markets4Europe Campaign, an EBF-led cross-
sectorial initiative aimed at proposing game-changing reforms to unlock deeper and truly 
integrated financial markets in Europe. The Campaign strongly supports the improvement 
of the broader post -trading environment through the implementation of all 
recommendations contained in the 2017 report of the  European Post Trade Forum  
(EPTF), ultimately aiming  to remove  long-standing obstacles to efficient clearing, 
settlement, and asset servicing in the EU. 
Against this background, to support thorough and continued public discussions, European 
banks stress the following high-level points: 
 
1. PROPOSED CHANGES TO THE SETTLEMENT DISCIPLINE REGIME 
 
• EBF views on the proposed changes to the Cash Penalties Regime 
 
European banks remain committed to the effective implementation of CSDR settlement 
fail penalties as introduced by the current CSDR and entered into force in February 2022.  
Specifically, notwithstanding initial difficulties registered at the start of the live phase of 
collection and distribution of pen alties, EBF members remain convinced of the need to 
insist on a proper functioning of the settlement fail cash penalty mechanism to ensure 
increased settlement efficiency in the EU.  
Against this background , pursuant to  the objective of improving the effec tiveness and 
operability of European financial markets, EBF members support the European 
Commission's decision to prioritize the use of late matching and failed settlement 
penalties as the most appropriate tool to reduce settlement fail rates  across 
asset classes in the EU. 
Specifically, EBF members welcome the clarifications of scope introduced by  Article 
1(2)(a) of  this proposa l to exclude  settlement fails "caused by factors not 
attributable to the participants in a transaction" from the application of penalties. It 
is a logical step to give ESMA the task of specifying  the details of such case in the new 
Article 7(14a) of the CSDR (Article 1(2)(j) of the proposal.

• EBF views on the proposed changes to the Mandatory Buy-ins Regime 
 
European Banks remain strongly convinced that MBIs are not  appropriate or 
proportionate tool s for delivering settlement efficiency objectives. Against this  
background, the EBF welcomes the Commission’s proposal in Article 1(2) that the so-called 
Mandatory Buy-Ins (MBIs) pursuant to Article 7 of the CSDR should not immediately apply. 
In fact,  EBF Members remain of the view that  the application of MBIs for 
settlement efficiency gains  should be avoided . The buy -in is a trading -level 
instrument intended to protect the buyer and therefore belongs at trading level; enshrining 
the enforcement of buy-in arrangements at settlement wrongly mixes trading and 
settlement activities, thus committing actors that are not parties to the trade but are 
commissioned by the trading parties to fulfil the sales contract by settling the trade  on 
their behalf. 
Furthermore, as highlighted both in previous public consultations and by the Commission's 
impact assess ment -operational and implementation costs and complexity aside -, EBF 
Members emphasize that MBIs bear disproportionate negative effect s in terms of  
reduced market liquidity and excessive cost-increases for issuers and investors .  
Ultimately, they risk undermining the attractiveness and competitiveness of EU capital 
markets in contrast with the objectives of the CMU. 
Against this background, EBF Members emphasize that other more suitable means  of 
settlement efficiency improvement, both regulatory and industry -driven, should be 
deployed before considering the application of mandatory buy -in arrangements. These 
include the recalibration of penalty rates, use of partial settlements, the harmonization of 
CSD functionality and removal of the structural barriers in the region that prevent 
settlement on Intended Settlement Date (ISD). 
In addition, trading related measures such as amendments to the SSR for equities should 
be considered to ensure that ‘L ocate Rules’, including sanctions and transparency of net 
short positions are tighter. This would present a two -step approach of measures to 
improve the settlement of securities transactions by making targeted improvements at a 
trading and settlement level  through appropriate regulation rather than attempting to 
enforce trading related outcomes through a post-trade regulation. 
Having said that , EBF Members  welcome the intention of the Commission to 
improve the functioning of the MBI process . This requires effective changes to both 
the CSDR and -more importantly- related Regulatory Technical Standards (RTS) . In this 
context, we strongly emphasize that it is absolute key that the text at L1 should 
define only general principles of the Regulation on Central Depositories without  
establishing in detail the technical characteristics of the implementation of buy -
in schemes, with reference to its application to the settlement chain, whi ch should be 
defined at L2 by an appropriate re-drafting process of RTS by ESMA. 
 
2. PROPOSED CHANGES TO THE PROVISION OF BANK -TYPE ANCILLARY 
SERVICES 
 
EBF members underline the need to ensure that any changes to the current rules 
should not result in a relaxation of the prudential requirements  (as per the 
regulatory technical standards defined in the Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 
2017/390) that apply to CSDs wishing to provide banking-type ancillary services.

We believe that the CSDs’ critical role as central market infrastructures for core functions

Against this back ground, while ensuring the diversity and competitiveness of EU

ANNEX I – DETAILED RECOMMENDATIONS ON THE PROPOSED CHANGES 
TO THE SETTLEMENT DISCIPLINE REGIME 
 
• Detailed recommendations on the Cash Penalties Regime 
 
a) “Golden Source” of CSDR reference data 
The lack of a “golden source” of CSDR reference data challenges the operation of cash 
penalties. Cash penalty, and ultimately buy -in, reference data is currently based on 3 
ESMA databases (FIRDS, FITRS and SSR) and requires considerable data mining efforts 
by each CSD. There is no cen tral pricing source with each CSD using their own vendors , 
leading to inconsistency and lack of transparency. This makes it extremely challenging for 
market participants to effectively monitor the application of and their exposure to cash 
penalties, as evidenced by the fact that data quality and consistency have been the source 
of many issues in the first months of operation of the cash penalty regime. Therefore, EBF 
Members recommend that an additional subparagraph/point be added to Article 
1(2) of the proposal mandating ESMA to establish and maintain a golden source  
of all CSDR relevant data. 
The above-mentioned CSDR reference data should include: i) the nature of the instrument 
(in or out of scope) ii) the instrument classification and the applicable penalty rate and iii) 
the daily reference price. The proposed centralized management of CSDR reference data 
by ESMA could generate many substantial ben efits e.g.: i) to provide all market 
participants with clarity on critical aspects of the penalties calculations and ii) to decrease 
the likelihood of disputes. 
b) Scope 
The EBF strongly supports the exemptions from cash penalties “where those 
settlement fails are caused by factors not attributable to the participants to the 
transaction” introduced by Article 1(2)(a) of the proposal.  This clarifies several open 
questions and will avoid triggering penalties for events which are not in the target of the 
settlement discipline regime , such as corporate actions, application of EU -sanctions and 
collateralization transactions.  
For instance, EBF Members emphasize that any instructions generated by the CSDs, such 
as in the case of market claims, which currently can incur back-dated ‘late matching fail 
penalties’ through no fault of either participant, should be excluded from the penalty 
regime, as neither participant can be fairly penalized for the failure to settle on intended 
settlement date.  
As such we recommend that this is clarified by amending the first paragraph of Article 16.3 
of the RTS accordingly : “Where a settlement instruction has been entered into the 
securities settlement system or has been matched after the intended settlement date, 
cash penalties shall be calculated and applied as from the intended settlement date. If the 
settlement instruction was entered into the securities settlement system by the CSD, cash 
penalties shall be calculated and applied only once the settlement instruction is matched”. 
 
At the same time, the proposed amendment to Article 7.2 of CSDR also suggests 
exempting “operations that do not involve two trading parties”  from the scope of 
the penalty mechanism. This could be problematic for CSDs to determine from the

Against this background , EBF Members emphasize the need to incorporate a correct 
and appropriate definition of ‘trading party’ into CSDR Level 1 to be used  
consistently throughout  the settlement discipline regime . Furthermore, this definition 
should ensure that a retail client is not considered as being a ‘Trading party’ to avoid any 
unintended consequences in direct holding markets (i.e., where the retail investor holds a 
segregated securities account directly at the CSD). 
Also, EBF Members suggest that L1 should leave the possibility open to differentiate 
the scope for penalties and buy-ins in terms of types of transaction. In this context, 
ESMA should be given the task of specifying the exact definitions of these transaction 
types, as well as the relative status as in scope or out of scope, in the delegated act to be 
introduced under 7.14a of the REFIT proposal. 
c) Temporary suspension for cash penalties 
Article 7.13(a) of the proposal creates a possibility of a temporary suspension of the 
mandatory buy -in mechanism. It would be valuable also to create a possibility for 
temporary suspension of the cash penalty regime –for instance, if a CSD experiences an 
outage that will impede a participant’s ability to settle. 
d) Cash penalties collection and distribution by CSDs 
EBF Members suggest  amending Art. 19 of Delegated Regulation (EU) 2018/1229  to 
ensure that the responsibility in relation to the collection and distribution of penalties falls 
entirely with the CSDs  for both CCP -cleared and uncleared transactions , as opposed to 
both the CCPs and the CSDs  having to manage two parallel penalty regimes . In fact, the 
process of collection and redistribution under the regulatory framework currently in force 
substantially increases the complexity and technology required by all parties involved, 
creating an additional layer of actors to such collection/distribution process. A 
simplification of the process (currently split in two) by the means of a unique, integrated, 
and standardized procedure for the collection and distribution of penalties, valid for any 
financial instrument or transactions in scope would streamline such 
collection/redistribution activity for all the parties involved, reduce the likelihood for 
operational issues and reconciliation errors. 
e) CSDs’ operation of cash penalties 
Whilst accepting that greater detail should be in Level 2, the current drafting of the Level 
1 does not provide sufficient clarity around the finality of the collection and distribution 
process at CSD level.  For such a critical part of the settlement discipline regime  there 
should be a provision in the Level 1 whi ch outlines the CSD’s regulatory obligation in the 
same way that participants obligations are listed in article 7 . Such a  provision should 
indicate a clear timeframe for the CSDs to report, collect and complete the cash penalties 
process for any given mont h. Without such finality, CSD participants would effectively 
remain exposed to any operational delay or error by the CSDs who can seemingly report 
late and levy cash penalties indefinitely (during the past few months various EBF members 
have suffered cases of penalties being identified and reported by CSDs after the end of 
the monthly collection and distribution period). This results in a lack of integrity and finality

f) Penalties in direct-holding markets 
Since the implementation of penalties as of February 1, 2022, EBF Members active in  
direct holding markets we have recorded a significant number of very small penalties  
passing from  financial institution  to retail customers , including penalties that are 
rounded to zero. Despite the difference with omnibus markets, where retail clients are not 
direct participants of a  CSD, there is no rationale to justify addressing cash penalties to 
retail customers. Retail customers in segregated markets rely on the services offered by 
expert intermediaries. These intermediaries sh ould be put in the condition to absorb the 
penalty in place of their retail customers, since in most cases the latter cannot themselves 
be seen as originators of the fail.  
Hence, EBF Members strongly suggest adding a  recital at L1 to foresee the appropria te 
legal protection for intermediaries to absorb the cash penalties. This should be further 
clarified by the necessary interventions at L2. 
 
• Detailed recommendations on the Mandatory Buy-ins Regime 
 
a) On the “two-steps approach” 
The Commission’s proposal envisages a two-step approach (Article 1(2)(b)) permitting 
the Commission to (re)introduce mandatory buy -ins by means of an implementing act if 
certain conditions are met.  
EBF Members welcome this approach as it allows for the application of more suitable 
options to increase settlement efficiency rates. However, EBF Members emphasize that 
several open points concerning its application should be addressed:  
1) Lead time: EBF Members strongly emphasize that industry participants would need 
appropriate lead time to mobilize the resources and  prepare the wide range of activities 
that are required upon the activation of a MBI  regime. By the same token, the intention 
to activate MBIs should be communicated well in advance.  
2) MBI suspension regime: EBF Members believe that  additional clarity regarding the 
suspension of MBIs following their activation is needed . In general terms , EBF Members 
share the commission’s view that  a MBI regime should be activated on a defined 
conditional basis. With a similar approach , the MBI regime could be disapplied once the 
level of settlement efficiency returns to an “appropriate level” . In this respect, EBF 
Members underline that the introduction of a “suspension mechanism” (Point (h) of Art. 
1(2) adding a new paragraph 13 a in Article 7 of the CSDR), albeit positive, should be 
further expanded, as it is currently designed to address only “extraordinary” situations 
that may arise because of the activation of MBIs.  
3) Underlying data: in the light of the material impact that a MBI regime is likely to 
generate, we deem of paramount importance that such a move should be based on an 
exhaustive and accurate set of data and information. 
Against this background, to ensure the best possible implementation for all parties, the 
EBF strongly recommends keeping the L1 at the level of principles, while allowing 
for an exhaustive re-drafting of RTS to define the technical requirements for the 
implementation of MBIs.

In the same way, the EBF also considers it essential to closely involve ESMA in the 
decision to apply MBIs. The new Article 7(2a) of the CSDR might therefore be redrafted 
to stipulate that: 
o ESMA will be involved in examining the conditions set out in Article  7(2a) (a) to (c) 
of the CSDR and will publish reports of its findings. 
o ESMA should be mandated to establish and publish a clear and exhaustive list 
of the financial instruments covered by the scope of settlement discipline under 
Article 7 of the CSDR and to specify which market values of these financial 
instruments should be used for calculating penalties and buy -in cash compensation 
(see our proposal on a golden source above). 
o The Commission may only take a decision to introduce buy -ins based on  ESMA’s 
reports and proposals if, based on  these findings and after examining the 
proportionality of mandatory buy-in measures, ESMA concludes that such measures 
“constitute a proportionate means” and h as submitted an appropriate proposal to 
the Commission. 
o Therefore, the Commission should not decide by means of an implementing act 
without the prior technical advice of ESMA. 
o The implementing act should provide for a sufficient implementation period and 
the imposition of a n accompanying RTS  to integrate the consequences of the 
introduction of the MBI (repapering, review of the market makers’ offers). 
o Regarding the application of settlement efficiency rates  as condition to activate 
MBI provisions, as defined in Art. 2a(a) of the new CSDR, we consider it key that the 
indicators to be used should be carefully chosen . For instance, Settlement 
instructions that fail on ISD but settle before ISD+4 (for liquid equities) or ISD+7 
(for other instruments) would not be resolved any quicker by the introduction of 
mandatory buy-ins. Therefore, it is critical that any policy decision related to the 
application of MBI is based on data relating to settlement fails that remain still 
outstanding after 4 or 7 business days, and not settlement fails on ISD. 
▪ Further, with the proposed exemptions to the transaction scope (7.2, 7.4(c), 
7.4(d) and 7.14a) it is essential that the scope of the CSDs obligation to report 
settlement fails under RTS Article 14 is revised to ensure th at the transaction 
types identified as applicable for the mandatory buy -in are duly distinguished 
from and measured separately to the broader transaction types submitted for 
settlement. Article 74.1(a) if the REFIT proposal should consider this also. 
o  EBF Members remain skeptical about the application of the condition defined by Art. 
2a (b) of the new CSDR. the comparison with 'third-country capital markets' risks 
dismissing the structural specificities of the European market (composed of financial 
poles interconnected by multiple infrastructures based on the integrated functioning 
of the parties ), when other markets, even if larger in volumes, present a less 
heterogeneous, more centralized structure . Furthermore, despite this, there is no 
capital market outside the EU that has a comparable settlement discipline regime. 
In this context, it is worth considering that European problems require European 
solutions, including how to identify and tackle barriers to cross -border settlement 
that are unique to EU markets. 
b) MBIs should not be addressed at settlement level 
The current text assigns responsibility for the enforcement of the MBI s to CSDs and CSD 
participants, imposing undue risks  on intermediaries who are not responsible for the  
conditions of the original trade or for the operation of the  buy-in process. The buy-in is a 
trading level instrument for protecting buyers and, as such, it belongs at trading level.

Consequently, EBF Members are of the view that the obligation to initiate a buy-in should 
rest entirely with the receiving trading party. This has already been considered at Level 2: 
Delegated Regulation (EU) 2018/1229 makes trading parties responsible for initiating buy-
ins (c.f.: Articles 27, 29 and 31 of the Delegated Regulation). We believe that CSDR Level 
1 and accompanying RTS should be amended to reflect this  universally for all mandatory 
buy-in related provisions and for ‘trading party’ to be included and defined under Article 
2.1 of the Level 1. 
In this sense, the EBF welcomes the amendment with respect to trading parties 
proposed in Article 1(2)(e). The proposed new Article 7(4)(d) of the CSDR now takes 
explicit account of the fact that buy -in should only apply to trading parties. This clarifies 
several open questi ons and will avoid triggering economically and operationally 
nonsensical buy-ins such as in the event of securities account transfers without a change 
of beneficial owner, collateralization, or primary market transactions. Moreover, it should 
be considered that, beyond transactions that “are not real trades”, there are “real trades” 
for which applying a MBI will have negative consequences (e.g., subscription / redemption 
of funds referred to in the recital but the buy-in itself). 
In addition, legal repaperi ng under paragraph 10(c) is a barrier which should be 
removed. Article 7.10(c) remains unchanged: “CSDs shall include in their internal rules an 
obligation for their participants to be subject to the measures referred to in paragraphs 3 
to 8”. This has an implication for Article 25 of the Level 2 which was a significant and costly 
global implementation hurdle, correctly acknowledged in the Impact Assessment. 
We recommend that this paragraph be amended so that “for transactions not cleared by 
a CCP or executed on a trading venue, the trading party  shall include in its terms of 
business (or equivalent client documentation) an obligation to apply the measures referred 
to in paragraphs 3 to 8”. 
The EBF welcomes article  7.3(a) of the R EFIT which proposes a ‘pass-on mechanism’. 
However, the drafting of paragraph 3(a) places the obligations at the level of the 
participant rather than the trading party which again imposes trading level obligations 
and risk on intermediaries who are not contractually party to the trade.  
In addition, directionally, the obligations are placed towards the ‘end receiving  
participant’ placing the burden towards the investor rather than towards the ultimate 
failing trading party.  
Also, the propos ed pass -on mechanism is envisaged under a “pure OTC transactions” 
angle. It doesn’t address the case of cleared transactions and doesn’t cover the situation 
of a clearing member on the buy-side suffering a MBI from the buyer while waiting for the 
result of the buy-in triggered by the CCP 
Further, we believe the regulatory text, as proposed, is too detailed for the Level 1 for 
such a comple x operational process and contains detail that has not been proven 
operationally which will challenge the drafting of the Level 2. We therefore recommend 
that paragraph 3a is revised to state: the ability to pass -on the buy -in obligations is 
introduced.  
Article 7(14) could then be amended to add: “the Commission shall be empowered to 
supplement this Regulation by adopting delegated acts in accordance with Article 67 
specifying parameters whereby the execution and management of a buy-in can be passed 
through the trading chain”. 
However, again, the term participant is used rather than trading party , which imposes 
undue financial risk on intermediaries to ‘make good’ the delta in trade price committed 
by their clients. We therefore recommend that the drafting is revised using the 
term trading party.

c) On the computation of “calendar days” for extension periods 
In Article 7(3) of the CSDR (Article 1(2)(c) of the proposal), the term “ calendar days” 
should be corrected to “ business days” with respect to the extension period for 
transactions on SME growth markets. This would correspond to the other deadlines in 
Article 7 of the CSDR as well as the terminology commonly used for capital market 
transactions.   
In addition, extension periods for CCP trades should align with OTC to reduce risk to 
market participants trading on vs off exchange. We believe that this is excessive detail for 
the Level 1 and that the Level 2 should clarify that extension periods be applied at an 
instrument level, regardless of place of trade or whether a trade is cleared or uncleared. 
d) Buy-in Agents 
EBF Members are of the view that the requirement to appoint a buy -in agent should be 
removed and turned into an optional right i.e., entitling trading parties to execut e their 
own buy-ins. 
e) Other 
In view of the new Article 7(2a) of the CSDR, Article 72 of the CSDR should be adjusted 
to require the deletion only of Article 15(2) of the SSR, which also provides for penalties, 
so that penalties are not incurred twice.  
Article 7(13) remains unchanged in the REFIT whereby only shares have a third-country 
exemption when “the principal venue for the trading of shares is located in a third country”. 
There is no similar exemption for any fixed income instruments. This creates an uneven 
application of the sc ope based on asset class and may create additional complexities for 
cross-border fail chains in third-country instruments which could unduly penalise European 
investors who are trading across jurisdictions. We therefore recommend that only debt 
and equity instruments issued in the EU itself should be in scope of an EU regime. Similarly, 
we recommend that only settlement instructions to / from EU (I)CSDs should be in scope 
of the SDR, thereby excluding cases where a delivering / receiving CSD is a third coun try 
CSD. 
Article 7(9) of the Level 1 remains unchanged in the REFIT and so continues to prevent 
the fair treatment of intermediaries who cannot influence the trading patterns and 
operational practices of their clients and are therefore unable to prevent settlement fails 
unless their own actions are directly responsible.  Since the relationship between the CSD 
participants and its clients is opaque to the CSD , the regulatory obligation to report / 
suspend participants should be removed for accounts designated as client accounts at the 
CSD. RTS Art.39 should be revised accordingly.

Amazon position on the Empowering Consumers for the Green 
Transition Initiative 
 
Amazon welcomes the new initiative on empowering consumers for the green transition as it will improve 
consumer protection against unfair practices and ensure they are empowered to make informed 
sustainable choices.  
 
We are committed to and invested in sustainability because it is a win all around – it is good for our 
customers, our communities, our planet and our business. We are continually looking for ways to make 
our business more sustainable as we work to meet The Climate Pledge, a commitment we co -founded 
with Global Optimism to reach net zero carbon by 2040 – ten years ahead of the Paris Agreement. As part 
of our sustainability commitments, we are already helping our customers to reuse, refurbish and recycle 
through Amazon Second Chance; and we help our sellers donate unsold stock to charities in markets 
where an enabling framework exists. To make it easy for customers to discover and shop for more 
sustainable products we launched Climate Pledge Friendly. Customers will see the Climate Pledge Friendly 
badge on more than 100,000 products in Europe to signify that the products have one  or more of 31 
different sustainability certifications that help preserve the natural world, such as reducing the carbon 
footprint of shipments to customers. Climate Pledge Friendly includes a wide range of highly regarded 
global certifications, including the EU Ecolabel, Blue Angel, and Nordic Swan.  Climate Pledge Friendly 
programme also includes Amazon’s own Compact by Design – an externally validated certification which 
encourages brands to design products that can reduce carbon emissions through increased efficiency and 
better packaging. 
 
In light of Amazon’s strong commitment to our customers and sustainability practices, we welcome efforts 
to protect and empower consumers against misleading claims and early obsolescence through simple and 
accessible information. Avoiding ‘greenwashing’ and related practices will help safeguard the interests of 
sustainability minded consumers and foster market incentives to service the demand for sustainable 
products and services. Tackling unfounded green claims and harmonising ways to substantiate them is 
therefore a welcome development as it will create a level -playing field and increase consumer trust in 
claims put on products. With these goals in mind, we consider the following aspects of the new proposed 
rules might need to be considered by the Parliame nt and the Council when adopting the final legislative 
text: 
 
 
Recommendations 
1. We encourage policymakers to consider the importance of flexibility for industry-led 
innovation and development of private sustainability labels. The list of practices banned 
under the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive in Annex 1 of the UCPD should therefore 
be limited to those private labels that are not third-party verified and audited. We would 
also welcome further clarifications on the definition of “certification schemes”. 
2. We recommend that private programmes, which point at environmental friendly products 
that are themselves third-party verified, should not be considered “generic sustainability 
claims” subject to the restrictions under Annex I of the UCPD.

1. Unfair Commercial Practice Directive:  
Allow third-party verified and sustainability labels  
Thanks to their visual simplicity, sustainability labels can help consumers make better informed decisions, 
provided the information is clear, adequately substantiated, and relevant. It also contributes to fair 
competition by allowing customers to easily understand and compare the product information provided. 
We encourage policymakers to consider the importance of flexibility for industry -led innovation and 
development of private sustainability labels. We believe that the proposal should only ban under Annex I 
of the UCPD those labels that have no third -party verification or that have not  been properly audited. 
Meanwhile, the proposal should continue to allow private labels, which are third -party verified through 
robust and credible auditing, to be used in order to encourage more and more companies to provide 
consumers with reliable sustainability information. As “certification schemes”, as defined in the proposal, 
do not yet exist for all aspects of environmental or social sustainability, restricting only to labels that are 
“open to all traders” would significantly reduce the scope of relevant sustainability information that can 
be shared with consumers . It would also significantly reduce innovation in that space, making it more 
complex to develop new labels to cover new progress in sustainability of products or ser vices. Allowing 
operators to innovate and establish robust and credible labels to account for sustainability aspects is 
therefore essential and we would recommend removing the requirement that certification scheme must 
be “open to all traders”. 
 
Additionally, we would also encourage policymakers to further clarify the definition of “certification 
scheme”, the monitoring requirements, and particularly what is meant by compliance -based “on 
international, Union or national standards and procedures”.   
 
Support programmes that facilitate access to credible and robust sustainability 
information 
 
Amazon takes due care in its Climate Pledge Friendly program to  only highlight products that meet high 
sustainability standards, verified through a wide range of external certifications, including governmental 
agencies, non-profits, and independent laboratories. Thanks to the programme, we make it easy for our 
customers to find pertinent information to make  an informed, sustainable purchasing decision , as only 
products meeting one or more of these certifications  will be labelled with the Climate Pledge Friendly 
badge.

We would caution against introducing measures that would restrict initiatives which incentivize 
sustainable consumption patterns.  This is why w e would further recommend clarifying that aggregator 
programmes, badges, or other visual tools , that facilitate consumers’ access to, understanding, and 
comparison of verified and substantiated  sustainability labels , should  not be considered “generic 
environmental claims” and therefore not subject to the restrictions under Annex I of the UCPD.  
 
Clarify the requirements to disclose negative impact of software updates 
 
Many devices provide for automatic software updates, in part to maximize the uptake of critical security 
fixes. Any requirement directed to prior notice to consumers, or giving customers the ability to opt out of 
updates, risks diminishing the uptake and leave customers unprotected. 
 
We would therefore welcome further clarifications on t he prohibition o f omitting information to 
consumers that  software updates will negatively impact a device . Negative impact is a vague and 
subjective concept, and we would encourage policymakers to come forward with further guidance and/or 
standards on what type of software updates would require such disclaimers to consumers.   
 
Recommendations: 
• We encourage policymakers to consider the importance of flexibility for industry -led innovation 
and development of private sustainability labels. The list of practices banned under the Unfair 
Commercial Practices Directive in Annex 1 should therefore be limited to those private labels that 
are not third-party verified and audited. We would also welcome further clarifications on the 
definition of “certification schemes”.  
• We recommend that private aggregator programmes, which point at environmentally friendly 
products that are themselves third-party verified, should not be considered “generic sustainability 
claims” subject to the restrictions under Annex I of the UCPD.     
• We recommend that policymakers provide further clarification and guidance on which software 
updates, which might have a negative impact on certain features of the goods, would have to be 
disclosed to consumers . Negative impact is a vague, subjective phrase and could discourage 
changes that improve customers’ experiences with the device.  
 
2. Consumers Right Directive: Informing consumers on durability and 
reparability of products 
Amazon welcomes the  new information requirements on repair and durability, which will provide the 
necessary information to consumers to make informed sustainable choices. We already provide 
information on guarantee, as well as availability of spare parts for many of Amazon’s products, as well as 
those third -party products that we sell directly  when such information is made available by the 
manufacturer. We would however argue that information about software updates for goods that include 
digital elements/content or services, should be limited to provision of information about security updates 
which are essential to consumer’s safe use of goods. Any additional information requirements, including 
on software updates, should also be aligned with other existing EU legislation.

Digitalisation makes it possible to provide clear and comparable information to all customers at the point 
of sale through online marketplaces. However, we would recommend clarifying that legal accountability 
for the provision on consumer information rests  with the manufacturers as sellers are dependent on 
manufacturers sharing that information and for it to be accurate.

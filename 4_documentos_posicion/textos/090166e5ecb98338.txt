EACA feedback  
on the 
Proposal for a Directive as regards empowering consumers for the green transition 
through better protection against unfair practices and better information  
 
We welcome the  initiative of  the Proposal to amend Directive  2005/29/EC (“Unfair 
Commercial Practices Directive”) as the right approach to enhance consumer protection  
related to environmental claims.  The advertising sector plays a significant role in enabling 
consumers to make clear and educated choices. 
Aware of this major responsibility, the industry has for many years contributed to adopt high 
self-regulatory standards regarding environmental claims. One of them is the International 
Chamber of Commerce Advertising and Marketing Communications Code, which covers the 
issue of misleading environmental claims in its chapter D. Besides, many self -regulatory 
organisations also enforce specific codes of conducts which specifically address green claims, 
or rules included in their general advertising codes which apply to these claims. Moreover, 
agencies and advertisers have recently signed up to the Global Guidance on Environm ental 
Claims, a cross -industry initiative commissioned by the WFA and supported by EACA which 
identifies six key principles that marketers need to follow to make sure they are seen as 
trustworthy and to avoid their brands being accused of misleading environmental claims.  
Therefore, while we believe that changes to the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive can 
foster both consumer trust and the competitiveness of the European Single Market , it is 
however important that these amendments  consider existing self-regulatory practices and 
that additional legislation  can be efficiently enforced by the relevant stakeholders.   To this 
end, we would like to submit several comments on the proposed legal framework:  
 
1. Definition of “environmental claims” 
We underline the necessity to have a clear and consistent definition of “environmental claims” 
as a key component to an efficient enforcement of the amended Directive by relevant 
stakeholders. Currently, the proposal  defines “environmental claims” as: “any message or 
representation, which is not mandatory under Union law or national law, including text, 
pictorial, graphic or symbolic  representation, in any form, including labels, brand names, 
company names or product names, in the context of a commercial communication, which 
states or implies that a product or trader has a positive or no impact on the environment or is

less damaging t o the environment than other products or traders, respectively, or has 
improved their impact over time”.  
We are concerned about associating environmental claims to “a message which implies that 
a product has a positive impact on the environment ” as we consider that this wording could 
lead to  a vast scope of  interpretations to be considered as environmental claims . For 
communications agencies, in charge of creating advertisements and marketing products and 
services on behalf of their clients, the current definition is likely to include, and thus to define 
as “environmental claims”,  advertising which does not necessarily intend to  promote the 
environmental impac t of a given product or service . This would lead to a high degree of 
uncertainty as to the legal framework applying for agencies which create and market 
advertising.  
Therefore, we suggest adapting the definition of “environmental claims” as follows:  
“any message or representation, which is n ot mandatory under Union law or national law, 
including text, pictorial, graphic or symbolic representation, in any form, including labels, 
brand names, company names or product names, in the context of a commercial 
communication, which clearly states or implies that a product or trader has a positive or no 
impact on the environment or is less damaging to the environment than other products or 
traders, respectively, or has improved their impact over time”. 
The same issue arise s in the context of Recital 8. EACA suggests adapting the wording as 
follows: 
In cases where the displaying of a sustainability label involves a commercial communication 
that suggests or creates  the impression  clearly states that a product has a positive o r no 
impact on the environment, or is less damaging to the environment than competing products, 
that sustainability label also should be considered as constituting an environmental claim. 
2. Examples of “generic environmental claims” in Recital 9 
Furthermore, recital 9 provide s a list of examples of generic environmental claims . We 
strongly believe that it should provide additional guidance  rather than simply mentioning 
“and similar statements”. This may create confusion  as to the scope of what constitutes 
generic environmental claims.  
Besides, the list referred to in Recital 9 does not  exactly match the recently updated UCPD 
Guidance’s list. We would suggest referring to the  ICC Framework as it  provides detailed 
explanation on a great number of expressions used in green claims. 
3. Sustainability labels  
EACA recognises the goal of labels to be understandable to the average consumer. A general 
prohibition for companies to advertise through a label that does not rely on a certification 
scheme, as defined in the Article  2(s) ( new), might discourage them from undertaking 
sustainability improvements. In particular, small and medium -sized enterprises may

encounter difficulties when developing new products that have a lesser impact on the 
environment, as they may not be able  to affix logos due to a lack of human, financial, legal 
and technical resources to comply with such technical provisions. 
EACA suggests that SMEs may have the possibility to adhere to sustainability labels which are 
adapted to their compliance capabilities and thus rather simple to apply, while limiting high 
financial and administrative costs. 
 
Finally, we would like to point out the importance of the upcoming legislation t o help 
companies accelerate their green transition in being rather supportive than punitive . 
Therefore, we would welcome the Commission taking into account the aforementioned 
clarification points as they provide further efficient guidance for the communications industry 
throughout Europe.

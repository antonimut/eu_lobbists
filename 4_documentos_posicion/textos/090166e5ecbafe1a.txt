27th of May 2022 
ISEAL submission on EC proposal on empowering consumers for the green 
transition through better protection against unfair practices and better 
information 
ISEAL supports ambitious sustainability systems and their partners to tackle the world’s most pressing 
challenges. ISEAL develops guidance on good practices and brings together a range of multi-stakeholder 
initiatives that manage sustainability labels or business-to-business claims.  
We welcome the Commission’s Proposal for a Directive as regards empowering consumers for the green 
transition through better protection against unfair practices and better information. We support the 
Commission’s interest in raising the bar on credible sustainability claims and we believe that stronger criteria 
for claims-making can play a critical role in eliminating misleading or confusing claims or labels in the EU 
marketplace.  
We share the objectives put forward in the legislative proposal by the Commission: sustainability or green 
claims in the EU marketplace should be clear, relevant, and substantiated to enable consumers to make more 
informed and sustainable purchasing decisions. To further this agenda, we want to provide the following views 
on the current proposal and the role of credible sustainability systems.  
1. We support the proposal stressing that “displaying of sustainability labels which are not based on a 
certification scheme or not established by public authorities should be prohibited” as well as that the 
“certification scheme should fulfil minimum transparency and credibility conditions.” However, we note 
that the only additional detail in relation to these conditions comes in the definition of a certification 
scheme, “a third-party verification scheme that is open under transparent, fair and non-discriminatory 
terms to all traders willing and able to comply with the scheme’s requirements, which certifies that a 
product complies with certain requirements, and for which the monitoring of compliance is objective, 
based on international, Union or national standards and procedures and carried out by a party 
independent from both the scheme owner and the trader.” We believe that additional detail in 
interpreting this definition is needed to support reliable and consistent interpretation and application 
by the EU member states. 
2. We agree that it is important that certification schemes are accessible and open (as in the proposal’s 
definition) and have verification / monitoring of compliance that is objective and is carried out by an

independent (third) party. We also support that the monitoring is based on international, EU or 
national standards and procedures. However, in the case of sustainability standards, our view is that 
minimum transparency and credibility conditions extend beyond the scope of certification activities. 
The ISEAL Credibility Principles and ISEAL Codes of Good Practice are widely recognised international 
references that complement generic standards on audit quality by considering what good practice for 
schemes looks like in the specific context of sustainability. Sustainability integrates environmental, 
social and economic considerations and it is not a fixed state. Sustainability standards and similar 
market-based approaches aim to provide reliable information about the adoption of sustainable 
practices and to drive change over time. A credible claim from any scheme should be proportional to 
the level of verification applied both to the adoption of practices and the ability of the scheme to 
deliver improvements in social and environmental performance over time.  
3. The proposed definition for third-party verification scheme should be supplemented by minimum 
process and governance criteria for schemes. The criteria should go beyond the terms of access and 
monitoring of compliance through audits (which collect point-in-time information) to also consider the 
broader transparency, fairness, and equity of the scheme overall. Key practices that we would consider 
minimum criteria include:  
a. transparent communication of the scheme’s sustainability objectives 
b. meaningful involvement of stakeholders in establishing the performance criteria that support 
these sustainability objectives 
c. robust evaluation of how practices that meet these criteria deliver performance improvements 
d. effective oversight of certification and control of claims  
e. transparent communication of evaluation results and of the evidence that supports any claims 
made 
The ISEAL Credibility Principles and the ISEAL Codes of Good Practice offer a framework for defining 
such scheme responsibilities. We would recommend that criteria for key scheme responsibilities are 
included when interpreting the definition of “minimum transparency and credibility conditions.” 
4. In addition, if verification / monitoring of compliance is based on international, EU or national 
standards and procedures, it will be important to clarify which types of reference standards and 
procedures are included. We would recommend finding a balance between ensuring good practice and 
fostering innovation. For instance, new technologies and techniques (such as collecting information 
through remote sensing and/or through worker voice tools) have the potential to increase the 
efficiency and reliability of verification / monitoring of compliance as compared to approaches that rely 
solely on traditional in-person audits. Innovative approaches should not be excluded from the market 
and there should be minimum transparency and credibility criteria that support a level-playing field 
between these new approaches and conventional approaches to certification. the ISEAL Credibility 
Principles and the ISEAL Codes of Good Practice provides a robust framework for these objectives too.

5. we look forward to the publication of the EC Green Claims proposal and we want to emphasise the 
need for complementarity between these two proposals. In that context, we note the importance for 
the EC of maintaining an eye on the intent of these legislative initiatives to enable conditions that 
incentivise improved sustainability outcomes. It will be important to recognise the market relevance of 
production-phase supply chain certification schemes and the role they play in ensuring improved 
sustainability outcomes in critical production and commodity sectors. From the perspective of ensuring 
clarity and accuracy of communication to consumers, we would like to stress the importance of these 
types of systems and their respective claims in enabling consumers to make more informed and 
sustainable purchasing decisions.  
We would like to congratulate the Commission again for its forward-looking agenda in pursuit of stronger 
social and environmental outcomes and remain available for cooperating.

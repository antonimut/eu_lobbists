The OMCT and IRCT Contribution to the Consultation on the Victims' Rights 
Directive 
 
 
Geneva – Brussels, 31 May 2022 
 
The World Organisation Against Torture (OMCT)  and the International Rehabilitation Council for 
Torture Victims (IRCT) welcomes this consultation on the EU rules on victims’ rights. The adoption of 
the Victim’s Rights Directive (VRD)  considerably strengthened the rights of victims and their family 
members to information, participation in legal proceedings, support and prot ection. However, the 
Directive failed to provide victims of torture and other ill -treatment the level of attention, protection 
and specific support they need.  
 
The absolute prohibition of torture and other cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishme nt 
is part of the core human rights obligations of the EU and its Member States (MS). First, the United 
Nations Convention Against Torture has been ratified by all EU M S. Secondly, Article 4 of the Charter 
of Fundamental Rights of the EU prohibits torture and inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment. 
Finally, the absolute prohibition of torture and other ill -treatment has been enshrined in settled 
international case law, including the ruling of the International Criminal Tribunal for the Former 
Yugoslavia (ICTY), in the case of Prosecutor v Anto Furundzija, which established that “because of the 
importance of the values it [prohibition of torture] protects, this principle has evolved in to a 
peremptory norm or jus cogens, that is, a norm that enjoys a higher rank in the international hierarchy 
than treaty law and even “ordinary” customary rules.”  
 
Despite all of the above, victims of torture and other ill -treatment continue not to be duly protected 
by EU Member States, and a significative number of them have not criminalised torture in accordance 
to their obligations under the UN Convention Against Torture. Victims who have undergone torture 
and other ill -treatment have specific needs and are entitled to specific forms of protection and 
reparation, given the nature and effects of this crime (see for reference, General Comment no. 3 (2014) 
of the United Nations Committee against Torture). Therefore, a specific mention and recognition of 
victims of torture and other ill -treatment as a category with specific needs and revictimisation risks , 
should be included in the preamble (numeral 57) and in Article 22.3  
 
Torture is the intentional destruction of a human being by another. The methods by which severe pain 
and suffering are inflicted vary, but they all have the same objective: to break a person, erase them as 
an individual, deny they are human. Because the effects of torture and other forms of ill-treatment are 
so utterly devastating physically and psychologically, those who survived have urgent and specific 
needs. Medical care, including psychological attention, are absolute priorities, together with legal 
assistance and psychosocial support. Victims of torture and other ill-treatment must also have the right 
to an effective remedy, reparation, redress, including full rehabilitation, right to truth and guarantees 
of non-repetition.  
 
According to international law, torture is always ‘… inflicted by or at the instigation of or with the 
consent or acquiescence of a public official or other person acting in an official capacity’1. The resulting 
imbalance of power makes this crime even more appalling and seriously hinder victims’ capacity to 
seek redress. The fact that the perpetrator often belongs to the same institution that is supposed to

investigate the cr ime puts victims of torture in a particularly vulnerable position. Furthermore, the 
sensitive nature of torture and other ill -treatment leads to the difficulty in recognising victims, their

Below we express three main arguments to be considered in the Directive: 
 
1. Definition of victim should be expanded 
 
In order to be in line with the UN Convention Against Torture 2, a convention ratified by all EU 
Member States, the Directive must expand its concept of victimhood to persons who suffered 
harm, both directly and/or indirectly. The impacts of torture are not limited to the individual 
who was subjected to it. Its immediate family members and dependants also suffer the harm 
of the crime and need support. Currently the Directive only covers family m embers and 
dependents of a victim who died as a result of the crime. The Directive needs to expand the 
scope of victim definition to include family members of persons who have been tortured or ill-
treated even if they survived.  
 
2. Victims of torture need recognition, special protection and support 
 
The scope of the Directive is limited to victims of crimes that are included in the Member States 
respective criminal codes. That leaves out of scope torture victims in MS where torture is not 
specifically criminalised – despite the universal ratification of the UN Convention by MS. Even 
if a MS does not have torture and other ill-treatment as a criminal offence, or if they include 
it but not following international standards, torture victims should be recognised a s such and 
enjoy the rights recognised by the VRD. 
 
This recognition is essential given the nature of the crime of torture and ill-treatment. Torture 
causes extremely severe harm, is a very sensitive crime that is vastly underreported and takes 
place withi n a huge imbalance of power between the perpetrator (a state actor) and the 
victim. That is why victims of torture and other forms of ill -treatment need the special 
protection and support already included in chapters 1 and 4 of the VRD. Additionally, the 
Directive must ensure that torture victims have access to restitution, compensation, holistic 
rehabilitation, satisfaction and guarantees of non -repetition. In order to achieve this, the 
Directive should explicitly include victims of torture as a special group. 
 
Within victims of torture, women, girls and boys and persons deprived of their liberty are 
particularly vulnerable and should be given special protection and assistance. The latter 
includes, persons detained in State detention facilities, women’s detention facilities, juvenile 
system, psychiatric establishments, immigration detention facilities, private-run facilities as 
well as during extra-custodial use of force by police officers (among others).

3. Training of relevant professionals 
 
One of the reasons of the endemic underreporting of torture allegations is the lack of training 
of relevant professionals on how to identify victims of torture and refrain from resorting to it. 
Thus, it is utterly important that police officers, judges, prosecu tors, lawyers and any other 
relevant public official are trained on the main anti -torture human rights standards . That 
should include the UN Convention against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading 
Treatment or Punishment and its General Comments, the European Convention on Human 
Rights and the European Prison Rules, among others. More specifically, the training should 
include the UN Manual on the Effective Investigation and Documentation of Torture and Other 
Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment , also known as Istanbul Protocol,  
which contains extensive and internationally recognised guidelines on how to identify and 
document torture cases.

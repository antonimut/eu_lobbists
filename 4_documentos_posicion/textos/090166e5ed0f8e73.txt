Introduction  
VSE welcomes the initiative of the European Commission  to revise the text of th e EU Victims’ Rights 
Directive and appreciates the wide ranging and ambition of the questionnaire. VSE consulted with its 
members and wider network to answer the questionnaire. Whilst we are supportive of most of the 
questions, some of the proposals seem disconnected to the importance of including civil society actors 
within support and justice processes. In the first part of this paper, we would like to provide specific 
comments on some of the questions of the questionnaires  (the limited number of characters in the 
online survey did not allow us to respond there)  and in the second part we explai n our overarching 
vision for the revision of the Directive and what could be further adjustments, that were not addressed 
in the questionnaire.  
 
I. Detailed answers and comments to the online questionnaire  
 
1. Victims’ ability to access information concerning their rights 
Question 1(a): VSE strongly support the introduction of an obligation for Member States to operate a 
single victim helpline, namely the 116 006 helpline . However, it’s important to emphasise that this 
helpline should not only operate as a point of information and ‘first contact point to direct the victim 
to the service(s) they might need ’ but primarily to provide emotional supp ort, information and 
referral. The Commission Decision on 116 numbers is clear on the objectives of the 116 006 helpline: 
“The service enables victims of crime to get emotional support in such circumstances, to be informed 
about their rights and about ways to claim their rights, and to be referred to the relevant 
organisations. In particular, it provides information about (a) local police and criminal justice 
proceedings; (b) possibilities of compensation and insurance matters. It also provides support in 
finding other sources of help relevant to the victims of crime.”1. 
Further info on VSE recommendations on 116 006 can be found in Section II, title 2.2 of this paper.

2. Victims’ ability to access support services 
Question 3(a): Whilst VSE supports the general notion of European Commission developing guidelines 
on the implementation of the Directive, as a starting point we b elieve that the Directive should set 
out specific provisions on the standards by which services should operate, rather than leaving this to 
guidelines. We believe that the Commission should be more ambitious on the provision of victims’ 
access to support services in the revision of the Directive. Many Member States have used the vague 
drafting of the current Directive to fail on victims’ need to support, and many of them still do not have 
national generic victim support organisation across their country. The revised Directive should include 
sufficient detailed obligations on how support services should operate and include minimum 
standards, that could be supported by guidelines, but this is a crucial issue to be included in legislation.  
Question 3(b): VSE supports the general notion that in any country, there is a combination of generic 
and specialist services operating in collaboration with each other. The Directive should promote this 
approach whilst avoiding creating the risk that Member States dilute lim ited funding amongst large 
numbers of organisations which will limit the ability to ensure those services operate effectively for 
different groups across the territory of the State. It is likely that the best way to achieve this balance 
is by establishing a national support framework which can determine for each country what are the 
most appropriate support priorities,  including need for specialist services,  whilst ensuring that as a 
minimum a national generic victim support service exists which is able to properly cater to the needs 
of all victims and can refer victims to specialist services. To support decisions around service provision 
Member States should carry out national and community needs assessment.  
Question 3(c): other forms of support services s hould include: court service including court 
accompaniment, trial preparation as well as referral to other services as appropriate. 
Question 3(e): while a 'One-stop shop approach' may be the best solution for some victims or some 
situations, it is not necessary the most suitable for all victims nor the most efficient. Based on the 
proposal, the E uropean Commission is conflating two different types of solutions: one is a multi-
agency centre or one stop shop which is a single centre where multiple agencie s come together to 
meet all of the needs of specific groups of victims; and the other would be a mechanism for ensuring 
that all those responsible for working with victims across the country are properly coordinated.  
VSE is of the opinion that a multi-agency centre can be important, particular for the most vulnerable 
victims or specific types of situation, e.g. Child victims (Barnahus), domestic violence (MARAC), sexual 
violence victims (SAC C). However, they should not be considered the only solut ion, even in these 
cases, and should be part of a wider support framework to ensure that no victims are missed and 
victims have sufficient choice.  
On the other hand, a collaborative network of agencies and civil society actors working with victims is 
a separate but equally important requirement for an effective support framework, which VSE supports 
as part of its own support framework. Key elements of that collaboration include full mapping of 
services that exist, effective referral between organisations, consistent information about available 
services to victims through multiple sources , and mechanisms to facilitate the development of 
cooperation between organisations within the network.

3. Victims’ ability to access justice and exercise their rights 
Question 5(c): VSE supports a provision for victims to have access to free legal aid throughout legal 
proceedings irrespective of their level of income, however we acknowledge the difficulty to put this 
in practice for all victims in all cases as this would have significant cost implications. Our view is that if 
free legal aid proves to be difficult to be achieved, then free legal assistance can be delivered through 
multiple ways and not only through provisions of pro bono legal scheme. In some cases, it may be 
sufficient for some victims to be assisted by in-house legal advice coming from victim support services.  
What is important is that at the very least the Directive ensures that all victims within criminal 
proceedings have access to some sort of free legal assistance, from legal advice to legal representative, 
depending on their needs.  
Question 5(d): This question was raised to VSE members but no consensus on a clear answer was 
reached. The key question is whether the priority is to have the status of  party to proceedings or 
whether it is more important for the existing rights in the directive to be applied to all victims of crime 
irrespective of status. VSE would argue for the latter in the first instance and calls on the European 
Commission to remove  the ‘in accordance with the role of the victim’ clause from all applicable 
articles.  
VSE believes that in the cases where victims have a formal party status, sufficient support, both legal 
and emotional, is required to fully benefit from that position. 
 
4. Protection of victims 
Question 7(b): We answered ‘Don’t know’ about the establishment of a telephone helpline available 
24/7 specifically for protection measures as it is not clear for us  what this service would do, how it 
would operate and what problems it would help resolve. 
 
5. Training 
Question 9(a): We answered ‘disagree’ for this question as we think it’s crucial that professionals are 
not only encouraged to attend training but Member States should ensure that all professionals in 
contact with victims re ceive training. We recognise that Member States did not agree on this 
requirement for the current Directive due to arguments around the independence of the judiciary and 
prosecution, we note that the Commission has been ambitious in article 37 of the propo sal for a 
Directive combating violence against women by introducing a requirement for “professionals likely to 
come into contact with victims, including law enforcement authorities, court staff, judges and 
prosecutors, lawyers, providers of victim support and restorative justice services, healthcare 
professionals, social services, education and other relevant staff, receive both generic and specialist 
training and targeted information”. If the Commission believes that the independence arguments are 
not valid for that proposal, it should pursue the same approach in the Victims’ Rights Directive. There 
should not be any differentiation on training obligation for professionals on specific types of crime.

6. Cross-cutting issues 
Question 11(e): we only answered ‘agree’ (as opposed to strongly agree) to this question as we believe 
that such working group should include civil society actors, including victim support services and 
victims themselves. There is a risk that, when governmental actors monitor data collection or analysis 
alone, they minimise what data should be collected or don’t consider the needs of the victims when 
doing so. There is a crucial need for a multi -disciplinary approach and the inclusion of  multi-sector 
actors to this. It is also important th at the European Commission consistently include civil society in 
decision-making, coordination and delivery of victims’ rights.  
Question 11(f): VSE supports the Commission’s initiative to include provisions on digitalisation of 
justice in the revised Directive, based on the Commission’s recent proposal for digitalisation of judicial 
cooperation. However, in doing so, the Commission needs to should ensure that victims’ rights are 
promoted and that Member States are putting victims’ needs at the center. Only effective victim 
participation in justice proceedings, including technical tailored support, a simple language they can 
comprehend, flexibility and safety in terms of evidence provision, and other proposed measures, can 
guarantee that their rights are  effectively implemented. The Commission should also ensure that 
secondary victimisation is minimised while victims’ safe and active participation in proceedings is 
enhanced. The risks related to the modernisation of judicial cooperation should not overcom e the 
rights and needs of victims of crime. Proper safeguards should be foreseen for victims in providing 
evidence through digital means. Finally, civil society, and in particular victim support services, should 
be included in the development of digital tools and procedures as well as the training of professionals 
in the process of modernising judicial cooperation. 
Question 11(g): While we agree with the introduction of such provision, we regret the lack of 
coordination and involvement of civil society in cross -border cases. We acknowledge that countries 
operate different support and coordination framework, however in cases where bo th State and civil 
society actors are operating together on cross-border cases, there is a better understanding of victims’ 
needs and minimum level of secondary victimisation. We recommend that the Commission proposes 
a coordination mechanism which includes civil society actors for both policy development and delivery 
of services.

II. VSE’s overarching recommendations for the revision of the 
Victims’ Rights Directive  
The Directive has been a key instrument which has made a difference to the lives of millions of victims 
of a crime every year. However, gaps in the full and effective implementation of the Directive remain 
and many Member States are still failing to meet their obligations to support and protect victims. 
Moreover, the current text of the D irective is not going far enough to ensure effective support and 
protection to victims.  
The revision of the Directive has the power to strengthen existing rights, establish clearer obligations 
for Member States and establish new rights for victims.  
VSE believes that this can be done through:  
- Strengthening existing rights with clear, more detailed drafting;  
- Developing systemic solutions in particular through a national co -ordination system to 
improve coordination, planning and oversight;  
- Empowering Stat es, victims and organisations to ensure proper implementation through 
evidence, data and enforcement.  
Actions should focus in particular on priority issues relating to information, support, reporting, justice, 
protection, coordination and other cross cutting issues. 
When developing a revised text, the European Commission should ensure that unforeseen 
consequences are avoided. In particular, situations where the State, in implementing new obligations 
or rights, takes over the responsibilities, activities or  funding of existing service providers, especially 
civil society service providers.  
Understanding that EU action is delimited by legal basis, subsidiarity, proportionality and feasibility, 
based on the feedback and experience from VSE members and its wide r partnership network across 
Europe, VSE has highlighted below key recommendations for the revision of the Victims’ Rights 
Directive.  
 
1. Communication with victims  
Despite the rights and obligations set out in the Victims’ Rights Directive, many barriers remain for 
victims to access timely, consistent and easy to understand information, in a way that ensures they 
actually understand it and are able to act on it.  
With a majority of professionals recognising that the current system of information provision to 
victims is ineffective2, VSE suggests the transition from information rights to a coordinated system 
of communication to victims, in which State and non-State bodies must communicate information in 
a coordinated and systemic manner and that follows the following principles:

- Information is available and delivered consistently through different point of contacts with 
victims (law enforcement, victim support, healthcare, campaigns…)  
- Information provided in a combination of formats

- Information is accurate and regularly updated  
- Information is timely and repeated over time  
- Information is in a language the victim understands  
- Information is adapted to individual needs  
Provisions should focus on the information being effectively communicated to victims , where it is 
understood and can be acted upon, whilst victims are empowered to communicate their needs and 
problems.  
 
To that end, provisions of an updated Directive should include:  
 
1.1 A coordinated system of communication 
Set out a more detailed regime by detailing what information should be provided , by who (first 
authorities, victim support, other authorities), how (define simple and accessible language, set out 
different formats) and when (information available before the crime happens, repeated as long as 
needed in a coordinated manner).   
 
The provisions should define the role of and obligations of each authority in providing information and 
detail what information should be provided, when and how, and ensure the development of both  
follow-up mechanisms and inter-agency cooperation and communication mechanisms, as to ensure 
all bodies/authorities are able to communicate information in a coordinated manner. It should explore 
how all authorities in contact with victims, including healthcare, social services, education staff, etc., 
have a role in providing basic information to victims on existing services and where to find helpful 
resources.   
Clear provisions would help in preventing confusion among stakeholders as to their responsibility in 
communicating information, leading to gaps in the information provided, and ensuring that the 
communication of information does not rely on individuals and individual skills, creating inconsistency.  
 
Lack of clarity regarding who should provide information about State compensation: “In Germany, 
there was a lack of clarity as to who is responsible for provid ing information about state 
compensation. Only one interviewee saw himself as being responsible in this regard, and it was a 
member of a support organisa tion. Similarly, in the Netherlands, there was a strong consensus 
among the interviewed practitioners that the police do not inform victims about their rights to claim 
state compensation, and that it is left to victim support organisations or lawyers to provide this 
information”3

In Germany, about half of the interviewees in the survey stated that from the first contact with the

As to how, the Directive should firstly define what simple and easy to understand information means, 
as to achieve a better implementation by Member States. The language currently used remains very 
complex, based on technical and legal language, sometimes using a r eproduction of the legal 
instruments5. This is the case both for information materials provided (written form like brochures 
and leaflets) and information orally communicated to the victims, whether provided by law 
enforcement authorities or judicial authorities6.  
‘A recurring theme across the EU Member States where this research took place is that the police 
use leaflets, brochures or a written letter of rights. Practitioners interviewed were often critical of 
the language and contents of such tools, as they are in a formal, legalistic and difficult language.’7  
 
In Portugal, in practice, throughout the country, when presenting a complaint victims are, first, 
informed of their rights orally and, afterwards, they receive a copy of the Victims’ Statute. The  
information transmitted orally is supposedly adapted according to the victims’ personal 
circumstances and especial vulnerability, if there is one. Even though victims are given a copy of the 
Victims’ Statute and that information is, sometimes, transmitted  orally, which is a commendable 
effort, the truth is that there is no assurance that all victims understand this information. In fact, 
the Victims’ Statute copies are only available in Portuguese, which means that victims who do not 
speak nor understand Po rtuguese do not receive adapted information. Additionally, the Victims’ 
Statute is a rather long legislative instrument and its language is not appropriate to all victims.’8  
 
 
1.2 Delivery of information through multiple points of contact 
Secondly, the Directive should organise a regime where information exists and  is available and 
consistently delivered through multiple points of contact with victims: hospitals, schools, police 
stations, insurance companies...  The updated Directive should ensure that information exists and is 
available at each step of the victims’ journey, including before crime, at the first contact, throughout 
proceedings and when receive assistance from victim support services.

1.3 Timing of communicating with victims / provision of information 
As to when, the updated Directive should ensure that professionals know and understand when to 
provide information to victims, in a timely manner, by detailing which information should be provided 
at what stage. Most often the amount of information provided at the first contact is extensive and 
can overwhelm the victim. The provision often does not take into account which information victims 
need at a certain moment and which information can be provided at a later stage, also not taking into 
account the capacity of the victim to absorb and understand the information provided12.  
In addition, many victims still do not receive timely information about their case and crimi nal 
proceedings, including the release or escape of the perpetrator or suspect from detention.  
Only 29,9% of victims always receive information about release or escape of the perpetrator, with 
only 30% professionals experience systematic compliance with this right in all cases when victims 
request to know about such circumstances13.

1.4 Remove exemptions 
The Directive should remove exemptions, according to which information is communicated only upon 
request of the victim and/or is communicated only depending on the victim’s role (e.g. victim is party 
to proceedings).  Such exemptions rely on the presumption that victims will receive timely information 
on the possibility to access/receive such information (e.g. information about their case in Article 6), 
which is often not the case. Whether provided by first contact authorities, judicial authorities or victim 
support, information remains incomplete.   
 
According to research, information provided to the victim at first contact is still not at a satisfactory 
level14. Level of information provided: 
- Full information: 20% 
- Most information: 38%  
- Partial information: 26%  
- Little information: 14%  
- No information: 2%  
 
Information, advice and support related to the rights of victims provided by victim support 
services15:  
- Most victims, 40.9%, often receive this information from victim support services.  
- Only 33% always receive this information.  
 
 
1.5 Interpretation and translation available and accessible to all victims who need it 
The Directive should ensure that the  right to interpretation and translation  is ensured beyond 
making a complaint  and participating in criminal proceedings, at each step of the journey. With 
accessibility of translators/interpreters being a major barrier to a ccessing that right, the revised 
Directive should look at maximising accessibility – however ensuring some quality standards are in 
place to ensure that translators/interpreters can effectively respond to victims’ needs .  Research 
indeed shows that victims who speak another language than the national language  still encounter 
many barriers in accessing information in a language they understand, including information materials 
and in accessing translation/interpretation. This issue has been identified at different stages, from the 
moment victims make a complaint (article 5) and throughout criminal proceedings (article 7).  Victims 
are most of the time unable to make a complaint in their own language, and face a lack of availability 
of translators/interpreters to do so. When translators/interpreters are available, they often lack 
training in trauma-informed and victim-sensitive communication and lack understanding of the legal 
system.  
 
According to research conducted by VSE, only 38% of victims able to make a complaint in their 
own language16.

2. Coordinated framework of support  
While the Victims Right’s Directive reflect some of the minimum services that should be offered by 
support providers, it does not set out the framework in which professional services should operate, 
nor the standards to follow to ensure quality of support. The revised Directive should therefore move 
towards a clear and comprehensive national support framework through:  
2.1 Ensuring that victims support services exist  
More detailed obligations for Member States to ensure that victim support services exist and a re 
established in all EU Member States: Articles 8 and 9 need to better drafted so that Member States 
have to ensure that victim support services exist and are accessible in their country. This can be done 
by requiring that Member States need to have a combination of different coordinated support services 
working in cooperation with each other:  
- organisations that support all victims of crime: their sole responsibility is to provide support 
to victims and they are specialised in delivering support in a tailored manner (gendered, child 
sensitive, etc.). They are nation-wide, holistic and can assess victims’ needs following defined 
quality standards; 
- organisations that support only certain groups of victims (e.g. women victims of violence, 
LGBTI+, children victims, etc.) and  
- organisations or institutions whose sole responsibility isn’t to support victims but which need 
a sufficient level of expertise in victimisation because they may encounter and work with 
victims (special victim support units in police and prosecution services, hospitals with a sexual 
assault centre, social workers with specific training, etc.)  
A 'One-stop shop approach' should not be the only solution suggested to deliver support. While this 
option may benefit some victims, other may not find this approach suitable for their need (a person 
who does not want to report the crime to the police for example). 
Stronger obligat ion for funding of support services : based on the proposal for a Directive on 
combating violence against women, Member States should ensure that support services, both 
governmental and non -governmental, are provided with sufficient human and financial reso urces. 
Public funding could be conditioned to the respect of defined and adequate quality standards for the 
delivery of support. Funding should also be made available in sustained way, and should cover several 
years. Having to apply for funding every single year provides for a very unstable way of working, and 
makes long -term investments and developments of the services very difficult to achieve.  
Organisations often rely on a combination of national and local government funding (annual or multi-
annual), EU funding, private donations, and other sources such as fee waivers, pro bono services and 
volunteering.

Research show that 98.25% of professionals believe that further funding for victim support services 
is needed to be made available in their countries. 96,25% of respondents believe that more funding 
is needed to provide better support for victims18. 
 
In Sweden, on average, municipalities pay 3 SEK (0.29 euro) per inhabitant to victim support 
services. 
 
Any cost in providing victim support should be regarded as an investment. In time, through adequate 
victim support services, the State should see large returns on its investment through reduced health 
care costs, increased productivity and a more effective justice system. In view of this, upfront funding 
must be found to ensure effective services and rights.  Investing in adequate victim support services 
will help victims return to work quickly and reduce health implications they may experience after being 
victimised, thus reducing the burden of crime on the healthcare system. Different options exist to fund 
victim support services19 and the European Commission should encourage States to ensure sufficient 
funding for the set up and running of victim support services in their country.  
Research on social return investment shows that for every euro invested the State receives at least 
5 euros back. In Northern Ireland, an analysis conducted in 2016 showed that for every £1 invested 
in Victim Support Northern Ireland the likely social value created is approximately £920.  
 
VSE conducted a similar cost benefit analysis for the introduction of victim support services in 
Serbia, and found that for every 1 euro spent on victim support, State receives back at least 4.34 
euros benefit.  
 
2.2 Ensuring that victim support services are accessible  
Better definition of accessibility of support service: The Directive needs to set out in explicit way what 
access to support means. Accessibility needs to be understood in the broadest sense of the word and 
to mean that victim support services need to be easily reached or entered, be easy to obtain or use; 
and be easily understood or appreciated. Criteria can be developed in that sense (geographic  
coverage, physical access, awareness of service, …).  
When considering access to services, several broad categories of factors can be taken into account21: 
- The physical ease with which a victim may reach that service.  
- Other types of barriers which may hinder a person’s ability to reach a service. For example, if 
the service is only open during restricted hours, or if certain eligibility criteria are applied e.g. 
the need to report the crime or co-operate with the police.  
- The awareness that victims have of a service. In simple terms, if victims don’t know a service 
exists, they will not have access to it.

3. Access justice and exercise their rights  
The Victims’ Rights Directive should be revised so that victims can fully enjoy their rights throughout 
the criminal proceedings, be heard and supported, in order to maximise their participation in the 
justice system while minimising secondary victimisation.  
3.1 Emotional and legal support  
Victims’ ability to access justice and exercise their rights often depends on their ability to participate 
in criminal proceedings with the necessary emotional and legal support. VSE thus supports the 
introduction of the following measures:  
- Accompaniment by a professional for all victims of a crime throughout the proceedings , 
from the moment of making a complaint, as an autonomous right  
- Permanent court-based support services to support victims (and witnesses) arriving at court 
without pre-booked support 
- Broader access to free legal representation for victims, at least for serious crimes and 
whenever it is essential for victims to fully enjoy their participatory rights, and removing 
current accessibility and availability barriers in Member States.    
There should be broader access to free legal representation for victims, at least for serious crimes and 
whenever it is essential for victims to fully enjoy their participatory rights. 
 
In the 2019 FRA study, over 70 percent of t he victims interviewed indicated that they would have 
liked to receive more legal advice. Due to the lack of information about their rights and the lack 
of advice on how to enjoy these rights, victims seem even more discontent in countries where 
they have broader participatory rights, including the right to become party to the criminal 
proceedings27. Almost 80 percent of interviewed practitioners in study agreed that victims should 
be informed more effectively about the criminal proceedings and their potential roles in them.28

4.2 Measures to prevent secondary victimisation 
VSE supports the inclusion of broader measures for the victim’s protection from seco ndary 
victimisation, in particular the minimisation of contact between the victim and the offender. 
The physical design of courts and police stations is essential in that regard. We believe that separate 
court entrances and waiting areas should be made available in all existing court buildings. Whenever 
new courts are constructed, it should be ensured they include such separate spaces. 
In some Member States, a new design for existing courts is being implemented progressively as the 
new court buildings are already built with different waiting areas. 
As for older buildings, in a worrying number of cases, the professionals indicate that facilities simply 
do not exist (separate waiting areas in more than one third of all cases, separate entrances in over 
40% and even the simple measure of scheduling appointments at different times is not available in 
more than 16% of cases).38 
 
Online access to courts should also be facilitated, establishing access to digital tools for evidence 
provision, following of proceedings or submission of claims, among others. 
A 2019 FRA study shows that a large majority of victim interviewed considered the presence of the 
offender during the criminal proceedings as intimidating. It also indicates that current measures to 
protect victims from instances of secondary victimisation appear insufficient.39 
The EU Justice Scoreboard found that victims can only access their ongoing case electronically in 
14 Member-States, to different degrees. It also found that victims can submit written statements 
online (including initial applications) in 15 Member-States.40 Only 11 Member-States provide the

it is sometimes the preference of judges for traditional means that hinders its use.41 
 
4.3 Measures to protect the privacy of victims  
The personal information of victims which is not essential for the circumstances of the case, including 
their home address and phone number, should be retracted before the case file is shared with the 
defence and, where applicable upon request, with the broader general public. VSE further b elieves 
that measures pertaining to the protection of the victim’s privacy should be available to all victims if 
they so require, and not be reserved for specific types of crimes. 
In relation to media intrusion, the existence in all Member States of self-regulatory measures by the 
media, which ensure the balance between the right to privacy of victims and freedom of expression, 
should be guaranteed, as already set out in the Directive.

5. Training  
Training of professionals who may come into contact with victims is of utmost importance to ensure 
that victims are treated in a respectful, professional and non -discriminatory manner. Training is 
particularly important for justice practitioners, such as law enforcement agencies, judges, 
prosecutors, lawyers. Improved responses to victims and witnesses of crime can empower individuals 
and can make them more willing to disclose victimisation and more supportive of the justice process. 
However, any other professionals who may enter in contact with victims should have minimum 
training requirement on basic element of victimisation.  
A revised Directive should require that Member States ensure that professionals who engage with 
victims as part of their job receive appropriate training and education, in particular on areas related 
to understanding victimisation and its impact, victims’ needs, effective and empathetic 
communication with victims, respectful and appropriate treatment, risk assessment, secondary 
victimisation. The Commission should therefore introduce the following points in the Directive:  
5.1 All professionals receive training 
The Directive should introduce a stronger obligation  for Member States to ensure that all 
professionals who may enter in contact with victims receive training and that they are  not only 
encouraged to do so , based on the proposal for a Directive on combating violence against women.  
These professionals should include: law enforcement authorities, court staff, judges and prosecutors, 
lawyers, providers of victim support and restorative justice services, healthcare professionals, social 
services, educational and other relevant staff.  
Research show that while most of professions have to complete some compulsory training modules 
in different areas, they are rarely required to complete a comprehensive training module which is 
dedicated to victims’ rights and needs, or a training module which aims to sensitise them regarding 
issues relevant to victims of crimes43.  
 
5.2 Content and format of the training should be detailed  
The Directive should set out what training should include at a minimum, such as recognition of signs 
of victimisation, understand impact of crime and the consequences of victimisation, soft skills, 
communication and empathy, risk assessment, …  
In addition, the Directive need to require that Member States support the design and delivery of 
training which is diverse, immersive and maximise the use of new technologies.

7. Other topics not covered by the questionnaire  
VSE would also like to highlight additional topics that are not covered in the online questionnaire but 
that should be considered by the European Commission when revising the Directive.  
7.1 Prevention measures 
On the basis of Chapter 5 of the proposal for a Directive combating violence against women, VSE 
suggests the introduction of a new chapter on prevention,  covering awareness-raising campaigns, 
education measures and programmes on the risk of victimisation, the consequences of crime and the 
role victim support services in society.  The chapter would also target specific measures for persons at 
greater risk and prevention measures within specific institutions, such as the workplace, school and 
universities, etc.  
7.2 Legal remedies  
In the case o f breaches in defense rights, very strong rights of redress exist with significant 
consequences for individuals, organisations, investigations and trials. Such rights should also exist for 
victims, and should not be limited to criminal proceedings.  
7.3 Practical implementation of Directive 
To maximise the transposition and practical implementation of the Directive, the Directive should 
include an obligation for the European Commission to produce practical implementation reports for 
each Member State, and not only compliance test. Measures should be put in place to empower the 
Commission to oversee the good implementation of the Directive and to be in a stronger position to 
carry out infringement proceedings as necessary.

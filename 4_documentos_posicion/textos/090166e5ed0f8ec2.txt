 
 
Revision of the Victims’ Rights Directive 
acquis: recommendations to better 
support trans victims of crime 
Contact: francesca@tgeu.org 
31 May 2022 
 
TGEU supports continued work on full implementation of the existing Victims’ Right 
Directive (hereafter VRD), including strengthened monitoring and reporting. In practice, trans 
people are not able to fully exercise their rights under the directive, as documented by FRA 
LGBTI survey data and anecdotal evidence from our member organisations. This must be 
improved. 
 
TGEU also supports amendments that strengthen the full protection and support for 
particularly vulnerable victims of crime including (un)documented trans migrants; trans sex 
workers and those in precarious or unregulated work; and trans people in detention.  
 
The follow areas are particularly relevant for trans victims of crime: 
 
Article 1 (1). Undocumented migrants 
● The implementation of this article needs to be more carefully monitored and the 
rights of victims protected, no matter their residency status. 
● TGEU’s member organisations in the Netherlands (TransUnited Europe), Sweden 
(RFSL), and Italy (MIT) report that trans migrants in their countries have been targeted 
for deportation after coming in contact with authorities regarding a crime committed 
against them. Other undocumented trans migrants are therefore very hesitant to 
report crimes. 
 
Victims’ support organisations and services 
● These provide essential services for trans people who often don’t feel safe going to 
formal institutions or police out of fear of harassment or a transphobic response. 
○ According to the FRA 2019 LGBTI survey, only one-fifth of trans people 
reported the last hate-motivated physical or sexual attack they experienced to 
any authority, and one-third of those who didn’t report to the police did not 
out of fear of a homophobic or transphobic response.  
● As limited data is available TGEU has collected anecdotal information from our 
member organisations who provide direct support to trans victims of crime on the 
ground. They have indicated that among the most important support they provide is 
accompanying a victim to authorities to report a crime. This is especially needed for

trans migrants (documented and undocumented) who may have limited local language 
knowledge and who need translation and interpretation support, as well as for other 
trans victims, including sex workers, who fear further victimisation.  
○ Often this is done voluntarily but as an essential component of enabling 
victims to access their rights needs to be structurally included into victims’ 
support frameworks (and funded). 
● The effectiveness of support services could be improved through strengthened third 
party reporting possibilities - allowing support services to report crimes themselves to 
authorities. This would help address the hesitance many trans people feel about 
directly reporting a crime to authorities and would improve data collection around 
rates and types of crime. With improved data collection (also addressed below) the 
exact needs of victims’ can be better met.  
● Structural support for trans-inclusive psychological and legal support services is itself 
key to strengthening their third-party reporting function: For example, TGEU’s 
ProTrans1 partners’ ability to collect testimonies and data from victims of bias-
motivated crimes was most successful when other services a (potential) victim might 
want to use were also available: e.g., post-traumatic counselling, accompanying the 
victim to the police, and providing legal advice and legal representation. 
● As laid out in the EU LGBTIQ Equality Strategy, it is essential that funding 
opportunities are provided directly to community-led service providers that are trans-
sensitive, either from the European Commission or from Member States themselves. 
 
Article 23. Right to protection of victims with specific protection needs during 
criminal proceedings 
● TGEU recommend that article 23.2(d) be amended to allow the victim to choose an 
interviewer of the sex or gender they feel most safe with:  
○ Article 23.2(d): all interviews with victims of sexual violence, gender-based 
violence, or violence in close relationships, unless conducted by a prosecutor 
or a judge, being conducted by a person of the same sex [or gender] as 
[chosen by] the victim, if the victim so wishes, provided that the course of the 
criminal proceedings will not be prejudiced. 
 
Intersex victims 
● In order to ensure appropriate support and protection for intersex victims of crime, 
who are at significant risk of experiencing violence and harassment, TGEU calls for 
the explicit inclusion of sex characteristics as grounds in the preamble of the VRD: 
○  recital 9 on non-discrimination 
○  recital 17 on gender-based violence

○ including “sex characteristics” in recital 56 on individual assessment: this is 
important so that intersex persons, who are at risk of intersexphobic or bias-
motivated attacks based on their sex characteristics, can be better identified 
as needing special protection 
● TGEU considers this important because some trans people are also intersex, and 
because it is essential for the safety of our broader community that all LGBTI people 
are appropriately protected and can access their rights as victims of crime.  
 
Training of staff 
● To prevent repeat victimisation and to address issues of underreporting, all staff who 
come in contact with victims of crime need to be trained in the specific needs of trans 
people, including those at high risk of experiencing intersectional discrimination, such 
as trans people with a disability, trans migrants, trans sex workers, and young trans 
people. This training must be compulsory. 
● Concretely, staff must be sensitive to a trans victim who does not have documents 
that match one’s identity, and use their chosen name and pronouns; they must take 
appropriate steps to keep a sex worker or migrant safe from being targeted for arrest 
or deportation; and be able to support young trans people, particularly minors who 
may not have parents/guardians to provide necessary consent in formal processes. 
 
Shelters 
● Trans people often face harassment and violence at shelters, discouraging them from 
attempting to make use of the shelter. In other cases, trans people are completely cut 
off from accessing them; this is especially problematic for trans women in women’s 
shelters who are often denied access. Trans people need to have access to safe 
spaces following a violent attack.  
● This can be improved by ensuring additional beds at shelters are set aside for trans 
people, and by making funding for victim support services tied to the conditionality to 
not discriminate in providing these services on grounds of gender identity or gender 
expression. 
 
Data collection 
● Limited data is currently available in the EU of the true extent of crimes against trans 
and other LGBTI people, especially of violent crimes. However, what has been 
documented shows that violence against trans people occurs at an alarming rate.  
○ The FRA 2019 LGBTI equality survey2 found that 34% of trans respondents 
had experienced a violent or sexual attack in the preceding five years.  
 
2 https://fra.europa.eu/en/data-and-maps/2020/lgbti-survey-data-explorer  
 
○ In its most recent Trans Murder Monitoring update3, TGEU reported 
that between October 2020 and September 2021, eight trans people 
were murdered in EU Member States, all of whom were trans women or 
transfeminine people.  
● To improve our collective understanding of the nature and extent of crimes against 
trans and other LGBTI people, TGEU supports comprehensive and systematic data 
collection - as well as provisions for human and financial resources - beyond age and 
gender to include other characteristics and possible bias motivations (i.e., sexual 
orientation, gender identity, gender expression, sex characteristics; and whether it 
was a homophobic, transphobic, intersexphobic attack).  
● This data is essential to truly understanding the prevalence of crime against trans and 
LGBTI people, which informs policy, support services, and funding needs.  
● For specific guidance on how to collect data that is segregated by sexual orientation, 
gender identity, gender expression and sex characteristics, see: https://tgeu.org/wp-
content/uploads/2021/12/2021-attachment-briefing-for-european-statistics-on-
population-consultation.pdf 
 
Monitoring and reporting 
● So that trans victims of crime are able to fully access their rights, implementation of 
the VRD needs to be strengthened across all areas, and this can be improved through 
regular and compulsory monitoring and reporting by member states. 
● Additionally, the Commission can support this by providing pro-active and clear 
guidance that the MS are to implement the VRD without discrimination on grounds of 
the gender identity, gender expression or sex characteristics of a victim.

EWL’s recommendations on the Victim’s Rights Strategy: 
STRENGTHEN  PROTECTION AND SUPPORT OF VICTIM’S OF  
MALE VIOLENCE AGAINST WOMEN AND GIRLS 
 
The European Women’s Lobby (EWL) welcomes the commitment of the European Commission to 
strengthen the support, protection and the rights of victims in the European Union (EU) and to develop 
an EU Strategy on Victims' Rights for 2020-2024. 
 
The EWL is the largest umbrella organisation of women's associations in the EU, working to promote 
women's rights and equality between women and men, representing a total of more than 2000 
associations1. Putting an end to violence against women and creating a society of peace is one of the 
priority aims  of EWL, working with its members at national and EU level and experts in the EWL 
Observatory on violence against women and girls2. 
  
Despite the current framework on victims’ rights developed by the EU and the Council of Europe in the 
last two decades, ma ny victims of male violence against women in the EU are not granted their rights. 
There is not enough access to specialised support and protection services that acknowledge and address 
the particular vulnerability of victims and the risk of secondary and r epeat victimisation, retaliation and 
coercion. An EWL Observatory analysis on the implementation of the Council of Europe Convention on 
combating and preventing violence against women and domestic violence, the Istanbul Convention, has 
recently shown the l ack of adequate understanding of the structural causes, characteristics and 
consequences of the phenomenon of male violence against women in both legislation and policies. This 
has created serious consequences of secondary victimisation of women as there a re failures in early 
identification of victims, as women are not believed during the criminal and judicial proceedings, and as 
there are failures to provide adequate support and protection (see Annex I).

reported the most serious incident of partner violence to the police and the most serious incident of non-

In the current global context of the COVID-19 pandemic, early evidence shows a notable increase of male 
violence against women and girls and high risk of harm. We must use this moment to recognise that our 
current political responses have been insufficient in recognising inequality, harassment and violence so 
many women face on a daily basis; and in ensuring victims’ rights, support and protection mechanisms. 
To ensure Governments never again leave women and girls in such situations of risk and vulnerability, 
political partners, frontline services, women’s organisations and  those affected should act together to 
ensure the realisation of the EU and European framework of protection of victim’s rights4.  
Develop a specific action plan focused o n victims of all forms of male violence against women and 
embrace a gendered perspective, in coherence with the new Gender Equality Strategy. 
 
The European Women’s Lobby considers that the Victims’ Rights Strategy should seek to strengthen the 
support, protection and rights of victims of all forms of male violence against women. It should be 
aligned and coherent with the recently adopted “A Union of Equality: Gender equality Strategy 2020 -
2025”. In this Strategy, the EU commits to do all it can to prevent and combat gender -based violence 
(defined as the violence that is directed against a woman because she is a woman or that affects women 
disproportionately), support and protect its victims and hold perpetrators accountable.   
 
In coherence with the new Gen der Equality Strategy, the Victims’ Rights Strategy should have a specific 
focus on victims of all forms of violence against women. A specific action plan should be developed aiming 
at further developing the rights of victims of male violence against wome n and girls and to meet the 
standards of the Council of Europe Convention on preventing and combating violence against women 
(Istanbul Convention), as further detailed below.

In line with Article 6 of the Istanbul Convention, 5 the Victims’ Rights Strategy should embrace a gender -
sensitive perspective and recognise that male violence against women is a distinctly gendered 
phenomenon6. The Action Plan should propose to deepen on the implementation of the current (and 
future) Victims’ rights instruments on the basis of a structural understanding of the root causes of violence 
against women and acknowledging the specific needs of protection of its victims. 
 
This Action Plan should be part of the larger EU strategy to ensure a comprehensive EU legal framework 
to disrupt the continuum of violence against women and support its victims (See EWL recommendations 
in annex I).  As recommended to the European Commission, an EU Coordinator on ending male violence 
against women and girls  should be appointed to lead the development of this comprehensive legal 
framework and should act in conjunction with the EU Coordinator on victim’s rights and the EU Anti -
Trafficking Coordinator to implement the action plan, which should consider the aspects detailed below.  
 
1. Meet the standards of the Istanbul Convention 
 
The Gender Equality Strategy sets a priority concluding the EU’s accession to the Council of Europe 
Convention on preventing and combating violence against women (Istanbul Convention) and commits to 
propose measures to achieve the same objectives as the Istanbul Convention.  
 
In order to reinforce protection and support to victims of violence against women, the Victim’s Rights 
Strategy should prioritise achieving the same objectives and standards as the Istanbul Convention at EU 
level. Efforts in the Strategy should be placed to ensure that the EU legal framework on victim’s rights is 
in alignment with the Istanbul Convention when it comes to protection of women victims of violence to 
reinforce accountability mechanisms. 
 
The Strategy should acknowledge the fact that the Victims’ Rights Directive and the other EU instruments 
have a more limited scope when compared to the Istanbul Convention as regards the protection of women 
victims of violence and should commit to assess and close these gaps.

2. Assess and enhance the EU legal framework to support and protect victims’ of male violence 
against women and girls 
  
The  Victims’ Rights Strategy action plan to protect victims of violence against women should commit to 
develop an assessment of the EU legal framework on victim’s rights, and should identify gaps and 
areas where the current framework needs enhancement when it comes to adequate protection of 
victims of male violence. This assessment should be an integral part of the overall assessment of the EU 
legal framework on combating all forms of violence against women.  
 
Special attention should be brought to: 
 
● Directive 2012/29/EU (The Victims' Directive) on common minimum standards on the rights, support and 
protection of victims. 
● Directive 2011/36/EU on preventing and combatting trafficking in human beings and protecting its 
victims. 
● Directive 2011/93/EU combating the sexual abuse and sexual exploitation of children and child 
pornography. 
● Directive 2011/99/EU on the European Protection Order (EPO)  
● Regulation (EU) No 606/2013 on mutual recognition of protection measures in civil matters 
● Council Directive 2004/80/EC of 29 April 2004 relating to compensation to crime victims  
● Directive 2014/41/EU regarding the European Investigation Order in criminal matters  
 
This assessment should be done to ensure alignment of the EU legal framework with the following 
international instruments: 
 
●  The Istanbul Convention in all its provisions. Special attention should be considered to the following 
chapters and articles of the Convention: 
○ Article 3 on definitions. 
○ Article 6 on gender-sensitive policies  
○ Chapter II on Integrated policies and data collection. 
○ Chapter III on prevention, specifically articles on awareness raising, education, training of 
professionals, preventative intervention and treatment programmes. 
○ Chapter IV on protection and support  
○ Article 29 on civil lawsuits and remedies, article 30 on compensation and article 31 on custody, 
visitation rights and safety.  
○ Articles 45 on sanctions and measures, 46 on aggravating circumstances, 47 on sentences passed 
by another Party and 48 on prohibition of mandatory alternative dispute resolution processes 
and sentencing. 
○ Chapter VI on Investigation, prosecution, procedural law and protective measures.

This legal assessment should  be coupled with a mapping analysis of the implementation of the current 
legal framework on victims’ rights to women victims of male violence a. According to Article 29 of the 
Victims’ Rights Directive, the Commission must submit a report to the European P arliament and to the 
Council, assessing Member States’ compliance with the directive, “accompanied, if necessary, by 
legislative proposals”. As this report is still pending, EWL recommends an exhaustive analysis of how the 
directive’s provisions are reflected in national law and also are effective in practice, looking extensively at 
the status of the special support and protection services that victims of male violence require “because 
of the high risk of secondary and repeat victimisation, of intimidation and of retaliation connected with 
such violence”, including violence that is committed in a close -relationship and sexual violence (Recital 
17, 18 and 57 of the Directive). This implementation assessment should consider as well the greater risk 
faced by groups of women who are subject to multiple and overlapping forms of discrimination. 
 
 
3. Enhanced support to victims of male violence against wome and girls in a Directive on preventing 
and combating violence against women. 
 
The European Commission must propose an EU Directive establishing minimum rules concerning the 
definition of criminal offences and sanctions in the area of violence against women, in order to ensure 
direct effect in the legal order, which will help harmonising the principle of victimhood across the EU7 . 
(See EWL Recommendations in annex I).

The Directive should introduce common provisions, taking into account a gendered perspective, to 
strengthen the prevention of this crime and minimum rules for the protection of the victims thereof, in 
alignment with the provisions of the Istanbul Convention. The Directive should extensively address the 
particular needs of victims of violence against women.  
 
Currently, there are three instruments that strength en the Victims' Rights Directive for specific forms of 
crimes and groups of victims – who are considered more vulnerable and as such requiring dedicated 
provisions: the directive on trafficking in human beings; the directive on child sexual abuse; and the 
directive on combatting terrorism. 
 
However, despite the fact that the Victim’s rights Directive acknowledges women victims of gender-based 
violence as a vulnerable group that requires special support and protection 8, there is no complementary 
instrument devoted to ensure a set of dedicated provisions that take into account for the specific and 
structural nature of gender ‑based violence 9. Also, despite the recognition of “trafficking and sexual 
exploitation of women and children” in TFEU Art.38 as internati onal crimes, there remains no 
comprehensive legislation to tackle sexual exploitation specifically, meaning victims of this recognised EU 
crime cannot access the provisions of the Victims’ Rights Directive. 
 
The EU Directive should also mandate the need to include this structural understanding of the root causess 
of violence against women in the transposition and implementation of all its provisions, including the 
provisions with regards to victim’s rights. 
 
The Directive should call Member States to work closely with civil society organisations, including 
recognised and specialised non-governmental organisations in this field working with survivors of violence

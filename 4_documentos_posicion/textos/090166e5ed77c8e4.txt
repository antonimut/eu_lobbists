An enabling regulatory framework is critical for the refurbishment sector to unleash its full 
potential and help create a circular economy . In this position paper, refurbed would like to 
present concrete proposals for targeted improvements in the Commission’s proposals. 
 
 
 
The market for refurbished products is relatively young and growing in the EU. As such,  there 
is currently no consistent definition of “refurbishment” in the Single Market making it difficult to 
differentiate this service from repair and/or remanufacturing. Moreover, no obligation falls on 
refurbishers, often to the detriment of quality and safety.  
 
A. Operational definitions 
Refurbishment is giving devices a second life (through collection, testing and repair if needed) 
before returning them to other consumers or businesses. All data are wiped and the new owner 
can use the product as if it were new while benefiting from a professional warranty.  
Refurbishment is therefore a standalone process, very different from  remanufacturing and/or 
repair which are of lower standards. The Commission’ definition does not reflect the above 
elements and creates confusion about what refurbishment is about.  
 
Our 
proposal  
→ Update the definitions related to manufacturing (Article 2.16) and refurbishment (Article 2.18) to 
account for their key characteristics and clearly differentiate one activity from another.  
 
 

3 
 
B. Missing definition of spare parts 
A comprehensive circular economy is based on refurbishment (reuse) and repair. Spare parts 
are the core pillar of this second-hand sector of activities , enabling longer products’ lifespan. 
The Commission refers to spare parts in the Annex I related to product parameters of the ESPR 
but does not define the concept.  
 
Our 
proposal  
→ European institutions should  provide a definition of spare part s that addresses the  issue of 
quality, without which the objective to foster repaired second-hand products will remain a vain 
wish.  
 
C. Operational criteria for the refurbishment sector 
Refurbishment needs to be expressly addressed in the ESPR and refurbishers identified as 
specific actors vis-à-vis other categories of professionals, such as manufacturers, dealers or 
service providers.  
A lack of legal framework creates loopholes allowing unqualified people  to provide 
refurbishment services, often to the detriment of quality and safety, leading consumers to turn 
away from this solution. Without a clear, specific and consistent legal framework , the 
refurbishment sector will not be in a position to meet the EU objectives for circular economy.   
 
Our 
proposal  
→ European institutions should set objective criteria (for example minimal quality of spare parts; 
steps to follow for the refurbishment process) and obligations (for example creation of a 
certificate) for the refurbishment sector. This would allow refurbishment to be carried out by 
skilled people in order to ensure product safety and quality. Ultimately, public authorities should 
create national refurbishers’ registers based on the criteria set by the EU.

To create a circular economy, changes must target the whole product lifecycle. This has to start 
with the product design and the availability of all relevant information.  refurbed welcomes the 
Commission’s proposals on performance/information requirements but believes these could be 
further strengthened to ensure no obstacles can be implemented to prevent reuse and repair.  
A) Changing the status quo with manufacturers 
A sustainable product means both making it last longer  and facilitating reuse /repair where 
necessary. Yet, too often, manufacturers impede reuse by preventing or limiting independent 
refurbishment. Those restricting practices  (spare parts availability, interoperability between 
software and components, traceability on what was made in the product’s former life, etc.) are 
brakes to developing a thriving circular economy in Europe while locking-in end users.  
The EPSR should guarantee a level playing field between all the actors of the value chain where 
manufacturers can no longer decide unilaterally the components/software to be used or made 
available. The Commission partly addresses these issues in Annex I  related to product 
parameters but leaves out some key aspects:  
- Encouraging ease of repair and maintenance: The Commission remains vague on what 
should be the key characteristics of spare parts despite their importance.  
 
Our 
proposal  
→ Include within the spare parts characteristics the origin, quality, number of uses and previous act 
of repairs.  
 
- Avoidance of technical solutions preventing refurbishment/repair : The Commissions 
remains too generic and should further specified the technical solutions it refers to.  
 
Our 
proposal  
→ Include the following examples: limiting interoperability between software and components; use 
of contractual, hardware or software technique preventing refurbishment/repair.  
 
B) Put refurbishment on an equal footing with other economic activities 
As indicated above, refurbishment is a standalone process which is different  from 
remanufacturing and/or repair. Yet, The Commission often forgets to refer to such activity  
throughout the ESPR, especially when it comes to: 
- Actors that shall have access to and/or may introduce  information in the product 
passport (article 8).   
- Interested parties involved in the Ecodesign Forum (article 17).  
 
Our 
proposal  
→ Refer to the refurbishment sector where necessary as this activity is and will be central to achieve 
the EU circular objective. 
 
C) Divert unsold products to circular solutions 
A persistent brake to circular economy is the demand and supply imbalance for secondary raw 
materials. In the same way as the recycling industry, refurbishers may lack supply of used 
products and components.

Therefore, refurbed welcomes the Commission’s proposals to prohibit economic operators from 
destroying unsold consumer products  and foster transparency about the destination of their 
discarded unsold consumer products.  
 
Our 
proposal  
→ These additional resources will remain untapped without an obligation to divert those products 
towards economic services in line with the waste hierarchy  in order to be reintegrated in the 
circular economy loop.

A) Economic incentives for refurbished products 
refurbed strongly supports the Commission’s proposal giving Member States the possibility to 
provide economic incentives to products with the higher class of performance.  Such measure 
is necessary to correct market failure and counter-balance negative externalities.  
refurbed recalls that refurbished products come with less CO2 and raw material consumption. 
This lesser environmental/carbon footprint should be rewarded just like products with high class 
of performances  given their numerous benefits for consumers, local employment and the 
environment. 
 
Our 
proposal  → Include refurbished products within the products eligible to economic incentives.  
 
B) Making clear and key information available to empower consumers 
Consumers need reliable and relevant information in order to be able to make informed choices 
and embrace a circular economy. The Directive on Empowering Consumers for the Green 
Transition goes in the right direction by introducing additional pre-contractual information to be 
provided to consumers.  
The introduction of a reparability score, or other repair information (availability of spare parts, 
repair manual) should the former be unavailable, are a strong signal towards consumers, which 
will influence their demand. Yet, refurbed believes consumers should be further encouraged in 
this habit evolution with adequate tools in order to make enlightened and confident choices.  
 
Our 
proposal  
→ Include in pre-contractual information the average price of spare parts, their origin and number 
of previous uses as those are of prime interest for consumers.  
→ Develop a label attesting to the quality of refurbishment in order to consolidate consumer’s trust 
in their choice.  
 
C) refurbed requests regulatory consistency on marketplaces 
Marketplaces represent a specific category of players in the refurbishment industry as they 
provide the platform for transactions between resellers and consumers. refurbed also 
guarantees a high level of quality and consumer protection by selecting all resellers present on 
their platform and enforcing strict quality management requirements. 
Article 29 of the proposed ESPR  sets out the obligations of online marketplaces and online 
search engines in particular concerning cooperation with dealers and market surveillance 
authorities. It also specifies that market surveillance authorities should have the power to order 
an online marketplace to remove illegal content. 
 
Our 
proposal  
→ Align the final version of article 29 with the requirements set in the Digital Services Act in order 
to avoid regulatory contradictions.

Article 2(16).  'remanufacturing’ means an industrial process in which a product is 
produced from objects that are waste, products or components and in which at least 
one change is made to the product that affects the safety, performan ce, purpose or 
type of the product typically placed on the market with a commercial guarantee. 
‘Remanufacturing’ means an industrial process made by the manufacturer and 
affiliated companies  in which a product is produced from objects that are waste, 
products or components and in which at least one change is made to the product that 
affects the safety, performance, purpose or type of the product typically placed on 
the market with a commercial guarantee. 
Article 2(18). ‘refurbishment’ means preparing or modifying an object that is waste or 
a product to restore its performance or functionality within the intended use, range 
of performance and maintenance originally conceived at the design stage, or to meet 
applicable technical standards or regulatory requirements, with the result of making 
a fully functional product. 
‘Refurbishment’ means testing, deletion of personal data, and where necessary, the 
maintenance or repair of  an object that is a p roduct or waste by a registered 
professional to check or  restore its performance , quality or functionality within the 
intended use, range of performance and maintenance originally conceived at the 
design stage, or to meet applicable technical standards or regulatory requirements, 
with the result of making a fully functional product, with a professional warranty,  
before making it available on the market.

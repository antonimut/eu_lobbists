INDUSTRIAL EMISSIONS DIRECTIVE 
 
Revision of the Industrial Emissions 
Directive 
EUROGYPSUM’s position  
on the Commission’s proposal 
 
10 JUNE 2022 
 
 
The European Union’s Industrial Emissions Directive (IED) is a major policy instrument 
aimed at preventing or reducing emissions into air, water and soil, as well as preventing 
waste generation by industrial installations. When adopting a new proposal to r evise the 
IED on 4 April 2022
1, the European Commission set the objective of  s upporting the 
European Green Deal and the EU’s Zero Pollution Ambition by reviewing the existing 
measures to address pollution from large industrial installations  and ensuring t hey are 
fully consistent with climate, energy and circular economy policies.  
 
Eurogypsum, the European federation representing the gypsum extractive and processing 
sector, as well as the producers of gypsum solutions, such as plaster and plasterboard, 
supports the European Green Deal’s objectives and is committed to supplying materials 
and products which enable Europe’s transition to a climate neutral , pollution free 
and circular economy, particularly with solutions for building renovation and lightweight 
construction. Gypsum being an eternally recyclable material, it is an essential mineral in 
construction, for the manufacturing of plaster and plasterboard solutions, as well as in the 
production of cement. We contribute to Europe’s strategic autonomy by an exclusively 
domestic supply of gypsum, the EU being a net exporter to other geographies. Our sector 
also considers it as a cru cial duty to use natural resources in the most efficient and 
environmentally sustainable  way, and to reduce all externalities  of our extractive, 
processing and manufacturing activities to the minimum.  
 
A number of policy and regulatory tools are in place at EU and national level to address 
environmental aspects of industrial production . Their articulation with an expanded 
IED needs to be considered carefully, to avoid excessive or unnecessary regulations. 
 
The new IED proposal looks in particular at expanding the scope of the directive, in order 
to cover some extractive activities . As in previous statements made before the 
presentation of the IED proposal, Eurogypsum would express some concern over  this 
proposed expansion. Our perception (based on more than 150 gypsum extractive sites in 
Europe), is that the proposal is not justified, given the case of other minerals being 
excluded from the scope of IED. There does not appear to be any  added value of using 
the IED as the most suitable tool  to regulate well -managed, low impact and small- scale 
activities. In addition, the very nature of mineral extraction requires a tailor-made

We are particularly surprised and concerned by the specific consideration of gypsum 
supply within the new proposal, as opposed to other construction minerals. Gypsum, 
which is primarily used for the manufacturing of construction products 2, is usually and 
rightly classified as a construction mineral by EU, National Authorities and statistical 
classification systems for economic activities, such as NACE 34. While the definition of 
“industrial minerals”, which is proposed for addition as point (48) in Article 3 of the IED 
explicitly excludes “construction minerals”, we fail to u nderstand the inclusion of gypsum 
under industrial minerals  - for which the extraction and treatment is proposed to  be 
covered by the IED. The Commission’s staff working documents indicate a clear decision 
to exempt quarrying activities from the scope of the directive. As outlined by the name of 
the statistical classification of gypsum extraction under the Eurostat NACE codes , our 
activities clearly fall under the category of quarrying, together with other stones, sand and 
clay. Furthermore, much of the gypsum  extracted is used to create products covered by 
NACE code 23:62 (Manufacture of plaster products for construction purposes). This clearly 
illustrates that gypsum and plaster products are covered under the definition of 
construction minerals. 
 
We believe that the classification proposed in the IED proposal should therefore be revised 
in light of  the actual nature of extractive activities carried out. Eurogypsum and its 
members are happy to support EU institutions to obtain a more accurate view of gypsum 
extractive activities, supplying further information or organising site visits wherever useful.  
 
The extraction and treatment of gypsum  follows a process very similar to other 
construction minerals and will therefore have similar environmental impact s, protective 
and regulatory measures in place. The main features of gypsum extractive methods and 
treatments are as follows: 
• The extraction of gypsum rock is carried out – either above or below the ground - 
purely by physical means, e.g. explosive blasts or ripping.  
• Engines and conveyor belts are used for loading and hauling of the mineral, not 
pipelines. 
• Mineral crushing is rarely conducted in the quarry. Where it is carried out in the 
quarry, the process involves the same mechanical/physical methods  as for 
other stones (jaw crushers, impactor crushers). 
• The separation of sulphate rock and overburden is conducted exclusively with 
physical/mechanical means. No chemical , other extraction  or treatment 
techniques are used ; no heat processing  is required in gypsum quarries . The 
impurities present in the deposit are usually carbonates, clays, limestone, dolomite,

• Gypsum dust  generated during extractive activities is soluble and does not 
harm the respiratory system, in contrast to other mineral dust. As an example, 
in Germany, soluble gypsum is excluded from the application of the general dust 
limits under occupational exposure limits (OELs) . Regarding the environment, 
gypsum dust has positive effe cts comparable with the application of lime , as it 
provides calcium as a source for buffering and ion exchange and decreases 
aluminium toxicity in soils – hence the commercial use of gypsum in agriculture 
and forestry.  Besides this, gypsum is also authorised as a food and feed  
additive in Europe (E516) at a level of quantum satis.  
• Gypsum extraction tailings are not complex: Impurities and low-grade gypsum, 
which are discarded for the supply of material, were actually already present in the 
deposit. These are not substances resulting from the processing of materials , but 
actually impurities already present in the deposit. No environmentally hazardous 
mining waste, heaps or by -products are generated during the extraction. The 
deposit is fully utilised; the overburden and dead rock is completely returned for 
recultivation of the quarry area, to keep the soil functions that the rock itself does 
not significantly provide. The sulphate in the gypsum (or anhydrite) is naturally 
part of the geogenic “preload”, as is the chemistry of all other layers of rock. All 
elements are fully examined and checked within the framework of emissions and/or 
mining permits – with an environmental impact assessment (EIA) required in both 
cases.  
 
While we understand that the environmental impact of extractive methods and treatments 
is a decisive factor in the IED’s classification of quarrying and mining activities when it 
comes to the directive’s application scope, all the elements above demonstrat e the very 
similar nature of such extractive methods  for gypsum as well as other construction 
minerals. We therefore call for consistent treatment of all construction minerals, including 
gypsum. 
 
In addition to the above, the extraction of gypsum follows very strict rules , is heavily 
regulated and the companies involved exercise high standards of self-regulation. 
• Where gypsum is quarried as a near-surface mineral , all extractive activities 
already have to comply with the EU nature protection framework, including the 
Natura 2000 and Birds and Habitats Directives. An impact assessment is performed 
at the planning stage, before the start of the permit procedure.  
• Through strict aftercare procedures, gypsum quarries provide a high potential 
to develop and enhanc e biodiversity. Many examples in our sector
5 show that 
gypsum quarrying is perfectly possible in Natura 2000 sites  and other sites with 
high protection status, due to the responsible approach taken by the various 
operators.  
• The protection of ecosystems and the environment is a major duty for our 
industry, also during operations. In October 2021, Eurogypsum signed a C ode of 
Conduct
6 on Species Protection in the Extractive Sector, together with BirdLife  
Europe & Central Asia and other extractive sector associations , to create, support 
and protect temporary biotopes inside operating quarries. The Code was endorsed 
by the European Commission’s DG Environment.

Furthermore, the economic structure of gypsum quarries shows similarities with other 
extractive sites currently considered for exemption under Article 3 (48). The 154 European 
gypsum quarries are in their broad majority sites with 10-15 employees, and often less 
than 10. Only a handful of large quarries have production ranges above 100,000 and the 
European average for a gypsum quarry is close to 40-50,000 tonnes per year. In addition, 
a large number of quarries produce less than 15,000 tonnes per year. We therefore believe 
that on the basis of proportionality and consistency, there is clear case for an exemption 
for our sector.  
 
In light of the above, Eurogypsum would invite EU institutions to ensure a real level -
playing field between actors of the quarrying sector, by amending the proposal according 
to our suggestions in the attached annex. The European gypsum sector is particularly 
committed to avoiding pollution in extractive activities, complying with strict standards set 
by legislation and voluntary initiatives . We look forward to a constructive dialogue with 
institutions and other stakeholders to achieve the most  favourable framework for a safe 
and sustainable sourcing of materials, essential to achieve the European Green Deal’s 
ambitions.

Justification 
Gypsum is a construction mineral, using no chemical, heat or water, but only mechanical and 
physical extraction methods. More than 90% of the quarried gypsum is used for construction 
purposes. Gypsum should therefore be removed from the proposed list of industrial minerals 
in Annex I to the Industrial Emissions Directive.

Amendment B  
Proposal for a directive 
Article 1 – point (3) – point (f) – point (48a) (new) 
Directive 2010/75/EU 
Article 3 – point 48a (new) 
 
Text proposed by the Commission Amendment 
 
(48a) ‘construction minerals’ means 
minerals used for the manufacturing of 
construction products , including 
aggregates, brick clays, gypsum , natural 
ornamental and dimension stone;  
Justification 
For legal certainty, a definition should be added for “construction minerals”, which are 
excluded from the scope of “industrial minerals”. Based on the European Commission’s 
homepage dedicated to construction minerals, the listed applications of raw materials 
indicated in the regular studies by the EU Joint Research Centre on the EU’s list of Critical 
Raw Materials, as well as Eurostat NACE statistical classification of economic activities, the 
definition of “construction minerals” should include aggregates, brick clays, gypsum, 
natural ornamental and dimension stone.

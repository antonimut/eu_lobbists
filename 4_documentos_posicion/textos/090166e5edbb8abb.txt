Subject: Proposal for a Regulation establishing ecodesign requirements 
for sustainable products 
 
Plastics Recyclers Europe (PRE), the association representing the plastics recycling industry, 
welcomes the publication of the proposal for a Regulation establishing ecodesign 
requirements for sustainable products (ESPR) and repealing Directive 2009/125/EC, as an 
opportunity to foster product sustainability in the EU, in the context of the EU Green Deal and 
the Circular Economy Action Plan. 
PRE welcomed the revision of the several ecodesign Regulations integrating design for 
recycling and with recycled content requirements, and we look forward to the establishment 
of ecodesign criteria for an increased set of products. We especially support the extension of 
the Regulation’s scope to furniture and textiles, key product groups to which design for 
recycling and recycled content requirements would apply. 
PRE sees the ESPR as an opportunity to set comprehensive ecodesign requirements for an 
increased number of products to be placed on the EU market, which will allow for 
harmonisation of rules across the Member States (MS) while incentivising the development 
of sustainable products, central element for the transition to a circular economy. PRE believes, 
that provided products are designed to be placed on the market with their end-of-life in mind, 
the development of circular and sustainable products will be fostered, with frontrunners 
already setting the path for using recycled plastics in industry sectors other than packaging. 
This will also allow reaching the EU Green Deal’s objective that all packaging shall either be 
reusable or recyclable by 2030 in an economically viable manner.  
Often perceived as a challenge to economic viability, recycling must be supported by 
legislative measures addressing increasing quantities of recyclable materials for waste 
management operations that sit higher in the waste hierarchy. To this end, targets have 
proven to be effective only where a reliable methodology can be enforced in a harmonised 
way at the EU level, also to monitor the advancement towards both waste generation reduction 
and increased circular products being placed on the market. Standards also help in the 
achievement of such results, especially when driven by bottom-up interests in the industry.  
Overall, the role of the EU is to guide the industry towards the transition to a circular economy, 
offering a legislative framework filling the current policy gaps concerning interpretations and 
definitions, often very different in the EU MS national legislations.

1. Recyclability requirements for products 
Recycling and recyclability refer to two different notions related to the circularity of products. 
In the former, the focus is on the end-of-life management of products being placed on the 
market, the latter focus on the design stage, and is aimed at placing on the market products 
compatible with the existing recycling infrastructure (in terms of sortability, separability of 
components, presence of glues, types of polymers used…). 
Design for recycling is key in the concept of recyclability and enabling the use of recycled 
materials in new products. By taking into account the product’s end-of-life treatment in design 
decisions, the circularity of such products is improved. Innovations in this field could also 
translate into closing the loop for different industry sectors other than packaging, by 
developing products compatible with existing waste management processes. 
Despite its mention in Recital 9, PRE noticed that the term “recyclability” was not defined in 
Art. 2, nor included as part of the ecodesign requirements in Art. 1 and 5, with a mere reference 
to the “possibility of (…) recycling”.2 This does not reflect the importance of recyclability in 
product design, which is paramount to creating sustainable products. Poor design negatively 
impacts the potential to recycle products and to generate quality recycled materials, leading 
to a loss of valuable resources. Therefore, PRE calls for a definition of “recyclability” in Art. 1, 
Art. 2 and 5 of the ESPR proposal. 
PRE considers ‘recyclability’ as the precondition for a product or packaging to be recycled, 
where four conditions are met: 
1. The product must be made with a plastic that is collected for recycling, has market 
value and/or is supported by a legislatively mandated program. 
2. The product must be sorted and aggregated into defined streams for recycling 
processes. 
3. The product can be processed and reclaimed/recycled with commercial recycling 
processes. 
4. The recycled plastic becomes a raw material that is used in the production of new 
products. 
 
Without clearly defining recyclability for products and only referring to the WFD recycling 
definition, the ecodesign requirements to be developed by the EC Delegated Acts could 
potentially set divergent measures on recyclability. Definitions are very relevant for a 
Regulation to ensure that measures are easy to be implemented in the EU MS national 
legislations. In this regard, the definition for ‘ecodesign’ in Art 2(6) should also provide for a

In support of increasing plastic products’ recyclability, PRE has developed with RecyClass,3 a 
methodology to assess the recyclability of plastic packaging. This value chain platform 
gathers brand owners, recyclers, and converters collaborating on establishing clear guidance 
on design for recycling for packaging. 
The overall objective is to improve plastic packaging to make them compatible with existing 
plastic waste streams and recycling processes. Based on testing completed at the sorting 
and recycling levels, it has developed ‘Design for Recycling Guidelines’4 which provide insight 
on the compatibility of different elements of a packaging, such as caps, labels or adhesives, 
with given recycling streams and thereby, serve as a valuable tool for the design of plastic 
packaging to be recyclable. The methodology of RecyClass will be further developed to target 
applications for plastics other than packaging. 
Given the development of product-specific Delegated Acts as part of the new Ecodesign 
Regulation, the design for recycling criteria established in RecyClass can be used as a basis 
to define recyclability as part of the ecodesign requirements for specific categories of 
products. 
 
2. Recycled content and calculation method 
The increased use of recycled content is essential to improve products’ sustainability while 
reducing their negative environmental impacts. Thus, PRE welcomes the inclusion of recycled 
content as part of the ecodesign requirements.5 
As specified in Art. 32, measurement and calculation methods are necessary to demonstrate 
compliance with the ecodesign requirements. Such methods will be set out in the relevant 
product-specific Delegated Acts. 
Considering recycled content, PRE strongly believes that a harmonised and transparent 
methodology is needed to calculate the amount of recycled content contained in products. 
This methodology must guarantee the traceability of waste along the value chain, as it is 
paramount to ensure reliable and accurate claims on recycled content in products.6

Further, credit transfers and allocation of recycled inputs must not be allowed, as it provides 
for the possibility to transfer such inputs between recycled outputs, recycling sites and a 
balancing period (e.g., the period during which the recycled content calculation is undertaken), 
thereby losing waste traceability. 
We would also like to outline the need for the new Ecodesign Regulation to align the 
calculation method for recycling and recycled content with the ones developed in the context 
of the Waste Framework Directive, Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive, and Single-Use 
Plastics Directive, to avoid confusion for the recycling value chain actors.  
Further, the methods used for measuring the amount of recycled content in products have a 
direct effect on the claims. PRE believes it is essential that claims on recycled content reflect 
the sustainability of the associated product, including considerations of the environmental 
impacts of its production and recycling processes. In this sense, the criteria established under 
the EU Taxonomy Regulation Delegated Act on climate mitigation and adaptation, for the 
manufacture of plastics, must demonstrate that their life cycle GHG emissions are lower than 
the production of virgin plastics should be considered for products’ sustainability. 
Overall, PRE stresses the need to establish a calculation method for recycled content which 
incentivises the production and use of waste-derived materials rather than allowing for 
artificial claims to be made on recycled materials in products, driven by market demand 
without pushing the industry to improve the design of their products and increasing recycled 
content. 
 
3. Digital Product Passport  
PRE supports the establishment of a digital product passport aimed at improving access to 
information about products’ characteristics to both consumers and economic operators. We 
would however like to point out potential issues for plastics recyclers. 
First, as mentioned in Art. 7 paragraph 5(c), the information contained in the digital product 
passport should include “the concentration, maximum concentration or concentration range 
of the substances of concern, at the level of the product, its main components, or spare 
parts”.7 While agreeing with the need for transparency of the chemicals contained in plastic 
products, the implementation of this system will prove to be difficult at the recycler level. 
When a plastic recycler receives a batch of sorted plastic waste, it contains a variety of plastic 
articles depending on the waste streams. Items are usually compressed and/or damaged 
when arriving at the recycling facility, and often arrive in a shredded state, with single items 
not recognisable. Therefore, it is not practically possible to look at and scan each item’s digital 
passport code, since this step is not compatible with the recycling processes separating 
plastics from contaminants and different polymers (based on a certain property like polymer,

practical implementation of the digital product passport is crucially needed to ensure its 
efficiency. 
 
4. Substances of concern 
The current EU legislative framework on chemicals includes the REACH Regulation (EC) No 
1907/2006, the CLP Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008, and the POP Regulation (EU) 2019/1021, 
which already provide for extensive and appropriate rules. 
Requirements related to substances of concern are included in the ESPR, as part of the 
ecodesign requirements and information requirements in the Digital Product Passport.8  
In the Impact Assessment of the ESPR proposal, it is mentioned that the use of certain 
substances in specific products can be restricted when the main reason is to improve a 
product’s performance and that the substitution of substances in existing products known to 
hamper recycling can be promoted by banning or imposing maximum concentration 
thresholds.9 
Concentration thresholds already apply for products and recyclates, as under the POP, RoHS 
and REACH Regulations Regulation, meaning that the introduction of different concentration 
limits would come in conflict with such Regulations while increasing the complexity for the 
recycling value chain actors.  
Further, careful assessment in the substitution of substances is needed, to avoid regrettable 
substitution by which a substance is replaced by another which will likely be restricted for 
several years. In addition, consideration for the impact of substances on the recycling process 
is also important, as the use of some substances can render plastics non-recyclable, thus 
going against the goal of increasing the circularity of products.

substances which are already covered by the chemicals legislative framework.

About Plastics Recyclers Europe 
Plastics Recyclers Europe (PRE) is an organization representing the voice of the European plastics recyclers who reprocess 
plastic waste into high -quality material destined for the production of new articles. Recyclers are important  facilitators of the 
circularity of plastics and the transition towards the circular economy. Plastics recycling in Europe is a rapidly growing sector 
representing €3bn in turnover.

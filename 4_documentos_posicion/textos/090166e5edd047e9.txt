 
 
 
 
 
 
Federchimica’s response to public consultation on the proposed 
Ecodesign requirements for sustainable products regulation (ESPR) 
 
 
Federchimica recognises the overall object ives of the European Commission proposal on the 
Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR) and believes that the Regulation will have a 
significant impact on its members as producers of chemicals (substances and mixtures). To achieve 
the ambitious targe ts of the ESPR it is necessary to involve the chemical industry, which is well 
positioned to contribute to sustainability through its products and technologies. 
 
Federchimica has analysed the proposal focusing on the implementability, workability and clarity of 
this proposed regulation, and on how this legislation interacts with existing legislations that regulate 
sustainability requirements of products. 
 
Federchimica points out the complexity of this regulatory framework going into a significant level of 
details regarding requirements for products, components, intermediate products and substances and 
the many interfaces with existing legislations.  
 
Federchimica’s comments aim at the improvement of the ESPR proposal, assuring simultaneously 
its workability and the competitivity of European companies. 
 
Key Messages 
• Avoidance of duplication of existing legislations, specifically chemical regulation for 
substances, and a clear interface between the different legislative frameworks  are 
fundamental topics to be addressed. 
• Digital Product Passport:  the information requirements relating to  chemical substances 
should be workable. 
• Sustainability requirements should be comparable, enforceable, and limited to the most  
relevant criteria and substances in the final product.  
• Allow additional methods to assess environmental impact  (e.g. Life Cycle Approach, ISO 
14025 and ISO 14040 series) and amend the PEF methodology to assess the benefits of all 
circular feedstocks. 
• Full EU wide harmonisation: rules for specific product parameters should be the same in all 
European countries. Clarify that Member States should not have the possibility to set specific 
product parameters. 
• No disadvantages for Europeans producers vs. non-EU competitors. 
• Strong incentives to bring at scale the market of sustainable products and a pha sed 
implementation timeline.

Avoidance of duplication of existing  legislations, specifically chemical 
regulation for substances : a clear interface between legislati ve 
frameworks 
 
The ESPR is closely related to other legislative frameworks, such as drawn from the waste, 
chemicals, industrial emissions and consumer policies. It will be of upmost importance to ensure an 
adequate workability of such legislative interfaces, without overlaps, by setting a clear scope for the 
requirements covered under each of the legislative frameworks. For the interfaces concerning 
horizontal European Union law applicable to all or a wide number of sectors, Federchimica would 
recommend including the key elements of these interfaces within the ESPR regulation  (or in 
delegated acts, if product -specific). This will enhance transparency, clarity , predictability and 
harmonization across the sectors, facilitating the implementation of the ESPR requirements by the 
concerned industry. 
Federchimica recognises the attention that the European Commission intends to put in avoiding 
double-regulation, both for products that are already subject to product-specific legislation and for all 
those aspects already normed through the REACH and CLP regulations on substances. 
In particular, Federchimica acknowledges that ESPR does not enable the restriction of substances 
based on chemical safety (art. 6(3) and recital 22), that will remain entirely within REACH and that 
should be based solely on risk-based approach.  
However, it is not totally clear how the ESPR restriction process  “primarily for reasons other 
than chemical or food safety, of substances present in products or used in their manufacturing 
processes which negatively affect products’ sustainability” will be implemented in practice , 
especially taking in to consideration t he statement “chemical safety is a recognized element of 
product sustainability” ( recital 22). Since ‘chemical safety’ should not be managed by the ESPR 
Federchimica considers that ESPR should clarify how this fit with existing legislation on chemical 
management. 
Therefore, the process for managing a chemical under ESPR must be clarified and 
Federchimica asks to the European Commission to explicitly include the interface between 
REACH and ESPR (notably in terms of scope, e.g., article vs. products, intermediate products vs. 
intermediates) as well as the procedure that will be used to re gulate the use of substance s in the 
legislative text of the ESPR.  
In principle, the restriction process is (and it should remain) regulated under REACH. If a product-
specific restriction is needed under the ESPR for environmental sustainability reason, it should be 
based on an impact assessment of the entire value chain involved and it should be a dynamic 
process to be revised in case of changed conditions. 
Therefore, in case the EU Commission intends to restrict Substances of Concern via ESPR for 
reasons other than chemical safety, there should be a defined procedure allowing for stakeholder 
consultation and the substantial involvement of ECHA. 
 
Substances of concern (SoC) 
 
Sustainability and hazardous substances are not mutually exclusive. In fact, it has to be taken into 
account that SoC can not only significantly contribute to the goals of the Green  Deal, but in some 
cases they are indispensable (e.g. in terms of durability or performance of products/articles ). This 
should be considered when developing sustainability product related criteria. 
 
 
The harmonization of definitions of SoC sh ould be pursued . The definition of SoC under the 
ESPR should be aligned  with the interpretation provided in the Chemical Strategy for 
Sustainability (CSS), and in other pieces of legislation such as biocides  regulation, more focused 
on the most concerned hazard substances. 
The SoC definition under Article 2 (28) (b)  of ESPR would not be manageable. Therefore, 
Federchimica requests to the European Commission to reduce the categories included in the 
definition to the more severe. 
Moreover, Federchimica suggests to set up a step by step approach, starting from substances 
included in Article 2 (28) (a) and gradually moving to substance included in Article 2 (28) (b).  
This approach will facilitate the implementation by companies (especially SMEs), allocating 
economic and administrative burden in a broader range o f time, assuring the compliance starting 
from the most hazardous substances.  
Furthermore, Article 2 (28) (c) defines that any substance hampering recycling can be a SoC. The 
chemical industry understands the European Commission’s aim to  cover substances hampering a 
specific sustainability driver (re -use and recycling of materials are specifically mentioned). For 
example, this could be a substance that would not allow the recycling of packaging. This raises the 
following considerations: 
• Recyclability strongly depends on the recycling method, which evolve over time – similar 
reasoning applies for reusability.  
• The use of secondary raw materials strongly depends on mandatory requirements related to 
specific regulations for product’s applications and actually is not allowed for all uses.  
 
Recyclability should be evaluated against state -of-the art recycling technologies, as well as other 
waste management decisions such as the organisation of closed loops, as well as restriction of 
secondary raw materials for well -defined uses, but allow ing their use in other applications to 
maximise resource efficiency.  
Moreover, a technology neutral approach should be considered, taking into account the ongoing 
technological developments (e.g. advanced recycling) that would allow to deal with legacy 
substances that make recycling via traditional processes (e.g., mechanical recycling) more 
challenging.  
Having a clarification on the wording of Article 2 (28) (c) would avoid hav ing substances restricted 
based on outdated information on the feasibility of recycling properties. Furthermore, Federchimica 
recommends amending Article 2 (28) (c) in order to consider recycling aspects in the scoring 
process of the PEF and other sustainability requirements.  
 
Given that the Ecodesign requirements shall also apply to substances used in the manufacturing 
process, Federchimica recommends that ESPR should only regulate the substances that are 
and remain in the end -product; that can be measured; and if there is a direct consequence for 
sustainability or recycling. Please note that the substances in production processes outside the 
EU cannot be checked, leading to reduce the competitiveness for EU producers compared to 
extra EU companies. 
 
Digital Product Passport (DPP) 
 
Federchimica acknowledges and supports the need to increase transparency and ensure traceability 
of chemicals in products, its components and intermediates used along the value chain. The Digital 
Product Passport (DPP), as introduced under the ESPR should assure that purpose. 
 
 
Nevertheless, Federchimica is concerned on the burden on companies (especially SMEs)  in 
term of resources to assure the DPP set-up, implementation and workability. Industrial sectors must 
be adequately supported in the DPP implementation by Authorities, assuring their competitiveness.  
Crucial for the success of the workability of the DPP, Federchimica also suggests to consider the 
user-friendliness of DPP as a vehicle to involve all the supply chain actors.  
Protection of intellectual property (IP) and confidential business information (CBI) is also essential 
for business competition and is a continuous driver for innovation. Although the ESPR considers 
provisions that allow for the protection of IP/CBI (e.g., Article 5 (5) (e), Article 10 (h)) it is not yet clear 
what will be the procedure for a non -disclosure request due to IP/CBI reasons. We would 
recommend that only the actors directly involved in the value chain gets a ccess to information 
necessary for the final product passport (one up, one down principle ) and the specific set of 
information should be tailored according to the type of actor (e .g., specific economic operator, 
recycler etc.). 
When considering the feasibility of the information requirements, Federchimica would like to share 
with the European Commission its concern on the feasibility and workability to track all  the 
substances of concern throughout the life cycle of products, as mentioned under Article 7 (i.e., more 
than 8,000 substances according to the Cefic-Ricardo Impact Assessment Study on CSS).  
Federchimica recommends that the tracking of substances of concern should only apply to 
the relevant substances present in end products. Moreover, Federchimica suggests that ESPR 
regulation should only address the substances which negatively affect product’s sustainability 
aspects, including recyclability on which performance criteria can be established (Article 6).  
Federchimica recommends a threshold -based approach  directly inserted into the ESPR 
Regulation as it is currently applied overall under REACH and CLP. The threshold should be equal 
to or less stringent than those already indicated in the regulations on substances  (i.e. 0.1% for 
SVHC).  
We support the Com mission proposal that unit of reference of DPP should be established on the 
basis of  the nature of the specific product. Most information requirements (e.g., carbon and 
environmental footprint) should be set at the level of the product model. This will facilitate the uptake 
of the DPP, as companies can use existing data systems to impleme nt the DPP, thereby avoiding 
unnecessary costs (incl. environmental costs related to data storage) and complexities associated 
with information at the level of the individual product.  
Article 9, paragraph 1 c requires product identification for the DPP in accordance with norm ISO/IEC 
15459:2015. However, this is an unnecessary constriction. To ensure a robust, low cost, simple and 
easy to use solution for globally unique identification of products the ESPR should set as mandatory 
minimum requirement for globally unique product instance identification the “IEC 61406 Identification 
Link”. For use cases where a standardized encoding of information into the globally unique ID has a 
benefit for the customer or the supply chain, the IEC 61406 can optionally be co mbined with other 
standards, e.g., ISO IEC 15418 and ISO IEC 15459-x. 
Federchimica would like to highlight the risk of providing the same information under different 
legislations. Synergies are already in place to share information under other legislative frameworks 
(e.g., Poison Center notifications, SCIP notifications). Federchimica encourages the European 
Commission to continue taking the necessary actions to avoid duplication of requirements in order 
to maintain the quality of the data shared and to reduce administrative burden for stakeholders.  
 
 
 
 
Product Environmental Footprint 
 
Article 5 (m) refers to environmental and carbon footprint as product aspects that could be the object 
of Ecodesign requirements for appropriate product groups for all stages of their life cycle.  
Federchimica suggests clarifying the measuring method to be used to calculate the environmental 
product footprint, in order to ensure homogeneity in the approach and enforceability.  
We would welcome additional clarification from the European Commission on how the current 
limitations of the PEF assessment approach will be addressed to allow companies to perform a 
reliable assessment of the carbon and environmental footprints.  
PEF should not be considered as the only methodology to calculate the environmental 
product footprint for the following reasons: 
• The PEF databases and Category Rules (PEFCRs) still need further development. Currently, 
these PEFCRs are only available, or in the process of being developed, for around 25 product 
categories. Establishing new PEFCRs will require considerable time and effort. 
• Even where a PEFCR has been developed for a specific product category, the outcome may not 
offer sufficient granularity to permit comparative quantitative assessments between individual 
products if it is (overly) reliant on the use of generic secondary data for key phases of a product’s 
lifecycle  
• It only covers 16 impact categories, leaving aside several key environmental impact categories 
(e.g., biodiversity) that may be relevant for many products. 
 
As such, we recommend that EU policymakers broaden the definition of “environmental 
footprint” to environmental impacts categories based on the Product Environmental 
Footprint (PEF) or other scientifically validated standards (for example, ISO 14025, ISO 14040 
series). 
Moreover, there are some other limitations of the approach that would need to be addressed before 
setting regulatory requirements based on PEF methodology. Some examples are: the circularity of 
feedstock, the use of biomass,  the biogenic carbon uptake, the limits of USEtox to assess toxicity 
and the potential role of complementary methods (e.g., ProScale) to overcome these limitations.  
 
A phased implementation timeline and incentives  
 
An adequate timeline  is crucial t o ensure  the implementation of the ESPR, its subsequent 
delegated acts and to include a stronger consultation process with all stakeholders. 
Moreover, transitional periods in delegated acts implementation are essential to: 
• provide industry with the legal certainty and adequate time to make the needed design 
changes, scale them up, adapt supply chains while minimizing the generation of waste 
associated with the transition to new Ecodesign standards and to set-up the DPP.  
• preserve the competitiveness of the EU industry employing thousands of highly qualified 
workers. Many European plants have highly automated production lines for which adaptation 
to new production processes takes more time and is more cost intensive. 
• reduce the risk of relocation of manufacturing facilities to non -EU countries with less robust 
environmental standards.  
 
When setting the implementation timelines, Federchimica encourages the European Commission to 
take into consideration the difficulties that the  companies already encounter to obtain the relevant 
 
 
information for compliance from non-European manufacturers, in particular for substances that are 
exclusively used in manufacturing processes. 
Finally, to ensure a successful transition towards a more sustainable products market, strong  
incentives need to be established by legislators to support innovation, value chain  collaborations, 
fair prices for consumers, level playing field.

 RESPONSE TO PUBLIC CONSULTATION 
JUNE 2022  
 
 
 
Cefic response to public consultation on the proposed 
Ecodesign requirements for sustainable products 
regulation (ESPR) 
Cefic welcomes the European Commission proposal on the Ecodesign for 
Sustainable Products Regulation and supports the overall objectives of 
this ambitious initiative. Well implemented, the ESPR is an opportunity 
for industry to take an active role in the European market transition 
towards sustainable products. Cefic has analysed the proposal with a 
focus on the implementability, workability and clarity of this proposed 
regulation, and with a focus on how this legislation interacts with 
existing legislations that regulate sustainability requirements of 
products.  
 
The proposed regulatory framework is going into a significant level of details regarding 
requirements for products and their components, intermediate products and substances, 
considering the whole life cycle of products (from production process to end of life), as well as 
the many interfaces with existing legislations. 
This contribution is seeking to bring further clarification on certain aspects of the ESPR and is 
offering some experience around the following elements that are needed to achieve a 
successful transition towards a sustainable products market: 
1. A clear interface between various legislative frameworks to avoid duplication of 
existing chemicals legislation. 
2. Feasible information requirements for chemical substances (via the Digital Product 
Passport). 
3. Additional methods to assess environmental impact and amend the PEF methodology 
to assess the benefits of all circular feedstocks. 
4. Strong incentives to bring at scale the market of sustainable products. 
5. Full EU-wide harmonisation of Ecodesign criteria. 
6. A transparent and phased implementation timeline.

1. A clear interface between various legislative frameworks to avoid 
duplication of existing chemicals legislation 
Cefic welcomes the European Commission’s focus on avoiding double regulation for products that are 
already subject to product-specific legislation and the approach whereby REACH remains the main 
legislation to assess and regulate the safety of chemical substances. 
1.1. Scope of the restrictions of chemicals under ESPR should be made clear 
Cefic welcomes the distinction that the European Commission provides when considering management 
measures for chemical safety reasons compared to reasons that negatively affect products’ sustainability. 
However, how this will happen in practice remains unclear. According to recital 22, “chemical safety is a 
recognised element of product sustainability”. As a consequence, the scope of “reasons relating primarily 
to chemical safety” remains ambiguous. 
We consider that the ‘chemical safety’ should not be managed by the ESPR, but that the ESPR should 
clarify how this fits with existing legislation on chemical management (REACH and other Union law). 
1.2. The process of restricting chemicals under ESPR and the interface between REACH and ESPR 
must be clarified and described in detail 
The meaning of restricting chemicals (including intermediates) for sustainability reasons in a product-
related context within the ESPR should be clarified, and the interface between REACH and the ESPR must 
be clearly defined. In case the EU Commission intends to restrict Substances of Concern via ESPR for 
reasons other than chemical safety, there should be a defined procedure allowing for stakeholder 
consultation and the involvement of ECHA, including the Risk Assessment Committee and the Socio-
Economic Committee (in line with the One Substance One Assessment (OSOA) approach from the 
Chemical Strategy for Sustainability). 
Cefic asks the European Commission to explicitly include the interface between REACH and ESPR (notably 
in terms of scope, e.g., article vs. products, intermediate products vs. intermediates) as well as the 
procedure that will be used to restrict the use of substances in the legislative text of the ESPR. This would 
increase the harmonisation of the restrictions of substances approach across sectors by improving the 
transparency and predictability of the processes to manage them. 
1.3. Interfaces with existing and new legislative acts will need to be adequately clarified to 
avoid double-regulation for specific requirements. 
Cefic welcomes the attention that the European Commission has put in its proposal on avoiding double-
regulation for products that are already subject to product-specific legislation. For transparency and 
predictability purposes, Cefic recommends including the key elements of the interfaces concerning 
horizontal Union law applicable to all or a wide number of sectors within the ESPR regulation (or in 
delegated acts, if product-specific). In particular:  
• the interface between REACH and the ESPR, including the definition and management of SoC.  
• the interface between the ESPR and existing sector specific legislation (e.g., Restriction of 
Hazardous Substances, Construction Products Regulation, Packaging and Packaging Waste 
Directive, etc.).

1.4. The state-of-the art recycling technologies should be recognized when defining SoC 
according to Article 2 (28) (c) 
The chemical industry understands that Article 2 (28) (c) would cover substances hampering a specific 
sustainability driver (re-use and recycling of materials are specifically mentioned). For example, this could 
be a substance that would not allow the recycling of products. 
This raises the following considerations: 
• Recyclability strongly depends on the recycling method, which evolves over time – similar 
reasoning applies to reusability. 
• Depending on the technology and the quality of the secondary raw materials they might be 
restricted for specific uses. 
As a consequence, further specification in Article 2 (28) (c) would be welcomed to ensure that 
recyclability is evaluated against state-of-the art recycling technologies, as well as other waste 
management decisions such as the organisation of closed loops on waste collection and treatment or the 
restriction of secondary raw materials for well-defined uses but allowed the use in other applications to 
maximise resource efficiency. 
Cefic suggests complementing paragraph (c) with the technology neutral approach, taking into account 
the state-of-the-art technological developments (e.g., chemical recycling) that would allow to deal with 
legacy substances that make recycling via traditional processes (e.g., mechanical recycling) more 
challenging. Having a clarification on the wording of Article 2 (28) (c) would avoid having substances 
restricted based on outdated information on the feasibility of its recycling properties.  
1.5. ESPR should only allow for restriction of substances that remain in the end product 
Ecodesign requirements also apply to substances used in the manufacturing process. We suggest to only 
regulate under the ESPR substances that are present in the end product and can be measured and 
where there is a direct consequence for sustainability or recycling. If the ESPR framework sets 
restrictions on substances used in production even if they are not present in the product, the constraint 
will only be put on EU manufacturers, as non-EU producers are not subject to the same rules. This would 
lead to disadvantage for EU producers compared to non-EU companies. 
2. Feasible information requirements for chemical substances 
(communicated via the Digital Product Passport) 
Cefic acknowledges and fully supports the need to increase transparency and ensure traceability of 
chemicals in products, its components and intermediate products used along the value chain to achieve 
sustainable products. The Digital Product Passport (DPP), as introduced under the ESPR, should help in 
achieving this purpose. 
2.1. Engaging all actors in the supply chain 
DPP as a tool should be designed such that it is workable and implementable by all actors of the supply 
chain. We support criteria deemed crucial by the ESPR proposal including interoperability, data 
ownership, need-to-know principle, and data security. In addition, Cefic suggests considering the user 
friendliness of DPP as a criterion to embark all actors of the supply chain, including SMEs. 
The ESPR should also require stakeholders outside of the EU value chains to cooperate and provide all the 
relevant information to EU market players and to be fully compliant with DPP’s information sharing 
provisions.

2.2. The procedure for non-disclosure requests should be described in the ESPR. 
Protection of intellectual property (IP) and confidential business information (CBI) is crucial to sustain 
investment in innovation and to allow businesses to compete for the benefit of consumers. Although the 
ESPR considers provisions that allow for the protection of IP/CBI – e.g., Article 5 (5) (e), Article 7 (5), 
Article 10 (h) – it is not yet clear what will be the procedure for a non-disclosure request due to IP/CBI 
reasons. Interested operators should have the possibility to flag information requirements whose 
disclosure would be likely to undermine legitimate commercial interests, including intellectual property 
rights in the course of the preparation of delegated acts setting information requirements. In this regard, 
we would also recommend that only the direct seller or buyer in the value chain gets access to 
information necessary for the final product passport (one up, one down principle). 
2.3. Tracking of Substances of Concern should only apply to the relevant substances present in 
end products 
When considering the feasibility of the information requirements, Cefic is concerned about the feasibility 
and workability to track all (i.e., could be in the range of 12 000 substances according to the Cefic-Ricardo 
Impact Assessment Study on CSS)1 the substances of concern throughout the life cycle of products, as 
mentioned under Article 7 (5), including imports. We suggest specifying that it should only concern the 
SoC which negatively affect product’s sustainability aspects, including recyclability (Annex I) on which 
performance criteria can be established (Article 6). We would further appreciate clarification on whether 
Article 7 (5) (c) can be interpreted as a declaration threshold to be communicated in the delegated acts. 
Cefic welcomes a threshold-based approach, as it is currently applied under REACH and CLP . In addition, 
validated and affordable analytical methods for measuring and tracking substances of concern should be 
available.  
2.4. Information requirements should be set up at the level of the model 
Excluding the unique product identifier, most information requirements (e.g., carbon and environmental 
footprint) should be set at the level of the product model, since in most cases products belonging to the 
same product model share similar environmental characteristics. This will facilitate the uptake of the DPP, 
as companies can use existing data systems designed at model level to implement the DPP, thereby 
avoiding unnecessary costs (incl. environmental costs related to data storage) and complexities 
associated with information at the level of the individual product and frequent update of DPP.  
2.5. Flexibility on the use of other standards should be allowed to establish the unique product 
identifier 
Article 9 (1) (c) requires product identification for the DPP in accordance with norm ISO/IEC 15459:2015. 
However, this is an unnecessary constriction. To ensure a robust, low cost, simple and easy-to-use solution 
for globally unique identification of products, the ESPR should set as mandatory minimum requirement for 
globally unique product instance identification the “IEC 61406 Identification Link”.  For use cases where a 
standardized encoding of information into the globally unique ID has a benefit for the customer or the 
supply chain, the IEC 61406 can optionally be combined with other standards, e.g., ISO IEC 15418 and ISO 
IEC 15459-x.

2.6. ESPR should ensure avoidance of duplication of information requirements from other 
Union laws on chemicals 
The chemical industry would like to highlight the risk of providing the same information under different 
pieces of legislation. Synergies are already in place to share information under other legislative 
frameworks (e.g., Poison Center notifications, SCIP notifications). We encourage the European 
Commission to continue taking the necessary actions to avoid duplication of requirements to maintain 
the quality of the data shared and to reduce administrative burden for stakeholders. 
3. Additional methods to assess environmental impact and amend the 
PEF methodology to assess the benefits of all circular feedstocks 
Article 5 (m) refers to environmental and carbon footprint as product aspects that could be the object of 
Ecodesign requirements for appropriate product groups for all stages of their life cycle, and Article 2 (23) 
makes specific reference to the Product Environmental Footprint (PEF) method as a basis for deriving 
such information. We support the Commission's proposal to settle specific Ecodesign requirements for a 
specific product group (Article 5 (2)). However, we suggest clarifying that the method used to measure 
the environmental product footprint will depend on the product category, to ensure homogeneity in the 
approach as well as enforceability.  
The chemical industry would welcome additional clarification from the European Commission on how the 
current limitations of the PEF assessment approach will be addressed to allow companies to perform a 
reliable assessment of the carbon and environmental footprints. There are indeed some limitations of the 
approach that would need to be addressed before setting regulatory requirements based on this 
methodology. Some examples are the circularity of feedstock, the use of biomass, the biogenic carbon 
uptake or the limits of USEtox to assess toxicity and the potential role of complementary methods (e.g., 
ProScale) to overcome these limitations. 
PEF should not be considered as the only methodology to calculate the environmental product footprint 
for the following reasons, in addition to the above limitations: 
• It covers 16 impact categories, leaving aside several key environmental impact categories (e.g., 
biodiversity) that may be relevant for many products. 
• The PEF databases and Category Rules (PEFCRs) still need further development. Currently, these 
PEFCRs are only available, or in the process of being developed, for around 25 product categories. 
Establishing new PEFCRs will require considerable time and effort. 
• Even where a PEFCR has been developed for a specific product category, the outcome may not 
offer sufficient granularity to permit comparative quantitative assessments between individual 
products if it is (overly) reliant on the use of generic secondary data for key phases of a product’s 
lifecycle. 
Therefore, we recommend that EU policymakers broaden the definition of “environmental footprint” to 
environmental impacts categories included in ot her scientifically validated standards (for example, 
ISO 14040 series). 
Understanding that the PEF methodology is not exclusive of the ESPR, w e ask the European Commission 
for additional clarification on how the current limitations of the PEF method will be addressed and whether 
additional methods to address these limitations under PEF will be considered taking into account also a 
general limitation in the access to data to have an appropriate assessment avoiding misuse o r confused 
communication in the supply chain.

4. Strong incentives to bring at scale the market of sustainable products 
The European Commission aims to make sustainable products the norm. For this transformation  to be 
successful, the right set of incentives, governance and collaborating networks will need to be put in place 
by authorities under the ESPR framework to support the EU chemical industry and its downstream users. 
ESPR is an ambitious initiative in terms of scope , timeline and market creation. This ambitious initiative . 
will not be successful unless ESPR is integrating and is complemented by strong incentives to support 
innovation, value chain collaborations, fair prizes for consumers, level playing field , easier market access 
for European companies and self-sufficiency for the European market. Cefic recommends tracking progress 
by measuring the uptake of ESPR compliant goods into the market  and foreseeing a mechanism 
addressing potential shortcomings involving key stakeholders . Finally, the criteria for the Green Public 
Procurement should foster a real stimulus for the market of sustainable products. For this, the criteria need 
to be proportionate and based on scientific and economic evidence.  
We consider that ESPR is a good step in the direction of a well -functioning of the internal market and the 
free circulation of goods within. Cefic relies on the ESPR to install an increased harmonisation of incentives 
across the European Union. Cefic asks the European Commission to clarify the wording used for Article 57 
in order to clearly identify whether the aim of this article is to put the responsibility of the establishment 
of incentives on the Member States or rather to frame the incentive s that the Member States can 
implement at their own initiative (meaning that the European Commission is also empowered to set up 
incentives under the ESPR).  
5. A full EU-wide harmonisation of Ecodesign criteria 
We are concerned that the current wording of the proposal may lead to significant market fragmentation. 
Indeed, Article 3 (4) gives the possibility to Member States to set Ecodesign requirements for product 
parameters not covered by EU delegated acts, whenever the EU Commission has not explicitly stated that 
Ecodesign requirements are not necessary for that product parameter. This would give leeway for 
Member States to set national rules for specific product parameters, implying a significant risk of 
fragmentation of the EU Single Market. Not only this goes against the spirit of the Commission proposal, 
but it also will have negative impacts from both a sustainability and competitiveness standpoint: 
• Companies will have less incentives to undertake risky investments in sustainable innovations if 
they do not have the legal certainty that they can deploy such innovations throughout the Single 
Market. 
• Having different product design for different European markets will require companies to have 
different production processes across the EU, leading to unnecessary duplication of 
manufacturing equipment and supply chain complexities entailing significant costs for companies 
operating within the EU. There would be an increase in product scrapping and waste, as 
companies would not be able to sell send unsold products to other markets. 
Cefic therefore recommends policymakers to strengthen harmonisation provisions by ensuring that 
Member States cannot restrict the placing on the market of products on grounds of Ecodesign 
requirements relating to any of the product parameters referred to in Annex I. This is in line with the 
current text of the Ecodesign Directive. 
6. A transparent and phased implementation timeline 
Clear and realistic implementation timelines for both the ESPR and the subsequent delegated acts as well 
as a robust stakeholder consultation process are crucial for the implementation of this legislation. 
7 
Realistic transition times should be established from the date of entry into force of any new Ecodesign 
requirements (Article 5 (5)). Transition times are essential: 
• To provide industry with the legal certainty and adequate time to make the needed design 
changes, scale them up, bring them to the market, adapt supply chains while minimizing the 
generation of waste associated with the transition to new Ecodesign standards and to set up the 
DPP. 
• To set-up a well-functioning DPP from the start. 
• To allow time to adapt production processes, as many European plants have highly automated 
production lines and adaptation of these processes in line with the ESPR requirements takes time 
and requires significant investments.  
• To reduce the risk of relocation of manufacturing facilities to non-EU countries with less robust 
environmental standards. 
When setting the implementation timelines, we encourage the European Commission to take into 
consideration the difficulties that the companies already encounter to obtain the relevant information for 
compliance from non-European manufacturers, in particular for substances that are exclusively used in 
manufacturing processes.

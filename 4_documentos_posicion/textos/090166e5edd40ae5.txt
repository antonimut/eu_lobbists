Proposal for Ecodesign for Sustainable Products 
Regulation 
Position Paper of Environmental Action Germany 
DUH welcomes the EU’s intention to make sustainable products the norm as part of the Sustainable Prod-
ucts Initiative and the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR). Up to 80  % of the environ-
mental impact of products is determined at the design phase. To reduce ‘the negative life cycle environ-
mental impacts of products’, as the explanatory memorandum claims as one of the main objectives of this 
regulation, it is therefore necessary to set ambitious goals regarding the design phase of these products. 
Therefore, it is laudable that key factors for a sustainable transi tion are part of the Regulation, such as 
durability, reusability, repairability and resource efficiency. A large scope of products is taken into consid-
eration, a lifecycle approach covering the whole value chain is intended to be used and information re-
quirements shall be regulated in product passports. Furthermore, it is important that the ESPR addresses 
the destruction of unsold goods. Nevertheless, in DUH's view, there are some weak points in the draft. 
The lack of specification of a mechanism that ensures the achievement of the high ambitions and a suffi-
ciently fast timetable are particularly problematic. 
To ensure that the initiative meets its objectives with respect to specific product groups, we would like to 
comment on general requirements, specific articles of the regulatory proposal and finally important eco 
design rules regarding specific product groups. 
About DUH

1. General Requirements 
A. Ensure effectiveness of legal acts within the framework through concrete guiding 
targets 
While the ambition, scope and objectives of the regulation can be applauded, as a whole, the presented 
proposal is unfit to reach these goals, since all concrete measures are delegated to future regulatory acts 
and no clear mechanism that actually ensures that the ambitious goals of the ESPR are met within the 
planned legal acts is implemented. 
To make sure that the delegated acts on the environmental sustainability and ecodesign of product groups 
or products actually diminish environmental harm, concrete guiding targets should be included within 
the ESPR. These guiding targets should define the contribution of the delegated acts towards the EU’s 
Climate and Circular Economy Goals. This contribution should be made as concrete as possible. 
As the Commission specified in the accompanying press release, the introduction of the ESPR “can lead to 
132 mtoe of primary energy savings , which corresponds roughly to 150 billion cubic meters of natural 
gas” - this should be reflected within the ESPR itself and serve as a  concrete quantitative target when 
implementing the delegated acts.  
Furthermore, the binding implementation of the waste hierarchy  also within the delegated acts will be 
crucial to ensure that future product design does contribute to the reduction of consumption of primary 
resources and related emission savings. Prioritizing the application of the first two steps of the waste 
hierarchy, notably through provisions on durability, reusability and actual reuse as well as repairability/re-
furbishment, will be crucial to reach the desired effects on the product design phase.  
A mandatory contribution of delegated acts to achieving savings regarding consumption of primary re-
sources and energy must be legally established within the ESPR using absolute quantitative g uiding tar-
gets. 
Setting such guiding targets is of great importance, since the actual implementation of the ESPR lies within 
the delegated acts that specify the concrete ecodesign requirements. As opposed to other legal norms, 
delegated acts adopted by the Commission enter into force if no objection is raised by the Council or the 
Parliament, and these objections require a majority of the Council of Ministers or the members of the 
European Parliament. Therefore, concrete superordinate guiding targets that m andate a minimum con-
tribution towards reaching EU’s Climate and Circular Economy Goals need to be in place, since no further 
influence on the content of the delegated acts is possible outside a simple rejection. 
 
B. Enabling a fast transition towards sustainability regarding a large proportion of 
products 
To stay within the 1,5 degree goal, prevent irreversible loss of biodiversity or resources and protect the 
livelihoods of future generations, swift action needs to be taken. The implementation of the ESPR is one 
important component of the European transition to a truly sustainable future. However, the current draft 
proposes that 30 new delegated acts shall be prepared and adopted between 2024 and 2030, so about 
4 regulations every year . This would be too slow to provide the necessary incentives for the much 
needed transformation. It will not be possible to cover a wide range of products or product groups, pro-
longing the required adjustment of products entering the market. 
Therefore, the Commission should allocate further resources to the preparation of the delegated acts , 
so that at least double of what is proposed now can be prepared and adopted.

Furthermore, the development of horizontal requirements covering multiple product groups should be 
prioritized, so that a maximum number of products is quickly and significantly improved. This of course 
has to be followed up by more specific requirements.  
This is especially important regarding already existing product-specific legislation and because of the prin-
ciple of the “hierarchy of norms” - delegated acts will merely be able to specify, and not go beyond, par-
allel product-specific legislation (such as the PPWD or the batteries regulation), while Regulations can 
serve as a true “lex specialis” and be more ambitious . By making the overarching requirements as all -
encompassing as possible , a lack of ambition for products that are already regulated in other product -
specific legislation, or the resource-intensive revision of those, can be avoided.  
We think that the scope of the ESPR should cover all key product groups and key sectors such as batter-
ies, packaging and construction products. Since these, because of their importance, are the very product 
groups that have already been addressed by product -specific legislation, we think the aim should really 
be to cover as many criteria as possible in the horizontal legislation. In addition, it is not clear to us how it 
will be determined whether existing legislation does or does not sufficiently address sustainability criteria. 
We would suggest that this should be measured mandatorily against the concrete guiding targets  we 
have suggested above. A process on how this can be done should be set out in a legal norm subordinate 
to this Regulation. 
Furthermore, it seems preferable n ot to delay discussions and decisions to future legal acts, where they 
can already happen within this process: This applies to, for example, a ban of destruction of unsold goods 
(esp. for electronics and textiles), mandatory repair information such as manu als and repair instructions 
under open access format, as well as on a first work plan. 
 
C. The danger of “industry self regulation” (Article 18) 
Even though the long timeframe in the drafting and implementation of product -related delegated acts is 
far from optimal, we strongly oppose the possibility, laid down in Article 18, that such delegated acts may 
be replaced through so -called “self-regulation”, i.e. voluntary agreements by a minimum of 80 % of the 
industry and approved by the Commission. We view this ap proach as very dangerous. In our experience, 
voluntary agreements have lacked ambition and have mostly failed to deliver actual results. That is what 
makes the ESPR necessary in the first place. Clear rules must be set by the legislator , accompanied by a 
consultation procedure in which all stakeholders are included (and especially consumer protection and 
environmental NGOs). If the industry wants to make ambitious suggestions in this process, which we have 
usually not experienced in most cases in the past, they are free to do so in such a format. The same applies 
to acting as a frontrunner and implementing ecodesign measures already before the implementation of 
a delegated act. 
We understand that the lack of resources by the EU commission might be one reason for Article 18, how-
ever this needs to be solved otherwise, and not by accepting the dangers of an unregulated market and 
unambitious industry self-regulations that have no control/participation mechanism other than the Com-
mission. 
Industry stakeholders are, and have been, free to act as frontrunners and set a benchmark. They will be 
able to go beyond the scope of the respective delegated acts and we would welcome that. Replacing 
delegated acts by voluntary agreements, however, would undermine the entire purpose of the regulation.

D. Importance of controlling and sanctioning circumvention (Article 33) 
DUH welcomes the Commission’s plan to better regulate the conformity of products, especially the com-
mon practice of circumvention. The explicit addressing of products that can detect and manipulate the 
testing process in order to achieve a more favourable result is overdue after the experience with   
Dieselgate. 
We call on the Commission to take the opportunity to prescribe appropriate control and sanction  
mechanisms in addition to the fundamental ban on building such products and putting them on the mar-
ket. The experience with Dieselgate shows that the common practice of exhaust gas manipulation was 
only uncovered by subsequent measurements by the US authoritie s. In Europe, on the other hand, the 
applicable regulations are still not sufficiently independently controlled and sanctioned by the competent 
authorities. 
E. Illegal Imports 
The boom of online marketplaces in the wake of the corona crisis drastically increased unfair competition 
between retailers and online marketplaces at the expense of consumers and the environment. Massive 
amounts of products are sold within the EU via virtual marketplaces such as Amazon, AliExpress or Wish 
that often do not comply with the existing environmental and consumer protection regulations . These 
non-compliant products include counterfeits as well as unsafe products that pose a major risk to health 
and safety and are often potentially life threatening, such as electronic goods, children's toys, cosmetics 
or car parts. At the same time, it is not possible to take effective action against this infringement that 
originates from outside of the EU, as platform operators are not legally responsible for the products they 
offer. This legal loophole allows the growing market segment of sellers outside of the EU via online plat-
forms to ignore EU legislation and thereby threatens the existence of many retailers while endangering 
the environment as well as consumers. 
Due to the horizontal approach of the ESPR, it is the responsibility of the ESPR to ensure that the products 
imported into the EU fulfill the outlined requirements in this legislation. Thus, online platforms and ful-
fillment service providers must be taking into account to ensure ESPR requirements are met for the prod-
ucts they enable the sale from manufacturers and distributors outside of the EU: 
 Online platforms and fulfillment service providers that enable products to be sold through their 
services shall be defined as an actor in the supply chain by legal definition  - e.g., as importer - 
and appropriate obligations. Thus, if no other actor located in the EU is liable to consumers, online 
platforms and fulfillment service providers must take full responsibility of the products they enable 
the sale. 
 Online platforms and fulfillment service providers must be subject to comprehensive due dili-
gence obligations for the products for which they enable the sale. This includes for example: 
o Checking that manufacturer and distributor obligations concerning the ESPR are met. 
o Online platforms shall only offer products for sale in a Member State, if these products 
comply with the ESPR requirements but not if neither the manufacturer nor the distributor 
of that product or fulfillment service provider is located in the Member State. 
o Fulfillment service providers shall ensure that products they import into a Member State 
comply with the ESPR, if the manuf acturer or distributor of such product is not located in 
the Member State. 
o Online platforms and fulfillment service providers shall document and report information 
about product destruction on behalf of manufacturer or distributor.

3. Requirements for specific product groups 
3.1. Construction material 
The construction industry is booming, which is creating great challenges in the area of environmental and 
resource protection. This is increasingly catching the attention of policy-makers, who have so far concen-
trated primarily on energy-efficiency in buildings. Measures in the construction sector, however, need to 
be addressed along the whole life cycle to lift the full potential. 
 Since the CPR is not covering raw materials like glass or steal which is also heavily imported from 
non-EU countries, the ESPR must ensure the sustainability requirements for such raw materials 
adequately. 
 Material composition of the panels (e.g harmful substances or critical raw materials) and the pro-
portion of recycled materials used. 
3.2. Electrical and electronic equipment (EEE) 
In general, it is important that there are mandatory minimum ecodesign criteria for all types of electrical 
and electronic equipment (EEE). These requirements should promote durability, repairability, reusability, 
recyclability, recycled content and decrease environmental impacts during production, use and disposal. 
Horizontal requirements will help to mitigate environmental impacts from a huge number of different EEE 
on the market which – although being designed for different applications – have many environmental 
impacts in common.

a) Durability 
Durability is a key aspect for sustainability of EEE because it mitigates environmental impacts from re-
source consumption and disposal through a longer lifetime. EEE should be constructed in a way that they 
are not sensitive against specific defects. Especially vulnerable pieces are usually connectors, switches 
and fastenings from plastics. A warranty by the manufacturer assures the customer that t he device will 
function for a certain period of time and also confirms the promise of quality. Long guarantees can there-
fore help to extend lifetimes of EEE. However, it is important that producers should give clear preference 
to repair over replacement with new equipment in a warranty case. The statutory warranty and reversal 
of the burden of proof should be increased from 6 months to 2 years for EEE and rechargeable batteries 
in order to guide product design towards durable and repairable products. 
Criteria for batteries and devices: 
 Protection against dust and water 
o For portable devices that are likely to be used outside (mobile phones, garden devices, 
tools etc.), protection against water and dust according to standard IP54 should be proven. 
 Protection against damage if knocked 
o Devices and battery should pass a knocking test according to standard IEC 62841 -1:2014 
and still remain full functionality. 
 The operating instructions and the Internet should contain instructions for a long service life of 
the device/ battery (optimal charging behavior, optimal storage conditions), information on nec-
essary maintenance work, guarantee conditions and simple instructions for action in the event of 
typical malfunctions. 
 Device/ battery has battery charge level indicato r (at least with 25  % accuracy) to allow users to 
optimize battery charging behavior. 
 Device and battery should be covered by a warranty period of 7 years from the date of purchase. 
For batteries, a residual capacity of 80 % should be guaranteed and testing of this capacity should 
be free of charge for customers at the end of warranty period. Also, newly purchased spare parts 
should have a separate warranty. All devices must be thoroughly checked for reparability in case 
of damage within the warranty period . Repairs should usually take no longer than two to three 
weeks or otherwise a replacement device should be provided to the customer. 
b) Repairability and Reusability 
Reparability and Reusability are important criteria through which producers can influence du rability of 
their devices. Modular design, low requirements for specific tools and good availability of spare parts/ 
wearing parts enhance reparability. For a successful repair or reuse, knowledge of the specific device type 
is crucial which should be ensu red through a durable type label. Repairs should be possible for profes-
sional repair companies as well as private users. Producers should provide detailed information online for 
both target groups to facilitate repairs, e.g. through repair instructions, vi deo trainings, troubleshooting 
tools, exploded drawings etc. At least a list of common faults and first measures for their elimination 
should be provided online. The availability of wear and spare parts is an essential prerequisite for repara-
bility. Relevant criteria are the availability period, the procurement channel, the delivery time and the 
price. Wearing and spare parts should be available as long -term as possible and also be accessible for 
independent repairers and private consumers. Repairs should be promoted by a reduced VAT rate.

Criteria for batteries and devices: 
 Maintenance work as well as exchange of wearing and spare parts should be easily possible with-
out damaging the device and without requiring special tools. Firmly installed crucial wearing parts 
such as batteries that are not removable/ exchangeable are not acceptable. 
 Consumers should be informed via internet and manual about exchangeability and availability of 
spare parts. 
 Every device and battery should be labeled with a durable a nd specific type label. We support 
mandatory product passports using QR-codes for product identifications. 
 The operating instructions for the device/ battery must be provided on the internet by specifying 
the device/ battery designation and can completely be downloaded free of charge. 
 Repair instructions for typical defects, information on replacing wear and spare parts and exploded 
drawings must be available free of charge on the Internet for at least 7 years after last purchasing. 
 Wearing and spare parts should be available for at least 7 years after the end of production. They 
should be available latest 2 weeks after ordering and should cost no more than 20 % of the devices 
original price. 
 Software updates for a product must be provided for at least 7 yea rs of the product and ensure 
the safe use of the battery without limiting its functionality. Otherwise, the source code should be 
published. 
c) Recyclability 
Recyclability is an important sustainability criterion since it contributes to reduce the use of primary re-
sources. Therefore, recyclability must be promoted, although durability, reparability and reusability 
should be prioritized. Important measures supporting recyclability are to ensure a good disassembly of 
materials and components (e.g. though avo idance of material composites), usage of uniform materials, 
good labelling of materials and low proportions of pollutants and impurities. Recycling should always be 
the last option if possibility of reuse was assessed and is not possible. 
Criteria for the devices: 
 The device should fulfill principles for construction of recyclable devices according to VDI-guideline 
2243. 
 A disassembly instruction is available for the device. 
 For casing and large-sized parts made from plastic, uniform polymers should be used. Homo-, co-
polymers and blends are acceptable if they do not affect recyclability. 
Criteria for devices and batteries: 
 Device and battery should have a recyclability of at least 95 % (we understand recyclability as the 
share of a product that can be regain ed as secondary material after the discarded product was 
officially collected and treated in a regular recycling process). 
 Plastic parts above 50 g or with a surface > 200 mm² should be labelled according to ISO 11469.

d) Raw materials and use of recycled materials 
If possible, sustainable raw materials should be used for the production of EEE to reduce social and envi-
ronmental impacts in mining countries. Specific materials, such as cobalt, are particularly problematic and 
should be avoided as far as possi ble. To achieve improvements in this matter, transparency is a crucial 
building block, making it necessary that all producers reveal used materials as well as its origin. 
Usage of recycled material can considerably contribute to reduce these environmental impacts. For EEE, 
already secondary materials such as iron, copper, aluminum, gold, silver and platin um are available on 
the marked, but its material circulation and wide application must further be promoted. Considerable 
potential still exists in the field of application of plastic recyclates. It is thus necessary to establish financial 
incentives and mandatory minimum recycled content targets to promote particularly the use of plastic 
recyclates in EEE and batteries. 
Criteria devices and batteries: 
 The product advertisement on the Internet lists all raw materials used, the recycled content and 
the countries of origin of the primary raw materials for the device and for the battery. 
e) Pollutant content 
Minimizing the pollutant content in EEE has benefits for consumers (health safety) as well as for an envi-
ronmental safe recycling. 
Criteria devices: 
 Compliance with the pollutant limits in the device according to EU Directive 2015/863/EU (RoHS 
Directive) should be demonstrated without the exceptions in Annex III. 
 For handles, the requirements of the GS quality seal regarding polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons 
(PAH) of category 2 are met. The limit value of 10 mg/kg for the sum of 15 PAHs should not be 
exceeded. 
Criteria devices and batteries 
 The housing of the device and batteries must not contain any carcinogenic, mutagenic or repro-
toxic substances according to EC 1272/2008, any substances labeled as "substances of concern" 
according to REACH, any halogen-containing polymers, any halogen-organic flame retardants, any 
flame retardants toxic to aquatic organisms or labeled H410 or R 50/53 according to EC 1272/2008. 
Exceptions may apply for unavoidable impurities, fluoroorganic additives in plastics < 0.5  % and 
plastic parts ≤ 25 g. 
f) Standardization 
A better development of standardized parts for EEE is required to allow a more efficient use of resources 
in such devices. A standardization of wearing parts such as batteries can facilitate that users use several 
devices with one battery or, if the device is purchased new, it may not be necessary to buy a new battery 
or chargers. The use of standardized wearing/ spare parts in different devices also supports the long-term 
availability of these parts, so that replacement is ensured in the event of a defect. In addition, the subse-
quent upgradeability of devices with newly developed batteries is supported. To increase the environ-
mental benefit of standardization, producers must be motivated to reduce the delivery catch of their de-
vices without accessories and battery. 
Standardization should be developed as far as possible within manufacturers product lines, but also cross-
manufacturers. Uniform connectors for chargers of mobile phones are a first important step, but stand-
ardization has to be extended also to other important wearing and spare parts such as batteries, displays,

switches, connecting elements, handles, accessories, lids etc. Manufacturers should be motivated to use 
standardized parts and to publish technical specifications of their devices/ batteries. 
Criteria devices: 
 Producers should be obliged to only offer one charger for all devices of the same voltage class and 
be motivated to use standardized chargers, batteries, accessories, wearing parts etc. that are also 
usable for devices from other manufacturers. 
 The device must be deliverable without accessories (as chargers, tools, batteries) as well as bat-

g) Energy efficiency 
Charging and discharging of a b attery must be as efficient as possible. Energy efficiency should not de-
crease significantly due to the aging of the battery. The charger should have a low energy consumption if 
the battery is not connected (stand-by). 
Criteria devices: 
 Producers should pr ovide information to customers about energy -efficient use of the battery as 
well as the ecological advantages of using renewable energy. 
h) Production Process 
During production, environmental impacts through energy consumption, raw material extraction, water 
use, industrial processing and waste generation should be minimized. Producers should demand the use 
of high environmental standards (e.g. use of renewable energy) also from suppliers and subcontractors. 
Particularly, transparency for customers about envir onmental emissions of products should be much 
more promoted (e.g. through the promotion of reliable labels) to enhance environmental purchasing de-
cisions. 
Criteria devices and batteries: 
 For all devices and batteries, information on the energy requirement,  the share of renewable en-
ergies and the GHG emissions caused during production as well as the calculation method must 
be published on the Internet. 
 The maximum CO2 footprint emission thresholds must require the use of green energy in EEE and 
battery production. 
 For the production of EEE and batteries, the use of 100  % renewable energy should be the aim. 
Manufacturers should only be able to claim the use of renewable energy if they can prove this via 
direct connection to the renewable energy plant or a contract demonstrating a temporal (in real 
time or at least every hour) and geographical link between energy supply and use. Contracts such 
as Guarantees of Origin alone should not be accepted as valid evidence.  
i) End-of-life 
Retailers must give clearly visible information on the take -back of devices and batteries in the operating 
instructions, in the product advertising on the internet and in the purchasing process for d irect sales. In 
addition to the current legal requirements, this information should also include information on take-back 
possibilities for WEEE and used batteries, the ecological significance of separate collection, environmental 
benefits of reuse and the  risks of improper disposal. Batteries and lamps must be easily removable to 
allow consumers a separation of these parts before disposal, because they affect recycling processes.

j) Product passport 
We vote for a digital product passport for EEE and batteries allowin g quick environment -related infor-

3.3. Batteries 
Against the background of the current expansion of electromobility in connection with a clean transp ort 
transition, the sustainability of batteries becomes a decisive aspect. Future batteries must have minimal 
environmental and social impacts during manufacturing, use and disposal. Key aspects for a good envi-
ronmental performance of batteries are closely related to resource protection, such as durability, collec-
tion, recycling and second-life. The basis for this must now be created through ambitious requirements . 
Please note that we also defined some specific criteria concerning batteries in the section o n EEE since 
some of the criteria should be similar for both product categories. 
a) Definition 
All Batteries from light means of transport (LMT) – including light means of transport without the necessity 
to operate while  seated – should be defined as portable batteries, as they often end up with private 
households for disposal (Battery Regulation Art 2 (7)) 
b) Carbon Footprint 
The most energy - and carbon -intensive part of manufacturing of batteries (esp. Lithium -Ion-Batteries 
(LIB)) is the pro duction chain of battery cells . As cell production is mainly powered by electricity, these 
emissions can be influenced by changing the energy source. The type of electricity used is therefore crucial 
to determining how green a battery actually is. 
 Information should be provided both on CO 2 (kg CO2 per kWh battery produced) to highlight the 
lowest carbon batteries and most efficient manufacturing process 
 The current Product Environmental Footprint Category Rules (PEFCR) for batteries should be up-
dated to include upstream emissions (related to material extraction and refining) and must incen-
tivise the use of renewable energy across the battery life cycle (extraction, production, use and 
recycling). A balance of interests should also be ensured by including civil society in the update of 
the rules. 
 The maximum carbon footprint emission thresholds must require the use of green energy in bat-
tery production. Prior to this, incentives should be offered to promote the use of green electricity 
as much as possible. 
 Where companies do not provide specific data, default carbon intensity values should be used 
based on the average carbon emissions data of the country where the electrodes, electrolytes and 
cells were produced. Companies should only be allowed to use lower emission factors where they 
can reliably prove that their individual processes or energy sources are cleaner.

 Manufacturers should only be able to claim the use of renewable energy if they can prove this via 
direct connection to the renewable ene rgy plant or a contract demonstrating a temporal (in real 
time or at least every hour) and geographical link between energy supply and use. Contracts such

In addition to reporting the carbon footprint data in kg CO2 per kWh battery produced (needed to be able

cially not in the short term. 
c) Recyclability 
We propose the subsequent criteria to enhance recyclability of batteries: 
 Batteries should have a recyclability of at least 95 % (we understand recyclability as the share of a 
product that can be regained as secondary material after the discarded product was officially col-
lected and treated in a regular recycling process).  
 Plastic parts above 50 g or with a surface > 200 mm² should be labelled according to ISO 11469 
 Battery sub system and all materials used in the battery should be disclosed on the battery (e.g. 
through qr code) as well as during product promotion (internet). For this, the standard IEC 
62902:2019 is recommended. 
d) Raw materials and use of recycled materials 
As Europe is facing a growing demand for batteries, it is important to provide policy support for recycled 
content as a driver for uptake of secondary materials, and to secure the market against volatility of pri ce 
of primary materials. For the calculation and verification of recycled content there should be an ambitious 
and transparent methodology. 
Only post -consumer recyclates from recycled batteries (without production waste and material from 
other waste streams) should be used as recycled content, to promote closed-loop recycling and to avoid 
recyclates that are available anyway being diverted to meet targets, without actually recycling more. Re-
cycled content targets should be applied to all types of batteries (Portable, EV, LMT, industrial and auto-
motive batteries). 
e) Replaceability, Repairability and Reuse 
Extending the lifetime of batteries and the devices they power can contribute to reduce environmental 
impacts. Important requirements to extend durability concern replaceability of batteries, repairability and 
reusability. 
Ensuring that batteries are removable and replaceable in devices is a basic requirement because it enables 
devices to have a functional lifetime longer than the expected life of their batteri es and it allows battery 
and device to be more easily reused, refurbished, repaired or recycled. Also , batteries can be reused in 
alternative applications once they reach a reduced state of health (e.g. energy storage). 
The following requirements/ criteria are important to ensure replaceability of batteries: 
 Portable batteries incorporated in appliances and LMZ shall be readily removable and replaceable 
by the end-user and by independent operators.

 The scope of battery removability and replaceability should  be extended – in particular to LMT 
batteries. A minimum set of standards for basic components should also be established for electric 
vehicle (EV) and industrial batteries. 
 Industrial and EV batteries shall be readily removable and replaceable by independent operators.  
 Exemptions should be reserved for niche applications only, and where applicable accompanied by 
an information requirement on the impact on the product lifetime. 
 Battery removability and replaceability should be more precisely defined and based on reversible 
non-destructive disassembly using commonly available tools. 
 Battery replaceability for portable batteries should be possible for professional repairers as well 
as end-users. 
 All types of batteries must be easily replaceable by professional repairers, and portable ones by 
end-users and community repair initiatives. 
General criteria concerning reparability of batteries we propose: 
 All batteries must be exchangeable from devices (see above). 
 Batteries containing several cell packs must be modular in design, in order to replace broken packs 
and extend the lifetime of the whole battery-pack. 
 In all cases, professional repairers should encompass both affiliated and independent repairers. 
 Batteries, as well as their main components (cell packs, casing and mainboard), should be available 
for the expected lifetime of the application as spare parts with a reasonable and non -discrimina-
tory price for independent qualified personnel. 
 Repair instructions should be available for all types of batteries and all devices with batteries, at 
least for professional repairers. Portable batteries should have repair instructions suitable for end-
users and community repair initiatives. 
 For batteries with a con trol software, the manufacturer should provide software updates for the 
expected lifetime of the product that ensure the safe use of the battery without limiting its func-
tionality, or otherwise publish the source code. 
 Software must not be used to impinge battery replacement – for example using updates or serial-
isation. 
 Battery management systems in all types of batteries should be readable by end-users. 
 Further criteria that are similarly relevant for EEE can be found in chapter on EEE (part reparability 
and reusability), e.g. concerning consumer information. 
f) Second-life for EV-Batteries 
Second-life batteries can extend the lifespan of the battery with the twofold effect of significantly improv-
ing the life cycle footprint of the battery and avoiding the pro duction of new batteries. Using second life 
(repurposed) EV batteries as stationary electricity storage units is likely to offer the greatest potential of 
environmental benefits. To ensure that discarded batteries are prepared for re-use, the following recom-
mendations should be made: 
 Before an industrial, automotive or EV battery is recycled, it should be checked for the possibility 
of reuse. In those cases where reuse is technically possible, economically feasible and where there 
is a market for the reused battery, the battery must be reused. Definition of reuse targets may be 
beneficial here.

 Information needed to allow for repurposing or remanufacturing should go beyond the state of 
health parameter and also include data necessary to evaluate the remainin g lifetime, such as en-
ergy or power throughput, errors or temperature history. 
 Recycling should only be possible insofar as reuse or remanufacturing is technically and economi-
cally not feasible. 
 The testing for reuse should be documented and passed on to authorities. 
Standardization is also a very important aspect to avoid early disposal of usable batteries and promote 
second life usage. Portable batteries must be usable in every battery -driven device of the same voltage 
class from one producer and producer s must be motivated to advance cross -manufacturer standardiza-
tion of batteries. Also in the field of mobility, standardization of EV batteries is beneficial for efficient 
resource use. 
g) Due Dilligence 
As the demand for batteries (in particular EV batteries)  grows, it is crucial that the transition to a zero 
carbon economy does not come at the expense of environmental, social and human rights abuses in coun-
tries where battery materials are sourced. Policy makers need to ensure that mining companies clean up 
their supply chains and put in place robust governance structures to ensure ethical sourcing of battery 
materials and prevent human rights and environmental abuses. Strong due diligence requirements for 
batteries should become the blueprint for future horizontal due diligence legislation. 
 For batteries, the raw materials covered under due diligence requirements should include at least 
copper, iron and aluminum (bauxite). Copper and cobalt are often mined together, where cobalt 
is a byproduct of copper (and nickel) mining, e.g. in the Copper -Cobalt belt in the Democratic Re-
public of the Congo (DRC). Since they are mined together/close to each other, the environm ental 
impact is often similar. Without the inclusion of copper, the risk is that the new rules will create a 
two-tier system with ‘clean’ cobalt mined alongside ‘dirty’ copper, with no oversight or controls 
on the latter. The demand for iron and aluminum (bauxite) for vehicles with electric engines is 
projected to increase by 13 to 14 times between 2019 and 2030. Future technologies could mas-
sively increase the demand for aluminum. Regarding the extraction of those metals, human rights 
violations and environmental destruction have been documented in-depth. 
 Stronger environmental protections on global mining practices are also needed and mining com-
panies should comply with requirements as set out in the Initiative for Responsible Mining Assur-
ance (IRMA)’s standard on environmental responsibility (Principle 4), which is today’s best practice 
for responsible sourcing and beyond. 
 Companies participating in voluntary supply chain certification schemes should not be assumed to 
be automatically compliant with the le gislation but must continuously meet the requirements, 
including ensuring supply chain assessment, transparency, third party audits, grievance mecha-
nisms, and consultation with affected communities. 
 Extend due diligence requirements to cover economic opera tors that place portable batteries on 
the EU market. 
 Batteries may only be imported if it can be proven that equivalent EU environmental and occupa-
tional health and safety standards have been met.  
 End of life batteries should only be exported to non-EU countries if it can be proven that the same 
environmental and occupational health and safety standards apply to waste treatment in the des-
tination facilities as in der EU.

 Establish an EU moratorium on deep seabed mining until the effects on the marine envir onment, 
biodiversity and human activities have been sufficiently researched, the risks are understood and 
it can be demonstrated that deep seabed mining can be managed in a way that ensures the effec-
tive protection of the marine environment and without net loss of biodiversity. 
h) Performance and Durability Requirements 
 Should be apply to all portable batteries (not only the ones in general use), LMT, industrial- and 
EV batteries and should be independent of capacity. 
 Instead of the nominal capacity, the actual measured capacity should be defined as the starting 
point for the specifications, otherwise manufacturers can arbitrarily change the nominal capacity 
to falsely comply with the specifications. 
 Phase out of non -rechargeable portable batteries must be  promoted by a levy placed on single-
use batteries. 
Specific criteria we propose: 
 Capacity loss 
o Capacity of batteries should not decrease significantly during use. Devices should enable 
at least 1000 cycles without losing more capacity than 80 %. 
o Since current standards for measuring this capacity loss (e.g. IEC 61960 -3:2017) are not 
realistic, new standards with more realistic testing conditions must be developed. 
 Protection against heat, coldness and temperature variations. 
 The battery should still reach 75 % of rated capacity after 1200 loading cycles at 0 °C und 40 °C. 
 For further criteria such as warranty, charge level display, protection against dust, water, damage 
and consumer instructions please see chapter on EEE. 
i) Battery-Passport and market promotion of sustainable batteries 
There should be a digital battery passport for all types of batteries independent of the capacity of the 
battery. The battery passport shall be accessible online, through electronic systems and via QR code and 
should include information on the state of the health of the battery, carbon footprint, energy and water 
consumption, share of renewable energies, repairability and information regarding the repair, all used 
raw materials, contained pollutants, warranty information and recycled content. 
Incentives for sustainable batteries: 
● Consumers should be able to better identify more environmentally friendly portable batteries 
through mandatory labelling (e.g. with regard to their carbon footprint, rechargeability, repaira-
bility or recyclability). 
● The EU Ecolabel should also apply to batteries and all applications containing batteries. 
● Public procurement should give mandatory preference to the best available environmentally 
friendly batteries through a product database. 
● To make it easier for procurement offices to choose, the creation of a product database should 
show which batteries (applications) are particularly environmentally friendly.

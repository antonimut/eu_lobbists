UNESID Principles for the Review of the Industrial Emissions Directive (IED) 
If the revision is chosen at this stage, prudence must prevail. 
Some are calling for the possibility of putting the IED review on hold until the serious (political and energy) situation 
in EU is clarified. The status quo is changing at the moment, and it is currently unknown what energy sources might 
even be available to E U after the crisis caused by Russia's invasion of Ukraine. This combines with the nee d for 
industrial decarbonisation and thus the revision of the directive governing this transition (emissions trading). Not 
forgetting to mention the risk of an overlapping legislative approach to the path to decarbonisation, "legal 
obligation vs. emissions trading". 
What is clearly needed is maximum flexibility in the decarbonisation process, which is a process of high uncertainty, 
especially in the current international context. Decarbonisation plans and emission values associated with 
emerging technologies cannot be written in stone, nor can their "implementation" be forced "by law" within a 
certain exact timeframe (Transformation Plan in Article 14 (a) (f) Environmental Management System).  
If other requirements are to be intensified (in other legislative developments), such as an even more intensive 
industrial transformation towards decarbonisation, this will have to be governed by support to industry, as this is 
unaffordable in an environment of international competition and very high energy prices.  
However, UNESID considers it UNACCEPTABLE that such support could be linked to State Aid schemes, instead of 
harmonised pan-European schemes. It would result in a disparate E U, going against one of the european  basis, 
which is the EU single market, with fewer budgetary restrictions, doping its industry, and in other countries it would 
be impossible to carry out such actions. Products from countries that stimulate publicly funded investment in their 
industry to d ecarbonise will benefit from their supremacy in a single market, while those that have not received 
sufficient support for transformation will become less competitive, ceding market share to the former.  
Given this situation, in the event of opting for the revision of the Directive (and always taking into account what 
UNESID stated above), the basic requirement for it must be prudence, something that seems to go in the opposite 
direction of the Commission's proposal. The EID, with the associated Integrated E nvironmental Authorisation, is 
undoubtedly the touchstone of the environmental performance of industries, but it is not and should not be the 
only tool. Nor should it try to integrate other fields whose shadow over plants has to have a much broader vision,  
such as many aspects of European chemicals management, occupational hygiene aspects, interaction with the 
carbon price (as a principle chosen by E U in view of the decarbonisation of the economy), or aspects related to 
energy efficiency (scope of the Envir onmental Management System as set out in Article 14 (a) Environmental 
Management System). All these areas have their own regulations. 
It gives the impression that the Commission has tried to integrate many directives into a single one, which will also 
disintegrate the monitoring of the  Environmental Permit (AAI under the Spanish acronym), turning it into a tool 
that is very difficult to manage both for the installations and for the Administrations (environmental, energy, labour 
or even safety and product design and efficiency aspects). 
Environmental Management Systems (Article 14a). 
UNESID does not doubt that having an environmental management system yields positive results if it is properly 
managed, and is not a mere legal compliance. However, it is questio nable whether something specific should be 
demanded, beyond the existing, perfectly functioning systems, and even more so if it integrates many of the other 
areas mentioned above. To want to integrate all kinds of aspects into this system jeopardises the p roper 
functioning of existing management systems and goes against two of the main principles of management systems: 
voluntariness and tailor-made. On the other hand, some aspects could be required in the AAI, but WITHOUT having 
to integrate them in any kin d of MEGA environmental management system, which would exceed its functionality 
and put it at risk by bringing into it by law many aspects that are far from the environmental functioning of the 
factory and its emission levels. Evidence that the drafters of  the proposal have NOT managed an integrated 
environmental management system. 
REACH. Inventories and classifications yes, but do not overreach ( Article 14 d). 
While some basic aspect of chemicals management at facilities may have a place within the review,  the decision 
to anticipate complicated and very specific procedures for risk assessment of uses, risk assessment of alternatives,

Overlap with Occupational Hygiene or Health Regulations (Article 14, section e). 
The proposal includes the concept of people's health. Nobody doubts the importance of the concept, but what 
UNESID questions is whether this is the right tool for managing this aspect. 
In addition, there is a risk of overlap with occupational health and safety and environmental health information, 
which has also been fed by secondary information from the REACH and CLP Regulations. 
Transformation/Decarbonisation Plans (Article 14, paragraph f). 
Drawing up a plan for adaptation to the EU Green Deal objectives is reasonable, as long as it is considered 
exclusively as a technology foresight exercise and a benchmark for the installation. There are many doubts about 
the appropriateness of integration in this proposal. 
Integrating the drafting of decarbonisation plans into an Industrial Emissions Directive that addresses the 
environmental performance of the installation does no t make clear sense. The temporary penetration of these 
technologies will largely depend on boundary conditions, such as the technological development of direct iron ore 
reduction plants, availability of H 2 , price of non -fossil energy, access to and compet itiveness of emission -neutral 
electricity, etc. Therefore, it is not clear to the industry what the interest of integrating this planning into the IEA 
is. In addition, even independently of the above, an industrial plan in an extremely competitive sector i s too 
sensitive a piece of information to be reflected in a public or partially public document.  
In addition, specific issues are raised such as the scope of such plans, their content, associated roles and 
responsibilities, who will be responsible for thei r evaluation, the need for regular updating and the associated 
accountability system. 
In any case, it is assumed that decarbonisation plans must be living documents that adapt to changing conditions, 
such as the option to access European funds, the energy market, accessibility and real availability of alternative 
fuels or the functioning of other measures to protect against environmental and/or climate dumping.  
Transparency yes, but restrained (Articles 5 and 24). 
Industry has no objection to more structure d and easily available access to IEAs, as long as commercially and 
industrially sensitive information has been extracted. Energy efficiency, strategic planning, chemical products used 
that could be subject to competitive advantages (e.g. those linked to gi ving special properties to the product that 
have a clear commercial influence), among others, constitute this type of information. The administration must 
preserve this sensitive information. This becomes even more relevant in the future context of decarbo nisation in 
which EU will entail many costs for both industries and society as a whole, and if it is not preserved, it could offer 
third countries a clear advantage over their European competition. 
Difficult approval process for Integrated Environmental Authorisations. 
The mere fact of having wanted to integrate such diverse areas makes their approval complicated, as it requires 
the participation of different departments each time. 
Similarly, each administration will be required to provide public and transp arent information on all third party 
issues that arise during the IEA approval process, as well as the reasons for not having required the minimum value 
of the BAT-AELs range in the permit. This has a clear interaction with the transparency heading and the  exposure 
of competitively sensitive information, and may also constitute a technical overload that many local authorities 
will not be able to manage properly (except with disproportionate technical staffing). Only those Autonomous 
Regions with a very spec ific weight of a certain industry will have developed the capacity to understand the real 
functioning of certain sectors, but that would overwhelm many other administrations.

Energy Efficiency (Article 1a, paragraph c). 
Industrial energy consumption is a cost an d therefore a competitiveness factor. Energy intensive industries are 
already obliged by other regulation to have to carry out verified energy audits, and it is even very likely, as they 
usually do, that they have to carry out the investments with the shor test payback periods. This requirement is 
therefore redundant for these sectors. 
The scrupulous management of sensitive information is a fundamental aspect, and as the IEA file is open to many 
opinions, the possibilities of leaks are very high. UNESID cannot judge the effect of other sectors that are covered 
by the EIA but not the obligation to have Energy Management Systems.  
The proposal would go through third party audits, with the signature by an accredited verifier or specific parts of 
the audit report that could be made public and subsequently monitored. Again this seems more reasonable to 
manage for compliance with the Energy Efficiency Directive, rather than for integration within the DEI and hence 
the AAI. 
Conditions in Life Cycle and Supply Chain (Article 14a, paragraph b). 
The industry is a strong advocate of product life cycle consideration, as steel has many advantages as a material.  
However, the option of including life cycle and supply chain development targets in the requirements of  the 
possible environmental management system may be more complex than the estimated benefits. Particularly in 
the area of material utilisation of waste, administrations have an important role to play if they streamline 
procedures so that materials such as  steel aggregates (by far the main output of the process) can compete on 
similar terms with natural materials. This is an aspect that depends almost entirely on national, rather than 
European, legislation and is beyond the control of the operator. 
 
Ambition of BAT-AELs. An evolution is reasonable, but a minimum value in all does not recognise the reality of 
technique and technology (Article 15). 
With the same prudent approach, setting by law that industrial emissions must be set within the minimum of the 
ranges listed as the different techniques demonstrates a complete lack of knowledge of the technology.  
The same well -maintained technology does NOT yield the same values depending on a myriad of parameters 
surrounding the factory, ranging from ambient humidity, temperature, combination of raw materials, hardness or 
temperature of the cooling water, etc. Many of these parameters interact with each other, making it impossible 
to maintain the minimum in all of them. Only very simple and very similar installation s with very low variability 
allow a uniform tabulation of emission values (at the technical minimum) in the permits, which seems to be the 
"optimum" in the revision of the Directive. 
Therefore, demanding the minimum possible compatible with the technology in the installation will require an 
exercise similar to the one in Seville, but particularised for each installation. The proof is that engineering 
companies that install a technique do not guarantee the same value in all conditions or offer a range for ea ch 
installation. 
As an alternative to the minimum value, a derogation system is allowed which must be reviewed in 4-year periods. 
This would lead either to disproportionate costs for the industries or to a disproportionate technical assessment 
for those re sponsible for assessing such a dossier, which is precisely the basis of the directive and the emission 
values. 
Indirect discharges (Article 15.1). 
UNESID appreciates the consideration of indirect discharges due to the treatment of wastewater in pooled 
treatment plants, a water management system widely demonstrated in our country.  
In many areas of Spain, water management is pooled through water management consortiums (industrial estates) 
where water is discharged to the collector, oriented towards a pooled treatment plant. The discharge to the

New techniques (Article 27). 
The creation of a centre to assess emerging techniques, which could well be combined with the EIPPCB or even 
equally in Seville, is welcome. 
However, emerging techniques need more than two years to be assessed . As mentioned above, the sensitivity of 
the performance of each technique, together with its variability ove r time, depends (in addition to its correct 
maintenance) on conditions such as humidity, temperature, etc. 
Two years is far from being considered a time for a technique to demonstrate its proper functioning on an industrial 
scale and thus the generation of  ET-AELS (Emission Values Associated with Emerging Techniques). The minimum 
should be 4 or 5 years in more than 3 locations with different environmental conditions, in order to ensure the 
representativeness of the sample. 
The industry is also concerned abo ut the role of the engineering companies behind the development and 
commercialisation of each technique. Their business will be to have their techniques/technologies reflected in 
these ET -AELS. This may give a reality check to the E U Commission, having to deal with many engineering 
companies each selling their own product vs. the others. Again, in this respect, the sensitivity of the information 
will be a very sensitive issue. 
Fines (Article 79). 
UNESID considers that a system of clear penalties for behavio ur that exceeds the conditions established in the 
permits, with intentionality and concomitance, and in which the economic benefit is voluntarily prioritised over 
the cost of correcting the situation, is reasonable. Penalties should be clearly differentiat ed from possible 
temporary or transitory non-compliance of a temporary or transitory nature, without intentionality, even if it could 
have been corrected with greater diligence or in a shorter period. 
The proposed penalties, given the extreme amount, shoul d correspond exclusively to intentional behaviour, with 
clear knowledge of the situation. 
There is an overlap with those activities such as many of UNESID's partners where environmental liability is 
applicable, and the assessment of risks, as well as the r aising of financial guarantees for their correction, where 
necessary. However, it incorporates the concept of damage to health that goes beyond environmental liability. 
Without undermining its importance, and as UNESID has previously indicated, it consider s that damage to health 
should remain outside the scope of the directive, because it depends on other legislation, complicating the 
management of the authorisations. 
 
Strengthening the Seville process. 
Experience in the development of BREFs has shown that the tendency of the various non-industry stakeholders is 
to want to integrate all aspects, which is again reflected in the approach taken in the proposed revision of the 
directive. 
UNESID advocates the opposite: to focus the effort of review, control and understanding of the most relevant 
parameters in each industrial activity. However, each country, together with the participating NGOs, brings its 
priorities and demands that they are reflected in the BREFs of each sector, complicating the Seville proc ess, both 
in the information requirements and in their analysis, and subsequently in their assessment and final agreement 
of BAT-AELs.

Clear and delimited information on what is most relevant for each sector would allow a better knowledge and

The Seville process would work much better if it is governed by the principle of clearly specifying what is most

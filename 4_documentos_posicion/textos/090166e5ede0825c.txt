Executive Summary 
The German insurance industry welcomes the opportunity to comment 
on the European Commission’s proposals. 
Unfortunately, the draft does not address the issue of the  “eternal 
right of withdrawal”, which we consider to be crucial. Legal certainty 
should be created in this context by placing an absolute time limit on 
the right of withdrawal, for instance one year after the conclusion of a 
contract. This would be in line with more recent developments in con-
sumer protection legislation , including the existing provisions of the  
Consumer Rights Directive and the discussions on the review of the 
Consumer Credit Directive. Such a time limit is not only required with 
regard to the now proposed amendments on the distance marketing 
of financial services but also and in particular with regard to the right 
of withdrawal pursuant to the Solvency II Directive , which would then 
be solely applicable to life insurances. According to current legislation, 
the lack of such a time limit allows for a rescission of long -term life 
insurance contracts even decades after a contract was concluded, at 
the expense of the other insurance customers. In addition, concise and 
comprehensible model instructions on withdrawal  – for instance 
along the lines of Annex I of the Consumer Rights Directive – should 
be provided at European level. Clarifications concerning the provisions 
on a withdrawal button would also lead to more practical arrange-
ments and to an increase in legal certainty. 
Notwithstanding the above, we support the approach to make provi-
sions on distance marketing subordinate to the more specific, sectoral 
rules. The wording concerning the issue of subsidiarity, however, 
should leave no room for  ambiguity to avoid legal uncertainty in the 
future.  
It might be even more useful for practitioners to instead incorporate 
those provisions of the current Distance Marketing Directive  II which 
will still be relevant in the future into industry-specific regulations. With 
regard to the insurance industry, this could be done by incorporating 
the right of withdrawal applying to non-life insurances into the Insur-
ance Distribution Directive (IDD). This way, uncertainties which might 
arise from subsidiarity provisions with unclear scope of application – 
also with regard to pre -contractual information requirements – could 
be prevented.

The German insurance industry welcomes the revision of Directive 
2002/65/EC concerning the distance marketing of consumer financial ser-
vices (hereinafter referred to as “Distance Marketing Directive II”). Above 
all, it is understandable that there is a general need for moderni sing the 
provisions on distance contracts as a result of the progress made in digital-
isation since the Directive was issued in 2002. In line with other, more recent 
European legislation, however, this would also include 
 
• amending the right of withdrawal applicable to insurance contract s 
by an absolute expiry rule (see I.) as well as 
• adopting European model instructions on withdrawal (see II.). 
 
Notwithstanding the above, we support repealing the Distance Marketing 
Directive II as proposed in the Commission’s draft. Insofar as the provisions 
that will continue to be relevant for the distance marketing of financial ser-
vices shall be incorporated into the Consumer Rights Directive 2011/83/EU, 
which applies across industries, however, we would like to comment as fol-
lows: 
 
The different needs of the various financial services sectors  could be met 
more effectively by incorporating respective provisions into industry-specific 
legislation. So far, they are summari sed in the Distance Marketing Di-
rective II without distinction. The upcoming modernisation could be used to 
make a clear distinction. With regard to the insurance industry, this could 
be done by incorporating the right of withdrawal applying to non-life insur-
ances into the Insurance Distribution Directive1. Overlaps between the pre-
vious Distance Marketing Directive II and product-specific legal provisions, 
in particular with regard to pre -contractual information requirements (see 
IV.), could thus be eliminated without creating any legal uncertaint ies due 
to unclear subsidiarity provisions.  
 
In detail: 
 
 
I. Right of withdrawal (Article 16b draft Consumer Rights Directive) 
 
The right of withdrawal, which has been stipulated in several industry-spe-
cific and cross -industry regulations, is an important tool of consumer pro-
tection. It gives customers the opportunity to reconsider their decision within 
a certain period of time after a contract has been concluded and to withdraw 
from the contract, if applicable. Adverse impacts of hasty decisi ons to the

detriment of the customers can thus be prevented without requiring a man-
datory cooling-off period prior to the conclusion of a contract. 
 
With regard to the insurance sector, the arrangements on the right of with-
drawal stipulated in the Distance Marketing Directive II and the Solvency II 
Directive2 (restricted to life insurances in this context) have been relevant 
so far. In German legislation, the provisions have been transposed in Sec-
tions 8 et seq. of the Insurance Contract Act (Versicherungsvertragsgesetz, 
VVG), exceeding the requirements of the EU legislation and applying to all 
insurance contracts irrespective of the line of business and the way in which 
a contract is concluded. 
 
1. Specific design of the proposed subsidiarity clause 
 
We welcome the intention of the European Commission to increase legal 
certainty in life insurance by applying the principle of subsidiarity to the pro-
visions on the right of withdrawal (see Article 16b(6) draft Consumer Rights 
Directive). Due to the business models of professional actors in connection 
with the “eternal right of withdrawal ”, which are described in more detail 
below, the respective provisions are particularly often subject to legal dis-
putes. Making the transposition easier for national legislators is one of the 
reasons why the provisions on subsidiarity should be drafted in a way that 
leaves no room for ambiguity. From our point of view, it is necessary to use 
an even more precise language in this context. 
 
In particular, there should be no reference  to “rules on the exercise of the 
right of withdrawal“ in industry-specific legal acts, but generally to “rules on 
the right of withdrawal”. Otherwise, the provision could be interpreted such 
that industry-specific rules which only stipulate abstract provisions specifi-
cally on the exercise of the right of withdrawal, such as Article 186 of the 
Solvency II Directive, for instance, are not covered. 
 
In addition, from our point of view, it wou ld be necessary to mention some 
particularly relevant industry-specific provisions where the principle of sub-
sidiarity will apply, such as Article 186 of the Solvency II Directive, but also 
Article 14(6) of the Mortgage Credit Directive, as examples in the clause 
itself. This would further strengthen the binding character as compared to 
the currently envisaged mentioning of these provisions in recital  13 of the 
draft. Finally, the aim should be to ensure maximum legal certainty by stip-
ulating that, in case that there are legal acts which have priority, the subsid-
iarity clause set out in Article 16b(6) of the draft Consumer Rights Directive 
explicitly excludes the application of paragraphs  1 to 5 of this Article. Un-
certainty regarding the scope of a priority application would thus be

2. The “eternal right of withdrawal” 
 
Irrespective of the proposed application of the principle of subsidiarity to the 
provisions on distance marketing, it is being criticised that the EU Commis-
sion’s draft does not address the issue of the “ eternal right of withdrawal”. 
The current version of the European reg ulations has led to the establish-
ment of such an indefinite right of withdrawal in Germany, which is not only 
relevant in the area of consumer credits but in particular in the area of life 
insurance products. The number of cases reported by insurers as wel l as 
the insurance ombudsman shows that the “eternal right of withdrawal” has 
become the main reason for legal disputes in life insurance. 
 
This is due to the fact that the withdrawal period does not start until certain 
information have been provi ded pursuant to the European regulations 3. If 
no proof of the provision of information can be provided later on (i.e. often 
many years later) or if the information do not meet the requirements which 
are frequently not specified until a later date, the right of withdrawal contin-
ues to apply4. National regulations which aimed at limiting the eternal right 
of withdrawal were rejected by the E uropean Court of Justice 5. Statutory 
model instructions at national level do not provide complete certain ty ei-
ther6. For providers of long -term contracts in life insurance, i.e. with accu-
mulation phases of 30 to 40 years as well as subsequent lifetime annuity in 
some cases, this means considerable uncertainty. The eternal right of with-
drawal is increasingly used for other than the initially intended purposes to 
increase individual investment returns at the expense of the other insurance 
customers years or even decades after a contract was concluded, making 
use of financial market developments that have occurred in the meantime7.

3. Necessary solution 
 
Since the “eternal right of withdrawal ” has been exercised , the European 
legislator is trying to create legal certainty within the context of the revision 
of the regulations concerned. It introduced an absolute time limit (12 months 
from the day a contract was concluded) for the right of withdrawal stipulated 
in the Distance Marketing Directive I8 as well as for the right of withdrawal 
stipulated in the Doorstep Selling Directive9, when the two acts were com-
bined in the present Consumer Rights Directive. The significance of legal 
certainty was explicitly highlighted in this context10. It is reported that there 
are now also efforts to develop a respective solution within the scope of the 
current revision of the Consumer Credit Directive. 
 
We believe it is thus all the more important to take similar remedial actions 
with regard to the particularly concerned life insurance contracts in this con-
text. If the Distance Marketing Dir ective II will no longer apply to life insur-
ances, as is currently intended, an amendment to the Solvency II Directive 
is required. The Directive is currently being reviewed and revised by the 
European legislator. In our view, this opportunity should be seized to har-
monise the right of withdrawal across European regulations und to create 
clarity for the benefit of policyholders and insurers. 
 
To guarantee maximum harmonisation, we recommend including a respec-
tive time limit in the new provision of Article  16b of the draft Consumer 
Rights Directive, which applies the principle of subsidiarity, as well.  
 
Article 10 Consumer Rights Directive should be used as a model here to 
ensure consistency, as highlighted in the Commission’s Proposal (page 4). 
The present proposal is contradictory: on the one hand,  the right of

A maximum withdrawal period  in accordance with  Article 10 Consumer

adequately informed about their right to withdraw from a contract, but it 
would also meet the providers’ need for legal certainty. At the same time, in 
line with consumer protection, individual customers and professional policy 
buyers would be prevented from misusing the right of withdrawal and thus 
indirectly reducing the profit of the other insurance customers. There are 
other reasons as well why placing a time limit on the right of withdrawal is 
in line with consumer interests: in case of actual mis-selling, consumers are 
entitled to rescind a contract by way of claiming compensation for damages 
even several years after a contract was concluded. This is a more adequate 
legal remedy than a purely formalistic eternal “withdrawal joker”. 
 
Moreover, national legislators should be given the possibility to terminate 
the eternal right of withdrawal also with regard to already existing contracts 
within the scope of a transitional provision. 
 
 
II. European model instructions on withdrawal 
 
As a general rule, information on the right of withdrawal shall be provided in 
a clear and comprehensible manner. This shall be explicitly clarified with 
regard to the recast of the Consumer Rights Directive, as has been the case 
previously in the Distance Marketing Directive II11. Extensive provisions on 
how to draft information on the right of withdrawal, which have resulted in 
the fact that the information texts now comprise several pages, however, 
make it difficult to achieve this goal. Short and concise European model 
instructions on how to provide information on the right of withdrawal would 
therefore be very helpful in this context. In Annex I of the Consumer Rights 
Directive, the legislator has set out a model withdrawal form . Respective 
provisions applying to the insurance industry – for life insurance contracts 
as well as for non-life insurance contracts – could be based on these model 
instructions.

III. Withdrawal button 
 
To the extent that the provision is relevant, introducing a withdrawal button, 
as set out in Article 16b(5) of the draft Consumer Rights Directive, involves 
legal uncertainties.  
 
According to the draft, the withdrawal button shall be placed “on the same 
electronic interface” as the one used to conclude the distance contract. The 
legislative proposal, however, does not define what an electronic interface 
shall mean. It is thus uncle ar whether the website or mobile app in their 
entirety or only a particular part of the application process can serve as an 
appropriate place for the withdrawal button. The second option, in particu-
lar, would result in practical difficulties in terms of im plementation. For in-
stance, if a customer concludes an insurance contract online, without sim-
ultaneously creating a customer account, the website contents will not be 
permanently available. The customer could then only get back to the exact 
web page where they concluded the contract by means of some technical 
detours, for instance a link in an e-mail.  
 
It would therefore be more practical to place the withdrawal button right 
where the customer can view their contract (in the customer portal, for in-
stance) or in the e-mail confirming the conclusion of a contract. This should 
be explicitly clarified in the legislative text.  
 
 
IV. Pre-contractual information requirements and adequate explana-
tions (Articles 16a and 16d draft Consumer Rights Directive) 
 
1. Specific design of the proposed subsidiarity clauses 
 
With regard to the pre -contractual information requirements and explana-
tions stipulated under Articles 16a and 16d of the draft Consumer Rights 
Directive, we support the approach to avoid overlaps with industry-specific 
regulations – to ensure legal certainty – by applying the principle of subsid-
iarity to the proposed provisions. However, here too, the proposed subsidi-
arity clauses (Article 16a(6) and Article  16d(4) draft Consumer Rights Di-
rective) cause confusion regarding their scope of application.  
 
The proviso set out in Article 16a(6) of the draft Consumer Rights Directive 
(“unless provided otherwise in that act”) , in particular, already leads to un-
certainty, since it is unclear what kind of information shall actually have pri-
ority in a particular case  and what shall not. As a result, there will be an 
opaque co-existence of different legal acts. 
 
 
page 9 / 10 
In addition: several European regulations have stipulated rules on pre-con-
tractual information requirements for insurance products: the Solvency II Di-
rective, the PRIIP Regulation12, the PEPP Regulation13 as well as the Insur-
ance Distribution Directive (IDD). The specific requirements, however, have 
been drafted differently in terms of the level of detail, depending on the ap-
plicable act.  
 
To prevent legal uncertainty, it should therefore be explicitly indicated in 
Article 16a(6) and Article 16d(4) of the draft Consumer Rights Directive – 
similar to what has been stated previously under point I. on the right of with-
drawal – in the form of the most relevant examples, which particular Union 
acts shall have priority in terms of application with regard to the provision of 
information (IDD or Solvency II Directive, for instance). Mo reover, with re-
gard to the proposed pre-contractual information requirements, too, it 
should be clarified that the subsidiarity aimed for under Article 16a( 6) ap-
plies to all previous paragraphs of this clause (i.e. paragraphs 1 to 5). Arti-
cle 16d(4) can serve as a model here.  
 
Nonetheless, we appreciate the fact that the European Commission re-
duces the information requirements for insurance products by applying the 
principle of subsidiarity to the pre-contractual information requirements stip-
ulated under Article 16a of the draft Consumer Rights Directive. In particular 
with regard to life insurance, however, there is additional need for revision 
and reduction. In this regard, we support the proposals set out i n the final 
technical advice by EIOPA on retail investor protection as part of the retail 
investment strategy of the European Commission14.  
 
2. Time at which information is provided 
 
Irrespective of the previously explained – necessary – clarifications within 
the context of the subsidiarity provisions, we would like to highlight the fol-
lowing issues:  
 
The provision stipulated under Article 16a(3) of the draft Consumer Rights 
Directive, which says that the trader shall provide pre -contractual infor-
mation at least one day before the consumer is bound by a distance con-
tract, is not practical.  
 
The possibility of concluding a distance insurance contract is particularly 
attractive when con sumers need insurance cover at short notice . This is

  
 
INTA 
 
Comments on EU proposal for regulation on geographical indications for  
wines, spirit drinks, and agricultural products, and quality schemes for agricultural 
products 
 
June 2022 
 
The International Trademark Association (INTA) through its Geographical Indications Committee 
submits the following comments on the European Commission’s recent proposal for regulation on 
European Union geographical indications (GIs) for wine, spirit drinks and agricultural products, 
and quality schemes for agricultural products, amending Regulations (EU) No 1308/2013, (EU) 
2017/1001 and (EU) 2019/787 and repealing Regulation (EU) No 1151/2012 (“Wine, Spirits, 
Agriculture Proposal”). 
INTA is committed to continuing to provide its insights as an organization that brings together the 
knowledge and expertise of brand owners and professionals, from more than 187 countries of the 
world, including 1351 in the European Union and the UK, dedicated to supporting trademarks and 
related intellectual property (IP) rights, including GIs, to foster consumer trust, economic growth, 
and innovation. INTA’s membership includes several GI associations. 
 
I. General Comments 
 
INTA supports the protection of GIs in accordance with its Board Resolution on ‘Protection of 
Geographical Indications’. INTA’s position is grounded in a desire for good governance and 
regulation, recognizing the principle of “first in time, first in right,” which in turn is based on the 
well-established principles of territoriality, exclusivity, priority, and good faith when resolving 
conflicts between geographical indications and trademarks, including collective and certification 
marks.  
 
We wish to stress, as a preliminary comment, that INTA does not advocate for expansion or 
contraction of GIs. We wish to focus on the quality of the systems that register them, so that they 
provide a clear and effective governance. INTA considers that the harmonization of protection for 
all GIs would be positive insofar as it would serve to increase legal certainty  and provided it does 
not involve disruption of existing legal rights, including trademarks. 
 
In this context, INTA recommends that any regulation of geographical indications have effective 
and transparent mechanisms for addressing: 
 
(i) applications for geographical indications, applications for amendments, and 
applications for cancellation; and

(ii) opposition, cancellation, and notification procedures that recognize pre-existing 
trademark rights with which geographical indications might conflict or cause

An excessive scope of protection could unduly prejudice the existing rights of trademark holders 
and limit the freedom to use generic terms. The GI protection regime in the European Union needs 
to be fair and effective. 
 
II. Specific comments 
 
To ensure a balanced system for the harmonized EU GI protection for non-
agricultural products, there should be clearly defined criteria for protection, 
transparent procedures, adequate boundaries between geographical indications and 
trademarks, a fair balance between public and private interests, and due respect for 
prior rights. Accordingly, any new system should reflect the following: 
 
• Scope of Protection: 
 
o The scope of protection for geographical indications should be tailored to 
prevent a misleading use but look to avoid any increase in potential conflicts. 
Further, the scope of protection for GIs in the EU should also be tailored to 
prevent the impairment of the right to use generic terms. INTA is pleased to 
note that Article 29 of the Wine, Spirits, Agriculture Proposal makes clear that 
generic terms shall not be registered as geographical indications. 
 
o In designing the scope of protection, it is important to bear in mind that an 
excessive scope of protection, e.g., going beyond consumer deception and 
including unclear and/or not clearly defined concepts, could unduly prejudice 
existing rights of trademark holders, create obstacles for traders in the 
development and innovation of new brands, and limit the freedom to use 
generic terms. On the other hand, greater clarity on the terms of protection 
would increase legal certainty and predictability. Any new system should avoid 
triggering misconceptions about protection to individual components or 
derivatives of geographical indications.  
 
o INTA is concerned that the prohibition on evocation of a geographical 
indication (Article 27(1)(b) of the Wine, Spirits, Agriculture Proposal), which is 
defined in Article 27(2) as arising “where a term, sign, or other labelling or 
packaging device presents a direct and clear link with the product covered by 
the registered geographical indication in the mind of the reasonably 
circumspect consumer, thereby exploiting, weakening, diluting or being 
detrimental to the reputation of the registered name,” creates such 
uncertainty, although INTA welcomes the intention to include a definition of 
“evocation” to provide more clarity to this term. However, the suggested article 
may rather lead to more uncertainty. On the one hand, Article 27(2) includes 
the word “in particular” and is therefore no closed definition, which begs the 
question of its remaining scope of protection. Furthermore, the protection 
covers not just the geographical indication itself but also any “term, sign, or 
other labelling or packaging device,” which is very broad and makes it difficult 
to ascertain what is prohibited. The fact that all that is required to be shown is

a “link” between that element and the geographical indication, without any 
need to demonstrate a likelihood of confusion, increases the difficulties. 
Therefore, INTA suggests that arguments, evidence and other sufficient 
elements that strengthen the conflict or likelihood of confusion, should be 
provided in order to demonstrate the “link” with the product covered by the 
registered geographical indication. 
 
o On the other hand, INTA is pleased to note that Article 27(7) makes clear that 
where a geographical indication comprises a compound term, part of which is 
generic, then the generic element does not enjoy protection. 
 
• Registration 
 
o The registration should require a formal procedure that ensures the 
fulfilment of the requirements for protection and transparency and provides 
clarity about the scope of protection of the terms to be protected and allows 
interested parties to voice their concerns in the context of the procedure.  
 
• Examination: 
 
o Examination should ensure compliance with the requirement of the link between 
the product and the location, with product specifications, and require evidence 
relating to the link to the territory. Particular care should be applied for non-
geographical terms to verify that the term at issue has acquired a geographic 
association. 
 
o Examination should include a review to ensure that protection of the GI would 
not result in a conflict with any prior trademarks and that protection of the GI 
would not impair the free use of any generic terms. 
 
o Compliance with the protection requirements should not only be a matter for 
examination in the country of origin but also subject to full scrutiny by the 
competent EU agency or body, and the facts be made accessible to the public. 
The examination should take into consideration market realities, such as 
indicated geographical area, including the production of a certain good in third 
countries and how that product is generally identified, as well as quality and 
reputation. This should be open to challenges by any interested party. 
 
o Article 9(4) of the Wine, Spirits, Agriculture Proposal describes the National 
Opposition procedure: It “shall ensure publication of the application for 
registration and provide for a period of at least 2 months from the date of 
publication within which any natural or legal person having a legitimate interest 
and established or resident on the territory of the Member State in which the 
product concerned originates may lodge an opposition to the application for 
registration with that Member State”. We would like to make two points: 
 
 Considering the complexity and importance of the matter, we consider that 
a minimum 2 month opposition period is too short and would suggest that 
 
 4  
this be extended to 3 months. This is the time allowed to oppose EU 
trademarks before the EUIPO. 
 
 There may be legal persons with a legitimate interest to oppose, for example 
owners of pre-existing trademark applications, but who are not established 
or resident in the Member State in which the product originates. This 
provision unfairly excludes them from protecting their rights, which as we 
note above are acknowledged as legitimate and important. We recommend 
that this provision be amended to simply enable those with a legitimate 
interest to oppose, regardless of where they are located. Even though other 
parties may use the Union opposition procedure, INTA believes that any 
issues regarding conflict with prior rights should be resolved at the earliest 
possible opportunity.  
 
• Non-EU GIs:  
 
o INTA is pleased to note that protection will be open to GIs of third countries 
without discrimination, and with direct access to the registration system by 
producers’ groups.  
 
• Safeguards & Resolution of Conflicts: 
 
o Appropriate safeguards should be included to avoid likelihood of confusion with 
existing trademarks, to ensure that conflicts between trademarks and GIs will be 
resolved in line with the principles of priority, exclusivity, good faith and territoriality, 
and that GIs should not be registered for generic terms in the European Union.  
 
o Article 20(1)(c) of the Wine, Spirits, Agriculture Proposal provides that an 
opposition may be made on the grounds that registration of the proposed 
geographical indication would “jeopardise the existence” of a trademark. Article 
31 of the Wine, Spirits, Agriculture Proposal states that “in the light of a 
trademark’s reputation or renown, registration of the name proposed as a 
geographical indication could mislead the consumer as to the true identity of 
the product.” 
 
o INTA respectfully submits that this qualified wording does not go far enough 
and that the existence of a validly registered trademark in the EU or one of its 
Member States should be sufficient grounds for opposition. 
 
o Article 63 of the Wine, Spirits, Agriculture Proposal sets out the grounds for 
opposition for Traditional Specialties Guaranteed but these do not include the 
existence of a pre-existing validly registered trademark or the fact that the term 
sought to be protected is generic. In light of the principles referred to above, 
INTA recommends that these be included as additional grounds.  
 
 Priority: 
 
o Protection under the system should not prejudice prior rights with an earlier 
priority, and in particular, the right to maintain the registration, use and renew 
a prior trademark should be guaranteed even in case that the GI is 
 
 5  
registered despite an opposition (including the possibility to file new 
trademark applications including the previously protected mark in order 
to allow for modernizations and updates of existing marks). While Article 35 
of the Wine, Spirits, Agriculture Proposal contains safeguard provisions for 
prior trademarks, it is not sufficiently clear in respect.  
 
• Cancellation & Amendment:  
 
o Appropriate cancellation and amendment procedures, open to any party with 
a legitimate interest, including the group or entity on behalf of which the non-
agricultural GI has been registered, should be in place to enable the 
amendment or cancellation of terms that were registered in spite of not 
fulfilling the protection requirements or which are infringing prior rights, impair 
the use of generic terms, as well as those terms for which protection is no 
longer justified due to changed factual circumstances (e.g. absence of 
presence on the market over a prolonged period of time, economic need, legal 
justification). 
 
o INTA notes that Article 26 of the Wine, Spirits, Agriculture Proposal allows 
cancellation when (a) the requirements for the product specification can no 
longer be complied with or (b) no product has been placed on the market for at 
least seven consecutive years under the geographical indication. However, 
there is no ability to cancel on the grounds that a geographical indication 
infringes a pre-existing validly registered trademark or is a generic term. There 
may be circumstances in which a party with a legitimate interest misses the 
opportunity to oppose registration on these grounds. We respectfully submit in 
the interests of fairness that they should be able to protect their rights through 
cancellation, and that these should be added as additional grounds. 
 
 
o For the reason stated above, INTA recommends that these should also be 
included as grounds for seeking cancellation of a traditional specialty 
guaranteed in terms of Article 68 of the Wine, Spirits, Agriculture Proposal.  
 
 
 Domain Name System: 
 
o Articles 27 and 34 of the Wine, Spirits, Agriculture Proposal would protect 
geographical indications from bad-faith use or registration, misuse, and dilution 
in domain names and would create a domain name information and alert system 
for GI applicants.      INTA agrees that geographical indications deserve 
protection in the domain name system, provided the implementation of articles 
27 and 34 takes into consideration the concerns raised by Brand Owners in 
previous international scenarios and cases. 
 
 Market Control  
 
o Given the fast pace at which markets transform and evolve, INTA supports the 
inclusion of controls and enforcement of geographical indication rights in the 
marketplace as seen in Article 42 of the Wine, Spirits, Agriculture Proposal.

Trademark owners whose product complies with geographical indication 
standards have endured the challenge of competing with counterfeit or fake 
products that are offered and promoted in marketplaces as legitimate that 
comply with the product specification of a geographical indication protected 
under this regulation. 
 
 
 Recognized Producer Groups 
 
o Article 33 of the Wine, Spirits, Agriculture Proposal, provides that EU Member 
States shall designate one producer group for each geographical indication 
originating in their territory. To be eligible for recognition the group should 
consist of two thirds of producers who account for two thirds of production of 
the geographical indication of origin. Recognized producers’ groups are given 
specific rights which include authority to liaise with anticounterfeiting bodies and 
file applications for enforcement with customs authorities.  INTA has two 
concerns about this Article: 
 
 First, INTA is concerned that only geographical indications produced in 
the EU are eligible to have a recognized producer group. This unfairly 
discriminates against geographical indications produced in other 
countries or regions outside the EU. 
 
 Second, the use of the word “shall” in Article 33 indicates that all 
geographical indications should have a recognized producer group, but 
the requirement that that group must consist of two thirds of producers 
and production, may mean that some geographical indications are 
excluded. Not only is this inconsistent but, it would be detrimental to 
producers and consumers if certain geographical indications do not 
benefit from the additional protections because the requirement cannot 
be met. 
 
o INTA therefore recommends that Article 33 be amended to allow countries 
outside the European Union to notify the European Commission that they have 
appointed a Recognized Producers Group for a geographical indication 
produced within their territory, which will then enjoy the same powers as 
Recognized Producers Groups within the EU. Further INTA recommends that 
the requirement that that group must represent a minimum number of producers 
or percentage of production be removed. Instead, it should be up to national 
authorities to determine which group is most appropriate.

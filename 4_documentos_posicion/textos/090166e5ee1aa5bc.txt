 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
CEVI’s position 
 
REFORM OF GEOGRAPHICAL INDICATIONS SCHEME 
 
The European Confederation of Independent Winegrowe rs (CEVI) represents and defends the 
interests of the European Independent Winegrowers. They grow their own vine and produce 
themselves their wine, and in most cases run family  businesses. They directly sell the fruits of their  
labour to the consumers, and actively contribute to  the development of the entrepreneurial fabric of 
the internal market. Independent winegrowers play a key role in many rural areas, and have a positive 
impact on the local dynamism and economy. 
 
The EU is the world’s leading producer of wine at m ore than 160 million hl, representing over 60% 
of world production. Nowadays, there are approximately 3.2 million ha of area under vines (45% of 
the world’s total) and 2.5 million agricultural hol dings grow vines. Moreover, up to 83% of the EU 
vine area is dedicated to the production of GI wines. One could say that without GI wines there is no 
European viticulture. European wine was by far the largest EU agricultural export, this data will need 
to be reassessed following the COVID19 crisis. 
 
CEVI welcomes the open consultation process on the EU’s food quality scheme. The EU drives for 
decades the production of quality products and that  preserves sustainable rural area. The new 
Common Agriculture Policy will reinforce several tools for the GIs and CEVI calls on the European 
Commission to support these innovative elements and  their implementation. CEVI believes that the 
priority of the EU quality scheme is to support GIs  producers and their long-term sustainable 
commitment.  
 
 
1.  The single CMO must be GI wine provisions unique framework  
GI wines rules are within the framework of the wine specific regulation, which is the Single Common 
Market Organisation (CMO). The EC through the GI re view proposes to split the wine policy into 
two legal instruments. We believe that this would deeply affect GI wines. 
First, unlike other sectors, the European wine sector is composed in majority of products under a GI 
(EU average: around 65%, and in some of the leading  wine producing Member States up to 90%). 
So, all the EU provisions on labelling, market measures, competiti on rules, vine planting 
authorisations, oenological practices, Interbranch and Producer Organisations are linked to the rules 
on GI wines. Changes relating to GI protection and GI sustainability must be understood and 
implemented in the broader wine policy perspective. 
 
Then, the administrative burden and cost affecting independent winegrowers is already very high and 
CEVI truly believes that splitting the provisions concerning the wine sector into two separate 
Regulations, the current proposal would constitute an administrat ive complexity. This proposal  is

CEVI wishes to improve the current provisions but calls upon co-legislators to shift them back in the

2.  DG AGRI must remain fully in charge of the product specification management 
In the EU, GIs are not mere Intellectual Property Rights (IPRs). There are other major dimensions to 
them. They are an agricultural and rural development tool, they allow for derogations to competition 
law (ex. rules on yields, plantations, etc.) and th eir specifications provide for adaptations to socie tal 
demands. All these elements are inherent to GIs and their policy. GI product specifications cannot be 
reduced to the protection of a geographical name. T he historical dimension of the law applicable to 
them is essential. The agricultural essence of wine GIs must be prevalent because without this notion 
there is no more wine GIs.  
 
Although, EUIPO’s "training" by DG AGRI over the last 4 years, it has a poor understanding of GIs 
and their characteristics. Examples (although EUIPO’s training, there are still some relevant topics to 
be fully considered):  
• GI groups have to bring many cases before the EUIPO Board of Appeal (i.e. important 
costs in terms of monitoring/appeal) to win disputes between GIs and trademarks.  
• The EUIPO considers that the question whether to consider a traditional term for wine 
in a GI as generic or not is still open. Whereas this seems quite straightforward.  
 
The EUIPO does not consider the fact that product s pecifications, and more generally GIs, are 
dynamic and constantly evolve. Over the last decade s, the GI specifications evolved thank to the 
permanent dialogue between the DG AGRI and the stakeholders. We will lose years of work without 
any guarantee of the EUIPO's willingness to dialogue. 
In case of delegation to EUIPO, there is no guarant ee that the delays will be reduced, based on the 
recently discover 4 years of “subcontracting”, we h ave not seen any improvement in the length of the 
procedures.  
EUIPO should be given new competences that will allow GI actors to benefit from its core expertise: 
the protection of IPRs. Indeed, EUIPO resources and technical support could support DG AGRI lead, 
in terms of database management, protection of GIs in domain names’ registration, fight against 
counterfeiting and piracy, support the EC in the opposition procedures for IP aspects.  
 
 
3.  The subsidiarity must be the rule to producer group management  
Currently, GI producer groups are managed at nation al level. Therefore, very different structures in 
place from one Member State to another. An efficient structure allows an efficient GI.  
The current wording of the text may put at risk models and structures that over the years have proved 
their worth in their respective Member States. More  subsidiarity is needed to ensure that Member 
States can consider its specificities and decide whether a group is representative of a given GI product. 
Looking at the CMO Regulation neither Producer Organisations nor Interbranch Organisations have 
specific representativeness criteria at the EU leve l. In both cases, Member State can determine 
whether they represent a significant part of the ec onomic activities linked to the production and/or 
transformation of a given product.  
 
 
 
One of the objectives of the GI system is to empower producers vis-à-vis the other parts of the supply 
chain (in particular the winegrowers in viticulture ). It is not clear how provisions such as including  
consumers, suppliers and retailers in GI producer g roups are consistent with the long-term task - 
endorsed by the European Commission - of strengthening the position of farmers in the food supply 
chain. We believe these provisions should be scrapped.  
 
 
4.  The three aspects of sustainability are rooted to GIs 
As a first step, all sustainability undertakings sh ould remain on a voluntary basis. CEVI considers 
that GIs are per se sustainable productions. The we alth of know-how and experience in the field of 
sustainability gained to date by the different actors of the European wine appellation sector constitutes 
a valuable starting point and asset for the achievement of the EU’s sustainability agenda.  
Independent winegrowers play a key role on economic, social and environmental sustainability in the 
rural areas. The production of non-localizable GI w ines maintains the dynamism and attractiveness 
of these rural areas, while shaping the landscape with a heightened environmental awareness. 
 
 
5.  The control must be simplified 
 
The control are essential to verify that a product designated by a geographical indication has been 
produced in compliance with the corresponding product specification.  
However, CEVI believes that these checks may be lim ited to administrative controls when they are 
secure and make it possible to ensure full compliance with the requirements and conditions laid down 
in the product specifications. This should lead to amend article 19 of the regulation (EU) 2019/34 to 
simplify the annual verification by limiting them to administrative checks only.  
 
 
6.  The protection must be stronger  
 
The EC proposals on the protection front go in the right direction and can be further strengthened.  
Protection provisions should focus on: 
• The labelling of GIs when used as ingredients in a processed product: the principles set out 
by the EC should be inverted. The practice to list the name with the sales denomination should 
be in principle forbidden. In exceptional cases where a given GI groups agrees to see its name 
associated with the product in question a procedure  should be in place for the group to give 
its consent.  
• Online protection: the text should better reflect t he progresses made in the Digital Services 
Act to provide a wider protection of GIs.  
• Protection of a GI wine name should also be envisag ed where a product refers to the use of 
barrels of that GI to age its product.  
 
There are some concerns about the EC proposals in t erms of defining genericity and evocation. The 
definition of genericity should be reviewed, and the definition of evocation should be left to the Courts 
to decide and handled on a case-by-case basis.

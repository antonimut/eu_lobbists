JRAIA’s comments on the Proposal for a Regulation of the European 
Parliament and of the Council on fluorinated greenhouse gases, 
amending Directive (EU) 2019/1937 and repealing 
 Regulation (EC) No 517/2014 
The Japan Refrigeration and Air Conditioning Industry Association (JRAIA) representing 
manufacturers of refrigeration, air conditioning and heat pump (RACHP) equipment in Japan 
would like to thank the Commission for the effort for revision of the EU F-Gas Regulation and 
the opportunity to provide our comments. In this paper, we would like to comment on the 
proposed revision of the Regulation. 
RACHP business sectors have contributed to maintaining/improving human life, through 
comfort air -conditioning, industrial air conditioning (necessary for many manufacturing 
processes, Data centers, etc.). RACHP products and in particular Heat Pumps are 
indispensable to sustainability. The review of the EU F-Gas Regulation is a focus of attention 
for the global RACHP industry facing very significant challenges. JRAIA is of the opinion that 
this revision is the most consequential because the F-Gas Regulation is one of the pillars for 
the achievement of carbon neutrality. All of the policies in place in the current F -Gas 
Regulation from 2014 can already be considered to be successful and have already led to 
very substantial decreases in GHG emissions. However, a clos e look at the Commission 
proposal makes us think that the proposal really may stall the growth of the RACHP industry. 
This would have negative impacts on accelerating electrification of heating through Heat 
Pumps, and moving away from boilers that run on fossil fuels. The F-Gas proposal in essence 
undermines and goes against the REPowerEU plan, both the initial version of March and the 
final Plan of May 2022. Not only do we feel strong apprehensions about many elements of 
the F-Gas proposal, but there are q uite a few inexplicable points which we believe need to 
be adapted, altered or at the very least clarified.   
  
Attached are JRAIA’s comments on the Proposed Revision. We would deeply appreciate it if 
our comments below could be taken into consideration.

JRAIA’s comments on the Proposed Revision of the EU F-Gas Regulation 
 Art ＃ JRAIA’s comments 
1 Art. 1(a) 
Subject-matter 
Inclusion of the phrase “safe use of alternatives” in the objective of the 
proposed Regulation is confusing.   
The European Commission explicitly envisions the use of specific refrigerants 
that are not intrinsically safe for use in the AC/HP industry. As the safe use of 
alternatives is fundamental, we fear the Commission is confusing means and 
ends and moving away from its long-held principle of technological neutrality.  
2 Art. 3 
Definitions 
There is a lack of definitions of “split air conditioner”, “split heat 
pump”, “self-contained”, “rated capacity”, “safety standards”, and 
“national safety standards”.  
These definitions are an integral part of the F-Gas Regulation. In proposed 
ANNEX IV, a rated capacity and a GWP limit seem to be considered as key 
elements. The fact that these crucial terms are not defined creates confusion, 
misinterpretation and misimplementation. This can be explained by taking “rated 
capacity” for example. When AC/HPs are what is called reversible type, they 
provide both heating and cooling. So the question is: which is the GWP limit for 
equipment with both a rated capacity for heating of 13kW and its rated capacity 
for cooling of 11kW, 150 or 750? 
There is a change in the definition of hermetically sealed equipment in 
Art. 3(9)  
JRAIA fails to see why the requirements for hermetically sealed equipment are 
limited to manufacturing process at the premises of the manufacturer of 
equipment. The proposed Regulation gives no convincing reason. The revision 
should validate our industry’s effort to reduce refrigerant leakage. This restriction 
should be removed from the definition.  
3 Art. 10

and training 
Clause on “access to update knowledge” in Art. 10(9) of the 2014 
Regulation has disappeared. Why?  
As the European Commission asserts the continuous and timely updating of 
safety standards is necessary, we think this clause should be restored. 
Mishandling highly flammable refrigerants such as propane due to human error 
can lead to fatal accidents. Continuous Professional Development (CPD) is 
imperative for personnel who handle, in particular, A3 refrigerants.

There is a lack of a clause on the synthesis report, which is stipulated 
by the 2014 F-Gas Regulation in Art. 11.6 
Based on Art. 11 point 6 in the 2014 Regulation, the European Commission 
published a synthesis report on information on national codes, standards or 
legislation of Member States with respect to replacement technologies. In the 
Explanatory Memorandum of the proposed Regulation (on page 5) the 
Commission states that “the continuous and timely updating of safety standards 
as well as codes and legislation at all levels, European, national, regional and 
local, is crucial to keep pace with the rapid development of technologies”. 
Therefore, not mentioning the synthesis report contradicts this awareness. We 
think the European Commission should bear primary responsibility of a synthesis 
report in the same way as the current F-Gas Regulation.

ANNEX VII 
More restrictive HFC quota levels and additional GWP limits should be 
removed. 
JRAIA understands the logic behind the introduction of these policies to some 
extent, but the European Commission should formulate its policy with its vision 
remaining fixed on the big picture. The proposed Regulation states that “today F-
Gas emissions amount to 2.5% of EU GHG emissions”. This fact implies these 
measures will not be what the European Commission can call “proportionate” 
from the ‘carbon neutrality’ perspective. Nowadays one thing the world agrees on 
is that reducing CO2 emissions in generating power is vital for achieving carbon 
neutrality. The EU as well as other industrialized nations, therefore, has pinned its 
hopes on increased use of renewables. Heat pump technology is sure to be a big 
driver for bumping up renewable energy use and its de-carbonization effect in 
combination with renewable energy is expected to be very significant. We can 
state categorically that this is right only when the right refrigerant is used for the 
right system. We are led to wonder if the European Commission is going to 
downplay its significance and even impede its dissemination. Therefore, HFC 
quota levels should remain in line with the current F-Gas Regulation by 
2030.  
Additional GWP limits are not needed as a transition to lower GWP refrigerants 
happen anyway under the strict HFC phase down from the current F-Gas 
Regulation.

11 ANNEX IV  Single rated capacity should not be adopted as determining factor for 
GWP limits for AC/HPs. 
There is a lack of scientific evidence for including rated capacity as determining 
factor. Refrigerant choice should be based on safety and energy efficiency. The 
first principle for ensuring safety is limited charge of refrigerant. There is also a 
global tendency for reconsidering evaluation methods of energy efficiency. The 
aim of it is to reflect actual performance in a real-life situation. New evaluation 
methods are, therefore, based on non-fixed compressor speeds. The single rated 
capacity used for convenience will not be coherent with internal regulations in the 
true meaning. 
12 ANNEX IV 
(17) 
Ban on plug-in room and other self-contained AC/HPs that contain 
fluorinated greenhouse gases with GWP of 150 or more from 2025 is not 
technically viable. 
In a current market situation, an abrupt refrigerant transition to these products 
that are not subject to any ban under the current regulation would be impossible 
to implement for manufacturers within a short 2 years between publication and 
implementation. The GWP values of current leading refrigerants for the 
equipment far exceeds 150. Given the GWP limit, A3 refrigerant candidates 
featuring strong flammability are within the GWP range. According to current EN 
safety standards, the equipment using A3 refrigerants will entail safety limits. 
Therefore, we cannot understand why the similar safety exemption to split AC/HP 
systems (18.b & c: “except when required to meet safety standards”) is not 
described with regard to the self-contained equipment. From a certain point of 
view, this clause may be interpreted as being limited to the small-sized self-
contained equipment that needs no safety exemption. The reference should be 
given to size of the equipment to which this clause applies so that equipment 
manufactures can give priority to what to work on.  
The implementation of the revised F-Gas Regulation must be preceded by 
establishment and updates of a series of refrigerant-related safety standards and 
regulations to keep the safe life of all EU citizens.  
13 ANNEX IV 
(18) 
The GWP limit of 150 for stationary AC/HPs should be reconsidered. 
JRAIA stresses the urgent necessity of rethinking 150 GWP limit, which is a 
dramatic drop from 750. The proposed Regulation states that it follows “the 
energy efficiency first principle by only taking into account alternatives that are 
being at least as energy efficient as equipment using a traditional F-Gas”. We are 
completely confident that A2L refrigerants quite clearly allow the equipment in 
which they are used to be significantly more energy efficient. We think that 
lowering the GWP limit to 150 is not based on credible evidence and the 
European Commission should make a well-informed decision. For further detail of 
JRAIA’s position on 150 GWP limit, please see the ATTACHMENT 2.

ATTACHMENT 2  
JRAIA’s comments on GWP150 limit for stationary AC/HPs or  
rated capacity of up to and including 12kW 
Introduction 
･ In this paper, we would like to express our views, especially on the proposal of GWP150 limit for split 
stationary AC/HPs of a rated capacity of up to and including 12kW, which is currently proposed in the 
review of F-Gas regulation. 
･ For views on the proposed GWP750 limit for split stationary AC/HPs of a rated capacity of more than 
12kW and the increasing HFC phase-down ambition, please refer to the JRAIA position paper dated 
May 24, 2021(please refer to ATTACHMENT 3). 
･ JRAIA believes that the "S+3E"* 1 perspective is essential in the choice of alternative refrigerants. 
When trying to lower the GWP of refrigerants, factors such as their flammability and the energy 
efficiency of equipment will become issues. There is a trade-off between these factors. 
･ Therefore, we believe that a well-balanced policy should be considered based not only on the GWP 
of refrigerants, but also on a comprehensive evaluation of the “S+3E”, and social sustainability and 
inclusivity as a precondition.  
･ In addition, five EU member states are currently considering PFAS restrictions under the REACH 
regulation. According to the definition and scope presented in July 2021 and February 2022, almost 
all alternative refrigerants are proposed to be covered, except for some HFCs/HFOs. However, JRAIA 
believes that all refrigerants should be managed under the F-Gas regulation, not by PFAS restrictions.  
 
The impact on refrigerant choice when the GWP150 limit is implemented  
 Given the policy option of GWP150 limit for split stationary AC/HPs (capacity of  ≦12kW) in the 
Commission proposal, JRAIA believes that the refrigerant options will be very limited , including 
refrigerants with higher flammability such as R290 (propane).  
･ As described below, there are issues that cannot be solved at present in terms of safety, energy 
efficiency, and economic feasibility, and it is unrealistic to use such refrigerants as an alternative.  
 
Safety risks of using propane for split stationary AC/HPs 
･ International Standard ISO 817:2014 segregates the flammability of refrigerants into 4 categories as 
follows: Class 1 (No flame propagation), Class 2L (Lower flammability), Class 2 (Flammable) and 
Class 3 (Higher flammability). 
･ Currently, R32, which is widely used for stationary AC/HPs of a rated capacity  of ≦12kW, is 
classified as Class 2L. Class 2L refrigerants present the lowest risk of the 3 flammable categories 
and are defined as having a burning velocity of less than 10 cm per second. The characteristic of 
this low burning velocity is that the flame front is difficult to propagate readily in a horizontal 
direction.This is because the buoyancy-induced flow in the unburned and burned gases due to 
combustion has a higher velocity than the burning velocity. This effectively means that a Class 2L 
refrigerant is difficult to explode, even if ignited, because the flame does not propagate rapidly 
outwards, and the heat as well as the burned gases produce slowly. We have done thorough risk 
assessments of A2L refirgerant-based equipment, resulting in having confirmed safety.

 However, this standard mainly focuses on the equipment use stage, and does not specify in detail 
transport, storage, installation, service, maintenance, repair, and disposal stages before and after the 
use. Under the current circumstances where relevant training and standards have not been 
established and operated, during installation, service, maintenance and repair, the risk of refrigerant 
leaks increases as the contractor works on site to install/remove refrigerant piping and add refrigerant 
charge. In addition,  the possibility of a certain amount of human error is pointed out even by trained 
and certified personnel, although the possibility of errors is even greater when the work is done by 
personnel who have not been trained or certified. At the time of disposal, the equipment is not always 
handled  properly, and there is also a possibility that the equipment will be  disposed of illegally. 
Furthermore, intentional criminal acts such as arson and theft can also occur. In fact, several 
accidents have occurred due to equipment using A3 refrigerant.*2 
･ In view of burning velocity and combustion energy, the possibility of a  serious fire accident is much 
higher for equipment using A3 refrigerant such as propane than equipment using A2L refrigerant. At 
present, the market is not ready to handle highly flammable substances such as propane as a 
refrigerant for split stationary AC/HPs, which could lead to serious accidents. Therefore, from a safety 
point of view, using propane as a refrigerant for split stationary AC/HPs is very risky and not realistic. 
･ In France and Italy, there are still some cases where even equipment using A2L  refrigerant is not 
allowed to be used due to national building safety regulations and codes. In these cases, the use of 
equipment with A3 refrigerant has not even been discussed. 
 
Energy efficiency drops in split stationary AC/HP using propane or alternative 
refrigerants with GWP of around 150, going against decarbonization 
･ According to the results of a study of TEWI and LCCP with a drop-in refrigerant with GWP of around 
150, these indicators were worse than expected. Since about 80% of the total CO 2 emissions in the 
product lifecycle are energy-induced and the remaining 20% by refrigerants, deterioration in energy 
efficiency results in an increase in total emissions. 
･ As for propane, in case of split stationary AC/HPs with small capacity (e.g., less than 7kW), the 
lineup using propane is limited to equipment with small charge amount, as shown in Fig. 2 and 3, 
due to the use limit by IEC60335-2-40 described above. In order to increase the energy efficiency of 
a product, it is necessary to increase the size of the heat exchanger, etc. Equipment with high 
energy efficiency tends to have a large product size and a large refrigerant charge amount. In other 
words, if we try to achieve high energy efficiency in equipment using propane, we will not be able to 
meet the use limit according to IEC60335-2-40 Ed.7, or there will be no margin against the limit, 
making it difficult to respond to the future tightening of energy efficiency to reduce CO2 emissions. 
･ Introducing regulations that focus solely on the GWP of refrigerants is a move that goes against 
existing energy efficiency policies, Green Deal and Energy Efficiency First Principle. European 
citizens will not be able to enjoy the benefits of further energy efficiency, which will be a burden for 
society as a whole. It will also limit the potential of heat pumps, which is  key to decarbonization. It is 
important to make a refrigerant choice that takes into account not only GWP of the refrigerant, but 
also the energy efficiency of the equipment and its total emissions over its lifecycle.

Less economic feasibility leading to an increased burden on society 
･ When using the alternative refrigerants mentioned above, it is necessary to increase the size of the 
compressor and increase the pipe diameter due to the physical properties of the refrigerants. This 
may increase the product size by several tens of percent compared to current equipment, and 
increase the cost of parts due to increased material usage. In addition, larger and heavi er products

･ As for propane, for split stationary AC/HPs, it is technically difficult to replace the current refrigerant

Summary 
･ Based on an overall assessment from the perspective of the “S+3E”, the GWP150 limit for split 
stationary AC/HPs of a rated capacity of up to and including12kW is not an appropriate policy because 
it faces challenges related to safety, energy efficiency, equipment and interconnecting-tube size, and 
economic feasibility. The impact of very limited refrigerant choices by other regulation should also be 
taken into account. For the above reasons, the GWP 150 limit for split stationary AC/HPs is unjustified, 
and JRAIA claims that additional GWP restrictions are unnecessary. 
･ In addition, even though IEC 60335 -2-40 Ed7 has bee n approved, more time will be needed to 
harmonize the EN 60335-2-40 standard. 
･ In order to achieve carbon neutrality while ensuring social sustainability and inclusivity, it is important 
to promote decarboni sation and environmental responsiveness that encompasses the entire life 
cycle, and refrigerant restrictions should be considered within the same framework. Policies that 
promote appropriate refrigerant use (preventing refrigerant leakage, promoting steady refrigerant 
recovery and recycling) are desirable to promote phase-down of refrigerants.

JRAIA’s comments on Stakeholder workshop for Evaluation and Impact 
assessment for amending Regulation (EU) No517/2014 
 
 
The Japan Refrigeration and Air Conditioning Industry Association (JRAIA) representing 
manufacturers of refrigeration and air conditioning equipment in Japan would like to thank 
the Commission for inviting us to the stakeholder workshop  took place on 6th May 2021 
and the opportunity to provide our comments. In this paper, we would like to comment on 
the proposed policy options presented in the Briefing Paper and the Stakeholder workshop. 
Thanks to the Briefing Paper, JRAIA has reaffirmed that the F-Gas Regulation plays a key 
role in reducing GHG emissions in the EU, and that the GHG emission reduction potential 
of the refrigeration, air conditioning and heat pump equipment (RACHP) sector is 
particularly high among various industry sectors.  We very much welcome the explicit 
reference in the Briefing Paper (see page 4) to the 1 st Objective of the current F -gas 
Regulation, namely “encourage use of alternative substances or technologies when they 
result in lower GHG emissions without compromising safety, fun ctionality, and energy 
efficiency”. 
Currently, the world is working toward the realization of the Sustainable Development 
Goals, which is set as an international goal, by 2030, and there is a global tendency toward 
companies being increasingly evaluated from the perspective of ESG (Environment, Social 
and Governance). JRAIA highlights that RACHP manufacturing and sales companies play 
a major role and are already doing so in achieving a better environment including the 2030 
and 2050 EU climate goals.  
Given that one of the purposes of the review of the F-Gas Regulation is to contribute to 
pursuing the goals of the EU Climate Law and its goals for 2030 and 2050, JRAIA 
understands that policy options focus mainly on the achievement of the maximum potential 
of GHG emission reduction by the F-Gas Regulation. The revision of the F-Gas Regulation 
this time will mark a watershed for the RACHP sector. Due to the fact that our sector has 
made very significant efforts to implement the F -Gas Regulation since 2014, we think 
changes in the F-Gas Regulation should be agreed on thorough prudent discussion by 
mutual consultations, and we believe that all industry association s need to be treated as 
co-equal stakeholders throughout the policy discussions. We deeply appreciate it if you 
could take comments below into consideration.  
 
 
1. Increasing HFC phase-down ambition beyond Montreal goals will have profound 
implications 
Policy option A1 describes the higher ambition of HFC phase-down steps. It suggests that 
HFC reduction targets  of 2030 onwards would extend beyond what is stipulated in the 
Kigali Amendment of the Montreal Protocol. JRAIA is concerned that the RACHP industry 
may be subjected to unbridled ambition because no numerical targets and no compelling

basis for such higher ambition are described.  Nowadays it is clear that promising 
technologies to use renewable energy such as f-gas based heat pump and f-gas based 
binary cycle power generation will show a great potential for CO 2 emission reductions. It 
is, therefore, vital to bear it in mind that such higher ambition may contradict with the 
objective of the Briefing Paper that pursues the optimum balance between GHG emission 
reduction and cost effectiveness  further down the line.  Moreover, the Montreal goal of 
85% by 2036 will bring us very close to ‘F-Gas neutrality’. The remaining applications will 
be, most likely, essential uses of F -gases. Eliminating essential uses would have 
unforeseen, unpredictable, and unwanted consequences. 
On the other hand, the EU has already ratified the Kigali Amendment which stipulates 
different level of quantitative limita tion of the production and consumption of HFCs form 
the F -Gas Regulation.  As a result, objective B “Seeking alignment with the Montreal 
Protocol” proposes diverse policy changes to fill the gaps between the F-Gas Regulation 
and the Montreal Protocol. From what we can gather from the survey described in the 
Briefing Paper, such policy changes, however, would definitely cast a significant burden 
on the RACHP sector.  Therefore, JRAIA is led to believe that simultaneous 
implementation of policy option B (align with Montreal) and policy option A1 (go beyond 
Montreal) is sure to cause further market turmoil. JRAIA would like to request the 
Commission to prioritize a smooth transition to the Montreal Protocol goals for 2034 and 
2036. In addition, along with the transition, improvement of the recovery and reclamation 
of f-gas refrigerants should be also worked on . As the survey shows, the addition and 
tightening of the regulation will increase management costs in various industries. 
Therefore, if the revised regulation differs from both the current F-Gas Regulation and the 
Montreal Protocol, it may lead to unexpected cost increases . As a result, it may 
undermine the price competitiveness of RACHP products, resulting in delaying the speed 
of replacement with energy-saving products that contribute to GHG emission reduction. 
 
2. New POM prohibition for stationary ACs & HPs of up to 12kW rated capacity 
Policy option A2 states that the GWP value of the refrigerant for stationary air conditioners 
(ACs) and Heat Pumps (HPs) with a rated capacity of 12  kW or less are limited to less 
than 150. The Briefing Paper suggests that it would be necessary to reduce the amount of 
CO2-equivalent refrigerant (GWP x refrigerant amount) more severely in the case of high 
consumption scenario in the HFC phase -down after 2030, which is not stipulated by the 
current F-Gas Regulation, due to the transition to the Montreal Protocol. It can be easily 
inferred that this policy regarding the GWP limit was proposed with this in mind. Although 
some stakeholders claim that reducing the GWP value of the refrigerant s contributes to 
the reduction of CO 2 emissions to some extent , we emphasize that refrigerant policies 
should be contemplated in consideration of safety, economy, and energy -efficiency in 
addition to GWP. Therefore, more carefully-developed policies can be more effective and 
contribute to more reduction of CO 2 emissions. On the other hand, the Briefing Paper 
does not define heat pump products. Typical HPs are classified into two types:  Air-to-Air 
(ATA) type and Air-to-Water (ATW) type. JRAIA thinks that policy option A2 limits at least 
such both types from the description that the market for heat pumps is expected to grow  
  
 
 
12 
 
rapidly, and they are positioned as a fossil fuel boiler altern ative(1). In this case, the 
regulations on ACs and HPs should be clearly distinguished and separated. The reason 
for this is ATW-HPs have a wide range of applications, from residential space heating and 
hot water supply to industrial process heating, with water temperature ranges depending 
on applications(2). Therefore, JRAIA is of the opinion that it is not appropriate to classify 
them only by single rated capacity, and that it is reasonable to classify and regulate HPs 
by application, required water temperature, construction, or their combination.  
JRAIA is a bit confused regarding limiting the GWPs of the refrigerants to less than 150, 
considering that the policy options described in this Briefing Paper are screened results 
based on the five criteria including technical availability, impact on other policies, cost 
effectiveness, and stakeholder support. Of these criteria, it is not easy to estimate and 
evaluate the cost effectiveness of the transition to lower GWP refrigerants. In the Briefing 
Paper, it is described that the policy evaluation from this point of view is insufficient .  
Therefore, JRAIA thinks that the GWP value of 150 was determined based only on the 
technical availability with reference to the products on the market. However, according to 
a study on TEWI and LCCP, which are typical indicators of CO 2 emissions, when a 
refrigerant charged in a AC unit is drop-in replaced with lower GWP refrigerant of around  
150, those indicators of the unit increase more than expected(3). Therefore, target GWP of 
150 is not reasonable value from the viewpoint of CO 2 emission reduction. These results 
are due to the fact that in ACs, about 80% of these indicators come from the amount of 
electricity consumed by the unit over its lifetime which depends on energy efficiency  
(indirect emission)  and the remaining about 20% are due to refrigerant leakage  (direct 
emission). This indicates energy efficiency deterioration ends u p with increases in CO 2 
emissions. In other words, it is necessary to consider not only GWP , but also energy 
efficiency over the life cycle of the product. This may lead to the fact that  ignoring energy 
efficiency would go against one of the key goals of t he European Green Deal and the EU 
Climate Law . In addition, it is necessary to ensure safety for the use of flammable 
refrigerants. HC refrigerants, which are highly flammable, are among refrigerants with 
GWP value less than 150. In general, some of the ri sks involved in installation, 
maintenance and removal of equipment cannot be foreseen. Others cannot be managed  
by equipment manufacturers. It is, therefore, pivot to establish a system for the 
certification of qualifications through training, education on highly flammable refrigerants, 
as already proposed in policy option C1.  
We strongly urge for the elements of safety, functionality and energy efficiency NOT to be 
abandoned but rather to receive increased attention. JRAIA strongly urges for research of 
this kind to be examined with utmost care in determining the GWP limit.  
 
3. New POM prohibition for stationary ACs & HPs of more than 12kW rated capacity 
Policy option A2 also proposes that the GWP value of the refrigerant used is limited to 
less than 750 for stationary ACs with a rated capacity of more than 12 kW.  Similar 
proposals have been made in Japan and the United States  (State of California), but the 
situation surrounding lower flammability (A2L) refrigerants are not that easy. While there 
are building codes that limit the use of A2L refrigerants in the United States, some 
 
 
13 
 
equipment installers and building managers are expressing concerns over the use of A2L 
refrigerants in Japan . These issues still remain one of the major obstacle s for the 
implementation of the regulation s. The Briefing Paper recognizes the use of flammable 
refrigerants might not be feasible from the safety point of view in equipment with a large 
amount of refrigerant. JRAIA stresses that these circumstances should be factored into 
the revision of the F-Gas Regulation. Overcoming these hurdles will require even more  
efforts and time-consuming procedures when using highly flammable refrigerants such as 
Hydrocarbons. We therefore strongly believe that increased consideration on safe use of 
products and measures is required throughout the product life cycle. 
 
4. Maximum substitution scenario cannot be taken seriously - without being able to 
validate underpinning data 
In order to motivate smooth transition to low-GWP refrigerants, we would like to request to 
make public and share the evidence data for the maximum reduction potential shown in 
Table 10 (page 31), and the quantitative data of GHG reduction contributed by the F-Gas 
Regulation within the GHG emission reduction plan as spelled out in the EU Climate Law 
and in the European Green Deal. The projections and estimates provided in Table 10  
appear to be a bit far -fetched. These projections can’t be taken seriously unless the 
validity of the data that underpin them has been fully established.

About JRAIA 
The Japan Refrigeration and Air Conditioning Industry Association (JRAIA) was originally established in 
February 1949 as the Japan Refrigerating Machine Manufacturers Association which was thereafter 
reorganized in February 1969 to become an incorporated association and renamed as it is at present.  
JRAIA is the industry association representing over 160 manufacturers of refrigeration and air conditioning 
equipment in Japan. We, the members of JRAIA, have so far been dedicated to offering quality products 
to the markets of EU. JRAIA aims to promote and improve production, distribu tion and consumption of 
refrigeration and air conditioning equipment and their applied products, as well as auxiliary devices and 
components, automatic controls and accessories and thereby contribute to the steady development of 
Japanese industry and the improvement in people's standard of living.

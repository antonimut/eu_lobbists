Siemens Energy supports a regulatory framework that enables 
an F-gas free power grid  
Science has shown us that Europe must become climate neutral by 2050. Cli-
mate neutrality has become law in Europe in 2019. This requires massive in-
vestments in energy generation and infrastructure. The electricity system will 
gradually become the backbone of Europe's energy supply for our homes, 
workplaces, and industry.  
The war in Ukraine and the energy price crisis have reminded everyone that 
this transition requires policy makers to be mindful of costs and resilience: 
energy must remain affordable and strategic independence is key in order to 
remain politically autonomous even in challenging times. 
With the review of the F-Gas Regulation, the European Commission has pre-
sented innovative legislation that combines ambition, flexibility, and foresight 
to modernize European electricity grids . It plays into the hands of Europe's 
industrial strengths and succeeds in providing a holistic response to the indi-
vidual energy and climate policy challenges. We therefore explicitly welcome 
the European Commission's proposal.  
 
GWP below 10 and extended containment measures for F -gas 
free power grids  
F-gases are no longer needed in grid technology. The European Commission's 
proposal to set the technology benchmark for grid technology at a global 
warming potential (GWP) < 10 paves the way for years of research and devel-
opment investment in Europe to turn into jobs and industrial growth and to 
set new global standards. The benchmark will unite all manufactures around 
zero impact on the climate and the environment. 
Weakening the threshold risks leading to the opposite: other manufacturers 
will be pulled into using harmful F-gases and PFAS. As a result, Europe’s elec-
tricity grid will risk relying entirely on two non-European manufacturers which 
are holding exclusive patents for the se F-gases and some of the necessary 
hardware. GWP < 10 means more choice and full consistency with the transi-
tion towards a sustainable economy. 
Therefore, we call on the co-legislators to ensure this is reflected in the legis-
lation with a technology benchmark for grid technology at a GWP < 10. 
We also strongly recommend that the co -legislators complement this tech-
nology benchmark by extending the existing containment measures to all F -
gases used in grid technology  (add Iso-C3F7CN to Annex I ). This will further

Zero Harm with F-gas free technologies 
F-gas free technologies have zero impact by using clean air as an insulating 
gas. They have a GWP of zero and have no negative impact on human health, 
worker safety or the environment. These technologies are fully aligned with 
Europe’s climate and environmental ambitions and are in fact the only set of 
technologies to achieve net zero emissions  in power grids. Furthermore, the 
CO2 footprint of the switchgear is also reduced to its minimum (based on a 
life-cycle assessment).   
The picture looks different for F-gases such as fluoronitrile (Iso-C3F7CN) and 
SF6. SF6 has an impact on the climate that is 25,000 times worse than CO2. For 
fluoronitrile, the impact is still almost 3,000 times worse than CO2. Used in a 
gas mixture, fluoronitrile still has a GWP of around 500.  
Leakage and handling emissions in switchgear are despite the greatest care 
unavoidable. However, whilst emissions from clean air technology is harmless 
to the environment (GWP = 0), emissions from SF6 and F-gas based technolo-
gies is  of great concern . At empirical leakage rates of ~0.5 0%, roughly 50 
tonnes of F -gases would be released into the atmosphere every year.  
For fluoronitrile that corresponds to twice the yearly emissions of London and 
for SF6 to the yearly emissions of the entire United Kingdom.  
The F-gas fluoronitrile also belongs to the group of per- and polyfluoroalkyl 
substances, so-called PFAS. When released into the atmosphere, these toxic 
chemicals accumulate in humans and the environment. Alarming PFAS con-
taminations have recently been discovered acr oss Europe in Belgium1, Italy2 
and France3. Unfortunately, these are not rare occurrences as PFAS are inher-
ently difficult to handle. A group of five Member States (Norway, Denmark, 
Germany, Sweden and The Netherlands) have therefore initiated the REACH  
restriction process for these  forever chemicals. To protect the environment 
without hampering innovation, it is important that Europe avoids the use of 
PFAS where better alternatives exist . Without any doubts this is the case in 
grid technologies.  
Therefore, co-legislators need to ensure that the revision of the F -Gas Regu-
lation does not allow for fluoronitrile – or other F-gases – in switchgear.

Can we afford not to use F-gas free technologies? 
Affordability is about more than just upfront investment costs – operational 
costs and “ gas costs” need to be included in the equation. Impacts on the 
climate, environment, human safety, and our strategic autonomy have a price 
tag, too.  
No operational costs: F-gas free technologies do not require special gas han-
dling tools or training and have no costs related to maintenance and decom-
missioning. Operators also face no costs related to reporting requirements or 
containment measures.  
Lowest “gas costs”: Clean air can be produced by many suppliers and is six 
times cheaper than SF6 and 70 times cheaper than fluoronitrile. Clean air is a 
commodity that can be produced by many suppliers and the growing demand 
for these gases to replace SF6 will keep prices low. In addition, fluoronitrile is 
produced by one single company, exposing the entire electricity sector to 
their price policy. On Europe’s quest for more strategic autonomy, the conti-
nent should not create new dependencies.  
Limited impact on capital expenditure: The F-Gas Regulation impacts the to-
tal EU investment envelope for the high-voltage sector which is roughly € 500 
million / year. Switching to F -gas free power grids leads to additional capital 
investments of 100 – 150 million €/ year across the entire EU compared to 
today’s SF6 equipment.  
Over the assets’ life cycle, these costs are entirely levelled out. The net costs 
to society are even negative when considering the avoided costs to the cli-
mate and the environment and the value of choice and avoided dependen-
cies.  
No cost increase for households:  As an example, grid tariffs in Germany rep-
resent one quarter or 7.6ct/kWh of the total electricity costs for households. 
Out of these, investment measures vary considerably, but are usually below 
25%. Only 4% concern switchgear and are impacted by the F-Gas Regulation. 
So, if the investment measures reflected only the capital costs, the net impact 
would be around 0.076 cent/kWh. But as they cover both capital investments 
and operational costs, the cost effect to consumers is entirely neutral. Hence 
the F-Gas Regulation will not increase electricity costs for consumers.

Reliable operations , m ore choice and avoiding the pitfalls of 
new dependencies 
Reliability of F-gas free switchgear is of the highest priority to Siemens Energy 
and our market companions. All F -gas free products have the same perfor-
mance as SF6 products.  
Switchgear technology has greatly advanced since the last iteration of the F -
Gas Regulation. Siemens Energy and many other suppliers have developed 
switchgear that reliably works without F -gases.4 The technology is based on 
vacuum and natural gases (like N2, O2 or CO2) and has been in successful and 
reliable operation for over 10 years. Now a complete portfolio of F -gas free 
switchgear is becoming available on the market. All with a GWP of < 1. There-
fore, the sector is convinced that it is possible to provide F-gas free switchgear 
with the same high reliability standards as SF6 products.  
The timeline proposed in the F -Gas Regulation gives sufficient time for tech-
nology gaps to close and operators to prepare. All manufacturers have six 
years to make technologies up to 145 kV F-gas free and even nine years for 
>145 kV. Moving from F-gas solutions to F-gas free solutions is feasible within 
a couple of years.

In addition, the Commission proposes a cascading approach – F-gases must 
have a GWP < 10 and only if that is infeasible, gases with a GWP up to 2000 
are allowed. This allows ope rators to use switchgear with F -gases whenever 
F-gas free alternatives are not available.  
At first sight, the limitation to F-gases with a GWP < 10 seems to limit choice, 
but in fact, a GWP < 10 benchmark is paramount to create choice and avoid 
new dependencies as it encourages and allows operators to use natural gases. 
F-gas free switchgear technology is not dependent on one single US corpora-
tion that holds a blocking patent  for fluoronitrile and thus controls the mar-
ket, restricting competition. Also, some of the relevant hardware components 
for the use of fluoronitrile are patented by another non-European supplier.  
None of the technologies with GWP < 10 are protected by patents . They can 
be used by all manufacturers. The gases used in these technologies are com-
modities that a re widely available . Users and manufactures using natural 
gases are not exposed to one single gas supplier.  
The war in Ukraine has taught us the costs of strong dependencies. As a direct 
consequence, Europe will accelerate turning its power infrastructure into the 
backbone of its primary energy supply. We must not fall into the trap of re-
placing one dependency with another.

Feedback on the Revision of the Construction Products 
Regulation (CPR) 
29 June 2022 
 
About us 
The Wienerberger group operates 215 production sites in 28 countries with 17,624  
employees and is the world’s largest producer of clay bricks and blocks and the market 
leader in clay roof tiles in Europe as well as concrete pavers in Central -Eastern Europe 
and pipe systems in Europe. Wienerberger is fully committed to the goals of the EU Green 
Deal and will in particular make its contribution to achieving climate neutrality by 2050. 
We have many ideas and projects for achieving climate neutrality and invest well over  
€ 60 million annually in innovative processes and products as well as  in research and 
development in the ESG area. 
Position on the revised Construction Products Regulation (CPR) 
Wienerberger wants to continue to contribute to a well -functioning single market for 
construction products as well as drive the green and digital transition. A functioning and 
stable legal framework  for construction products  is of utmost importance for our 
company in order to remain competitive and to foster innovative strength. Therefore, we 
would like to give our input on the revision of the CPR, the design of which is vital for an 
innovative, climate-resilient and competitive building sector in the whole of the EU. 
First of all, Wienerberger welcomes that the new proposal supports the principles of 
circularity and environmental sustainability, thereby contributing to the EU climate goals 
as well as strengthening the EU Single Market for construction manuf acturers. 
Furthermore, it is very positive that  the CE marking is maintained and that  information 
sharing between economic operators is facilitated  by improved efficiency  through 
digitalisation (e.g. combining  and digit ising the Declaration of Performance and the  
Declaration of Conformity ). We also welcome that ecodesign requirements for 
construction products will be laid down under the revised CPR, according to the proposal 
for the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation  (ESPR). This will help to avoid 
duplication of efforts and unnecessary regulatory burden for manufacturers . 
Nevertheless, the  new CPR  proposal contains  in our opinion  some points which need 
further clarification, elaboration, and/or revision.

Feedback on the new provisions 
⎯ Art. 3 (Definitions) 
“(1) ‘construction product’ means any formed or formless physical item, including its 
packaging […]” 
 We fully commit to comply with specific sustainability requirements for our construction 
products and their packaging. However, we do not consider that packaging is an essential 
part of the construction product as it is not incorporated in a permanent mann er in 
construction works. Wienerberger therefore asks to remove packaging from the definition 
of “construction product”. 
 
“reused and remanufactured products” 
 We welcome the intention to define reused and remanufactured products. For clarity, in 
article 3.24 we would propose to include a reference to Article 3.13 of Directive 2008/98/EC 
(definition of reuse). In addition, further clarification with regards to the notions of cleaning 
and common civil engineering knowledge would be welcome. We ask to include Article 3.13 
of Directive 2008/98/EC in the definition of reused products. 
 
“(45) ‘permalink’ means an internet link to a website which is stable both for its content 
and the address (‘URL’)” 
 According to the Commission Delegated Regulation No 157/2014 of 30 October 2013 on 
the conditions for making a declaration of performance on construction products available 
on a website, economic operators shall ensure that the declaration of performance may 
be accessed by the recipients of construction product s free of charge for a period of 10 
years after the construction product has been placed on the market. We therefore suggest 
that the permalink availability should be limited to 10 years in the definition in order to be 
consistent with this regulation. 
 
⎯ Art. 4 (Essential characteristics of products) 
Article 4 states that the Commission is empowered to supplement by way of derogation 
to cover regulatory needs of the Member States to this regulation by means of delegated 
acts by establishing for specific prod uct families and categories, voluntary or mandatory 
essential characteristics in identified cases as well as threshold levels and classes of 
performance in relation to the essential characteristics to be declared by manufacturers. 
The Commission is empowered to amend Annex I Part A by means of delegated acts in 
order to adapt it to technical progress and to cover new risks and environmental aspects. 
 We are deeply concerned about the delegated acts procedure. We highlight that a prop er 
participation of stakeholders is of critical importance to gather technical knowledge and 
expertise on those issues and to avoid creating legal uncertainty. Threshold classes should 
be adopted via harmonised technical specifications through the standardisation process. 
 We support that the declaration of environmental performance should be based on the 
standard EN 15804.

 It is important to ensure proportionality when creating delegated acts (e.g. coverage of new 
risks) in order to ensure that the industry can adapt appropriately and in a timely manner. 
 The setting of threshold levels has to be considered carefully: thresholds should con sider 
feasibility and utility of thresholds for the respective product  (e.g. minimum recycled 
content). 
 The Commission should only make use of delegated acts in exceptional and carefully 
considered circumstances. A stable legal framework is needed in order to guarantee legal 
certainty as well as planning security for the sector. The adoption of delegated acts should 
not infringe this stable legal framework. 
 If the Commission decides to resort to the adoption of delegated acts in the CPR, delegated 
acts expected to have major implications  should at least undergo an impact assessment 
as is the case in the new E SPR. The latter – which the CPR is aligned with – lays out that 
delegated acts including ecodesign requirements should undergo a dedicated impact 
assessment and stakeholder consultation  in line with the Commission’s Better Regulation 
guidelines (ESPR, recital 18). 
 
⎯ Art. 5 (Product requirements) 
Article 5 paragraph 1 states that construction products shall satisfy applicable product 
requirements set out in Annex I part D and the product requirements laid down in  
Annex I part B and C as specified for the respective product family. 
 Annex I part C sets out the requirements for the safety of professionals throughout the life 
of the product, or reuse or recycle. Wienerberger fears that these requirements will create 
a heavy bureaucratic burden for the manufacturer and shift the considerable degree of 
responsibility for safety throughout the product's lifetime (and for further use and 
recycling) to the manufacturer. 
 Wienerberger points out that the complete generic product information set out in Annex I 
Part D 1.3 cannot be provided by the manufacturer (e.g. “maintenance needs” and “safety 
during use”). These requirements are related to the way the product is installed in the 
building, and therefore not always determinable a priori when placing the product on the 
market. 
 All in all, a realistic consideration is needed to determine which  requirements can 
reasonably fall into a manufacturer’s scope of  responsibility and which requirements 
cannot be expected to be fulfilled by a manufacturer. 
 
Article 5 paragraphs 2 and 3 states that the Commission is allowed to adopt delegated 
acts to specify product requirements and by laying down the corresponding assessment 
methods for specific product families. 
 Wienerberger stresses the valuable contribution of technical expertise from the industry 
and from standardi sation committees to define both  appropriate product requirements 
and assessment methods.

⎯ Art. 6 (Assessment and verifica tion systems and their product specific 
modalities) 
Article 6 paragraph 3 states that the Commission is empowered to introduce additional 
assessment or verification steps in the systems of Annex V, in order to counter non -
compliances of notified bodies or manufacturers or in view of adaptation to technical 
progress. Moreover, there may be different assessment and verification systems to the 
same product family or category when differentiating by essential characteristic or 
product requirement. 
 Wienerberger highlights that adding additional verification or assessment would create an 
additional bureaucratic hurdle in placing products on the market. 
 Assessment and verification systems need to be clear, coherent, and easily implementable. 
 
⎯ Art. 12 (Modified declaration of performance for used, remanufactured and 
surplus products) 
Wienerberger supports circular economy principles for construction products, including 
the reuse of products. Article 12 paragraph 2 states that where there is no declaration of 
performance (DoP) available for a used product issued by the initial manufacturer or 
another economic operator, an economic operator may issue a new declaration of 
performance without undergoing a full procedure where it limits the intended use to 
“decoration”. 
 For us, a  product used for decoration purposes is no longer considered a construction 
product. The concept of “decoration” should be removed as it does not fall within the scope 
of the CPR. 
 The DoP situation for a used product used for a structural function is not addressed  and 
requires clarification. In our view it is essential to develop a stable and mature framework 
for a “second hand” construction product market. 
 Wienerberger underlines the importance of ensuring a level playing field for new and used 
products. 
 
⎯ Art. 13 (Declaration of conformity) 
In principle, Wienerberger agrees with the requirements for the declaration of conformity 
(DoC). However, Wienerberger stresses the risk of increased administrative burden and 
unjustified complexity for manufacturers. 
 
⎯ Art. 21 (Obligations of manufacturers) 
Article 21 paragraph 5 states that the manufacturer shall label a product as “only for 
professional use ,” if it is n ot intended for consumers or other non -professional users. 
Wienerberger believes that this provision can constitute a major issue for manufacturers. 
As a general principle, construction products are used by professionals. However, the use 
of those products  by unprofessional users  (“DIY”) cannot be controlled or avoided and 
depends on the knowledge of the end-user and the complexity of the product.

 Wienerberger asks to define construction products clearly as products for specialists. The 
use of the labelling “only for professional use” should be clarified. 
 
Article 21 paragraph 7 states that the manufacturer shall upload the data of the DoP, of 
the DoC and all technical information in the EU product database or system.  
 Wienerberger expresses some concerns when it comes to the mandatory registration of 
products in a Union database as it could lead to an additional burden for manufacturers. 
Also, the upload of requested technical documentation is especially burdensome  because 
of the expected volume and superflu ity as this information is to a great extent part of the 
knowledge of professional users. The information will also depend on local legislation (e.g. 
health, maximum allowed weight) and is therefore beyond the scope of the CPR. 
 Wienerberger would like to get further clarification on this database and its added value, 
especially on how it will substitute or complement existing private commercial databases. 
 
⎯ Art. 22 (Additional environmental obligations of manufacturers)  
Although sustainability and environmental aspects are most relevant at building level  
rather than a t product level,  Wienerberger welcomes the growing need for 
communicating on the environmenta l performance of construction products.  We are 
committed to deliver information related to the environmental performance of products 
through a life cycle assessment (LCA) approach and the use of voluntary Environmental 
Product Declarations (EPDs) . Since long, the assessment tools at building level exist and 
EN 15804, developed within CEN/TC 350, provide s a harmonised methodology at 
European level to determine the product input for the assessment at building level.  
 Wienerberger requests that the same methodology to assess environmental performance 
is used for all construction products. This methodology should be based on CEN/TC 350 
developments such as EN 15804.  
 Further clarification should be made on the use of the “software made freely available on 
the website of the European Commission”. 
 
⎯ Art. 65 (Use of simplified procedures by micro-enterprises) 
 In principle, simplification of procedures for the industry are always welcome  but 
Wienerberger highlights that a level playing field should be ensured for all enterprises. 
 
⎯ Art. 68 (Complaint Portal) 
The use of this portal should be clearly defined to prevent potential misuse. 
 Wienerberger requires further clarification on the setting up of the complaint portal as any 
natural or legal person will be able to share complaints.  How will  the relevance of 
complaints be determined? Will criteria be developed? If yes, what kind of criteria?

⎯ Art. 72 (Complying products which nevertheless present a risk) 
Article 72 states that certain products that are in compliance with  this Regulation could 
nevertheless present a risk for the fulfilment of the basic requirements for construction 
works, to the health or safety of persons, to the environment or to other aspects of public 
interest protection. 
 Wienerberger requires further clarification on this article as this provision presents a 
contradiction: a “product presenting a risk” is defined as “a product that […] has an 
inherent potential to affect adversely the health and safety of persons, the environment or 
the fulfilment of b asic requirements for construction works when incorporated in those 
works […]” . So how  could a product be compliant with the CPR (and its safety and 
sustainability requirements) and still present such an unacceptable risk? 
 
⎯ Art. 78 (EU construction products database or system) 
Article 78 states that a Union construction products database will be established by the 
European Commission and that economic operators may access all information in that 
database. 
 Wienerberger underlines that dat a privacy is key in the context of the EU construction 
database in order to ensure that no confidential information is made publicly available.  
The new EU construction database should eradicate any concerns about confidentiality 
issues. 
 Wienerberger would like to get further clarification on this database and its added value, 
especially on how it will substitute or complement existing private commercial databases. 
 
⎯ Art. 79 (Product contact points for construction) 
We welcome that product contact points for construction are to be equipped with more 
resources to better facilitate the work of economic operators. 
 More information and advisory support on product related provisions of this Regulation 
and on acts adopted in accordance with it are urgently needed for manufacturers in order 
for them to comply. 
 
⎯ Art. 87 (Delegated acts) 
The power to adopt delegated or implementing acts to supplement the CPR provisions is 
mentioned multiple times in the proposal, as example to establish essential requirements 
(Art. 4), to adopt product requirements for specific product groups (Art. 5), for assessment 
and verification systems and their product specific modalities ( Art. 6), for environmental 
obligations for manufacturers such as the “traffic light labelling” (Art. 22).  
 Wienerberger expresses concern about the Delegated Act procedure which does not allow 
for a proper consultation of stakeholders, such as the industry, on technical issues.   
 Wienerberger is committed to provide technical knowledge and expertise when required, 
especially through the CEN procedure.

 Manufacturers of construction products need a clearly defined process, a system that may 
be operated easily, and a legal framework that is stable and predictable, thereby allowing 
to remain competitive. 
 The purpose of a “traffic light labelling” needs further explication and justification. When a 
product is compliant with the CPR it will fulfil the necessary sustainability and safety 
requirements, which makes the use of a traffic-light labelling questionable. It does neither 
seem fair n or logical to put a compliant product at a disadvantage on the market . 
Moreover, labelling has a different relevance/context in the sector of construction products 
(i.e. construction products are not bought off a shelf like other products).  
 
⎯ Art. 92 (Repeal) 
The coexistence period between the current and the new CPR will last until 2045.  
 Although the market needs time to adapt to new legal requirements, Wienerberger 
expresses some concerns about this long coexistence period. It might create confusion on 
the market with two coexisting ways to issue CE marking and DoPs as well as harmoni sed 
standards and technical specifications. Wienerberger welcomes further clarification. 
 
⎯ Annex I Part A – Basic requirements for construction works and essential 
characteristics 
Wienerberger notices that the list of essential characteristics in annex I part A (2) matches 
the list of indicators from EN 15804 +A2/PEF, but that only the first indicator “climate 
change effect (GWP)” becomes mandatory.  Wienerberger wishes to draw the necessary 
attention to the fact that it is important to come to a correct picture of the complete 
environmental impact of a product (at building level), to take into account all indicators 
and also to make the assessment over the entire life cycle. 
 Wienerberger welcomes further clarification on the above -mentioned aspects, which 
should be based on EN 15804. 
 
⎯ Annex I Part B – Requirements ensuring the appropriate functioning and 
performance of products 
The requirements described in this part B are quite unclear. 
 Wienerberger requires more specific and detailed requirements to demonstrate 
compliance with regards to the functioning and performance of products.  
 Some of the topics listed in Annex I part B are subject to national regulations or building 
codes (e.g. way of installa tion and/or maintenance). It is not possible for a manufacturer 
to maintain a full list of these documents. 
 
⎯ Annex I Part C – Inherent product requirements 
Products shall be designed, manufactured, and packaged in such a way that inherent 
product safety risks are addressed in accordance with the state of the art.

 Certain mentioned risks are hardly determinable by a manufacturer, e.g. risk of 
misunderstanding instructions, risk of unintended inappropriate installation or use. The 
possibilities of su ch risks seem endless  and always depend on the user . Clarification is 
necessary. 
 
⎯ Annex I Part D – Product information requirements 
Wienerberger notices that a lot of requirements mentioned in part D are already covered 
in the DoP. 
 It seems that product information requirements are too extensive and might lead to a 
duplication of information that could be detrimental for the industry. The meaningfulness 
and verifiability of certain requirements need to be reconsidered (e.g. possibilities of 
misuse). Annex I parts B, C and D include a large amount of requirements, the necessity of 
which has to be reassessed in order to avoid overburdening companies. 
 We would like to point out that a lot of this product information is not already available 
when placing the product on the market because this can be related to the “design” of the 
building or the way a product is installed. 
 
⎯ Annex V – Assessment and verification systems (AVS) 
Wienerberger welcomes the introduction of the new 3+ system for the notified body’s 
control of environmental sustainability assessment in the proposal for Annex V. However, 
a number of drastic changes have been in cluded, which could lead to extra costs and 
burden for the manufacturers and consequently are difficult to accept. In the currently 
valid text, system 3 only involves the intervention of a notified laboratory for the 
assessment of performance based on test s, while the new text refers to a notified body 
that must prepare a certificate. We understand the introduction of the requirement of a 
number of ”random checks” per AVS to achieve more uniformity, but it is not clear how 
these “random checks” should be defined.  
 Wienerberger considers that the list of these “random checks” should be part of the 
harmonised technical specifications, and therefore defined in consultation with all relevant 
stakeholders. 
 It has to  be clarified how a zero tolerance for non -conformity will be  realistically 
implemented while ensuring competitiveness. 
 “sampling of the item to be taken as representative of the type ”: what is meant by “type”?  
Is this the product-type as defined in Art. 3(31)?  
 System 2+: what is the definition of “non-compliance” and “grave non-compliance”? 
 Wienerberger welcomes further clarification on the points mentioned above.

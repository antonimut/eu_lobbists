EHI’s preliminary views on the review of the F-gas regulation This paper presents concerns of the European Heating Industry on the Commission proposal for the F-gas regulation, it does not yet represent the full position on the topic.  EHI covers hydronic heaters, the heat pumps we are including in this paper are residential and commercial hydronic heat pumps (further applications such as air-conditioning equipment or chillers are out of the scope of the EHI considerations).  1. General messages ● EHI is committed to reduce CO2 emissions from heating appliances, including from fluorinated gases used in our heat pumps. ● The hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) phase down needs to consider the projected growth in REPowerEU of the heat pump market and the need for servicing and maintenance. ● Potential bans on the use of refrigerants need to reflect that there are differences in markets, categories and applications.  ● Simultaneously with the review of the F-gas regulation, some Member States are working on a proposal to ban PFAS in REACH. The scope of this restriction includes fluorinated gases and seems to mainly target HFOs, one of the low GWP alternatives for HFCs. To create a safe environment for investments and to avoid that no alternatives would be available for some applications in time, there is an urgent need to create legal certainty. We are working on a more detailed position, which we will share with you when available.  In below paragraphs, we would like to raise some concerns which we will further evaluate in view of the more detailed position paper.  2. REPowerEU and the F-gas proposal The REPowerEU communication aims at deploying 10 million hydronic heat pumps in the next 5 years.   To achieve this, the European heating industry is committed to ramp up their production to supply the necessary hydronic heat pumps by investing in new production lines and new factories for heat pumps.   However, the stricter HFC phase down in combination with the bans as proposed by the Commission could risk undermining the REPower EU targets. The Commission proposal will require a redesign of most of the heat pump portfolio of our members, meaning considerable investments in R&D in addition to the investments needed to meet the REPower EU targets within a very short time frame.  Although an increasing range of models is available using natural refrigerants, today, their volumes of sales are less than 10%. As such, the proposed new bans for heat pumps are not feasible within the time frame proposed.   We therefore ask the Commission, the Parliament and the Council to set the right priority and revise the requirements with this in mind.

3. The need for legal certainty concerning refrigerants in view of the ongoing PFAS discussion in REACH The F-gas regulation aims at cutting CO2 emissions from refrigerants, by phasing down HFCs. Typical alternatives for these refrigerants are hydrofluoroölefins (HFOs) and non-fluorinated refrigerants (natural refrigerants).   Today, however, HFOs are under consideration for PFAS restrictions under REACH, the aim of which is to avoid those persistent substances enter the environment. The only other alternative, natural refrigerants, is not a viable option for all applications and technologies, as a minimum, these include split applications, including in heat pump water heaters. We will give further detail about the use of natural refrigerants in a future update of this paper.   Although we fully agree with the objectives of the F-gas regulation and REACH, it is difficult to understand for our industry what to prioritize when it comes to environmental impact, whether it should be CO2 emissions or persistent substances.   To avoid further legal uncertainty and to avoid that no alternative refrigerants would be available for some applications and technologies in time, we urge the Commission, the Council and the Parliament to align the F-gas regulation with a potential restriction of PFAS for heat pumps as quickly as possible.   4. Ongoing developments in response to the current F-gas regulation and the risk of stranded investments  The HFC phase down in the current F-gas regulation has resulted in the development of hydronic heat pumps using HFCs with lower global warming potential (GWP), blends of HFOs and HFCs and natural refrigerants.   Many of the hydronic heat pumps with those alternative refrigerants have been developed recently and were recently launched or are scheduled to be launched on the market in the next year(s).   Manufacturers planned ahead based on current Regulation EU 517/2014, which has introduced a HFC phase down until 2030. The Commission proposal now introduces further reductions from 2024 onwards and does not consider the investments made by industry based on the current phase down steps. For manufacturers not to end up with stranded investments, e.g., due to the HFCs phase down or due to the proposed bans, sufficient time is needed to recuperate the costs from investments in R&D and production of these heat pumps.   5. HFC phase down We are currently evaluating the HFC phase down in Annex VII in terms of the accelerated roll out of heat pumps, considering the fact as explained above that not all newly developed residential and commercial hydronic heat pumps have GWPs < 150 or even < 750.   A study performed by the association EPEE, modelling the future HFC demand for the refrigeration, air conditioning and heat pump (RACHP) sector shows and considering the REPowerEU targets, shows that the HFC phase down could potentially mean a ban of new hydronic heat pump equipment using HFCs from 2027 onwards.   Development plans and related return on investments, run until 2030. An increase in stringency of the HFC phase down, needs to take this into account to avoid any stranded investments.

6. Bans on the placing on the market The Commission proposal introduces bans on the placing on the market of heat pumps in Annex IV (17) and (18). In its impact assessment, the Commission assumes that most hydronic heat pumps will move to R290 in the coming years. However, this does not consider the reality on the market.    Furthermore, the bans differentiate between split and self-contained but does not the location where the product is installed, which heavily impacts the quantity of the refrigerant that can be used. Not all monobloc (self-contained) units are installed outdoors. For R290 monobloc units installed indoors, and depending on the charge of R290 beyond 150g, additional safety measures apply e.g., pursuant to IEC 60335-2-40:2022, requiring additional time for implementation.   The potential use of propane in split hydronic heat pumps under 12 kW is very limited in the current harmonised EN 60335-2-40 (Ed 4.2) standard for the Low Voltage Directive.   We understand that the Commission wants to solve safety issues with the sentence ‘except when required to meet safety standards’, we do however believe that this wording will distort the free movement of goods and will not create a safe environment for investments. Next to standards also national building codes limit the use of flammable refrigerants. It is not clear how this will be enforced by market surveillance authorities.   Finally, the bans will also include spare parts; this means that when a heat pump breaks down the necessary spare parts for their repair will not be available. This cannot be the objective of the regulation, since it will lead to an early end of life, which will go against the principle of resource efficiency.   7. Lack of definitions creates legal uncertainty The Commission proposal does not include definitions to understand for some important requirements in the Regulations, e.g., product categories and exemptions, this might lead to misunderstanding and circumvention.   About EHI, the Association of the European Heating Industry EHI represents 90% of the European market for heat and hot water generation, heating controls and heat emitters, 75% of the hydronic heat pump market, 80% of the biomass central heating market (pellets, wood) and 70% of the solar thermal market. EHI Members produce advanced technologies for heating in buildings, including: heating systems, burners, boilers, heat pumps, components and system integrators, radiators, surface heating & cooling and renewable energy systems.

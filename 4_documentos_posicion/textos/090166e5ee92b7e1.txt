EAPA feedback on the new proposal of the 
European Commission laying down harmonised 
conditions for the marketing of construction 
products, amending Regulation (EU) 2019/1020 
and repealing Regulation (EU) 305/2011 
 
4 July 2022 
 
The European Asphalt Pavement Association (EAPA) is the voice of the asphalt paving industry  in 
Europe. With around 40 Members and Associated Members, EAPA represents most of the asphalt 
pavement industry engaged in asphalt production, asphalt road construction and maintenance all over 
Europe. 
EAPA works to ensure that the use of asphalt, as the optimum choice for the construction and 
maintenance of the vital European road infrastructure, is fully appreciated, promoted, and 
implemented. 
As a European Association, whose members are National Associations, we have not included in this 
document extensive comments on aspects of the new proposal that may be considered acceptable or 
not acceptable in different countries depending on the particularities of their own  markets, 
regulations, etc. 
Nevertheless, we consider that some general aspects need to be revised or better clarified towards 
the final version of the Construction Products Regulation . These are mainly related to the 
implementation, as the proposal leaves room for great uncertainty and interpretations.

1. Unblocking the technical harmonisation system 
Although this is one of the specific objectives of the new proposal, it is not fully clear how the 
proposed revised system, based on Standardisation Requests or the series of empowerments 
via Delegated Acts for the European Commission will actually help to overcome the real barriers, 
which have prevented (for years) CEN TCs from revising outdated product standards. For 
example, CEN TC227 has tried for several years to achieve citation of revised standards, which 
it was assessed not to be  possible, under  the old Mandate M/124  and prior to the 
Standardisation Request  process being fully established . The specific issue related to the 
inclusion of additional performance classes to address changing National requirements and 
technological advances, which could have been simply solved through a Delegated Act, but again 
was determined not to be permissible. This means that the EC already had the tools to solve the 
situation but failed to do so. The proposal seems to suggest that such an approach would now 
be deemed permissible, resulting in frustration and wasted and potentially duplicated effort by 
the TC and Working Groups. For this reason, it is not clear how a further specific empowerment 
of the European Commission  might produce any additional benefits in this regard  (as the 
technical expertise already exists within the Standardisation body structures). 
 
2. Standardisation Request 
The proposed standardisation  system is clearly based on the fra mework given by 
Standardisation Requests. These may be a temporary solution to update the old Mandates and 
allow the revision of outdated harmonised product standards. Nevertheless, it is not clear how 
the new Standardisation Requests, and Harmonized Standards based on them, will be updated 
in the future , particularly as the science of assessment of sustainability criteria continues to 
evolve. CEN TCs are largely “stuck” with long-outdated mandates and standards and limited 
flexibility t o update and enhance them from years of user experience. In this regard, it is 
important to highlight that the Acquis Revision Process, which has been launched as a specific 
solution to approve Standardisation Requests towards citation in the OJEU is progr essing 
extremely slow (only 2 product families at a time) due to the limited capacity of the European 
Commission. Numerous CEN TCs are ready to update the ir standards but remain inactive and 
waiting for a Standardisation Request (bottleneck  in the process), but also in the fear that the 
proposed CPR will subsequently ‘overwrite’ what is achieved by the Acquis Revision (based on 
the current CPR). CEN TCs need a flexible and dynamic system by which not only harmonised 
product standards but also the content of  the corresponding Standardisation Requests, can 
be systematically reviewed, as necessary. 
It is important to highlight that CEN is a transparent institution composed of Experts from 
Administrations, Industry and Academia, designated by all CEN Member Standardization Bodies 
and whose drafts are subjected to CEN Enquiries and Formal Votes across the EU and further, 
before being published.  These experts are commonly the same designated by EU Member 
States to produce the necessary inputs for the Standardization Requests on an understanding of 
Construction Products and their respective and cross-border markets.

3. CEN 
To assist  in unblocking the revision of harmonised product standards towards citation, 
competent Assessors from the EC should engage with the Subject Matter Experts in CEN TCs 
during the development and delivery of the Harmonised standards to better ensure they meet 
the legal requirements established in the CPR, rather than have the drafts rejected after much 
effort, at the end of the process.  
 
4. Materials manufactured on the construction site 
The new proposal has certain peculiarities for “materials manufactured on the construction site” 
(e.g. exemptions from drawing up a declaration of performance according to Article 10) . 
However, it is not clear what the European Commission  exactly means with this term. For 
example, a n asphalt mix manufactured with a temporary or mobile plant, in which milled 
material extracted from the road is  directly mixed on-site with new material, laid down and 
compacted into a new asphalt layer, could be considered within this definition , but exempted 
from Declaration of Performance and Conformity as expected for the same products to deliver 
the same performances but produced in static / established ‘factories’, with potential to distort 
market conditions. 
 
5. Combination with Acquis revision process  
It is not clear how the outcomes of the ongoing Acquis Revision Process will co-exist with, or 
be superseded by, the provisions of the new proposal. TC’s working to achieve Citation via the 
former (Acquis) route will not be keen to initiate subsequent r evision, wasted or duplicated 
effort in order to meet the latter route. 
 
6. Environmental declaration 
According to Article 11, t he declaration of performance shall at least cover the mandatory 
essential characteristics listed in Annex I Part A Point 2. The Commission proposal refers to 
“climate change effects” as the only mandatory essential characteristic listed in Annex I Part A 
Point 2 . In existing Standards such as EN 15804+A2 “Core rules for the product category of 
construction products” the term “Global Warming Potential” is used – clarification of 
consistency of terminology e.g. to match the core environmental impact indicators would be 
welcomed.  
 
7. Assessment and verification systems (AVS) 
ANNEX V sets out requirements for Notified Bodies in terms of “checks of x random points” 
under the various System sub -clauses. The nature of such checks and random points are not 
elaborated, nor are the requirements and nature of a certificate of performance and of 
conformity (for Notified Bodies) . The requirement for a Manufacturer to hold and evidence a 
Certificate from a Notified Body in unclear.

The additional costs of further administrative burden from providing declarations of conformity 
– previously assumed by Harmonized Standards – will be p assed on to Construction Products 
clients, many of whom are Member State Public Authorities. 
 
8. Product Requirements  
Annex I Part C 2.1 (a) and (c) are potentially conflictive – by maximising durability, the future 
availability of recycled content is likely to be reduced, and same availability will be determined 
by market forces rather than technical requirements . Item 2.1 (c) may be better phrased as 
“maximising available recycled content wherever possible ... (etc.)”. Maximising content to 
technical limits can be confused with maximising the use of all available recycled content – for 
example, 100 tonnes of recycled content could be added to either 1000 T or 10,000 T of new 
materials at a rate of, respectively, 10%, or 1%, but in both cases it represents 100% recycling, 
but could fail a specified ‘maximised recycled content’ requirement of 11% addition rate. 
 
9. Delegated Acts (Art.87)  
This article is of major concern as the Commission is empowered to change almost the whole 
CPR based on delegated acts, with strong indications of future intentions of same.    
This permits the Commission the ability to constantly amend the Construction Pr oducts 
Regulation which would result in further legal uncertainties, difficulties and costs of compliance 
for manufacturers to place their products on the market, adding a greater degree of complexity 
to an already complex legalisation.  
In addition, it is unclear under what circumstances the Commission will act on the 
empowerment to supplement this Regulation, by means of delegated acts. As such, it is difficult 
to assess the implications of Articles, Annexes and Clauses, which results from the execution of 
the Delegated Act.

  
 
 
INTA 
 
Comments on EU proposal for regulation on geographical indication protection for  
craft and industrial products 
 
June 2022 
 
The International Trademark Association (INTA) through its Geographical Indications (GIs) 
Committee submits the following comments on the European Commission’s recent proposal for 
regulation on geographical indication protection for craft and industrial products, amending 
Regulations (EU) 2017/1001 and (EU) 2019/1753 of the European Parliament and of the Council 
and Council Decision (EU) 2019/1754 (“Non-Agricultural Proposal”). 
INTA is committed to continuing to provide its insights as an organization that brings together the 
knowledge and expertise of brand owners and professionals, from more than 187 countries of the 
world, including 1351 in the European Union and the UK, dedicated to supporting trademarks and 
related intellectual property (IP) rights, including GIs, to foster consumer trust, economic growth, 
and innovation. INTA’s membership includes several GI associations. 
 
I. General Comments 
 
INTA supports the protection of GIs in accordance with its Board Resolution on ‘Protection of 
Geographical Indications’. INTA’s position is grounded in a desire for good governance and 
regulation, recognizing the principle of “first in time, first in right,” which in turn is based on the 
well-established principles of territoriality, exclusivity, priority, and good faith when resolving 
conflicts between geographical indications and trademarks, including collective and certification 
marks.  
 
We wish to stress, as a preliminary comment, that INTA does not advocate for expansion or 
contraction of GIs. We wish to focus on the quality of the systems that register them, so that they 
provide a clear and effective governance. INTA considers that the harmonization of protection for 
all GIs would be positive insofar as it would serve to increase legal certainty and provided it does 
not involve disruption of existing legal rights, including trademarks. 
 
In this context, INTA recommends that any regulation of geographical indications have effective 
and transparent mechanisms for addressing: 
 
(i) applications for geographical indications, applications for amendments, and 
applications for cancellation; and

(ii) opposition, cancellation, and notification procedures that recognize pre-existing 
trademark rights with which geographical indications might conflict or cause

An excessive scope of protection could unduly prejudice the existing rights of trademark holders 
and limit the freedom to use generic terms. The GI protection regime in the European Union needs 
to be fair and effective. 
 
II. Specific comments 
 
- To ensure a balanced system for the harmonized EU GI protection for non-
agricultural products, there should be clearly defined criteria for protection, 
transparent procedures, adequate boundaries between geographical indications 
and trademarks, a fair balance between public and private interests, and due 
respect for prior rights. Accordingly, any new system should reflect the following: 
 
• Scope of Protection: 
 
 The scope of protection for geographical indications should be tailored to 
prevent a misleading use but look to avoid any increase in potential 
conflicts. Further, the scope of protection for GIs in the EU should also be 
tailored to prevent the impairment of the right to use generic terms. INTA 
is pleased to note that Article 36 of the Non-Agricultural Proposal makes 
clear that generic terms shall not be registered as geographical 
indications. 
 
 In designing the scope of protection, it is important to bear in mind that 
an excessive scope of protection, e.g., going beyond consumer deception 
and including unclear and/or not clearly defined concepts, could unduly 
prejudice existing rights of trademark holders, create obstacles for traders 
in the development and innovation of new brands and limit the freedom 
to use generic terms. On the other hand, greater clarity on the terms of 
protection would increase legal certainty and predictability. Any new 
system should avoid triggering misconceptions about protection to 
individual components or derivatives of geographical indications.  
 
 INTA is concerned that the prohibition on evocation of a geographical 
indication (Article 35(1)(b) of the Non-Agricultural Proposal), which is 
defined in Article 35(2) as arising “where a term, sign, or other labelling 
or packaging device presents a direct and clear link with the product 
covered by the registered geographical indication in the mind of the 
reasonably circumspect consumer, thereby exploiting, weakening, 
diluting or being detrimental to the reputation of the registered name,” 
creates such uncertainty, although INTA welcomes the intention to 
include a definition of “evocation” to provide more clarity to this term. 
However, the suggested article may rather lead to more uncertainty. On 
the one hand, Article 27(2) includes the word “in particular” and is 
therefore no closed definition, which begs the question of its remaining 
scope of protection. Furthermore, the protection covers not just the 
geographical indication itself but also any “term, sign, or other labelling or 
 
 3  
packaging device,” which is very broad and makes it difficult to ascertain 
what is prohibited. The fact that all that is required to be shown is a “link” 
between that element and the geographical indication, without any need 
to demonstrate a likelihood of confusion, increases the difficulties. 
Therefore, INTA suggests that arguments, evidence and other sufficient 
elements that strengthen the conflict or likelihood of confusion, should be 
provided in order to demonstrate the “link” with the product covered by 
the registered geographical indication. 
 
 On the other hand, INTA is pleased to note that Article 35(7) makes clear 
that where a geographical indication comprises a compound term, part of 
which is generic, then the generic element does not enjoy protection. 
 
• Registration 
 
 The registration should require a formal procedure that ensures the 
fulfilment of the requirements for protection and transparency and 
provides clarity about the scope of protection of the terms to be protected 
and allows interested parties to voice their concerns in the context of the 
procedure.  
• Examination: 
 
o Examination should ensure compliance with the requirement of the link between 
the product and the location, with product specifications, and require evidence 
relating to the link to the territory. Particular care should be applied for non-
geographical terms to verify that the term at issue has acquired a geographic 
association. 
 
o Examination should include a review to ensure that protection of the GI would 
not result in a conflict with any prior trademarks and that protection of the GI 
would not impair the free use of any generic terms. 
 
o Compliance with the protection requirements should not only be a matter for 
examination in the country of origin but also subject to full scrutiny by the 
competent EU agency or body, and the facts be made accessible to the public. 
The examination should take into consideration market realities, such as 
indicated geographical area, including the production of a certain good in third 
countries and how that product is generally identified, as well as quality 
and reputation. This should be open to challenges by any interested party. 
 
o Article 13(1) of the Non-Agricultural Proposal describes the National Opposition 
procedure: It “shall ensure publication of the application and provide for a 
period of at least 60 days from the date of publication within which any person 
having a legitimate interest and established or resident on the territory of the 
Member State in charge of the national phase of the registration or the Member 
State in which the product concerned originates . . . may lodge an opposition 
to the application”. We would like to make two points: 
 Considering the complexity and importance of the matter, we 
consider that a minimum 60-day opposition period is too short 
 
 4  
and would suggest that this be extended to 3 months. This is 
the time allowed to oppose EU trademarks before the EUIPO. 
 There may be legal persons with a legitimate interest to oppose, 
for example owners of pre-existing trademark applications, but 
who are not established or resident in the Member State in 
which the product originates or which is in charge of the national 
phase of the registration. This provision unfairly excludes them 
from protecting their rights, which as we note above are 
acknowledged as legitimate and important. We recommend that 
this provision be amended to simply enable those with a 
legitimate interest to oppose, regardless of where they are 
located. Even though other parties may use the Union 
opposition procedure, INTA believes that any issues regarding 
conflict with prior rights should be resolved at the earliest 
possible opportunity.  
 
• Non-EU GIs: 
 
o INTA is pleased to note that protection will be open to GIs of third countries 
without discrimination, and with direct access to the registration system by 
producers’ groups.  
 
• Safeguards & Resolution of Conflicts: 
 
o Appropriate safeguards should be included to avoid likelihood of confusion with 
existing trademarks, to ensure that conflicts between trademarks and GIs will 
be resolved in line with the principles of priority, exclusivity, good faith and 
territoriality, and that GIs should not be registered for generic terms in the 
European Union.  
 
o Article 22(2)(c) of the Non-Agricultural Proposal provides that an opposition may 
be made on the grounds that registration of the proposed geographical 
indication would “jeopardise the existence” of a trademark. Article 39 of the Non-
Agricultural Proposal states that “in the light of a trade mark’s reputation or 
renown, registration of the name proposed as a geographical indication could 
mislead the consumer as to the true identity of the product.” 
 
o INTA respectfully submits that this qualified wording does not go far enough 
and that the existence of a validly registered trademark in the EU or one of its 
Member States should be sufficient grounds for opposition. 
 
Priority: 
 
o  Protection under the system should not prejudice prior rights with an earlier 
priority, and in particular, the right to maintain the registration, use and renew a 
prior trademark should be guaranteed even in case that the GI is 
registered despite an opposition (including the possibility to file new 
trademark applications including the previously protected mark in order 
to allow for modernizations and updates of existing marks). Whilst Article 42 
 
 5  
of the Non-Agricultural Proposal contains safeguard provisions for prior 
trademarks, it is not sufficiently clear in respect.  
 
• Cancellation & Amendment:  
 
o Appropriate cancellation and amendment procedures, open to any party with 
a legitimate interest, including the group or entity on behalf of which the non-
agricultural GI has been registered, should be in place to enable the 
amendment or cancellation of terms that were registered in spite of not 
fulfilling the protection requirements or which are infringing prior rights, impair 
the use of generic terms, as well as those terms for which protection is no 
longer justified due to changed factual circumstances (e.g. absence of 
presence on the market over a prolonged period of time, economic need, legal 
justification). 
 
o INTA notes that Article 29 of the Non-Agricultural Proposal allows cancellation 
when (a) the requirements for the product specification can no longer be 
complied with or (b) no product has been placed on the market for at least seven 
consecutive years under the geographical indication. However, there is no 
ability to cancel on the grounds that a geographical indication infringes a pre-
existing validly-registered trademark or is a generic term. There may be 
circumstances in which a party with a legitimate interest misses the opportunity 
to oppose registration on these grounds. We respectfully submit in the interests 
of fairness that they should be able to protect their rights through cancellation 
and that these should be added as additional grounds. 
 
o INTA also notes that Article 29(1) of the Non-Agricultural Proposal inadvertently 
omits the word “or” between subsections (a) and (b), and requests that it be 
included. 
 
 Domain Name System: 
 
o Articles 31 and 41 of the Non-Agri Proposal would protect geographical 
indications from bad-faith use or registration, misuse, and dilution in domain 
names and would create a domain name information and alert system for GI 
applicants.      INTA agrees that geographical indications deserve protection in 
the domain name system, provided the implementation of articles 31 and 41 
takes into consideration the concerns raised by Brand Owners in previous 
international scenarios and cases. 
 
 Market Control:  
 
o Given the fast pace at which markets transform and evolve, INTA supports the 
inclusion of controls and enforcement of geographical indications rights in the 
marketplace as seen in Article 48 of the Non-Agri Proposal. Trademark owners 
whose product complies with the geographical indication standards have 
endured the challenge of competing with counterfeit or fake products that are 
offered and promoted in marketplaces as legitimate that comply with the

product specification of a geographical indication protected under this 
regulation.

Construction Products Regulation: Cornerstone of the EU’s sustainable buildings policy  EU has prioritised doubling the annual renovation rate as part of the EU Green Deal.  An increase in the renovation rate will increase demand for construction products and may lead to more construction waste. The revision of the Construction Products Regulation (CPR) therefore comes at a crucial time to ensure circularity, safety, and sustainability of construction products.   ROCKWOOL welcomes the Commission’s proposal on the CPR revision, which is positive in many respects. However, implementation time is going to be crucial to keep up the ambition and to ensure that harmonized and verified info on circular, sustainable, and safe construction products will soon be available on the EU market.   An Ambitious CPR is Needed to Accelerate Progress on Decarbonisation and Competitiveness  ROCKWOOL believes that the following elements should be taken into account in the context of CPR revision to ensure alignment with the needs of consumers and businesses as well as the EU Green Deal:  1. The inclusion of sustainability requirements in the CPR is a welcome addition. The sustainability requirements defined under the ESPR framework must be tailored to construction products. It will be essential to define timelines and allocate sufficient European Commission resources to implement the ESPR and CPR. Under CPR, standards took up to twelve years to deliver; we cannot afford a similar delay with regards to sustainability criteria under ESPR and CPR.  The method for assessing and communicating the environmental performance of construction products should be harmonised in the CPR. Different methods – e.g. Environmental Product Declaration (EN 15804) versus Product Environmental Footprint (PEF) – can yield significantly different results in terms of the product footprint, making a proper comparison between products impossible. For construction products, EN 15804 should be the preferred route.  On circularity, the CPR should provide common definitions (e.g. for recyclability, recycled content, durability..) and respect the circularity hierarchy and incorporate extended producer responsibility. Once installed, insulation materials are in use for decades, meaning that durability/life span is the most important characteristic, and should be carefully balanced against requirements for recycled content and recyclability.

2. The stronger market surveillance proposals in the revised CPR are positive; however, implementation is the real bottleneck that needs to be addressed. Many characteristics of construction products, such as thermal performance and durability, cannot be measured by final consumers.  A study commissioned by ROCKWOOL found that non-compliance of energy related construction products such as insulation (meaning products not delivering the declared energy savings performance) has high societal costs, reducing expected energy savings by 33% between 2020-2030, which equals 5.3% of the EU’s energy consumption.  Therefore, for specific essential characteristics, manufacturers should operate under AVCP 1+ (proposed “AVS 1+”_ full notified body control including audit sample testing) to guarantee that manufacturers continuously produce at the level they declare. This could protect consumers and market credibility given the high expectations and ambition of the EU Green Deal for sustainable buildings. The costs for this third-party testing will be borne by manufacturers themselves rather than Member States bearing all the costs for the Market surveillance. The Micro enterprises’ exemptions already in place, will protect smaller companies from undue cost burdens.  3. Data interoperability, quality of EPD: In addition, the proposed Digital Product Passport (DPP) must extend to cover construction products to further boost market surveillance. Third party verification of the data and clearly defined objectives are key for DPPs to succeed. DPP for construction products must ensure interoperability and data exchange building on existing systems in use, such as the digital EPD and the ongoing work on building information models (BIM). The EPD and DPP must work in harmony to enable improved data sharing while allowing trustworthy sustainability claims. Overall, the CPR should support better data interoperability and transparency for consumers.   4. The CPR implementation calls for specific improvements to the standardisation system, which has seen significant delays in the development and revision of standards. Lengthy delays jeopardise the successful implementation of the CPR, as the framework is highly reliant on standards. A further and deeper revision of the Regulation (EU) No 1025/2012 should set up measures to improve the standardisation ecosystem to enable CPR implementation including: o Increasing human and economic resources available to the Commission to monitor the standardisation process and follow up on its implementation.  o Establish a clear framework and timeline to achieve timely action and avoid a painfully long process as in the previous CPR. Moreover efforts should also be taken to improve participation of independent experts in standardisation committees to ensure a fair and balanced process. Other measures could include training Working Group Leaders and Committee Chairs, improving procedures for handling negative votes, and pro-actively tracking development and revision of standards to address possible delays as early as possible.   5. Fire safety of buildings should be focused on in more detail, given the unprecedented pace at which the EU building stock is being changed in line with the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive, Renewable Energy Directive, and Renovation Wave. These changes, while positive for consumers’ comfort and energy efficiency, could affect fire safety.  One important aspect is to include smoke toxicity explicitly among the Basic Work Requirements for fire safety in Annex I. The safety of building occupants and first responders depends strongly on how the building performs during fire. Toxic smoke has been shown to kill more people than fire does, so adding toxic smoke among the Basic Work Requirements is key to ensure proper safety levels.

ECIA’s response to the Construction Products regulation proposal 
 
A green and digital transition requires transparency, supported by a stable regulatory 
framework. The European Commission's proposal for a new Construction Products 
Regulation (CPR) was long-awaited, in particular by the construction industry. As a member 
of the group of European construction product manufacturers, we as the European Cellulose 
Insulation Association, reiterate the importance of a functioning CPR. Only a reliable and 
correctly designed framework will allow us to meet the expectations of the European Green 
Deal.  
 
We are encouraged by the following characteristics and pillars of the Commission's 
proposal:  
• Emphasizing the principle of a single market for construction products 
• Making circularity and environmental sustainability key elements 
• Strengthening the CE marking of construction products 
• Putting digitization on the map 
• Enabling EADs/ETas development procedures 
• Reinforcing the role of market surveillance authorities 
 
However, multiple open questions remain unanswered, which we would like to pin-point at 
this stage: 
 
I What defines construction products? 
We acknowledge the importance of having a technology-open approach to defining products 
as such. For the sake of the CPR, an emphasis should be put on properties and 
performance, whilst not narrowing it down by production methods. Hence, 3D printing 
appears to be the odd one out within the framework. If a suggestion may be allowed, a case-
by-case approach to defining construction products appears to be most suitable to bridge 
possible misunderstandings. 
 
II Digitalisation be boldly pushed forward 
In line with the efforts of Construction Products Europe, we also support and encourage a 
more ambitious approach to digitalisation, enabling construction manufacturers to use a 
harmonised, machine-readable format under Articles 9 and 10 of the Eco-design for 
Sustainable Products Regulation proposal. The Declaration of Performance and Declaration 
of Conformity would then immediately deliver the content of the announced Digital Product 
Passport for construction products.  
 
III Delegated acts may cause legal uncertainties 
In an investment-heavy and long-term planning industry, with machine parks and machinery 
planned and delivered over decades, a well-understood and level playing field are key. 
Delegated acts, in the current form, are defined too loosely and hence may contribute to 
diverse opinions and interpretations, and consequently add to a lack of transparency. 
Delegated acts shall, after all, be reserved as a last resort, not as a free pass to address

IV Allow the European standardization system to work 
CEN/CENELEC and its established system should, in our opinion, continue to play the major 
fiddle in standardization. Only with the inclusion of the respective member states' experts 
and stakeholders, will the system live up to its expectations. In no case should a delegated 
act be used to circumvent European discussions. What shall be done on the European 
stage, however, is the clarification of processes and timelines, as well as the quality 
standards required to a standard. National experts and TCs shall be guided, not scolded, to 
spend hundreds of hours within standardization discussions. The gridlock of blocked 
standards, among them our very own, needs to be tackled at the earliest possible time. 
 
V Obligations vs. obstructions for manufacturers  
The request to issue a Declaration of Conformity in addition to the already existing 
Declaration of Performance is raising questions, in particular when it comes to bureaucratic 
burden and costs. This might be countered by voluntary harmonised standards, if existing.  
An additional obligation to be further discussed is an EU-wide construction data platform, 
whose added value to the already existing logbooks needs to be counter-checked.  
 
VI Focus on environmental obligations and sustainability principles  
For many years already, our industry has been voluntarily delivering Environmental Product 
Declarations (EPDs) based on EN 15804. We therefore welcome the integration of Life 
Cycle Assessment (LCA) of construction products in the proposal. For sake of transparency 
and clarity, we request that EN 15804:2012 +A2:2019 is clearly mentioned in the proposal 
as the official methodology to be used for the assessment of these obligations, thereby 
avoiding any misinterpretation. Furthermore, the Regulation should list those life cycle 
assessment indicators that would be required, when and whether these will become 
mandatory or voluntary over the time.  
 
VII Swift implementation of the legal framework 
Whilst the current draft foresees the repealing of the current, and admittedly mal-functioning 
CPR only by beginning of 2045, this would mean a two-decade long transition period.  
Besides an unlevel playing field defined under which CPR you may fall, this also would 
mean two parallel systems on European and National level. We would suggest that the 
transition is clear, avoiding distortions and ensuring that all necessary tools (e.g., voluntary 
harmonised standards) are made available right from the start.  
 
VIII Harmonised zone  
Whereas the intention described in the proposal goes in favour of strengthening the internal 
market by setting rules beyond which Member States cannot regulate, European 
Assessment Documents are no longer considered as Harmonised Technical Specification. 
These are excluded de facto from the Harmonised Zone, which is seen as being contrary to 
strengthening the single market, enabling in this case Member States to continue imposing 
further regulatory needs for those products covered by EDAs. The proposed exclusion of 
EADs from the Harmonised Zone needs to be reviewed in the framework of the whole EAD 
system and in the context of applicable obligations and exemptions. We recommend for 
EADs to be integrated in the so-called Harmonised zone and to be considered as an equal 
route for placing construction products on the market. This integration will enable 
construction products covered by Harmonised Standards and European Assessment 
Documents be considered on an equal basis and guarantee a level playing field for all 
construction manufacturers, whether their products are covered by Harmonised Standards 
or EADs.

IX Inherent Safety products requirements  
The list of risks is considered too long, and it would be difficult to implement for each product 
group. Allowing the EC to further amend this list would bring further confusion. We request 
full transparency on the application of such inherent safety requirements only when a 
Delegated Act is issued, and for the list of risks to be reduced. In this respect, we also ask 
for a clarification of the role of the CPR in demonstrating compliance with the General 
Products Safety Regulation.  
 
X Product Information Requirements (Annex I part D)  
The information manufacturers would have to deliver would be extensive and may not 
always bring added value but may increase the administrative and financial burden. Again, 
the proposal foresees the opportunity for the EC to amend this list through Delegated Acts 
leading to further uncertainties and confusion. We ask that the list of product Information is 
reconsidered considering the needs of all stakeholders but without overburdening the 
industry.  
 
XI Assessment and Verification Systems (Annex V)  
The obligations described in Annex V, in relation to the Assessment and Verification System, 
are worrying. Indeed, the motivation for the proposed shift of responsibilities from the notified 
bodies to the manufacturers in AVS 3 compared to the current AVCP system 3 is unclear 
and may increase the risk of non-compliant products manufactured outside the EU that may 
be placed on the market. The number of random checks to be performed by notified bodies 
seems unrealistic, especially for SMEs and in general where several AVS apply to a product, 
or where several Notified Bodies are involved. The verification system of allocating points 
ahead of issuing a certificate is bordering on a school system that may not be 
implementable. Lastly, whereas the industry welcomes the focus on environmental 
requirements, the proposed control of the environmental sustainability requirements by 
notified bodies does not offer any tolerance for incorrectness, which seem inappropriate 
given the known uncertainties of the assessment models and should therefore be re-
examined. We recommend that the Assessment and Verification System described in Annex 
V be reconsidered, allowing for a system that is workable for all players.  
 
The European Cellulose Insulation Association and its members welcome the legislative 
proposal, especially the opportunity it offers to strengthen the internal market. However, the 
draft legislation is seen as potentially burdensome and it would not offer the user-friendly 
system required by the construction industry to place their products on the market and, 
thereby, actively contribute to the goals set in the EU Green Deal.

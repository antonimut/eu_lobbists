Philips feedback to European Health Data Space proposal 
 
Philips welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on the proposal. Philips supports the European 
Commission’s intention for a European Health Data Space (EHDS) that strengthens individuals’ control 
over their health data, promotes access to health data for research, better health outcomes, and offers 
measures for thriving data sharing ecosystems and ensures that the EU remains competitive in the 
global context.  
 
The COVID-19 pandemic has accelerated the adoption of digital health technology. Telehealth and 
remote patient monitoring turned from a novelty into a necessity. Faced with an acute scarcity of 
resources, healthcare leaders also saw an urgent need to improve data sharing and care collaboration.  
As we continue to emerge from the pandemic, there is a unique opportunity to embed such digital, 
data-driven practices into everyday healthcare operations, and to rethink how and where care is 
delivered to respond to the global trends of rising chronic diseases , patient expectations for more 
convenient and personalized care experience, staff shortages and search for efficiencies to reduce 
waste and costs.  
 
At Philips, we envision the future of digital health as a connec ted and highly accessible network of 
virtual and in-person care, with real-time and predictive insights supporting care collaboration across 
the patient journey. In tandem, AI -enabled workflow optimization can help improve operational 
efficiency so that healthcare professionals get to focus on what they do best: providing patient care.  
We believe that digital transformation can support building resilient and future -proof healthcare 
systems. Yet challenges remain. 
 
While the overarching objective of the EHDS proposal is to make the use of health data more effective 
and improve the functioning of the internal market for digital health products and services, it should:  
 
- allow citizens and patients to take a more active role in their health and well-being by giving 
them more access and control over their health data and ensuring that data is kept safe and 
secure to build trust; 
- preserve incentives to invest in ways of generating value through data in a balanced and 
proportionate way;  
- create a ha rmonized framework with clear and consistent mechanisms for primary and 
secondary use of health data; 
- ensure consistency with the existing legislation, in particular the General Data Protection 
Regulation, the Medical Device Regulation and the ‘New Legislative Framework’, which already 
include terminology, definitions and requirements covering various aspects of products that 
would fall into the scope of the proposed EHDS, as well as the proposed AI Act and Data Act. 
 
In the ongoing legislative process, the EHDS proposal should therefore be further clarified to provide 
clear definitions , requirements and standards that are aligned with the existing  and future  EU 
legislation, as well as international standards, and where necessary define clear rules for their order of 
precedence/ prevalence.  
 
Scope 
 
- In order to align with the GDPR, we suggest amending Article 1.3 (b) which should read: ‘This 
Regulation applies to: […] (b) controllers and processors established in the Union processing 
personal electronic health data of Union citizens and third-country nationals legally residing in 
the territories of Member States’.

Primary use of electronic health data 
 
- Citizens should be empowered to access and manage their own health data. The 
interoperability of electronic health records and digital health tools should be improved to 
fully deliver on the right to portability of health data for citizens and to provide the essential 
clinical information in a patient-friendly language.  This is also needed to enable data altruism, 
where citizens can share their health data to advance science and car e – at scale across the 
EU. 
 
Electronic health record systems (EHR systems) 
 
Delineation between EHR systems, medical devices and high -risk AI systems  and the applicable 
requirements 
 
- While the Commission’s intention to fill a regulatory gap regarding EHR systems in the health 
domain is understandable, the proposed - very broad - definition of EHR system will potentially 
encompass medical devices which store, intermediate, import, expor t, convert, edit or view 
electronic health records  and thus make the delineation between EHR systems, medical 
devices and high-risk AI systems very challenging. While it will be often the case that a product 
may meet all three definitions, it is crucial th at the interplay and prevalence of the diverse 
requirements is clear and undebatable.  Article 14 of the EHDS, which intends to clarify the 
interplay with medical devices under MDR and high-risk AI systems proposed in the AI Act, is 
insufficiently clear - Recital 29 seems to indicate that if software falls within the definition of a 
medical device and/or high-risk AI under their respective regulations, it should be certified in 
accordance with those legislations.  
 
- Article 14.3 seems to suggest a set up similar to the relation between the Machinery Directive 
and the MDR, but only regarding products where the manufacturer would explicitly claim 
interoperability with EHDS. It is, however, unclear so far what rules will apply to products that 
meet all three definitions (of medical device , AI  system and EHR system ) while the 
manufacturer may not explicitly ‘claim interoperability ’. We have two suggestions in this 
regard:  
 
i) Assuming our interpretation of Article 14.3. is correct, the wording of this provision 
should be clarified to apply to all such devices meeting these overlapping definitions 
(i.e., medical devices that due to their features also qualify as EHR systems) to prevent 
requiring the manufacturer to conduct conformity assessments under all these three 
regulations (MDR, AI Act and EHDS).  
 
ii) Considering the mode of operation of medical devices, we also note that the current 
definition of EHR system is so broadly defined that almost all medical devices would 
always also be EHR systems which we doubt is the intent of this proposal. Therefore, 
our suggestion in this aspect also includes adapting the definition by, at least as a 
starting point, adding the wording ‘primarily intended by its manufacturer’ to prevent 
that a secondary aspect (namely processing of electronic health data) renders a

Interoperability and standards 
 
- Manufacturers of medical devices and providers of high -risk AI systems (as defined in the 
proposed AI Act) ‘claiming interoperability with the EHR systems ’ – as delineated by the 
current text but to be further clarified if the intent would be for all devices that meet these 
definitions, per facts - will have to prove compliance with the essential requirements on 
interoperability set out in the EHDS proposal and the common conformity specifications to be 
adopted by the European Commission via an implementing act. This requirement needs 
clarification as to whether the EHDS proposed (technical) essential requirements for EHR 
systems and such medical devices/AI systems complement or diverge from the requirements 
under the MDR, as well as the impact these requirements will have on compliance with those 
under the MDR. 
 
- The key common specifications  for interoperability and security in respect of the essential 
requirements listed in general terms in Annex II of the EHDS proposal are to be further defined. 
However, the use of common specifications for interop erability and security (and other 
elements specified in Article 23.3) may be problematic  as c ommon specifications may not 
accurately reflect state of the art, especially when considering cybersecurity developments  
and may not align, or could  even contradict international ly recognised interoperability 
standards and formats for creating, storing and exchanging electronic health records. The use 
of common specifications instead of internationally accepted (harmonized / recognized) 
standards may lead to using outdated data formats which are no longer secure, state of the 
art, and therefore unnecessarily restrictive, hampering innovation and introducing 
additional barriers for European companies operating globally, where they would have to 
implement multiple data formats to enter specific markets. 
 
- Essential requirements for  interoperability and security in A nnex II  should provide more 
specific criteria based on  which EHR systems and  interoperable medical device s can be 
certified. To remove any remaining ambiguity and enable faster time to market, the existing 
technical implementation guides should be used  as much as possible . Finally, the essential 
requirements should be specific as to i) the type of the data that is expected from EHR system 
and medical devices, ii) representation of the data and iii) how it is exchanged/or accessed by 
the recipients with(in) the ecosystem. 
 
- Achieving technical and semantic interoperability and seamless exchange of data and 
information is critical to the success of the European Health Data Space and improvements in 
clinical operations, patient outcomes and cost of healthcare. The interoperability of electronic 
health records, in line with the existing European Electronic Health Record Exchange Format 
and internationally rec ognized standards (e.g. HL7 FHIR , DICOM, DICOM Web, and IHE 
profiles), as well as semantic and technical interoperability should be strengthened.

To avoid ambiguity, each data category needs to be further defined for true (structure and 
semantic) interoperability between EHR-to-EHR and/or EHR-to-medical devices. In particular, 
when it comes to the medical imaging data category, the requirements need to be further 
defined as that could be interpreted in different ways by various stakeholders. 
  
- The governance framework should prioritize standardization needs and improve data 
interoperability. It should be a natural extension of existing structures, such as the E -health 
Network and the Multi -Stakeholder Platform (MSP) for ICT standardization, taking into 
account the reality of the existing global standardization arena. In particular, there should be 
a link to all relevant European and international standards development organizations (SDOs) 
of all sorts, including industry consortia, and not only to the legally recognized ESOs (CEN, 
CENELEC, ETSI) or their global equivalents (ISO, IEC, ITU). This must  be organized with due 
stakeholder engagement, in particular with industry. An important goal would be to align on 
shared views on the need for standards as input for SDOs, who could then base their own 
priority setting on better and more homogeneous market demand insights. 
 
- Where common specifications covering interoperability and security requirements of EHR 
systems affect medical devices (under MDR) or high -risk AI systems (under AI Act proposal) 
the drafting and adoption of those common specifications shall be  preceded by a 
consultation with the Medical Devices Coordination Group (MDCG)  or the proposed 
European AI Board. 
  
Secondary use of electronic health data 
 
Definitions 
 
- The definition of ‘ electronic health data’ in the proposal  includes both personal and non -
personal electronic health data. These concepts are indispensable not only to define the scope 
of the legislation but also for its objective to make the re -use of health data more effective . 
Further clarity is needed  especially on th e concept of ‘non -personal electronic health data ’ 
which is open to broad interpretation due to various applications of the GDPR and health data 
processing rules in Member States. Where non-personal electronic health data means health 
and genetic data in e lectronic form that fall outside of the definition  of personal data under 
the GDPR, it would mean that only anonymised data will be available for download to the data 
user under the EHDS . Considering that the effectiveness of anonymisation of e.g. medical 
images is being questioned, medical images may never become available for download, 
blocking the training, testing and evaluating of algorithms, which is part of the intent of the 
EHDS as pointed out in its Recital 41. Similar types of electronic health da ta may experience 
the same limitations. 
In this regard, the EHDS proposal should be supported by long -overdue updated guidelines 
from the European Data Protection Board on the concept of personal data and non-personal 
data, as well as the ones on anonymization techniques  to ensure legal certainty for 
stakeholders as to what the implications of the EHDS vis-à-vis different datasets could be.  
 
- The distinction between ‘data holder’, ‘user’, and ‘data recipient’ should be clarified  as the 
data value c hains are very complex. Furthermore, t he proposal does not differentiate nor

assign the roles of data controller and data processor  (concepts under the GDPR) , and also 
does not clarify the delineation between these and the data holder. For instance, a medi cal

General conditions for secondary use of data 
 
- In the case of ‘electronic health data containing various improvements such as correction, 
annotation, enrichment […]’ (Article 33.1 (o), it would help data and AI scientists to have more 
clarity on the concepts of ‘correction’, ‘annotation’ and ‘enrichment’ to determine what needs 
to be shared.  In addition, such corrected, annotated and enriched datasets received by the 
data holder follow ing a processing based on a data permit should be accompanied by 
information on the origin and the time of these improvements.   
 
- The list of the minimum categories of electronic health data for secondary use  (Article 33) 
should also include laboratory data which is crucial in the diagnosis and treatment of diseases 
as well in providing insights into how to improve outcomes at the population level. 
 
- Secondary use of electronic health data  for development and innovation activities for 
products and services in Article 34 .1 (f) should be broader and include those products and 
services that contribute to health, care and well-being of natural persons, as well as to the 
general interest of the society as intended by the proposal.  
 
- The obligation to share data should not hamper innovation . As currently proposed, it may 
discourage companies to invest in expanding technology if they are at risk of having to share 
what they have built up, not only in terms of data, datasets but also IP, trade secrets and 
valuable know-how as all these may be embedded in the data having to be shared. The EHDS 
proposal explicitly indicates that the obligation for data holder to disclose data also includes 
electronic health data protected by IP and trade secrets but requires healt h data access 
bodies to take all necessary measures to preserve confidentiality. What these measures 
could be is unclear at this stage and requires further clarification  to offer sufficient 
protection. 
 
- As currently proposed, d ata sharing obligation might breach IP of third parties or breach 
agreements that data holders might have with third parties. This should be further addressed 
in the next stages of the legislative process to include appropriate safeguards. 
 
International aspects  
 
- The proposed international data access and transfer requirements are at risk of imposing data 
localization and resulting in non -EU jurisdictions to implement, as a counter reaction, data 
localization as well leading to more data fragmentation.

Governance and coordination 
 
- The EHDS proposal, similarly, to other current legislative initiatives (e.g. , AI Act, Data Act) as 
well as existing legislation (e.g., GDPR, MDR), calls for the creation of subject matter dedicated 
(regulatory) bo dies and authorities. It also refers to the potential need for cooperation 
between those, i.e. , designated authorities under the EHDS and data protection ones 
established under the GDPR to ensure a consistent application. Further, we refer to the system 
of authorities dedicated to the existing MDR and proposed AI Act and Data Act. The notion of 
multiple competent authorities, considering their establishment and operationalization (not 
to mention potentially divergent approaches and agendas), may give rise t o serious 
compliance challenges for companies  but also represent unnecessary high and duplicated 
cost on society and taxpayers. We would therefore suggest combining these bodies with the 
existing ones to avoid unnecessary and costly proliferation of additional entities.  
 
Transition period 
 
- An adequate transition period of at least 48 months is needed  to allow for the necessary 
adjustments to be made by all the participants in scope of this proposal. 
 
We look forward to further engaging with the EU institutions in the legislative process  to create the 
European Health Data Space that makes the most of t he potential of digital health to provide high -
quality healthcare for all in the EU.

 Roche feedback on the European Health Data Space Regulation 
 Roche welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback to the European Commission’s Proposal for a Regulation of the European 
 Parliament and of the Council on the European Health Data Space (EHDS). As the first sectorial area for Common Data Spaces, 
 the success of the EHDS is key to realising the “European strategy for data” (the “Data Strategy”) released in 2020. 
 General considerations: 
 With the EHDS, the Commission aims to make the most of the potential of digital health to provide high-quality healthcare, 
 reduce inequalities and promote access to health data for research and innovation. The objectives set out for the regulatory 
 framework for EHDS aim to ensure access, sharing and optimal use of health data, foster a single market in digital health and 
 enhance development, deployment and application of trustworthy digital health products and services (including artificial 
 intelligence). 
 Roche supports the objectives set out towards establishing a EHDS in support of the Data Strategy and further to the proposed 
 Data Governance Act (DGA). The COVID-19 pandemic has further demonstrated the importance of a common, interoperable 
 EHDS, allowing for cross-border exchange and collaboration of high-quality, fit-for-purpose data in support of more sustainable 
 health systems. 
 Context for Roche Feedback: 
 Roche believes that a harmonised framework for health data at European Union (EU) level will be a key enabler of personalised 
 healthcare and improved patient outcomes. This will create the conditions for learning healthcare systems and will contribute to 
 unlocking much needed research and innovation hampered by the fragmentation of data access across the EU. 
 Roche’s perspective is based on its role within the health sector, as a trusted data partner, to progress the Data Strategy and 
 serve as a health data ecosystem partner within the EHDS. Roche looks forward to opportunities for further engagement and 
 participation as the work to progress the EHDS and its implementation continues. 
 Overall Comments 
 Legal Basis 
 Roche believes it is important to note that the legal basis for the EHDS is Article 114 TFEU which provides the basis for further 
 integration of the internal market to support economic objectives and Article 16 TFEU which protects personal data. However, 
 while  Article 168 TFEU of the treaty (which provides that a high level of human protection should be achieved in all EU 
 policies) is not the legal basis of this draft proposal, it is critical not to lose sight of the EHDS core objective:  improving health 
 outcomes of EU patients and citizens  . This objective, together but equal to furthering the integration of the internal market 
 and protecting data privacy, should be the guiding principle during the legislative dialogue leading to the adoption of the EHDS. 
 Personalised Medicine 
 The  European Strategy for Data  considers personalised medicine as: 
 'Personalised medicine will better respond to the patients’ needs by enabling doctors to take data-enabled decisions. 
 This will make it possible to tailor the right therapeutic strategy to the needs of the right person at the right time, 
 and/or to determine the predisposition to disease and/or to deliver timely and targeted prevention.' 
 How personalised medicine is considered in the strategy is in the context of care enabled by a primary use health data 
 ecosystem however the regulation puts forward personalised medicine only in the context of secondary use. To mitigate 
 confusion and in effort to align with the Data Strategy, we propose giving recognition to personalised medicine's application in 
 the primary use health data ecosystem and in terms of this regulation qualifying secondary use of data for the development of 
 personalised medicine products. 
 Interoperability & Data Quality Standards 
 As an advocate for ensuring interoperability among data sources to improve the delivery of (personalised) healthcare and 
 more sustainable healthcare systems, Roche applauds the strong focus on interoperability and data quality standards as a 
 foundation for the EHDS however believes there is opportunity to strengthen this focus particularly in implementation. In 
 addressing the fragmentation of standards and limited interoperability between healthcare systems, we encourage defining 
 standards in the form of documented, reusable agreements between data partners participating in the EHDS for both quality 
 and interoperability. These documented, reusable agreements will help data partners to publish, access, share and use data, 
 in particular:

 ●  Data quality standards that define and describe the quality of a data set; and 
 ●  Data interoperability standards that help data partners exchange, aggregate and compare data. 
 Roche believes access to high quality data is essential for good quality patient care, and for the broader health system’s 
 sustainability. Roche is committed to building on the  FAIR data principles  (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) and 
 driving a set of data quality standards across the healthcare ecosystem to enable insightful and responsible use of data. 
 Roche has adopted the standard indicators of data quality from the  Data Quality Management Model  and encourages the 
 EHDS to consider the following principles: 
 ●  Accuracy  – data reflects an event as it actually happened 
 ●  Consistency  – data is consistent across datasets, and over time 
 ●  Completeness  – data required for a given purpose is present; for example, a health record should contain all 
 pertinent documents with appropriate documentation 
 ●  Timeliness  - data is updated as an event occurs, such as when a treatment is performed, or results noted 
 ●  Accessibility  – data is available to authorised persons when and where needed 
 ●  Interpretability  – patients/users of data can properly understand the data 
 ●  Transparency  - data retention and expiration policies defined and agreed upon 
 Interoperability will bring further key benefits to the healthcare data ecosystem. Roche is committed to enabling and enhancing 
 interoperability of its internally sourced and/or managed datasets to enable participation in external data ecosystems and 
 encourages the EHDS to consider the following types of interoperability, with associated principles, to focus on: 
 ●  Technical Interoperability  – leveraging existing data exchange solutions (where possible) to enable 
 secure and efficient healthcare data sharing at scale while not creating technical dependencies of the 
 underlying platform; 
 ●  Syntactic Interoperability  – advocating for global convergence around a common set of language and 
 syntactic standards, and conforming to existing standards, and adopting emerging standards; 
 ●  Semantic Interoperability  – structuring data and metadata to agreed common data models and schema, 
 using existing standard classifications and vocabularies, and allowing for exchange of data within the 
 health data ecosystem; and 
 ●  Organisational Interoperability/Governance  – committing to participating within the health data 
 ecosystem, communicating the value of data sharing, safeguarding data authenticity and patient privacy, 
 and adopting robust data security and ethical standards. 
 Roche strongly encourages using existing standards and not creating new EU-specific standards for the purposes of the 
 EHDS. The standards considered should be considered against those in use within jurisdictions in which collaboration is 
 desired. Establishing new EU-specific standards holds potential to create unintended barriers to entering the EU market and 
 challenges the EU’s ability to participate in the global data economy. 
 Data Sharing Commitments 
 Roche believes data is integral to the patient journey and sharing of data enables personalised healthcare. A fully realised 
 personalised healthcare system needs to be supported by robust data ecosystems within and across organisations and 
 jurisdictions/geographies. For example, supporting the interoperability of systems and appropriate data use allows for the 
 sharing of data from diverse sources, which allows appropriate stakeholders to generate meaningful insights, inform 
 decision-making and drive innovation. This represents a promising approach to sustainable healthcare ecosystems that deliver 
 more tailored approaches to treatment and care, and improved patient outcomes. Deriving value from data requires a defined 
 purpose with clear objectives and accountabilities understood by all parties, including data subjects. 
 Roche supports sharing clinical data because it helps physicians, patients and healthcare providers to make informed 
 treatment decisions. Data Sharing can also enable researchers to more easily build on our research and the research of 
 others, in the hope of advancing scientific progress. 
 In line with existing laws and regulations, we have taken steps to advance how we share our clinical research, both within the 
 scientific community and the broader public. Today we seek to share our data in four important ways: 
 ●  We publish information from our protocols and clinical study results on clinical trial registries 
 ●  We disclose clinical study documentation 
 ●  We publish in peer-reviewed journals and share data in scientific meetings 
 ●  We provide qualified researchers access to individual patient data

 Access to Data 
 Access to identifiable EHR data by data curators should be preserved in order to promote the generation of 
 high-quality reliable real-world data for research purposes. 
 Data from Electronic Health Records (EHRs) may contain numerous types of data such as structured data entered into specific 
 fields within the EHR as well as unstructured data such as raw image files, radiology reports, diagnostic testing results and 
 physician notes. The curation, aggregation and anonymization of these data and the extraction of important data elements in 
 order to adequately construct longitudinal data sets, particularly in complex diseases such as cancer, requires access to “raw” 
 identifiable data directly from the EHR. These enriched datasets can lead to measurable benefits for patients. Currently, 
 real-world data (RWD) curators, such as Flatiron, may accomplish this by partnering directly with sites and obtaining a legal 
 basis (e.g. informed consent) for use of patient data for research purposes. Subsequently, data are utilised in a secure 
 processing environment to generate more complete, longitudinal, and anonymized datasets that can be used to generate 
 insights into a specific research question (e.g., understanding the natural history of disease or the real-world efficacy of a 
 medicinal product). Generally speaking, EHR providers may not be inclined to perform these tasks in order to enable the 
 generation of usable and complete datasets at a scale suitable to support regulatory decisions, as the processes to enrich the 
 data may depend on the research question at hand. Furthermore, absent a unique EU-wide patient identifier and consistent 
 and strict control of processes around data quality and provenance, the datasets available from an EHR may be fragmented 
 and unusable without the intervention of a data curator. For these reasons, the implementation of the EHDS should preserve 
 the ability of RWD providers to obtain access to site-level raw, identifiable data in order to perform data curation and generate 
 high-quality datasets for secondary use. Experienced data providers are adept at appropriately gaining access to healthcare 
 data, securely transforming those data into rich anonymized datasets, and making those datasets available for secondary use, 
 as appropriate. Roche encourages the Commission to consider this model and the potential impact to the robustness of the 
 real-world data should site-level access not be maintained. 
 Explanatory Memorandum 
 Policy Instrument & Value Proposition 
 Appreciating reference has been given to past efforts to establish more robust health data ecosystems within Europe through 
 more voluntary methods, providing Member States with further clarity on the rationale to pursue legislation as opposed to 
 directive(s)l, would support more effective implementation of the EHDS. 
 The complexity of implementation will be significant and every effort should be made to support simplification and encourage 
 participation. Towards this objective, consideration should be given to the value of the EHDS to the Member States as well as 
 other mechanisms to incentivise participation and exchange of data across borders. And consideration to the costs of 
 implementation, as these will vary across Member States, to minimise this potential barrier to implementation. 
 Implementation Considerations 
 Further consideration in supporting implementation should include: 
 ●  Assess the potential of establishing an EU Identifier to support patient portability as well as broader interoperability. 
 This should be considered beyond the health sector, to support the implementation of subsequent common data 
 spaces and the interoperability between them/sectors. 
 ●  Given current levels of fragmentation of the health data ecosystems, towards minimising the noise that could easily 
 be generated and become destructive, actions towards more constructive implementation discussions should be 
 taken, these include providing more specific timelines, focus areas/priorities and suggested best practices on how this 
 work can be accelerated at the Member State level using lessons learned more advanced Member States. 
 ●  Lessons learned from the  CMS Interoperability; Patient  Access Final Rule  in the United States (US) could  be useful in 
 working to enable connectivity of Wellness apps with EHRs, as well as how it supports patients in their engagement 
 with their health data including timely and useful access. Additionally the  ONC 21st Century Cures Act: 
 Interoperability, Information Blocking and ONC Health IT Certification Program Final Rule  encourages this 
 connectivity between EHRs and with Wellness Apps mitigating actions seen in the US market to challenge this 
 important exchange of data. 
 ●  Consideration should be given to what are sufficient technical skills across all domains, how this level of skill can 
 efficiently be obtained and the funding required to support this objective. 
 ●  Clarification of the criteria and expectations of data holders is needed to support contributions of data for the 
 purposes of secondary use to fully realise the potential of the EHDS, as this will largely inform what the potential is.

 ●  How Intellectual Property and Trade Secrets will be protected needs to be clarified and transparent. 
 ○  Specifically, we ask for clarification around the 2-month timeline as outlined in the legislation. In a research 
 and development scenario, a data curator or RWD provider may work with a drug sponsor to develop 
 real-world evidence using real-world data obtained from various sources of healthcare data. In these 
 contexts, confidential commercial information will be used to develop evidence to support regulatory 
 decision-making; a process that may take many years. During this time frame, these datasets should be 
 treated as confidential and proprietary and should be protected from data sharing requests until the study 
 results and data from a study become publicly available. Furthermore, from a data curation perspective, 
 proprietary technology and methods may be used to extract data from various sources, anonymize data, and 
 ultimately transform those data into usable information. Health data access bodies should be empowered to 
 work with data curators to ensure that appropriate IP protections are provided and maintained. Roche 
 encourages additional engagement and dialogue with all stakeholders, including data providers and data 
 curators, to establish measures to protect these important innovations that foster research and innovation. 
 Our Flatiron colleagues as well as Roche welcome the opportunity to participate in these discussions. 
 ●  Definition and expectations of privacy methodologies ie. anonymisation, pseudonymisation etc as well as security 
 standards needs to be clearly set out in effort to support building trust between data partners. This works to ensure 
 data subjects rights are protected and their data itself is protected. 
 Overall, towards an effective and efficient implementation Roche encourages following a minimisation principle when 
 considering the development of Implementing Acts. Ideally the development of an Implementing Act should only be pursued if 
 necessary to address a gap required for implementation and work towards simplification and not further complicating the 
 regulatory framework. This principle should also apply in the establishment of the governing and implementation bodies set out 
 in this regulation; health data access bodies, joint controllership groups etc. To support ease of navigation, reduce potential 
 redundancy of efforts and/or fragmentation in implementation efforts. 
 Chapter 1: General Provisions 
 Subject Matter and Scope 
 Roche is encouraged by the acknowledgement of the need for alignment with other Union legal acts, in particular the GDPR, 
 Data Governance Act, Artificial Intelligence Act, Data Act, Cybersecurity Act and Medical Devices Regulation. The holistic 
 regulatory framework this offers will serve as a strong foundation to enable the realisation of the EHDS. However we caution 
 against creating an overly burdensome regulatory environment for potential data partners. This collaboration and alignment 
 ideally should progress in a way that simplifies and encourages participation in the EHDS and future data spaces. 
 To that end, we also encourage alignment and consideration be given to Regulation 536/2014 on clinical trials on medicinal 
 products for human use as there are provisions related to disclosure of clinical trials data  in this regulation that should be 
 clearly articulated in the context of the future EHDS application. 
 Definitions 
 Data Quality – we would propose a more encompassing term that does not limit to secondary use only. As such we would 
 encourage adoption of the proposed definition in TEHDAS  European Health Data Space Data Quality Framework  Deliverable 
 6.1: 
 Data quality is defined as fitness for purpose for users’ needs in relation to health research, policy making and 
 regulation and that data reflect the reality, which they aim to represent. 
 This definition applies to both primary and secondary use and helps to encourage more constructive discussions on data 
 quality as it clearly speaks to how data quality depends on context and the data; this will be important to enabling 
 implementation. In alignment with the recommendation beyond this definition it puts forward the dimensions of data quality to 
 be considered towards reflecting the reality they aim to represent in our overall comments, we included some high-level 
 principles to consider in addition to those put forward in the TEHDAS recommendation. 
 Data Holder - is defined as "any natural or legal person, which is an entity or a body in the health or care sector, or performing 
 research in relation to these sectors". It is unclear whether or not the pharmaceutical and MedTech industry would be 
 concerned by the definition (i) as the health and care sector could be interpreted restrictively as only healthcare providers to 
 the exclusion of health products manufacturers and (ii) where they implement research as an ancillary activity. Paragraph 40 of 
 the preamble apparently considers that data from pharma and MedTech companies should only be accessible to public 
 authorities and regulators for specific purposes. Scope of the definition for data holder should further clarify how this definition 
 applies to the pharmaceutical and MedTech industry.

 Article 42: Fees 
 Overall: We encourage that the setting of pre-defined fee structures for data providers, data curators, and health data access 
 bodies are not a deterrent for continued innovation. 
 The curation of various sources of healthcare data into homogenous, high-quality and anonymized datasets for secondary use 
 can be extremely complex, and requires constant innovation in order to streamline processes for data collection and curation. 
 As an example, an electronic health record may not contain all data necessary to build a research dataset in a structured 
 format that is easily accessible. Data curators must establish ways to extract the necessary information from structure and 
 unstructured data (e.g., physician notes, radiology reports) in order to aggregate the information necessary to construct a full 
 dataset suitable for research. Additionally, a data curator may need to establish ways to link patient data from a variety of 
 different sources (e.g., EHRs, prescription records, radiology reports) in order to establish a truly longitudinal dataset that 
 represents a full patient journey that allows researchers to determine things such as when a patient received a treatment, the 
 side effects of the treatment, and ultimately a patient’s outcome. 
 The proposed legislation, the Commission seeks to set forth mechanisms to establish fees to be charged by health data 
 access bodies and data holders for making data available for secondary use. We encourage consideration be given to the 
 potential that a predefined fee structure may not adequately reflect the resources required in order to deliver a research-ready 
 dataset. Furthermore, datasets for secondary use may be curated and enriched for various purposes (e.g. clinical trial 
 enrichment, supporting a drug development program, or informing patient care), and may require differing amounts of technical 
 expertise to prepare. This makes a predefined fee structure untenable for enterprises that provide these services, and an 
 attempt to establish fee structures that do not adequately reflect the resources expended to prepare these datasets may 
 inadvertently hinder innovation in this space. Therefore, we propose that processes that set fees paid to data curators, data 
 holders, and health data access bodies for enriched and anonymized datasets allow for direct negotiations with these entities 
 and more agile processes to ensure innovation is enabled. 
 (1)  We would encourage a harmonised fee structure  that is proportionate to the data type, quality, size of dataset and intended 
 use. 
 (4)  While the size of the organisation could be considered  appropriate to enable small and medium enterprise participation in 
 the EHDS we hope this would not render premium fees for larger organisations discouraging their participation. Ideally the size 
 of an organisation will not be the driver of the fees set but just one of the considerations in the overall fee structure. 
 (5)  Any framework established should consider processes  to negotiate disagreement and how requests outside of standard 
 request should be assessed and the appropriate fee set.

 Concluding Remarks 
 As stated in our response to this and previous EU consultations we strongly encourage private sector participation in the 
 development and implementation of the EHDS. Both public and private sectors offer significant experience and expertise that 
 can ensure the development of a robust framework that enables data partners to achieve their respective objectives and 
 contribute to establishing Europe as a leader in the emerging data economy. In a world of exponential change and growth, the 
 way forward that enables the full potential of the EHDS and the European Strategy for Data is through public-private 
 partnership. 
 Roche is committed to being a trusted data partner and a good corporate citizen/industry leader in seeking to work across 
 boundaries in a new personalised healthcare ecosystem. In particular, Roche Romania's pledge to share experiences of 
 working with private/public data through the  B2G Expert Group  initiative aims to allow Roche investment in new regulatory 
 models which Roche is then contributing their experiences from learning what works with the wider European market. This is a 
 demonstration of the leadership and contributions that can come out from having private industry participation in the EHDS. 
 Thank you for the opportunity to provide input on the EHDS regulation as part of the journey towards the EHDS. We look 
 forward to  continuing this dialogue with the European Commission,Parliament and Member States, given our unique position 
 as a Pharmaceutical, Diagnostic and Data healthcare company that is working to support health systems in delivering more 
 sustainable healthcare systems for the benefit of society and patients.

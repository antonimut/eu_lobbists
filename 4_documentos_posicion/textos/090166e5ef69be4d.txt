Siemens Healthineers’ feedback to the European Commission’s proposal for a 
Regulation on the European Health Data Space (EHDS) 
 
Siemens Healthineers welcomes the European Commission’s goal of creating a  framework enabling access to 
health data and health data sharing across the European Union. It is a timely and highly anticipated initiative 
which empowers patients, while setting the building blocks for a European single market for (digital) health and 
tackling barriers which impede sharing of (health) data. Particularly for healthcare, data is a source underpinning 
innovation, making healthcare delivery more effective and targeted, including via digitalisation. These processes 
are fundamental for healthcare transformation and for granting access to healthcare for everyone. This may 
happen by: (i) bringing healthcare and expertise to remote and resource -poor locations in industrialised 
countries as well as developing nations, thereby removing or low ering barriers to accessing health services; (ii) 
telemedicine, reducing waiting and travel times; (iii) knowledge transfer between healthcare professionals in 
more convenient ways; and (iv) AI enabling broader access to diagnosis and treatment at a lower cost globally. 
Data-driven decision-making and measures based on current and accurate data are especially needed in times 
of a pandemic, thus improving the quality of health services. Health data and its sharing form an integral part of 
patient-centric and precision medicine1. 
We therefore very much welcome this significant initiative and  we are committed to collaborate with the 
European policymakers in order to  realise the potential of digital transformation of healthcare systems and 
healthcare delivery. 
Scope and definitions 
One of the central definitions of the draft EHDS ‘data holder’2 differs between the EHDS, the Data Act 3 and the 
Data Governance Act 4. The relationship between these definitions  remains unclear and requires clarification. 
The definitions also lack the reference to the controller: for example, a private entity acting on behalf of a 
controller may have the technical means to make data available, thus, by the definition qualifying as data holder. 
In particular, in context of  making the data available for research ( cf. EHDS) or public interest/emergency , a 
private entity would have to provide data which may be in contradiction with the instructions of a controller. 
Another definition that we believe needs clarification is ‘non-personal electronic health data ’. At this stage,  
potentially all data -driven results may fall under the scope, if any derivative data from original personal 
electronic health data  is considered.  Essentially, strictly interpret ing the definition, any  machine-learning of 
algorithms (e.g., AI) is a statistic representation of relevant data points and could be considered ‘non -personal 
electronic health data’. Any kind of statistics (i.e., aggregate of personal health data) would also be subject to

Further on, we would also welcome clarifications on various terminologies used in the draft EHDS, in particular

Interplay with other legislation applicable to Medical Devices ( MDs) & In Vitro Medical Devices 
(IVDs) 
As the medtech  sector is highly regulated, it is essential to have a framework that clearly articulates the 
interaction between different existing and negotiated pieces of legislation . The General Data Protection 
Regulation (“GDPR”), Medical Devices Regulations (“MDR”), Data Governance Act, the proposed AI Act and Data 
Act next to the draft EHDS  – all aim to regulate medical devices and thus need to be properly aligned.  A 
consistent interaction between the requirements, including product -related requirements , should not only 
enable but also encourage access to quality data for secondary use , while facilitating compliance. 
In addition, in the proposed Data Act the so c alled “accessibility of data by design ” requirement, lays the 
foundations for the EHD S5 to facilitate sharing data 6. The proposed EHDS requires manufacturers of the 
electronic health records ( “EHR”) systems to conduct a mandatory certification to ensure adherence to the 
interoperability requirements 7. Such product design requirements create two additional layers of product 
requirements to the already existing one provided through the Medical Devices Regulation (MDR) and In Vitro 
Medical Devices Regulation  (IVDR), as well as the proposed AI Act . The inte rplay between the sectorial 
regulations, AI Act, and Data Act is unclear, as well as the governance and the enforcement of such requirements.  
In addition to the convoluted framework that the mentioned regulations may cumulatively create, we are 
concerned with the over-regulation of the medical technology industry . As such , medical technology 
manufacturers would need to comply not only with the existing sectoral legislation but with several additional 
regulatory requirements arising from new legislation. In this regard, legal clarity is a must to avoid bottlenecks 
and delays in bringing new solutions into the market. 
Lastly, standardisation, common quality standards, formats, will be essential for interoperability and for linking 
EHR systems and other sources of health data. We recommend for specifications to be commonly agr eed and 
aligned with the ongoing activities on developing global standard s and harmonis ing existing standards that

Primary categories of health data 
It is commendable that the proposal includes certain types of data which have to be made available as a priority. 
Because of uniform format and quality, these data may be instrumental to provide means to improve patient 
outcomes. Alongside the data  already considered , we would see beneficial to add genetic data  to the list , 
especially sequenced data . Gene sequencing is a very cost- and time-consuming effort. It would be helpful if 
this data would be among  the priority data categories and could be shared in a structured format to avoid 
resequencing. 
In addition to the main data types, the EHDS should strive to incentivise a systematic registration of other health 
data types, which is currently missing.  
Secondary use of health data 
We welcome the inclusion of  a wide range of data types to be made available for the secondary use of h ealth 
data. This will enable more innovation in the digital health domain and deliver on the promises of the EHDS. Yet, 
we believe some finetuning and assessment of feasibility with respect to certain provisions are justified. For 
instance, the proposal provides for a data user to inform a health data access body of a finding which emerged 
during secondary data use that may impact health of a patient9. Since the data to be made available for 
secondary use is often subject to an anonymisation requirement, this provision would be unrealistic.  
Additionally, even if theoretically during research one could come across findings that are relevant for a natural 
person, there is no way of knowing if a person is already aware of the finding (e.g. , detection of cancer on the 
image). This may go against the requirement for effective anonymisation of the data and dramatically increase 
the risks for a natural person, including those stemming from compromised communication and the personal 
data being disclosed to an unauthorised party. 
On a related aspect, the safeguards for the health data to be made available for the secondary use may 
occasionally go against the benefits of such sharing. There are use cases, for which fully anonymised data would 
render any research on the specific themes irrelevant, for instance, when research is done on the subject of rare 
diseases. This occurs due to the fact that  available data is very limited, but also because specific identifiers are 
crucial to any tangible findings of such research . We therefore suggest reflecting in the legislative proposal an 
option where research could be conducted using personal data, subje ct to appropriate safeguards, with the 
purpose to enable deriving insights into specific healthcare conditions. 
In respect of the fees for making electronic health data available, the proposal does not account for the effort a 
data holder may have invested into the quality of a dataset. For instance, if upon receiving clinical data, it has 
been subject to annotation, e.g., of X -ray images by physicians. Should a request be made for such enriched  
dataset, the costs of efforts on improving the quality would not be possible to recover nor to reflect in the fees.

In this respect, it remains also unclear why micro enterprises would not have to make data available10. The data 
held by the start-ups and small enterprises bears value to health research. Over 90% of European companies are 
SMEs. In the medical technology sector, approximately 95% of innovation happens within the SMEs11. Exempting 
micro enterprises  from obligations to make data available may lead to the omission of significant insights 
indispensable to health research. 
According to the proposed Data Act, the validity of a data permit issued for the secondary use of data shall not 
exceed five years 12. We are concerned that this provision does not leave room for reflecting data users’ 
obligations under other regulations (cf. the proposed AI Act), where it may be required to keep the data and the 
related documentation for a longer period than five years  in order to p rove compliance with product safety 
requirements. We urge the policymakers to include the reference to legal obligations in other Union or national 
legislation which may justify longer validity term of a data permit. In general, the EHDS lacks specificity regarding 
the duration of access to data and the factors influencing it. On a related note, t he provision for mutual 
recognition of data permits13 across Members States should be strengthened. 
IPR issues 
The safeguards14 for the protection of intellectual property and trade secrets are insufficient.  The proposal fails 
to be specific on when certain data types will have to be shared by whom and with whom. A literal reading of 
the text would suggest that B2B data sharing obligations are applicable to all types of data re-used for purposes 
(d) to (h) in  Art. 34(1), not to mention (a) to (c) when it concerns public sector bodies and Union institutions, 
bodies, offices and agencies exercising their tasks. Similar to the discussions on the proposed Data Act, the EHDS 
compels d isclosure of data that is genera ted with significant private resourcing and is not balanced against 
strong safeguards for IP and trade secrets protection. 
GDPR and persisting risk of regulatory and implementation fragmentation 
Fragmentation in data protection regulation still presents a tangible concern. A uniform interpretation and 
application of the GDPR throughout the EU  is essential to ensure a level playing field and equal opportunities  
in the digital health ecosystem. Different national interpretations will continue to exacerbate the problem, if not 
adequately tackled by the EHDS. 
However, the draft EHDS enables Member States to further regulate the access to health data, as well as allows 
to decide whether a single body should be in charge of both primary and secondary data, or whether these tasks 
should be institutionally separated. This potentially aggravates further the existing  fragmentation and 
contributes to duplication of efforts . There remains a need for a uniform interpretation and application of the

In some respects, the proposed EHDS also fails to provide clear guidance on how to fulfil GDPR-driven obligations 
in practice. For instance, provision on joint controllership 15 of data access bodies and data users fails to be 
explicit regarding the division of liability for data misuse, as well as unclear on how a data subject could properly

for data held by another (joint) controller. 
Despite the aim of the EHDS propos al to tackle issues of GDPR, there is still an un addressed need in the  
harmonisation of interpretation of anonymisation and pseudonymisation concepts from the GDPR, as well as 
scientific research, including using health data . We urge for these issues to be resolved as otherwise the EHDS 
would miss a tremendous opportunity in delivering on its promises.  
Governance  
We welcome the creation of a dedicate d forum in the form of an EHDS Board to  coordinate Member States’ 
practices of the digital health authorities, as well as  to distil best practices  and facilitate cooperation for a 
uniform implementation of the Regulation. However, its composition remains restricted  and thus the Board 
limited in its reach to understand the underlying issues. We would rather suggest involving all the stakeholders, 
including industry , in order to leverage expertise and lessons learnt by all stakeholders that are working to 
establish an optimal (health) data and digital ecosystem. Such involvement should be reflected in the structure 
of the Board and institutionalised16.  
We would also urge the health data access bodies  to cooperate with data holders in addition to stakeholders  
(i.e., patient organisations, representatives of data subjects , healthcare professionals, researchers, and ethical 
committees) to ensure equal and equitable representation by all stakeholders 17. 
Legal basis for data access and EHDS 
We believe that it must be better clarified how the EHDS provides  legal basis for accessing health data  and its 
alignment with Art. 9 of the GDPR, which includes considerable restrictions. Further actions on improving clarity 
on this could be the following. 
Firstly, it would be of merit to establish the legal hierarchy for situations where the consent of a natural person 
is required by national law 18 and defining whether the EHDS regulation  would prevail over  national data 
protection legislation. Secondly, the EHDS would also benefit from clarity regarding the interplay between GDPR

basis of a consent can be used for scientific purposes independently of what use has been agreed  to in the

Conclusion 
We believe that this (non -exhaustive) list of the proposed suggestions on improving the proposed EHDS 
regarding the raised concerns, including concerning its interplay with the existing data and sectorial regulations 
on medical devices, could be used to finetune the legislative text so that the framework is able to facilitate the 
achievement of the EHDS’ goals. In the medical technology sector, the biggest challenge remains the regulatory 
fragmentation stemming largely from the GDPR implementation which inhibits seamless data sharing, both 
when needed for patients (especially, in cross-border cases) and for innovation and healthcare transformation 
purposes. We need to make sure that the EHDS may become an instrument tackling these issues, not 
compounding them. It is also interchangeably linked with the need to elaborate and harmonise the concepts of 
pseudonymisation and anonymisation, which are instrumental for the medical innovation.  
A well-designed and efficiently functioning EHDS can become the bedrock of Europe as a medical innovation 
hub globally. We, therefore, are looking forward to engaging with the European institutions and stakeholders in 
the upcoming dialogue on this legislative initiative and stand ready to provide any necessary expertise and jointly 
work on its improvement.

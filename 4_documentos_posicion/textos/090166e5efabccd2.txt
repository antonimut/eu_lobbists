 
Helping  the EU to preserve what matters  
 
Corbion’s input on the R evision of the Waste Framework Directive  
 
We thank the European Commission for the possibility to provide input to the public consultation in 
relation to the revision of the Waste Framework Directive  (WFD). Corbion welcomes the revision of 
the WFD and any effort made to decrease waste by designing  for circularity and using sustainable 
materials. That is because at Corbion, sustainability is at the heart of what we do.  Our purpose is to 
preserve what matters: Preserving food and food production, health, and the planet. We do this by 
using science to propel nature’s ingenuity.  
 
Food waste reduction by natural preservation 
Corbion is pleased to see the attention given to food waste prevention in the public consultation and 
the intention of the Commission to propose legally binding targets to reduce food waste, as we believe 
food preservation is an important instrument in the fight against climate change. F ood contributes 
greatly to climate change – and wasted food accounts for about 8 percent of all GHG  emissions.1 
When looking at meat, 20 % of meat produced is lost or wasted. Reducing global meat waste by 25% 
would prevent 170 million tons of C02 emissions. 2 Preservation can play a major role in preventing 
these kinds of food waste and substantially reduce carbon emissions linked to this waste. 
 
When looking at food preservation, Corbion believes one of the key elements is natural food 
preservation solutions. Natural food preservation does not only help in improving shelf- life through 
pathogen control, inhibiting mould and yeast inhibition, p rotection against clostridium and listeria 
control, decreasing spoilage and keeping the products fresh for longer, but also helps in keeping labels 
simple as they are made from ingredients that are understandable to consumers. Corbion uses plant 
extracts (including celery, acerola and rosemary extract) which can replicate the functionality provided 
by traditional synthetics. They are being used to preserve baked goods, meat alternatives, fresh 
seafood and meat and refrigerated products. By using solutions t hat put nature to work, natural 
ingredients help preserve our food without giving up any part of the quality eating experience. 
 
Corbion calls for more attention to be given to natural food preservation as part of the food prevention 
programmes Member States are obliged to set up in order to reduce food waste at each stage of the 
food supply chain . As indicated in the JRC brief on food waste, t he largest amount of food waste is 
generated during the consumption stage (46%) . By stimulating natural food preservation, national 
efforts against food waste  can be strengthened , waste at consumption stage can be lowered and 
general food waste is prevented.  Investing in natural preservation improves the utilisation of food , 
which prevents having to find alternatives to make good use of the produced food (e.g. reprocessing 
into non-food products, use as animal feed, …).

Food waste reduction by using bioplastics 
Bioplastics also have a great role to play in maximising waste prevention. Bioplastics not only provide 
additional benefits to food waste reduction, but also to waste reduction in general.  
 
• Food waste prevention: B ioplastics packaging with increased barrier properties  make it 
possible to extend the shelf -life of fresh produce , hence preventing food waste. Industrially 
compostable packaging and collection bags also enables the (organic) recycling of food waste 
which would otherwise be practically and economically prohibitive to recover.
 
• General waste reduction: N ew innovative bioplastic materials allow for reduction of overall 
amount of material needed as packaging can become  thinner, lighter, less complex. 
Bioplastics can also be appropriate in reusable models, reducing the generation of plastic 
waste. Moreover, biobased plastics such as poly lactic acid  (PLA) can be perfectly recycled , 
both mechanically and chemically.  
 
Therefore, Corbion believes the role of bioplastics in reducing and preventing (food) waste should be 
better reflected in the upcoming WFD. It recommends the following: 
 
Biowaste and compost quality 
Biowaste accounts in Europe for more than 100 million tons per years. Today, only 34% of biowaste 
and 16% of food waste are being collected. This situation must drastically improve over the next two 
years and in line with the obligation for separate collection of biowaste as set out in Article 22 of the 
WFD. Industrially compostable plastic bags for the separate collection of organic waste should be 
made mandatory to this end in all Member States, in line with the recommendations in the 
forthcoming EU Policy framework on biobased, biodegradable and compostable plastics. 
 
The more biowaste is collected, the higher the risk of contamination due to fossil-based plastics that 
individuals unintentionally dispose of in the biowaste stream . In order to avoid a decrease in quality 
of compost, we suggest setting  minimum requirements for the quality of the compost . This can be 
done by including a proper timeframe and treatment for composting in art. 22 sub 2(a), which would 
allow to stabilize the biowaste and produce mature compost and avoid any negative impacts on soil. 
 
 
Biobased & compostable plastics and composting 
Following the previous point, it is vital that also the input in biowaste stream is free from 
contaminations. According to composters, fossil- based plastic is one of the major contaminants of 
their input and thus has negative impacts on the final compost  (because it does not degrade). In this 
case biobased and compostable plastics offers a solution, because they fit the organic recycling 
 
process of bio-waste (see studies published in Italy3, the Netherlands4, Spain5, Germany6) and help to 
increase the amount of biowaste recycled.  
 
Furthermore, Corbion suggests reviewing the WFD as follows: 
• a)  Article 3.4, definition of biowaste should include compostable plastics certified according 
to EU standards7; 
• b)  Article 22.1, the provision giving the possibility to Member States to include compostable 
plastics in the biowaste should be revised into an obligation, which secures that compostable 
plastics are recycled in the proper way. 
 
Recognition of circularity of biobased plastics 
Since the revision of EU waste framework aims to improve the overall environmental outcome of 
waste management in line with the waste hierarchy, it is important to acknowledge the circularity of 
biobased plastics, which st arts by reflecting in EU policy that biobased plastics such as PLA can be 
perfectly recycled
, both mechanically and chemically.  
 
Sorting technologies have been improving over the years. In the plastic sector , NIR (near infra- red) 
would allow to reduce the high cross contamination of polymers. As producers of a biobased  and 
recyclable polymer (PLA), TotalEnergies Corbion has tested NIR on its PLA and found that can be sorted 
from plastic with a purity of 97%. 
 
Corbion therefore believes that mechanical and chemical recycling should become viable, 
economically feasible and commonly used end-of-life solutions for PLA-based products.  
 
Finally, the development of innovative materials should not be discriminated against other materials 
with well- established recycling value chains, especially if they can provide solutions in terms of 
circularity and carbon neutrality. 
 
And that aspect of carbon neutrality is inherent to PLA, thanks to its specific features of storing carbon 
and extending that storage via different recycling technologies

To further tap into the circular potential of biobased plastic materials, concreate actions are needed: 
• Chemical recycling [and composting] should be  explicitly recognized in the overall definition 
of recycling currently in art 3.17 of the WFD, guaranting a level-playing field. 
• It would be necessary to have a clear and harmonized labelling to help consumers to correctly 
dispose of their products. 
• Last but not least, Corbion suggests that EPR fees paid by the plastic sector should be ring-
fenced to an update of sorting and, more important, recycling facilities.

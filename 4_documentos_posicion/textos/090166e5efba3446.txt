ESWET contribution to the public consultation on the 
Environmental impact of waste management 
 
ESWET – the European Suppliers of Waste to Energy Technology represents 
companies that have built and supplied over 95% of the Waste -to-Energy plants 
in operation in Europe. It seeks to promote the technology, which, within the frame 
of the waste hierarchy, recovers energy from waste that would otherwise end up 
in landfills. 
ESWET welcomes the Co mmission’s public consultation  on the Environmental 
impact of waste management and is fully in support of waste reduction. However, 
while waste prevention is the most desirable goal in the long -term, waste 
generation is still on the rise and needs to be dealt with, including non-recyclable 
waste. Indeed, it is estimated that around 142 million tonnes of residual waste will 
need to be treated in 2035 even if the circular economy targets are met in the EU1. 
Following the waste hierarchy (Article 4, Directive 2008/98), priority needs to be 
given to waste reduction; preparing for re -use and recycling. However, not every 
waste is recyclable – waste can be contaminated or polluted, and most materials 
cannot be recycled indefinitely – and have to be recovered, or at the last resort, 
disposed of in landfills. 
In short 
 
1. The Waste Hierarchy need to be preserved as such 
2. More support is needed in diverting non-recyclable waste from landfills 
3. The recovery of both metals and minerals have to count as recycling 
4. The Polluter pays principle needs to be fully reinforced 
5. The full contribution of WtE in the circular economy should be recognised 
 
 
Environmental benefits of Waste-to-Energy 
 
Waste-to-Energy (WtE) plants use non -recyclable waste as a resource by  
turning it into electricity and heat for the community and industries, hence 
contributing to the circular economy . In 2019 in Europe, Waste-to-Energy plants 
produced around 43 billion kWh of electricity and 9 9 billion kWh of heat, which 
provided 20 million citizens with electricity and 17 million citizens with heat.2

WtE secures local, non-intermittent baseload energy to communities & industries, 
and represent a source of reliable energy in times of major crisis  in Europe. As 
about half of the waste treated is of biogenic origin, WtE is partly renewable3, and 
contribute to the decarbonisation of other sectors while treating residual, or even 
polluted waste not suitable for recycling. 
 
Indeed, WtE plants are a viable waste treatment solution t o prevent 
contamination. As seen during the COVID-19 pandemic, household residual waste 
can represent sanitary risks, with for instance  face masks and gloves. Here, WtE 
play a significant hygienisation role that cannot be overlooked in the current 
sanitary context.  As harmful substances can be collected with valuables, and must 
be segregated to protect human health and the environment, a safe option need 
to implemented to treat those contaminants. 
 
In this instance, WtE play this role hygienic and depollution role, contrary to other 
solutions such as mechanical and/or biological treatment (MBT or MRBT) who act 
as intermediate processing stages. 4 Indeed, alleged alternatives promoted at  EU 
level such as MRBT facilities have shown their limitations5, will always need WtE to 
hygienise residual waste – or in the worst case, landfills.  
 
As European installations are subject to stringent emissions standards and 
regulation, they actually act as a pollution sink 6, and contribute to remove 
pollutants from the circular economy. 
 
Waste-to-Energy in the circular economy

its Communication on the role of Waste-to-Energy in the circular economy. WtE is 
also an essential part of the full waste management value chain. Indeed,  the 
treatment of residual waste includes residues from recycling facilities , as 
underlined by the recycling sector itself 7, meaning also that maximising recycling 
implies ensuring that residues from recycling operations and material  already

On the other hand, non-recyclable waste still holds valuable materials that can be 
recovered after the thermal process.  Minerals constitute about 80 -85% of 
incineration bottom ash (IBA), metals 10-12% (steel, iron) and non-ferrous metals 
2-5% (mainly aluminum, but also copper and zinc). 
 
Metals separated and recycled from IBA are included in the amount of 
municipal waste recycled and contribute to the Member states’ waste management 
targets.11 This needs to be safeguarded as to reach the 65% of recycling target by 
2035. For minerals, depending on  Member states’ practices, they are utilised up 
to 100% as secondary raw materials  in construction. 12 Bottom ash is also well 
suited for construction and can be used  as an aggregate for base layers, for 
example in roads or parking areas.13 
 
However, to maximise their contribution to the circular economy the utilisation 
of minerals should be recogni sed on the same level as the recycling of 
metals. 
 
Furthermore, both metals and minerals recovered from IBA contribute to avoiding 
the polluting extraction and processing of virgin materials.14  
 
Using secondary raw materials is part of the transition to a ci rcular economy,  
where waste is perceived and treated as a resource, and aligned with the Green  
Deal’s objectives and the 2050 climate neutrality target. Ferrous and non -ferrous 
metals, such as copper or aluminum, are necessary materials in the energy  
transition, and recycling those components greatly contribute to reducing GHG  
emissions.15 The recovery of metals from IBA saves about 60kg of CO2eq per tonne 
of waste treated.16

How to improve waste management 
As recently recogni sed by the European Environmental Agency (EEA) 17, the 
generation of municipal waste, including non-recyclable waste, has not decreased 
since 2015: it stabilised, despite slight progress in recycling.

target for 2030 (EEA, 2022) 
 
 
This means that reaching the target of halving residual municipal waste by 
2030, a non-binding target set by the Circular Economy action plan, is unlikely 
to be met  (see graph above), will require  the EU and Member states to 
substantially step up their efforts on waste prevention.  
 
Efforts in recycling were then offset by increase in waste generation. While a target 
on waste prevention set at EU level has its merits, in this instance it did not provide 
substantial results yet, and will probably not be met. This can be explained by the 
fact that waste generation and waste management are complex issues, with 
significant differences from country to country, and even from region to region. 
Non-recyclable waste especially is heterogeneous and its composition can vary 
depending in seasonali ty, design changes, climate, variations in materials, etc.

Hence, an EU wide binding target on waste prevention would not be efficient, as it 
would not be adapted to the various situations across Member States. 
As recognised during an impact assessment fro m 2005 on previous waste 
legislation, “ binding waste prevention targets or mandatory waste 
prevention measures […] do not fit the need for waste prevention policies 
to be flexible and adapted to local circumstances”.18 
The existing legislation also provides guidelines and tools that are not sufficiently 
employed by Member states and European municipalities, such as the polluter 
pays principle (PPP). According to the European Court of Auditors (ECA), the  
current application of the PPP does not ensure that polluters cover the full cost of 
pollution22. Indeed, while “the costs of waste management, including for the  
necessary infrastructure and its operation, shall be borne by the original waste  
producer or by the current or previous waste holders” (Artic le 14, Directive 
2008/98), the application of this principle is inconsistent. 
 
The reinforcement of the PPP, through Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) for 
instance, would help reduce the costs of waste management for local authorities  
and taxpayers, and  incentivise a more sustainable design of products 19, hence  
reducing waste at the source. The PPP should be reinforced to make polluters pay, 
here the producers and holders of waste, whose responsibility is recognised by the 
current legislation. EPR schemes also contribute to improving separate collection 
and high-quality recycling. 
 
Reducing GHG emissions also relies on the implementation of the current  EU 
legislation. Indeed, the full application of the current EU waste legislation, 
including waste management targets, would deliver 150 Mt CO 2eq of GHG 
emissions savings by 2035.20 
 
Achieving the landfill target by 2035 
Landfills are one of the main emitters of methane emissions, a GHG gas 84-times 
more-potent than C O2 over a 20 -year period 21, and a key challenge in climate 
change mitigation. Indeed, as stated by President Von der Leyen at the COP26 in 
Glasgow: “Methane is one of the gases we can cut fastest. Doing that will 
immediately slow down climate change”.22

While some European landfills now recover methane and modern installations don’t 
represent a risk for human health and the environment, many outdated 
installations are at risk of leakages of other pollutants to groundwater, air 
and soil. Moreover, landfill sites occupy  valuable lands, reducing the availability 
of space for both urban areas and natural  habitats for wildlife. The rehabilitation 
of lands previously occupied by landfills requires time and investments, especially 
considering that there is a total of at least 500 000 operational and closed landfills 
in the EU. The impact on human health and the environment can be long-lasting: 
even a closed landfill site is  issuing leachate and GHG emissions for up to 25 
years23. Incidents in outdated installations, such as fires, are also a substantial risk 
of widespread pollution.24

Diverting waste from landfills is a priority for many Member states: recyclable to 
recycling operations, and non-recyclable to recovery operations such as Waste-to-

will still be necessary in the future. 
 
For the safe, sustainable treatment of residual waste, Waste-to-Energy is 
legitimately recognised as higher in the waste hierarchy . By generating 
energy, providing secondary raw materials from IBA, and from diverting waste 
from landfills, Waste-to-Energy can also be considered as carbon neutral, thanks 
to those carbon offsets.25 
 
 
In short 
 
While the primary role of Waste-to-Energy is to safely treat non-recyclable waste, 
its role in the circular economy is significant and cannot be overlooked , 
both in providing heat and electricity, but also as an  environmentally sound 
treatment of pollutants and residual waste, including from recycling facilities. The 
contribution of both metals and minerals from incineration bottom ash needs 
to be fully recognised, and the waste hierarchy preserved as such.

VALMET’S VIEW ON THE REVISION OF THE EU WASTE FRAMEWORK DIRECTIVE (WFD) 
 
 
Valmet welcomes the Commission’s initiative to revise the Waste Framework Directive 
(WFD) and would like to thank for the opportunity to contribute to the ongoing public 
consultation phase with some comments.  
 
Valmet agrees with the initiative’s objective to improve waste management by reducing 
waste generation including through re-use of products or components and reducing mixed 
waste and increasing preparation for re-use or recycling of waste by improving separate 
collection. 
 
However, Valmet finds it crucial to  point out existing issues with the WFD that must be 
taken into consideration in the upcoming revision. 
 
In this context, Valmet would like to emphasize that: 
• Mandating the separate collection of textile waste will not suffice to increase reuse 
and recycling rates – textile waste must be sorted, and this should be reflected in 
the WFD. 
• Energy recovery must be conducted at the highest efficiency and only from sorted 
residual waste streams not suitable for reuse and recycling (solid recovered fuels) 
– in this context, the outdated R1 criterion must be revised. 
• Disposal via landfilling and incineration should be limited to the minimum or even 
banned – what cannot be reused or recycled, should be incinerated at high 
efficiency waste-to-energy plants. 
  
 
TEXTILES IN THE REVISION OF THE WFD 
 
Valmet welcomes the proposal to introduce an Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) 
particularly in relation to textile products. Valmet also supports the mandated separate collection 
of textile waste from 2025, and any potential future requirements to prepare textile waste for reuse 
as a necessary first step and recycle remaining textiles into new textiles. However, it must be 
noted that separately collected textile waste is as such unusable – it must first be sorted and pre-
processed. Therefore, increasing textile waste collection rates will not suffice to increase reuse 
and recycling rates. In addition to mandating the separate collection of textile waste, sorting the 
waste first for reuse and subsequently sorting the remaining waste by fiber composition must also 
be ensured. 
 
Reuse 
 
Valmet supports the Commission’s proposal to consider requiring that separately collected textile 
waste is prepared for reuse as a necessary first step. It must however be noted that no automated 
procedures currently exist to identify the reusability of collected textile waste. 1 Sorting textiles 
manually is a time -consuming and labor  intensive practice, and  is therefore currently not

Recycling 
 
Currently, less than 1% of all textile waste is recycled into new textiles. While mandating separate 
collection of textiles will ensure a higher availability of material for recycling, it will not suffice to 
solve the low recycling rates. Textile waste deemed not suitable for reuse after manual sorting 
still cannot be recycled into new textiles as such – it must first be sorted by fiber composition and 
pre-processed. 
 
Existing fiber-to-fiber recycling technologies can work to reuse either natural, cellulosic fibers such 
as cotton, or synthetic fibers such as polyester. Therefore, textile waste must be sorted by fiber 
composition before it can be received at a fiber-to-fiber recycling plant. In addition, textile products 
containing buttons or zippers must first be pre-processed to remove them before being recyclable.  
 
Currently, few automated solutions are available for sorting textile waste by fiber composition. 
Such innovative solutions are yet to reach full scale, and thus also sorting by fiber composition is 
mostly done manually outside of the EU. Customers of Valmet practicing fiber-to-fiber recycling 
import their textile waste from third countries, where it has already been sorted by fiber 
composition and pre -processed to remove buttons and zippers.  For f iber-to-fiber recy cling 
operators to use textile waste collected in the EU instead, the waste must be sorted  and pre-
processed. 
 
Valmet supports the Commission’s initiative to introduce a Digital Products Passport under the  
Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulations, and the potential introduction of a digital label 
under the Textile Labelling Regulation, as  such a  reform could , apart from empowering 
consumers to make better choices, also contribute to enabling new automated sorting solutions. 
Valmet believes any digital labels or product passports must be introduced in a permanent form, 
for instance by printing a waterproof QR code directly on the fabric, or introducing a nonremovable 
chip. Traditional labels are often removed by consumers, making the sorting process vastly more 
difficult. 
 
The issue of sorting textile waste must be recognized and addressed in the upcoming revision of 
the WFD. Valmet suggests extending the requirement for Member States to set up separate 
collection of textiles to also sort and pre-process the collected waste. This requirement could take 
effect in intervals, with the sorting of polycotton consisting of 40 -50% cotton required by 2027, 
and more extensive or full sorting required by 2030 to ensure higher percentages of fibers going 
to recycling. While limited solutions exist currently for automated sorting by fiber composition, a 
legal sorting requirement would push innovation in the field. Financial aid should be considered 
for machine developers to support innovation. States  could set up sorting facilities or outsourc e 
sorting to private actors. Textile recycling operators would then buy the sorted and pre-processed 
textile waste, generating both income and jobs in Member States.  Valmet believes that the aim 
should be to create a  financially profitable market-based system for the sorting, pre-processing 
and recycling of textile waste, similarly as has been the case with paper recycling.  
 
Alternatively, sorting and pre-processing could be conducted by private actors in a similar manner 
as fiber-to-fiber recycling is currently done. However, minimal transportation from facility to facility 
should be ensured  for environment al and self-sufficiency reasons. Th ese issues must be 
addressed in the revision of the WFD.

ENERGY RECOVERY 
 
High efficiency energy recovery 
 
Waste incineration is necessary for the protection of the environment and human health. Energy 
recovery from waste, i.e., the incineration of waste for energy recovery (“waste-to-energy”, WtE), 
plays an important role in waste management, and in the Circular Economy. 
 
The Waste Framework Directive makes a distinction between the incineration of waste for the 
purpose of disposal (Article 3 No. 19 together with Annex I, D 10 ‘Incineration on land’ and D 11 
‘Incineration at sea’) and incineration for energy re covery (Article 3 No. 15 WFD together with 
Annex II, R1 ‘Use principally as a fuel or other means to generate energy’). This distinction is also 
reflected in the waste hierarchy defined in Article 4, insofar as incineration of waste for energy 
recovery is a recovery measure at the 4th level of the hierarchy, whereas incineration for disposal 
is a disposal measure at the 5th level of the hierarchy. 
 
Valmet finds this distinction vital, but believes more needs to be done to achieve sustainable 
incineration of waste. Incineration of waste for disposal , along with landfilling, should be limited 
to the minimum, or potentially even banned. We stand by the European Parliament’s opinion, that 
where waste incineration is used, it should ta ke place in the most advanced waste -to-energy 
facilities with high energy efficiency and low emissions within the EU. 2 Today, the distinction 
between incineration and energy recovery offered by the R1 definition is not enough to ensure 
efficient resource use. The R1 definition was introduced in the WFD decades ago, and now covers 
most, if not all,  of the waste incineration facilities in the EU . To ensure efficient resource use, 
Valmet firmly believes the R1 definition should be revised with ambition. The requirement should 
be that of the upper BAT AEELs range for gross electrical efficiency and gross energy efficiency3. 
For heat only production, the requirement should be set at 85 % boiler efficiency. 
 
Currently, there is no incentive in place to work towards sustainable waste incineration. Requiring 
high efficiency in energy recovery in the revision of the WFD would ensure resource efficiency in 
the use of fuel, maximize the possibility for increased energy production in waste-to-energy plants, 
and incentivize further innovation in the field for a more sustainable industry. The requirement for 
high efficiency must be introduced now, as new investments are constantly made in incineration 
plants adhering to the current ambitionless R1 criterion.  Such investments create a lock -in 
phenomenon for the coming 30-40 years. 
 
Incineration of sorted waste not suitable for reuse or recycling 
 
High efficiency energy recovery is however not sufficient to ensure sustainability in waste 
incineration. Only sorted waste, deemed not suitable for reuse, preparation for reuse, or efficient 
material recycling, should be allowed to be incinerated. Following the waste hierarchy strictly in 
this instance would be beneficial for the Circular Economy. Where no alternative disposal method 
exists for waste that can be reused or recycled, sorting will become mandatory, and reuse and 
recycling rates will increase.

However, as not everything can be recycled, Waste -to-Energy must be acknowledged as a 
desirable complementary manner to treat residual waste. WtE is an essential part of the full waste 
management value chain and when done only with residual sorted waste, it can help in reaching 
circular economy targets. In Valmet’s view, high efficiency energy recovery from non-hazardous 
waste streams (industrial waste, construction and demolition waste, waste from waste  
management facilities, waste from material recycling facilities, municipal solid waste  and similar 
non-hazardous commercial waste) that are not  suitable for re -use, preparation for re -use or 
efficient material recycling should be considered as the preferred form of energy recovery in the 
waste hierarchy. Such sustainable energy recovery from solid recovered fuels (SRF) must be 
recognized in the revision of the WFD. 
 
Excluding sustainable energy recovery from solid recovered fuels (SRF)  from the scope of the 
Directive would give a message both to the waste -to-energy producers and energy -intensive 
industries not to invest in more sustainable energy recovery from waste solutions. For technology 
suppliers, the message would be to curb investments in the development of high efficiency energy 
recovery from waste solutions. This would entail a lost opportunity in directing financing to those 
investments that could increase the sustainability of the sector. Further, it would result in a lost 
opportunity with a view to the EU’s 2050 climate neutrality target. 
 
EU legislation does not include a specification for the waste derived fuel descr ibed above. 
However, standard ‘EN ISO 21640:2021 - Solid recovered fuels, specifications and classes’ 
(adopted on 4 June 2021) gives a valid reference on what constitutes a waste -based fuel from 
non-hazardous waste streams not suitable for other purposes. It introduces a well-defined system 
for classification and specification of the waste material. This SRF standard could also be used 
as a definition in EU legislation. The application of such a reference would improve the link 
between waste-to-energy and Circular Economy in the EU market.  
 
Solid recovered fuels (SRF): 
 are produced from non-hazardous waste. 
 The input waste can be industrial waste, construction and demolition waste, waste from 
waste management facilities, waste from material recycling facili ties, municipal solid waste 
and similar non-hazardous commercial waste. 
 should be based on the use of waste streams that are not suitable for re -use, preparation 
for re-use or efficient material recycling. 
 compliance to the requirements in the standard sha ll be established by demonstrating that 
the results of the measured properties conform to the limit values defined. 
 are produced with the aim of using it for energy purposes at the highest possible energy 
efficiency. 
 
Valmet proposes an ambitious revision of the R1 criterion to encompass only high efficiency 
waste-to-energy incineration of solid recovered fuels (SRF).  Alternatively, a new criterion could 
be introduced, for instance in the EU sustainable finance taxonomy.  
 
 
DISPOSAL – LANDFILLING AND INCINERATION 
 
In the revision of the Waste Framework Directive, landfilling as a manner of waste disposal must 
be decreased substantially from the current level. 
 
Valmet also finds it vital to tackle the current imbalance in landfilling practices between Member 
States. Valmet supports the 10% landfill targets for municipal waste for 2035  set by the circular 
Economy action Plan (CEAP), however it must be acknowledged that reaching that target will be

substantially more difficult for those countries that currently divert a high percentage of waste to 
landfills, compared to those countries already having minimal landfilling. In some Member States, 
even 70% of all waste still goes to landfills. Valmet supports the legal action the Commission has 
taken against those Member States, but also believes that appropriate support should be ensured 
to assist Member States in moving was te out of the ir harmful landfill practices. In this way, the 
landfilling targets will be reached in a manner that is both efficient and just. 
 
As discussed in the previous section, where waste is incinerated, it should be done at high 
efficiency waste-to-energy plants and only from  SRF (solid recovered fuels from waste not 
suitable for reuse and recycling ), in line with circular economy targets. Incineration of waste for 
disposal must be limited to the minimum.  Updating the requirements for waste -to-energy 
incineration is vital also from the perspective of limiting incineration for disposal and landfilling to 
the minimum – where energy recovery is only possible from SRF, an incentive will be created to 
invest solely in those incineration plants accepting nothing but SRF. This in turn will drive the 
development of an adequate sorting infrastructure, leading to the promotion of circular economy 
targets via less waste being directed to incineration and landfills. Updating the requirements for 
energy recovery will also help to avoid a lock-in phenomenon in the coming decades to solutions 
not supporting the Circular Economy and waste hierarchy.

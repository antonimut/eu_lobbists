Executive Summary 
FuelsEurope represents the EU fuel manufacturers industry, whose members are primary producers of 
lubricant base stocks as well as finished lubricants products for both EU and global markets. Based on the 
national Extended producer responsibility legislation, several of our association’s members provide EPR 
funding based on the sale of lubricants to enable collection and regeneration . Furthermore, members 
are lubricant users in particular for using them as a product in the production facilitates and some 
members use base regenerated from waste oil in their finished lubricants. 
FuelsEurope supports the EU’s objective of climate neutrality by 2050, and welcomes the Circular 
Economy Action Plan as key enabler for the European Green Deal, projecting EU competitiveness in a 
global dimension and allowing the deployment of low -carbon technologies. FuelsEurope also supports 
any measure that would bring additional clarity to the Waste Hierarchy (ultimately linked to the overall 
environmental impact). We agree that high -quality recycling in the EU should be reached, without 
compromising the quality of the end products delivered to the market. 
We want to support the European Commission in identifying the most effective measures to ensure the 
collection and the environmentally sound treatment of Waste Oils (WOs) assuring at the same time the 
quality of produced Regenerated Base Oils (RRBO) that is required in the lubricants market. 
Below we have provided our response to the Public Consultation regarding WOs, in  four different 
categories in support of WO collection and quality of the end product (RRBO) from the regeneration 
process. 
1. Uncollected Waste Oil  
2. Waste Oil separate collection  
3. Waste Oil regeneration and mandatory use targets  
4. Waste Oil EPRs

1. Uncollected Waste Oil 
FuelsEurope  believe s that the maximisation of WO collection should be the top priority 
of any policy on WO.   
Overall there is a lack of reliable information on uncollected WO. The causes can be wide, but energy 
poverty and a malfunctioning collection system are at the top of likely causes/ drivers (see list at end of 
this section).  An extensive Life-Cycle Analysis (LCA) study carried out by UCSB in California for CalRecycle1 
was likely the first WO LCA to include consideration of the impacts of uncollected WO. An extension to 
this study , commissioned by American Petroleum Institute (API)2, further developed the assessment. 
These studies showed clearly that illegally disposed or uncollected oil is likely to have far greater impacts 
than those from properly collected, treated , and recycled WO. Perhaps more importantly, even small 
changes in collection rate could result in changes to the environmental impacts of the whole system 
that were far more important than changes in the relative amounts of WO treated by one well -
controlled process or another. 
We believe it is important to ensure that the effects of uncollected oil are included in any analysis for 
policy measures otherwise it is easy to overlook the importance of ensuring a high level of collection and 
the benefits that come from increasing collection rates.  Usually, the amount of uncollected oil is simply 
estimated as the difference between the estimated amount of waste oil generated and the amount of 
waste oil collected. Both values are uncertain. Comparing best practices against usual practice would be 
useful.  E.g. by tracking down and addressing the possible gaps in data – with careful consideration of 
potential misuses of WO – for example, specialist garage heaters, possible blending into fuels or other 
uses using surveys, spot checks, and following up on anecdotal information (e.g., checking practice in car 
workshops). At the same time, waste oil improperly disposed to land and water leaves a very distinctive 
effect and significant amounts being discharged to water courses would likely be reported, and impacts 
identified.  A process of making estimates and then checking against information on pollution incidents 
could help to narrow down uncertainty and help direct actions to reduce losses and damage. 
Some possible answers for why WO are uncollected and thus not treated in line with the waste 
hierarchy may be:  
• A significant amount of the overall WO stream may not be suitable for some management 
options. Some oily wastes have very high-water contents, other oils may be too contaminated to 
be treated in the general waste oil system – an obvious example being some electrical oils that 
contain unacceptable levels of polychlorinated biphenyl (PCB).  
• It is economically and environmentally sound to develop dedicated collection and treatment for 
re-use, e.g. much of the t ransformer oil that is used is separately collected and reprocessed for 
re-use in transformers rather than being introduced to the general waste oil stream.  
• There is no economy nor capacity to treat it in line with the waste hierarchy, and there may be 
long transports involved in treating the WO. Regeneration facilities tend to be small (relative to 
virgin base oil manufacturing facilities) and located close to major urban centers where there is

2. Waste Oil separate collection 
Better collection and sorting (including u nderstanding different qualities) of waste oil 
is fundamental.   
FuelsEurope supports measures that include provisions that ensure – where technically feasible and 
economically viable – that WOs are separately collected  to maximise their treatment. We believe that 
collection should be maximised and performed in a way that enable s any kind of efficient material 
regeneration/ recovery and recycling operation, following the differentiation among product categories. 
We are aligned with the objectives of the European Commission, in particular, with regards to increasing 
the amounts of waste oils collected, treated, and sorted in line with the waste hierarchy  based on their 
use and ‘quality’. We believe that a correct sorting of WO  based on the waste hierarc hy would help 
increase the quota of collectable WO, as well as their consequent use according to Waste Framework 
Directive (WFD) art. 21(1)(a)(b)(c)3. 
Concerning WO ‘quality’, we believe that proposed measures should not focus on waste material quality, 
but the focus should be on contaminant levels and the quality of base oil to be regenerated so that it 
is readily useable by the market. If too contaminated then make it fuel, if not consider regulations on 
regeneration to a quality standard that the market can use, i.e. API Group I-III base oils. Regenerators 
will set economic value based on ‘quality’ but need to be clear about what is unacceptable and hold 
generators accountable for contaminated materials. 
The price paid by recyclers to waste collectors is linked to the ‘quality’ of the WO, which if collected in 
separate WO streams would enhance recycling. For WO to be utilised most effectively, segregation of 
different types of waste would help significantly in the quality of the fee dstocks that could be recycled. 
As it may be more time -consuming and expensive to regenerate WO than proceeding down energy 
recovery routes, until there are better incentives for waste segregation, we believe it pushes people down 
routes that are lower in the hierarchy. If better quality waste streams were available through better 
segregation of waste streams, it may increase the price commanded by selling to recyclers and make it a 
more incentivised route, enhancing recycling routes overall.

Regarding mandatory quality control by waste collectors, we believe the focus should be on testing the 
WO for contaminants. Tracking and environmental standards for collecting and aggregating WO should 
be in place to ensure safety and protect the environment. 
We believe that education is where to start., i.e. help WO generators identify the concern about proper 
WO management, then make collection easier (public collection sites, create a communication plan, and 
include a phone app or website to communicate to generators to enable easier collection, subsidized WO 
collection always a plus, supportive policies that ease testing for contamination and if unsuitable for 
regeneration clear process on how to handle waste material , etc.). Further, we support producing 
mandatory guidelines for collection practices by waste collectors. 
One may also consider setting up a B -to-B system, which would facilitate the creation of a WO value 
chain, ensuring its ‘quality’ and boost ing collection simultaneously. It may lead to improved (upgraded) 
recycling facilities and an understanding of what quality is needed for regeneration, as it may differ 
whether a simple filtration process is applied or a re -refining process with more advanced technologies 
(e.g. distilling, hydrotreating). 
Although we understand the importance of easy-to-treat, well-defined, and readily collected WOs, we 
consider it crucial to match this with better collection and sorting (including understanding different 
qualities). To be able to focus on the whole spectra of WO,  one may consider introducing an easy way 
for collectors to test and confirm contamination and quality, i.e. detecting and preventing WO with toxic 
constituents (e.g., PCBs) from complicating the recyc ling process. Communication is key and ease of 
access to handle ‘non-regeneratable’ WO better. 
3. Waste oil regeneration targets  
Any regeneration rate target should  be set for  the RRBO  output  as part of the collected 
WO . A policy measure mandating a certain % use of regenerated base stock in the final 
product would be premature  due to quality concerns.  
According to the Commission and JRC findings, about 61% of the WO collected is regenerated  in the EU 
on average , and we welcome any additional info directly co ming from the MS to substantiate this 
evaluation. We would like underline that the mentioned 61% WO regenerated  is actually the amount 
of WO sent to regeneration on the basis of the quantity collected; this does not always mean that WO 
is regenerated into base oils/lubricants. A significant amount of this 61 % of regenerated products  is 
being used to produce also fuel and asphalt ‘flux’ material , which is  sold commercially ; these are 
unavoidable by -products during the regeneration process of making RRBOs, espec ially when more 
advanced processes are being used (e.g. distillation and hydrotreating). We believe that any regeneration

4. Waste oil EPRs 
EPR should be  a transparent system that documents how EPR funds are used to directly 
support increased collection.  
On the consideration of regulatory measures to reinforce the polluter pays principle by expanding E PR 
schemes to lubricants, we recommend considering the possible impact of existing, well-functioning WO 
EPR schemes in the EU . Further, we believe that flexibility in MS s’ compliance should be envisaged, 
providing the right support to MSs to help them develop a functioning secondary raw material ecosystem. 
In developing EPRs, we believe it is important to have a process that is transparent in terms of how to 
access WO collection, the cost to do so, and a transparent system that documents how EPR funds are 
used to directly support increased collection and not being put into a general tax fund for MSs/local 
provinces.

A1: Is waste oil regeneration to base oil always beneficial for the environment? 
Many studies have been completed looking at the life cycle impacts of alternative waste management 
options for waste oil.  The scope, quality, and value of these studies varies considerably. 
In recent years the projects conducted in the US – the CalRecy cle life cycle assessment 4 and 
accompanying economic studies5 and the API sponsored extension of the CalRecycle study6 – have been 
the most comprehensive studies carried out.  A recent revision and update of the GEIR sponsored LCA 
done by ifeu7 has been completed and published in Europe.  It has become clear from the studies done 
that the LCA of waste oil is not straightforward.  The lubricant and industrial oils market is complex, the 
properties of waste oils vary widely and the requirements are shifting, affecting both new product and 
quantity and quality of waste oil produced.  Furthermore, treatment technologies are changing.   
Although several LCA studies have been conducted there is no consensus on the optimum treatment 
technology but it is now widely recognised that increasing collection is the key to reducing 
environmental impacts of waste oil management systems.  However, considerable attention is focused 
on choice of treatment option and there is great pressure to favour regeneration over other options. It is 
clear that impacts from different treatment options vary depending on the technology, waste oil and 
operations and can overlap so that picking a clear and sole treatment option is unlikely to be justified on 
the basis of environmental performance, certainly not for all waste oils in all circumstances.  Key lessons 
from the API extension work include the crucial effect of increasing collection rates and the sensitivity of 
comparisons of treatment options to factors such as the particular levels of  pollution control and the 
‘displacements’ that occur in virgin product markets as a result of introducing products from used oil. Its 
key lessons and findings are also relevant to other time periods, states and countries. The study concludes 
that “policies that encourage a healthy portfolio of waste oil treatment options and that may lead to 
an increase in the overall market demand for waste oil, are likely to deliver increased collection rates, 
resulting in an overall significant reduction in environmental impacts”. 
Further, the US Department of Energy (DOE) recently  published a report regarding Used Oil 8 
Management and Beneficial Reuse9 which reviewed existing LCAs and stated that “either primary waste 
management strategy dominates the other in any of the major LCA impact categories, suggesting that 
each strategy has trade-offs”.  
The Commission can also use the results from a preliminary study on waste oil data and practices in the 
EU Member States 10. It is clear that data are imperfect and inconsistent and additional data and 
assessment will be required to develop soundly based and effective policy in the field.   
In addition to better analysis of the comparative environmental perfor mance of different systems 
considerable work has been done to develop more up to date and more extensive data bases of relevant 
properties of waste oil – in particular the composition.  The composition of waste oil affects the options 
for processing and impacts on assessments of comparative performance of different treatment options 
both in theory and in practice.

A2: Future proof waste oil management policy 
How the base oil mix in the future will have an impact on potential measures implemented we invite 
the Commission to take into consideration: 
• 2030 and 2050 timeframes; 
• Supposedly decreasing future use of the Internal Combustion Engine and increasing use of 
alternative energies; 
• The demand shift in product performance required to meet other European Green Deal 
policies, e.g. fuel economy/emissions. 
Lubricants are used in a wide range of applications from automotive through to process oils.  There are 
literally thousands of lubricants that are formulated to meet increasingly stringent specifications for 
individual applications. Over recent years more attention has been focused on how lubricants can affect 
the performance of processes and machinery and more attention on how lubricants can affect the 
environmental performance of processes and machinery as well. These changes are expected to continue 
and accelerate and will affect the production of base oil, the production and use of additives and the 
nature and amounts of waste oils. To take the example of automotive lubricants huge strides have been 
made with extending the drain intervals of vehicles, reducing the generation of waste oil, while at the 
same time greatly improving performance reducing emissions and fuel consumption.  These changes and 
the specifications that underpin them have driven a move to higher quality base oils as well as the need 
for specialised additives. 
We believe that it is important for any work to support policy development to consider the possible 
effects of the changing market for lubricants.  We have seen great changes in recent years which have 
resulted in lubricants which are quite different now than 20 years ago ; huge changes in typical drain 
intervals and industrial infrastructure affecting volumes and relative mix of different types of lubricant 
and significant changes in for mulation driving improved technical and environmental performance 
(reduced fuel consumption for one). New technologies for production of base oil have made higher 
quality base oil available and the marketplace continues to adapt. Figure 1 provides a graphic illustration 
of how the market for base oil by group may develop.

expected to occur will change both the volumes and quality of waste oil in ways that will have potentially 
huge impacts on the generation, collection, treatment and reuse of waste oil.  A number of scenarios that 
addressed these potential changes would significantly strengthen any analysis of waste oil policy.

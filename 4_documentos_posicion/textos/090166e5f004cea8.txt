ENSIE’s answer to the public consultation on the 
revision of the Waste Framework Directive (WFD) 
 
ENSIE, the European Network for Social Integration Enterprises, represents the interests of 
national and regional networks of ‘Work Integration Social Enterprises’ (WISEs) striving for more 
inclusive and integrated forms of employment at European level. WISEs are defined by three 
identifying pillars: they are enterprises whose main objective is the social and professional 
integration of disadvantaged people, they are at the core of the economic system and present a 
strong pedagogical dimension. ENSIE prepared this position with the support of RREUSE, the e 
European network representing social enterprises in the circular economy.  
ENSIE welcomes the current discussions around the revision of the Waste Framework Directive 
(WFD) as it is a key opportunity to ensure consistent application of the waste hierarchy throughout 
the EU, it is also an opportunity to develop synergies between the social an d circular economy 
objectives by supporting social enterprises active in the fields of reuse and preparation for re-use. 
Indeed, according to ENSIE’s annual Impact WISEs study reusing and recycling are the  areas in 
which WISEs are the most active at the EU level.  
The first objective of the revision of the WFD should be to reduce waste generation through more 
ambitious eco-design requirements for durability, reparability an d reusability. It is also crucial to 
develop clear guidelines for national end -of-waste criteria that do not prevent the correct 
application of the waste hierarchy which can prevent job creation. ENSIE supports RREUSE in 
their demand to make repair more af fordable and convenient to promote the reuse of products 
and save precious resources, especially in what concerns electronic waste  and to improve the 
logistics of waste collection systems to safeguard the reusability of products.  It is also crucial to 
set quantitative reuse and preparation for re -use targets separate from recycling to avoid 
premature recycling of reusable goods. 
ENSIE, in line with RREUSE demands, underline s that when Extended Producer Responsibility 
(EPR) Schemes are developed, they should consider social enterprises. Indeed, EPR schemes 
often lead to the creation of monopolies by producers who tend to se t up their own collection 
models. That is why it is crucial to have separate targets for preparation for re-use as it can provide 
the necessary incentives  for a thorough implementation of the waste hierarchy, provided that 
social enterprises are involved in the development and design of these targets to ensure that their 
application stimulates collaboration among different stakeholders. A portion of the EPR  fee can 
also be used to improve collection and directly support the activities of relevant social enterprises 
as well as covering the costs of residual waste disposal following a preparation for re-use process. 
It is also crucial to support social enterprises to unlock strong synergies in the economic, social 
and environmental sectors. Indeed, numerous social enterprises, and WISEs in particular are 
working in the reuse and recycling fields. While operating, they not only provide environmental

people,… In the last few years, the Commission started to recognise the importance of social 
enterprises in the circular economy and more ambitions have been set in the last years wit h the 
Social Economy Action Plan. This is why it is crucial that the role of social enterprises already 
recognized in recital 29 of the WFD is strengthened in further reviews to realize the objective of 
unlocking stronger synergies between the circular and  social economic policies, reflecting the 
ambition set in the SEAP. 
One of the most effective measures to  reach this goal is through the insertion of social clauses 
and reserved market in all public procurement procedures, merging circular and business models 
to create local jobs by favouring the reinsertion of disadvantaged groups in the labour market, 
while also advancing the transition towards a circular economy.  The use of socially responsible 
public procurement in waste management legislation is thus o f key importance in this regard, 
which can take the form of reserving public tenders to actors from the social economy as it is the 
case in Spain, where a recently approved law on waste mandates 50 % of public tenders related 
to the collection, transport, and  treatment of second -hand products to social enterprises. The 
possibility of supporting the role of social enterprises in enabling the transition to a circular 
economy by introducing similar clauses in the WFD should thus be further investigated. 
Fiscal tools can also be helpful to increase the attractiveness of reuse to consumers, which can 
take the form of VAT exemptions for repair services and second -hand products, but also tax 
reductions to encourage the donation of used goods to social enterprises.

Plastics Europe Feedback to the Waste 
Framework Directive Revision 
 
 
1. Plastics Europe welcomes the revision of the Waste Framework Directive (WFD) and 
ask for a paradigm shift in redefin ing waste and end-of-life articles as “valuable 
resources” for return to the circular economy.  
2. We call for a comprehensive revision of the WFD with a view to creat ing a “Circular 
Economy Framework Directive” that  should include the full implementation of the 
current WFD (2018). This will lead to a more harmonised approach in creating a 
Single Market for resource recovery to meet the required efficiencies. 
3. Plastics Europe shares the Commission’s concern on the lack of quality and 
quantities of recyclates and call for harmonised efficient collection and sorting 
systems to provide the required materials to stimulate the Circular Economy. 
4. The Waste Framework Directive should build on the EU’s twin green and digital 
transitions to a resilient economy  by incentivising the u se of digitalisation for 
collection, sorting, recycling, and recycled content in finished goods while helping 
track waste streams and improving material and data quality. 
5. We support the development of Reuse (and Refilling) product applications , where it 
makes sense from a life cycle perspective, in providing an effective way of reducing 
waste across all product applications and  saving resources and helping to improve 
the environmental footprint of our lifestyles . A harmonised framework for re-use is 
required that provides certainty, incentivises further investment in reuse / refill 
applications and supports innovative solutions (e.g. a definition of reuse that is 
consistent across all EU legislation covering a specific application. 
6. For the w ell-functioning of the EU Single Market and the development of the EU 
circular economy, a secondary raw material recognised in one EU Member State 
must also be recognised in all others. We support the development of End of Waste 
(EoW) criteria for all recycled materials including plastics and for all types of recycling 
technologies. 
7. The impact of food production on the global greenhouse gas emissions and resource 
use makes avoidable food waste unacceptable, which in many countries is the most 
rapidly increasing waste category in the last 20 years.  Active measures need to be 
implemented to prevent such waste and we call for a mandatory separate collection 
of organic household waste for organics recycling 
8. Chemical recycling offers a complementary solution to mechanical recycling , for 
those plastics that cannot be recycled by mechanical means, which results in the 
production of virgin like plastics from feedstocks such as  specific barrier packaging 
designed to provide extended shelf -life, mixed plastics that cannot be separated, 
plastics with legacy additives, composites, etc.

Plastics Europe welcomes the Waste Framework Directive Revision  (WFD) and we share the 
Commissions concern on low recycling rates, insufficient volumes of recyclates, and the lack of 
efficient waste-collection, sorting and recycling systems provision in  many Members States. We 
therefore call for an ambitious revision tackling the challenges and issues as outlined below. 
 
 
Implementation of the Waste Framework Directive 
The 2018 revision introduced several key requirements such as obligated separate collection 
obligation, EPR minimum requirements and preparing for reuse/recycling targets. It is essential that 
these existing requirements are correctly transposed, implemented, monitored, and enforced (using 
current infringement procedures when required) across the EU.  
 
The opportunity of digitalisation in improving our knowledge of waste 
The Waste Framework Directive should build on the EU’s twin green and digital transitions to 
incentivise the use of digitalisation for collection, sorting, recycling and recycled content in finished 
goods to help track waste streams and improve data quality while identifying and closing waste data 
gaps. This can be achieved by developing a Member States roadmap that includes concepts such 
as equipping separate collection containers, sorting facilities and recycling plants with digital tools, 
accompanied by similar innovations with digital marking of articles which enter waste streams.  
Harmonised databases are key for Member States that will allow comparability and data reporting, 
Data security must be a priority as well as managing the balance between administrative burden and 
value offered by digital data collection.  
 
Waste Hierarchy 
• We must rethink on how we look at waste  and embrace the need for a paradigm shift  in our 
thinking to redefine waste so as to consider end-of-life articles as “valuable resources” for return 
to the circular economy.  
• The waste hierarchy provides clear guidelines on how waste should be addressed, but this 
should be further developed with a view to creating a “Circular Economy Framework Directive” 
that moves from the current waste-oriented policies to a framework that supports circularity and 
aims for net zero emissions of the overall plastics system. We consider it a priority that the 
currently revised product regulations such as the PPWD and the ELV Directive should be brought 
into closer alignment with the Waste Framework Directive and accompanied by aligned 
harmonised definitions of key concepts such as ‘recyclable’, ‘recycled content’, ‘collection rates’, 
‘recycling rates’,  ’mechanical and chemical recycling ’, ‘mass balance ’ and ‘pre- and post -
consumer waste’. 
 
Prevention and reuse 
The current Waste Framework Directive, most recently amended in 2018, offers a menu of different 
and non-harmonised measures for Member States to adopt waste prevention measures. These were 
only partially implemented and applied and, in many cases, have proven to be ineffective and leading 
to the fragmentation of the EU Sin gle Market  with the  failure to deliver an effective waste 
management framework throughout Europe. This latest revision should ensure an EU harmonised

approach for managing waste that provides investor certainty in developing the enabling 
infrastructure to achieve the goals of turning waste into a valuable resource for the circular economy. 
 
In many instances Reuse (and Refilling) product applications can offer an effective way of reducing, 
for instance packaging waste, saving resources, and helping to improve the environmental footprint 
of our lifestyles. A recent  independent study commissioned by Plastics Europe, “ReShaping 
Plastics”, highlights the pote ntial of reuse systems and new delivery models for different plastic 
applications and how they play a significant role in reducing both the emissions and waste. Plastics 
Europe calls for an ambitious, effective, and sustainable EU reuse policy  that includes realistic 
targets agreed among stakeholders. Sufficient attention should be given to development of a simple 
methodology for the application of targets, if needed, based on scientific evidence regarding the 
environmental benefits of each measure. 
 
From an industry perspective, it is crucial that policymakers create a framework for re -use that 
provides certainty, incentivises further investment in reuse / refill applications and supports 
innovative solutions (e.g. a definition of reuse that is consistent across all EU legislation covering a 
specific application (such as packaging and single-use plastics). 
 
Collection and Sorting 
• Increase in separate collection and sorting of waste in all countries is key to securing the flow of 
high-quality material toward recyclers (accompanied by improvement of sorting of residual waste 
which shows a great potential for recyclable material which could be used as feedstock for 
chemical recycling therefore helping secure high quantity of recycled plastics.) 
o Need to be state of the art technology to allow supply of high -quality sorted material to 
mechanical rec ycling, and, complementarily, suitable material streams to chemical 
recycling and dissolution. 
o Easy access and easy labelling for consumers to promote citizen participation 
 
Recycling: 
• To facilitate the uptake of recyclate, the quality of the output of rec ycling streams is a decisive 
factor. The uptake of recycled content should be equally recognised if it is coming from the same 
sector (e.g. packaging to packaging) or is used in an open loop. The main objective should be to 
incentivise the use of sustainable feedstock to improve plastics circularity and reduce 
dependence on primary fossil feedstocks to manufacture plastic products.  
• Any consideration of different recycling technologies in the waste hierarchy should consider 
innovation, environmental footprint and quality of the output  without creating any technological 
hierarchy.  
• We call for the Commission’s support of innovative recycling technologies such as chemical 
recycling to keep all carbon in the loop and supply enough materials with the required safety and 
performance properties. Looking at the food waste reduction target needed, we see the diversity 
of plastics properties for different products as an important pillar to extend the s helf life of food 
and thereby preventing food spoilage. Chemical recycling offer s a complementary solution to

mechanical recycling in solving the target conflict for the circularity of specific barrier packaging 
and the whole life cycle impact of food waste reduction.  
• The acceleration of capacity growth of chemical recycling needs long term investor confidence 
and security, including the full acceptance of chemical recycling as a technology within the waste 
hierarchy as well as transparent and unbiased calculation rules for chemically recycled content 
which includes acceptance of the chain of custody model for Mass Balance that is already well 
accepted in other areas.  
• It is important to facilitate movement of plastic waste across the EU to ensure plastic waste 
management in Member States with  proper recycling technologies an d capabilities. 
Consideration should also be given to allow the import (e.g., via the OECD Decision) of all end 
of life plastics waste from least developed countries (LDCs) into  the OECD countries including 
the EU and US for (mechanical and chemical) recycling into value added applications. Such an 
approach w ill enable innovation for recycling technologies in OECD countries, thereby 
accelerating the development of a circular economy and thus helping to reduce the use of virgin 
plastics 
 
 
End of Waste and By-Products  
• For the well -functioning of the EU Single Market and the development of the EU circular 
economy, a secondary raw material recognised in one EU Member State must also be 
recognised in all others. We support the development of End of Waste (EoW) criteria for all 
recycled materials including plastics by all types of recycling technologies. 
• EoW criteria should solely be based on quality specifications for the end -product or customer 
specifications but not on the waste characteristics (i.e. contamination, hazard, type of polymer 
etc), their origin (e.g.  biowaste) and application (e.g. medical waste) nor the type of recycling 
technologies i.e. be technology neutral. 
• Waste criteria/specifications should only be used for the selection of recycling technologies not 
for determining EoW. Waste criteria such as ‘plastic waste, shall not contain leachable fluids 
such as oil, solvents, glues, paint, aqueous’ should not be part of End of Waste criteria. 
• Products and substances produced from the recycling of plastic wastes, and which are registered 
under REACH/CLP  have reached end of waste status in accordance with Article 6 and the 
definition of recycling in the Waste Framework Directive. An example includes pyrolysis oil which 
is used as feedstock in cracker plants for “Manufacture of bulk, large scale chemicals (including 
petroleum products)” and “Manufacture of fine chemicals” (Registration Dossier - ECHA 
(europa.eu); Pyrolysis oil REACH substance identity EC / List no.: 929-242-3). 
• We support in principle that waste streams remain wastes until all pre-treatment steps have been 
completed. There should not be generic descriptions of these pre -treatment steps since these 
are not appropriate for all types of wastes and recycling technologies and this could ultimately 
restrict recycling and innovation.  The following  example of generic description would not be 
appropriate for all types of wastes and recycling technologies but only for mechanical recycling - 
Plastic waste streams used as input shall, once received by the producer or importer, be kept 
permanently separate from  the contact with any other waste, including other plastic waste 
grades.

In addition, we support the development of EU guidelines to; 
1. Harmonise interpretation of EoW 
2. Define which type of recycling process is appropriate depending on the type of waste e.g. 
level of contamination from plastics or other materials and hazard characteristics. This would 
require some regular update to track new technologies and corresponding waste streams to 
be treated (efficiency and economics could improve due to new and improved technologies). 
 
Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) 
• Extended Producer Responsibility schemes are valuable tools for managing a producer’s 
operational responsibility in financing the end-of-life management of the products they place on 
the European market. At the same time, EPR incentivises the opportunity to secure an easy and 
open supply of high -quality waste to provide mechanically and  chemically recycled material to 
produce plastics and plastic products. It is a driver of circularity that secures the transformation 
of waste into the future feedstock for plastics products.  
• Different industry sectors (e.g. packaging, automotive, building and construction) have different 
requirements and n eeds for circularity. Nevertheless, we see, if done in accordance with the 
sector-specific requirements, a strong benefit of EPR systems for the majority of all industry 
sectors. The Waste Framework directive should give clear guidance supporting the circularity of 
materials in the European economy  and it is the most suitable framework to cover the general 
approach to EPR schemes in the EU, which may be tailored via sectoral legislation at a second 
stage. 
• The WFD shall give clear guidance ensuring harmonisation of systems across Europe promoting 
the Single Market space and easy access to sufficient qualities and volumes of recylates in 
Europe. Solid evaluation criteria , based on life cycle assessments , and methodologies are 
required to define EPR categories. Potential fee modulation, if applied, should be based on clear, 
predictable, and harmonised criteria. Investments of collected EPR funds  should be 
accompanied by a transparent investment framework that stimulates innovation and the 
development of the required infrastructure for achieving the Circular Economy goals  
 
Landfill and incineration 
• We call for a ban on the landfill and the waste incineration of recyclable materials to secure this 
essential feedstock for driving the Circular Economy.  
• Today landfill and incineration are still a major end of life route for plastics [ Circularity Report 
2022]. In future this must change a s more material is recycled  alongside other prevention 
measures the amounts destined for incineration will be reduced. The Reshaping plastics report 
clearly demonstrates the negative effects of incineration o n the GHG emissions of the plastics 
system [SystemIQ]. Member States and municipalities are required to develop a roadmap for the 
remaining service life of waste incineration capacity to ensure that recyclable wastes are directed 
to recycling processes and not committed to waste to energy facilities. 
 
Food waste & organic recycling 
 
  6/6 
• The impact of food production on the global greenhouse gas emissions and resource use makes 
avoidable food waste unacceptable, which in many countries is the most rapidly increasing waste 
category in the last 20 years.  Active measures need to be implemented to prevent such waste 
and where this is unavoidable such waste streams should not be landfilled. 
• We therefore welcome the efforts of the Commission to tackle this important issue. Most of the 
food waste in Europe is c reated within the households so there is a clear need for behavioural 
change interventions as well as technical innovations to address this . The important role of 
plastic packaging in shelf-life extension in households should not be ignored when considering 
changes in the plastic packaging regulations. To allow the use of high -tech packaging that 
prevents food loss, we see a need to further support the development o f innovative recycling 
technologies such as chemical recycling. Innovation in that field can bring together the need of 
preventing food waste and the need for circularity of plastics. 
• A transition to a Circular Economy includes a more intense look on the organic recycling of food 
waste, especially the huge amounts being generated by European households. The recycling of 
organic waste significantly lowers European GHG emissions (compared to incineration or landfill) 
and the application of mature composts play a significant role in the restoration of the biodiversity 
and fertility of our European agricultural soils. These sustainability benefits are connected to the 
quality of the soil amendment created which is directly linked to the treatment process for  
organics calling for minimum  quality criteria for the recycled output material. Certified 
compostable plastics support the separate collection of organic waste and their complete 
composting in industrial facilities.  
• Organic recycling can be a valuable so urce for secondary biobased raw material e.g. for the 
generation of bio-naphtha or the use of biogas in chemical production 
• Plastics Europe calls for a mandatory separate collection of organic household waste for 
organics recycling in the EU Member States.

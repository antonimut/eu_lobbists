Executive Summary 
 
In light of the targeted revision of the Waste Framework Directive 2008/98/EC (WFD), and its 
objective of increasing the level of protection of the environment and public health from the 
impacts of waste management, TOMRA, as a leading provider of automate d collection and 
sorting solutions for reuse and recycling, would like to share the experience and knowledge we 
have gathered over more than 50 years in the field of circularity, in order to feed into the 
development of the revised Directive and contribute to a thriving European circular economy.  
 
We support the revision of the WFD and its objective of improving waste management in the EU 
by, among other things, reducing mixed waste and increasing preparation for re -use and 
recycling of waste to achieve the set targets. 
 
Specifically, in addition to those laid out in the Consultation questionnaire, there are three 
specific elements we believe should be carefully considered in the revision process, as they have 
a key role to play in the achievement of the aforementioned objectives: 
 
1. Certain definitions and the EU waste hierarchy must be updated to reflect the current 
circular economy ambitions and ensure the promotion of high -quality recycling over 
downcycling and other forms of recovery. The waste hierarchy should  include 
additional categorisation to distinguish between closed-loop and open-loop recycling 
(with priority given to closed-loop recycling),  as well as between mechanical and 
chemical recycling to ensure that proven mechanical recycling processes are prioritised 
where technically possible.  
 
2. In addition to the need to further strengthening separate collection, mandatory mixed 
waste sorting prior to landfill and incineration should be introduced  in order to 
increase the capture rate of valuable recyclable materials and reduce waste ending up 
in landfill or incineration. Introduc ing mixed waste sorting combined  with advanced 
mechanical recycling processes can ensure that valuable resources are effectively 
circulated back into the  economy, reduces GHG  emissions from waste management, 
and brings EU Member States closer to reaching t heir municipal waste recycling 
targets.  
 
3. We believe the EU should pursue an ambitious implementation of the EU Textiles 
Strategy. In particular, the revision of the WFD should address certain downstream 
considerations linked to textile waste, including se tting binding recycling targets and 
introducing mandatory Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) to enable the scaling 
up of collection, sorting and recycling infrastructure. Of particular importance, 
automated sorting infrastructure is not yet widely appl ied for textile waste and is key  
to enable high-quality recycling and achieve circularity in the textiles sector.

Proposals in more detail 
 
1. Introduce clear definitions and refine the waste hierarchy  
Define the term “high -quality recycling” to close the loop on materials, as well as chemical 
recycling 
The term “high-quality recycling” is mentioned on several occasions in the current legal text 
of the WFD, however, a clear explanation of what the term stands for is lacking. We believe 
the revision of the WFD should introduce a clear definition of the term in the spirit of circular 
economy intentions to promote recycling of materials whereby they retain their highest and 
best use.  
In addition, the term chemical recycling can encompass different processes and technologies. 
For a meaningful discussion, it is absolutely necessary to introduce clear and harmonised 
definitions of the main categories of different chemical recycling processes/ technologies and 
to align relevant legislation and standards . There should be a differentiation between those 
processes whose outputs are in line with the recycling definition (e.g. materials) and those 
which are not (e.g. fuels). Fuels should be excluded from the definition of recycling. 
Prioritise mechanical recycling over chemical recycling 
In addition, with  today’s technologies, data suggests that mechanical recycling remains the 
most effective method to recycle plastics, when considering ecological and climate footprint, 
energy use, and economic  feasibility. Thus, mechanical recycling should always be the 
preferred method over chemical recycling when technically possible to treat plastic waste.  
Chemical recycling has yet to reach commercial and global scale and may be relevant for very 
challenging waste streams. It should not be used for waste streams with proven (advanced) 
mechanical recycling pathways.  
This distinction should be clearly reflected in the refined EU waste hierarchy. In addition, the 
EU waste hierarchy should differentiate between the different chemical recycling processes.  
Refine the waste hierarchy to favour closed-loop recycling over open-loop recycling  
The EU waste hierarchy establishes an order of waste management options with priority given 
to waste prevention, followe d by reuse and then recycling, and has been instrumental in 
guiding Member States’ policies and funding decisions. In the face of climate urgency and 
rising circular economy ambitions as well as technological developments, we believe it is now 
time to refine the waste hierarchy by introducing additional categories that will help provide 
more clarity and drive circularity.  
In particular with  regards to recycling, additional distinction should be introduced between 
closed-loop and open-loop recycling. With closed-loop recycling, materials are recycled back 
to the same or similar application, and this could be done for multiple cycles. Under op en-
loop recycling, a product at the end of its life is recycled into less demanding or lower quality 
applications whereby the materials are downgraded and not anymore fit for recycling for the 
same purpose. Thus, only closed -loop recycling enables the cont inuous recycling and return 
into the circular economy to ensure that primary raw materials are preserved in the long run.

A closed-loop system not only ensures high quality of the recyclates, it also provides a higher 
quantity of recyclates.  
A simple example demonstrates that by enhancing 
separate collection coupled with closed -loop 
recycling, effective capture and circulation of 
material increases dramatically.  For this example, 
we use the most successful system for closed-loop 
recycling of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) 
beverage containers. In markets with Deposit 
Return Systems (DRS) in place for beverage 
containers, PET bottles are collected in a dedicated 
closed loop to ensure they can be recycled back 
into new bottles in the most effective way possible. 
As a start, 100 bottles made from virgin plastic are 
placed on the market  and we can assess what 
happens under two scenarios: first, with a 49% 
capture rate through simple separate collection, 
and second, with 90% capture rate through a 
modern deposit return system , based on EU 
averages for the two collection systems. 
In the first scenario, using only the original 
material, 60 recycled bottles could be produced . 
Under the second scenario  however, the same calculations show that 208 recycled bottles 
could be produced out of the original 100 before the material is depleted (see graph). 
Compared to the 60 bottles in the first scenario, this is a tremendous increase in circularity 
by 247%.1 
Refining the EU waste hierarchy by introducing new categories of closed-loop recycling to be 
prioritised over open-loop recycling, and appropriate definitions and priority for mechanical 
recycling over chemical recycling , is crucial to provide clarity and incentives for investing in 
the right processes to drive true circularity.

2. Introduce Mandatory Mixed Waste Sorting prior to landfilling and incineration 
While separate collections are a key element of the circular economy, capture rates across 
Europe remain low, in particular for plastics. As a result, currently, mixed residual waste 2 
contains many valuable materials that could be recovered but instead en d up in landfill or 
incinerated. This causes significant negative environmental impacts (waste of resources, GHG 
emissions…), and seriously compromises the ability of Member States to achieve recycling 
targets set under EU waste legislation.  
Therefore, it is necessary to reconsider how mixed waste is being managed. With the current 
state of technology, it is possible to recover materials from mixed waste and make them 
available for (high -quality) recycling. In combination with advanced mechanical recycli ng 
techniques, recyclable materials (in particular plastics) extracted  from mixed waste can be 
transformed into recyclates with properties similar to virgin. This approach should be seen as 
complementary to separate collections to maximi se recycling and associated environmental 
benefits.  
Needed to mitigate environmental impacts of wasted resources and GHG emissions  
Currently, more than half of the recyclable material that could be recovered and recycled is 
lost to incineration or landfill  instead. In addition to losing valuable resources, leaving these 
materials in mixed waste has a significant impact on GHG emissions.  
Research conducted by sustainability consultancy Eunomia  on behalf of TOMRA  has shown 
that extracting recyclable materia ls from mixed waste can lead to significant reductions in 
GHG emissions3. Given that in Europe, around 90-100 million tonnes of waste are incinerated 
each year, and 65 million are landfilled, introducing mixed waste sorting prior to incineration 
and landfilling could lead to a reduction of around 55 million tonnes of CO2e/ year.  
Ensuring Member States can achieve waste management targets 
In addition, Member States will not be able to reach the recycling targets set in the EU waste 
legislation only with materials captured through separate collection . These are  both 
insufficient in itself and subject to significant losses in the subsequent processing steps.  
Currently, the collection for recycling rate of plastic packaging typically observed in Member 
States is around 40-50%, with the rest ending up in m ixed waste. Of the collected waste , a 
large proportion is not “recyclable” – either because not being designed for recycling or does 
not have a market demand and is therefore not even sorted out  for recycling. From the 
remaining fraction, the re are further losses of around 30% before the end material is  
effectively recycled.

This is further exacerbated by the recent rectification of the European calculation method for 
recycling rates4 which explicitly excludes sorting losses (which could be counted towards the 
achievement of the targets previously ). The effect of this revision is yet to be seen, but it is 
certain that it will bring down the reported recycling rates by Member States. For plastic 
packaging, the recycling rates would fall by at least 10 percentage points from the currently 
reported 42% (potential decrease to 29-32%)5. 
Expected improvements as a result of recent policies  will hopefully address the 
aforementioned leakages after the collection step (e.g. recyclability requirements). However, 
to achieve EU recycling targes, it will be absolutely necessary to capture and send for recycling 
higher amounts of materials relative to what is possible with separate collection techniques.  
As part of the research by sustainability consultancy Eunomia, they assessed the performance 
of three European case studies selected for their already good practice/performance . The 
analysis shows that even in those regions with existing mixed waste sorting practices, the 
overall municipal recycling rates  could increase  by 5.5 percentage points  on average  with 
further emphasis on mixed waste sorting.6 Extrapolated to a national/ EU-level, a requirement 
for implementing mixed waste sorting is therefore expected to have considerable impact on 
recycling rates – both overall municipal, and in particular packaging ones. 
A recent study from Norway aims to assess which solutions are best to enable the country to 
meet its circular economy targets. Based on comprehensive analysis of collection systems in 
429 municipalities, it found that mixed waste sorting can capture more than  double the 
amount of plastic packaging for recycling, as compared to kerbside and bring systems.7  
As a global frontrunner in improving the circularity of resources, TOMRA is partnering with 
key players to develop solutions to both recover valuable materials from mixed waste thus 
preventing they end  up in landfil l or incineration, and to effectively recycle  them. We have 
equipped over 300 successful mixed waste sorting plants worldwide and are pio neering 
advances in  mechanical recycl ing proving that  it is possible to recycle even materials 
recovered from mixed waste to the same quality as virgin and fit for a range of high -quality 
applications.8 
This goes to show that proven solutions exist: Mixed Waste Sorting prior to incineration and 
landfill can deliver significant benefits. By capturing valuable recyclable materials before they 
are disposed of, it reduces GHG emissions, and increases recycling rates. Furthermore, with 
advanced mechanical recycling, these materials, independently of where they are recovered 
from, can be recycled into a final product with the same quality performan ce as virgin 
materials, thereby reducing reliance on fossil fuels. In light of this, we call for the introduction 
under the revision of the WFD of provisions to make Mixed Waste Sorting mandatory prior 
to landfilling and incineration, in order to ensure that as much valuable recyclable materials 
are recovered before final disposal.

3. Address downstream considerations linked to Textiles Waste 
Currently, in the EU, the consumption of textiles, most of which are imported, accounts on 
average for the fourth highest negative impact on the environment and on climate change 
and third highest for water and land use from a global life cycle perspective. And yet, less than 
1% of textile waste globally feeds into new textiles.  
It is thus paramount that the EU pursue an ambitious implementation of the EU Strategy for 
Sustainable Textiles. Specifically, the revision of the WFD should address certain downstream 
considerations linked to Textile Waste, including setting binding recycling targets  and 
introducing mandatory Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)  to enable the scaling up of 
collection, sorting and recycling infrastructure. 
Of particular importance, automated sorting infrastructure is not yet widely applied for textile 
waste and is key to enable high-quality recycling and achieve circularity in the textiles sector. 
The importance of effective collection and automated sorting of textile waste for high-quality 
recycling  
The current situation is that typically collected post-consumer textiles are manually sorted for 
picking out the reusable, resalable items, then the 'waste' fraction of non -reusable material 
will, at best, be downcycled (e.g. into cleaning rags, insulation, etc.), or disposed of. Today, 
manual sorting does not meet the demands of recycling companies and the fashion industry 
because it is not able to provide the consistent quality and large volumes that are required 
for textile recyc ling at large scale. As a result, only a small quantity of discarded textiles is 
recycled back into fibre applications (new textiles). 
Automated sorting is the link that is currently lacking between textile collection and high -
quality textile recycling be cause it has proven extremely high efficiency and precision, 
exemplified by the world’s first fully automated textile sorting plant in Malmö, Sweden, co -
developed by TOMRA and STADLER.  
Equipped with four TOMRA AUTOSORT® units that use Near -Infrared (NIR) and Visible Light 
Spectroscopy (VIS) sorting technology to recognise and differentiate various types of textiles 
according to the fibres content and/or colours , the plant can process large volumes of non -
reusable/ non-rewearable textiles. The expected max imum capacity is 24 000 tons annually 
when at full scale. 
Other innovative technologies have already been developed or are already being developed 
– both for sorting waste textiles and for further processing (including numerous mechanical 
and chemical rec ycling processes). On the recycling processing side, there have been a 
number of key developments over the last years – some commercially available already, some 
still under pilot/demo phase but ready to start scaling up to large industrial sorting hubs. B y 
2024-2025 developing recycling technologies will also be ready and looking for feedstock for 
their recycling processes. A key issue is thus certainly going to be collection / availability of 
non-reusable textiles.  
 The importance of separate collection for both reusable vs non-reusable textile waste 
Currently, however, only limited quantities of textiles are collected separately in the EU. In 
most Member States, as mandatory by 2025, there is a collection system in place for textiles.

However, these capture only limited quantities and typically only focus on reusable / 
rewearable textiles. 
Many non-reusable textiles still end up in mixed waste bins and without further sorting from 
mixed waste are destined for incineration or l andfill. Non-reusable clothing would be also 
sorted out for disposal by NGOs or other organisations collecting textiles because their focus 
is on items which could be resold.  
Even the upcoming requirement for mandatory separate collection of textiles as of 2025 is 
not certain to divert enough materials from mixed waste. The example with packaging is 
telling: even after 20 -30 years of experience with separate collection via kerbside  or street 
containers, considerable amounts of valuable material still can be found in mixed waste (often 
up to 25% of recyclables such as plastics, paper, glass and metals). For packaging, the only 
proven system with consistently high capture rates is a DRS for beverage containers (average 
collection rate above 90%). Similar incentivised collection system could also be considered to 
increase separate collection rates for textiles. 
Therefore, we call for the introduction of EPR schemes with eco -modulation of fees 
mandated by the EU to stimulate collection and recycling. EPR schemes have proven to be a 
very effective tool for achieving circularity, as demonstrated in the case of packaging, and will 
also help finance the much -needed scaling up of collection, sorting and recycling 
infrastructure, in line with the “polluter pays'' principle. Furthermore, there is currently a lack 
of data and transparency on the flow of textiles in the supply chain on the EU market. 
Implementing EPR schemes will help centralise information and keep track of the amount of 
the material that is placed on the EU market, and subsequently treated. 
Setting clear reuse and recycling targets for Textile waste 
In addition, we welcome the launch of a study on introducing mandatory targets for preparing 
for re-use and recycling of textile waste . It is key that such provisions be introduced under 
the WFD.  
Indeed, the WFD currently sets a separate collection obligation without specific recycling 
targets for textile waste. In order to ensure that the collected textile waste is indeed recycled, 
the EU should set mandatory recycling targets for textile waste, following the ap proach set 
for other waste streams. Recycling targets will boost the supply of secondary raw materials 
and should consider the share of reusable and non-reusable textiles. 
Therefore, based on the results of the study, we call on the EU to set a clear path  by 
implementing mandatory recycling targets in the WFD, and support Member States in 
scaling up their sorting and recycling technology to treat textile waste.

Key Recommendations 
 
 
1. Refine the EU waste hierarchy  to reflect the current technological developments, 
introducing new categories of closed-loop recycling to be prioritised over open-loop 
recycling, and mechanical recycling over chemical recycling 
 
2. Introduce mandatory requirement of Mixed Waste Sorting prior to landfilling and 
incineration. 
 
3. Pursue an ambitious implementation of the EU Textiles Strategy,  setting 
mandatory targets for recycling of textile waste and promoting automated sorting 
to improve recycling

Cruelty Free Europe (CFE) welcomes the European Commission’s in itiative ‘Chemical safety – better 
access to chemicals data for safety assessments ’ ( Ref. Ares(2022)5249691), aiming to  horizontally 
apply the transparency principles already implemented in the EU food safety sector (‘Transparency 
Regulation’; Regulation (EU) 2019/1381)1 to other EU chemical management frameworks.  
The proposed initiative builds on  the existing EU legal requirement s to consider all available 
information in the assessment of chemical hazards and/or potential risks  (including the data from 
academic literature), as are already laid out in the individual sectoral legislation ( e.g. Annex VI of 
REACH, Regulation (EC) No 1907/2006;2 or Annex IV of BPR, Regulation (EU) No 528/20123) and as is 
in line with t he ethical imperative to achieve better quality regulatory decisions  as well as to  avoid 
animal testing – the latter of which is also a legal requirement under the Directive 2010/63/EU ‘on the 
protection of animals used for scientific purposes’ .4 However, caution must be taken so that the 
process of gathering and considering all existing relevant information does not simply result in that 
data only being used to screen substances for additional vertebrate testing.

CFE broadly supports the Commission’s data transparency initiatives and commends the Commission 
and the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) on the various efforts in this area, for example in efforts 
‘to crea te and operate an open platform for data on chemicals .’ However, certain aspects of the 
proposed initiative must be approached cautiously , for example the creation of a ‘data generation 
mechanism’, so as not to work against the aim of avoiding animal testing either directly, through the 
commissioning of new animal tests, or indirectly, for example by impeding research, development and 
validation efforts for non-animal new approach methods (NAMs). 
 
Supporting the ‘One Substance – One Assessment’ (OSOA) approach laid out in the Chemicals Strategy 
for Sustainability (CSS), the Commission proposes three key actions as part of this initiative: 
1. “remove legislative obstacles to the re -use of data and streamline the flow of data on 
chemicals between EU and national authorities”; 
2. “extend the principle of ‘open data’ and the relevant transparency principles from the EU’s 
food safety sector to other pieces of legislation on chemicals”; and to 
3. “enable EU and national authorities to commission the testing and monitor ing of chemical 
substances as part of the regulatory framework when further information is considered 
necessary”. 
CFE fully supports the first two proposed actions; we consider data transparency and re-use, as well 
as the streamlining of data sharing, to be essential components of effective and efficient chemicals 
management systems that maximise the use of existing data and thus avoid unnecessary new data 
generation, including by testing on animals. By ensuring as much existing data as possible is available 
for informing regulatory decision-making, such measures will improve the quality of those decisions 
as well as help to reduce delays in the implementation of risk management measures key to the 
protection of public and environmental health.  
The call for evidence states that “[u]nnecessary duplication of effort in data generation still occurs due 
to a lack of data sharing… ” – this is an unacceptable state of affairs, which goes directly against the 
EU’s commitment to reduce reliance on animal testing. In order to rectify this situation, it is  
imperative that risk assessors have open access to all potentially relevant, good quality and reliable 
data currently held by public authorities, private entities, and/or published in academic literature – 
this should be readily available to be used either directly for chemical hazard and/or risk assessment, 
in grouping and read -across approaches on related substances, and to aid the development and 
validation of new approaches.  
CFE strongly supports increased data sharing and the extension of the ‘open data’ policy to privately 
held data; implementation to the wider chemicals sector  would be beneficial to avoiding new tests 
performed on animals.  With this in mind, we support a general legal provision covering all EU 
chemical sectors setting out an exception from intellectual property rights protection  for use of all 
available information on chemicals by relevant Agencies and/or other EU bodies  in performance of 
their tasks.

Removing obstacles to the re-use of data  
The OECD recommendation 5 concerning access to chemical data has been recently amended to 
explicitly recognise the value that such access has to “the goal of reducing animal testing, including by 
avoiding duplicative testing, facilitating wider access to existing supporting data and by facilitating the 
development of non -animal based alternative technologies, through encouraging the exchange of 
proprietary health, safety and environmental test data and information ”. The rev ised 
Recommendation aims to balance access to chemical safety data with the protection of proprietary 
rights associated with that data, whilst recognising that openness and transparency is needed “so that 
the public can have … confidence in regulatory decisions regarding chemicals”.  
In addition to the abovementioned recommendation, a Best Practice Guide (BPG)6 is available which 
provides an overview of various approaches taken by governments, academia and industry to promote 
access to chemical safety data – including sharing data across governments and between companies. 
Also, we note that the OECD ‘Recommendation concerning the Exchange of Confidential Data on 
Chemicals’7 outlines principles for avoiding duplication of efforts in developing data on chemicals and 
to make better use of existing data, as well as for taking steps towards creating conditions allowing 
exchange of confidential data.  
We urge the Commission to support the implementation of these OECD Recommendations  when 
setting policies under the CSS and to continue to take an active role in updating the Best Practice 
Guide; we suggest that the findings from this part of the study are fed into the n ext update of the 
BPG. 
The following aspects, as covered by the BPG and which would contribute to the overall objectives of 
the abovementioned OECD Recommendations, should be reflected in the EU’s policies (emphasis 
added): 
• Disclosure of health, safety and environmental data to the maximum extent possible, while 
taking measures so as to not breach any proprietary rights or data exclusivity rules that may 
apply. 
• Implementation of effective mechanisms for the sharing of health, safety and environmental 
data between the data owner and a prospective purchaser, including industrial enti ties

Open data and transparency principles 
As of 27 March 2021, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) began carrying out its role as risk 
assessor in the EU food safety system under a  set of measures laid out in the new Transparency 
Regulation1 developed in response to an ECI on pesticides8 and the Fitness check of General Food Law 
regulation.9  
The aims of the new measures are to ensure the “highest transparency standards” and to “strengthen 
the reliability and transparency of scientific studies submitted to EFSA and reinforce governance of the 
Authority to ensure its long -term sustainability ” by enabling greater scrutiny of EFSA’s working 
processes and practices.10 
Among other things, the Transparency Regulation specifically aims to: 
• Allow open access to scientific studies and information submitted to EFSA by the registrants 
early in the process of risk assessment 
• Enable public consultations throughout the assessment process 
• Ensure that EFSA is notified of all commissioned studies to “ guarantee that companies 
applying for authorisations submit all relevant information”

Extending these principles to the remaining EU chemical sectors is a step in the right direction; it is 
important to ensure that there is transparency regarding the basis that risk management decisions 
are made upon and transparency regarding the uncertainties and shortcomin gs associated with the 
particular methodologies used (including the use of data from tests on animals). Also, the measures 
noted above – such as allowing open access to scientific studies and use of study data from academic 
literature – will save valuable resources by preventing unnecessary new data generation including 
through animal testing.  
 
EU and national authorities to commission testing (i.e., the ‘data generation mechanism’) 
The third key action calls for enabling the EU and national authorities to  commission the testing of 
chemical substances as part of a regulatory framework. This needs to be implemented carefully. We  
envisage that in certain scenarios, the ability for authorities to commission new tests could serve to 
reduce the total amount of new animal tests that might otherwise be conducted under the regulatory 
requirements currently in place; nevertheless, appropriate checks should be put in place  to ensure 
that any new tests on animals are only ever conducted as a last resort and there should be full 
transparency regarding which tests are being performed and why . One option could be to require 
that the details of any planned new test are published (e.g., in a Test Proposal or similar) prior to any 
new in vivo test being performed.  
The proposed changes to the CLP Regulation (Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008), 11 such as the 
introduction of new hazard classes and categories for endocrine disruptors,12 in combination with new 
information requirements under REACH,13 are already predicted to lead to a sharp increase in animal 
testing in the attempt to identify and classify chemical hazards. The suggested extension of the EU 
and national authorities’ mandate to commission testing under the CLP Regulation risks the entirely 
unfettered commissioning of new tests; for example, the Commission suggests the use of this

governance of a new data generation mechanism, the resulting system and the tests commissioned 
by EU and/or national authorities must support the transition toward a non -animal New Approach 
Methodology (NAM)-based chemicals regulatory system and away from outdated reliance on animal 
testing.  
 
Notification of commissioned studies 
To aid transparency, and where it can contribute to the avoidance of new animal testing, we support 
a notification system of planned new studies being put in place for the entire chemicals sector.  
 
EU and national authorities to commission monitoring of chemical substances 
Lack of high-quality, real-world exposure data is a significant issue for regulatory risk assessment of 
most chemicals in the EU today. 15 Open access to human and environmental monitoring data is an 
essential component to the wider use of innovative next generation risk assessment approaches that 
can harness real -world exposure measurements and estimations for supporting risk assessments, 
without recourse to animal testing.

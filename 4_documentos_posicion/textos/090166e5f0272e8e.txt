MOL Group’s position on the on the revision of the Waste Framework Directive (WFD) 
 
MOL Group is committed to helping the EU in reaching its circular economy objectives and is transforming 
to adapt to a circular and low -carbon world. Transition to a more circular economy requires significant 
investments, and EU waste rules can ensure regulatory certainty and increased incentives for investments 
in innovative solutions.  
Below we summarised our ke y points related to the stakeholder consultation that we believe will be 
crucial to achieve these objectives. 
 
1. WASTE REDUCTION  
Question d) – Barriers – It is difficult to answer the questionnaire in general about the barriers to “waste” 
prevention, as all waste streams have their different challenges and specificities when it comes to 
prevention.  
• “Prevention measures are not an explicit objective of commercial operations ”: in many 
industries eco-design and waste prevention initiatives are already in place, including a focus on 
durability and design -for-recycling. At the same time, in certain industries , more efforts are 
needed to improve durability and reduce overpackaging. 
• “Consumers are not used to taking prevention measures ”: most likely the issue is not about 
being used to it or their willingness, but the availability of repair and refurbishing services. 
• “Giving or selling products for reuse i s not convenient”: from a consumer point of view, we 
believe convenience is a key barrier to reuse. From a commercial point of view, it is not 
necessarily the convenience, but the practicality, hygiene and sustainability aspects of giving or 
selling products for reuse that might be a barrier. Therefore, a thorough life -cycle assessment 
needs to be carried out to prove that the caused environmental costs related to water, chemical, 
and energy use during reuse do not outweigh the benefits of a reusable product (e.g. packaging). 
• “Legal barriers  to waste prevention” : in the absence of EU -wide end -of-waste criteria, it is 
difficult to measure efforts on reuse, as reusable products often don’t appear as waste, and the 
preparation for reuse phase is not well captured in data collection.  
• “Ineffective cooperation between key players”: more cooperation would be needed along the 
value chain. The Circular Plastics Alliance (CPA) is a good example of such cooperation.  
 
Question e) – Existing EU measures – Overall, we believe that EU measures are in place to prevent waste, 
through the targets to increase reuse and recycling and to minimize landfilling of municipal solid waste. The 
development of a fully circular economy requires better implementation of these targets, to make sure that 
more waste is collected and directed towards reuse and recycling. National waste prevention measures and 
their effectiveness differ country by country, as we operate in multiple EU countries, we cannot comment.  
However, in terms of the support of advanced circular technologies like chemical recycling, more effective 
EU measures would be needed, including implementing rules on calculating the contribution of chemical 
recycling towards the EU recycling and recycled content targets.

Question f) – Possible EU measures – Waste prevention and its accepted forms are currently not well -
defined and need further elaboration in order to facilitate proper measures.  
Regarding w aste generation reduction targets : Before setting any legally binding waste generation 
reduction targets, we believe that a robust methodology is needed to identify the key sources of waste 
generation at the EU level. Rather than setting an EU -wide percentage reduction of current waste levels, 
measures should be differentiated based on  per capita performance of Member States compared to the 
European average. Moreover, we believe that sectoral targets and EPR schemes are much more efficient in 
reducing waste levels and incentivizing waste management than trying to address the generated waste per 
capita. It should be avoided that Member States are allowed different meas ures to achieve a certain level 
of waste reduction, as this could lead to diverging Member State approaches (e.g. bans of certain products 
and materials) and would harm the EU internal market. 
Regarding targets to increase reuse : In case considering  legally binding quantitati ve targets to increase 
reuse, methodology and guidance would have to be first established for measuring reuse, including End-of-
Waste criteria, that would clarify under what circumstances the preparation for reuse  is reported on and 
accepted and counting towards the targets. We believe that reuse targets should rather be set in sectoral 
legislation, e.g. packaging. 
 
2. FOOD WASTE 
Question g) – Benefits of reducing food waste – Plastic packaging is used in the food supply chain because 
it supports the safe distribution of food over long distances and minimises food waste by keeping food fresh 
for longer. By reducing food waste, it can significantly contribute to climate change mitigation. For example, 
the use of just 1.5g of plastic film for wrapping a cucumber can extend its shelf life from three days to 14 
days and selling grapes in plastic bags or trays has reduced in-store wastage of grapes by 20%. 
Reducing food waste can reduce costs for waste management, as currently a lot of food waste ends up in 
mixed municipal solid waste. Once the separate collection of bio -waste will become mandatory in Europe 
and will be efficiently organized, the reduction of food waste can still reduce costs as the treatment in 
biogas plants will depend on multiple types of feedstock sources. Increasing food waste feedstock will not 
be sufficient on its own. 
Question i) – Challenges – We believe it is very important to educate consumers , for them to adopt new 
habits in order to reduce food waste. The solution does not have to be about the reduction of food choices, 
but rather the decision of the individual to choose only the necessary amount of products. 
Question j) – Possible initiatives – In case considering food waste reduction targets, we believe this should 
not be set at an EU level, but rather by individual business operators, that can base such targets on 
individual performance measurement. The most efficient measures in retail operations are e.g. that only 
half a portion of a meal can be ordered in a restaurant on service stations, that sandwiches are packed in 
different sizes (e.g. small, medium, large), food donation possibility etc. Regarding fiscal incentives, allowing 
the businesses to offer products on shelves after the “best before” date would significantly contribute to 
the reduction of food waste.

3. SEPARATE COLLECTION 
Question n) – Existing EU measures – We believe that the EU has put in place relevant recycling targets 
for 2025, 2030 and 2035 to incentivize the separate collection of waste.  Further guidance on minimum 
requirements and some technical rules on measurement could be perhaps developed, however, what is 
more important is to implement the targets at national level taking into account the local circumstances. 
We operate in multiple EU countries. National separate collection systems and their effectiveness differ 
country by country, we cannot comment. 
 
Waste oils  – Possible EU measures – While the questionnaire asks individuals and households about  
habits regarding lubricant waste oil collection  and management, we would like to add a comment on 
policy measures regarding this waste stream. The collection of waste oils should be supported with further 
EU measures to increase the quantity as well as quality of collected waste oils.  On the quantity, it is 
important that waste oils get collected by incentivising small users to collect and take back their waste 
oils, so that after collection they can be treated in recycling or regeneration operations, and to avoid that 
these streams end up incinerated or leaking into the environment.  
On the quality of the collected waste oils, information provision should be mandatory  as well as certain 
quality standards, as it influences the subsequent treatment options . Firstly, waste codes (EWC) are too 
diverse and not user friendly, they do not help the proper separate collection and should be 
narrowed/rationalised. Simplification and education would be needed for users to be able to collect and 
report their waste properly, as well as s ome indication on labels about separate collection instructions 
could be useful. Secondly, market-based incentives should be considered to make sure that the quality of 
collected waste oils is not downgraded during collection. For example, rapid quality tests would be needed 
that allow the quick examination of waste oil quality already at the moment of takeover. This could 
disincentivize large users from degrading quality (e.g. with water) for the sake of increasing quantities. In 
product fee systems, repayment of product fees could be linked to quality.

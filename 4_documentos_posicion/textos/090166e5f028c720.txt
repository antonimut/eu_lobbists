Accompanying document to the EuroCommerce 
response to the consultation on the revision of the 
Waste Framework Directive 
 
Our sector welcomes the planned revision of the Waste Framework Directive (WFD). Retailers and 
wholesalers support the circular economy and work towards reducing waste in general, from 
packaging and food waste to other waste streams like textiles and waste  from electrical and 
electronic equipment. With the revision of the WFD there is a big opportunity to make a positive 
impact on climate and resource use by adopting circular business models and create a real Single 
Market for Secondary Raw Materials. In or der to be able to use more recycled content in new 
products, our members and their suppliers depend on a steady supply of secondary raw materials in 
sufficient quality and volume. 
 
This statement aims to summarise key points from the retail and wholesale s ector about the Waste 
Framework Directive and to provide feedback about the issues and measures mentioned in the 
public consultation to support the Commission’s work in revising the Waste Framework Directive: 
 
• The Commission should use the revision to give  incentives for and to remove regulatory barriers 
that hinder the Circular Economy.  
• One needs to keep in mind that food and non -food products are not the same and different 
measures and actions should be applied to each category. Issues like reuse or remanufacturing 
are mainly relevant for non -food products and the issue of how to reduce the amount of food 
waste need to be considered separately. 
• The Commission should pursue harmonisation and improved interoperability of waste legislation 
in general and in particular of all elements of Extended Producer Responsibility schemes (scope 
definition, reporting, eco-modulation etc). 
• Make sure that new Extended Producer Responsibility schemes for new waste streams like 
textiles are harmonised at the EU level from the beginning and share common principles and 
definitions. 
• Ensure coherence of the Waste Framework Directive with all legislative proposals related to 
products and waste, in particular the Sustainable Products Initiative/Eco -design for Sustainable 
Products Regulation proposal, the Waste Shipment Regulation and the Packaging and Packaging 
Waste Directive.

statement itself i s very general and needs clarification. What exactly is meant here with the term 
waste prevention? In our view waste prevention could take place at every stage of the product’s life 
cycle – for example the (re)design phase of a product or later when a product’s life is prolonged 
through reuse t o delay it becoming waste.  In addition, f or our sector we can say that there is a 
difference between food and non-food products. For perishable food products waste prevention can

In regard to the barrier “Loss of convenience for consumers” we would also comment that the 
question is not clear. From a business side we can report that companies are actively developing 
circular business models that are attractive for consumers and do not correspond with a loss of 
convenience. However, we can imagine that from the perspective of a consumer a (temporary) loss 
of convenience could occur because habits and behaviour might need to change. 
 
In the context of barrier “Ineffective cooperation between key players (e.g., waste collectors and 
reuse centres)”, this is a key challenge hamperi ng the implementation of projects on sorting and 
recycling of waste throughout the supply chain because vertical agreements have to fulfil many legal 
requirements, representing a hurdle against cooperation on such issues among supply chain actors. 
This is one reason why ineffective cooperation exists. 
 
In addition to the barriers listed in question d we would like to mention the following barriers that 
hamper waste reduction efforts: 
 
• No EU-wide end-of-waste criteria for key waste streams like textile waste. 
• On food waste:  
− missing policy enablers on key issues like support for awareness raising campaigns for 
consumers,  
− amending legislation according to new purchasing behaviour such as frozen food 
legislation to adapt to online purchases,  
− limit the administrative requirements to facilitate food donations,  
− support the implementation of policy and fiscal incentives to promote further food 
donations, like VAT reductions. 
• For textiles waste: 
− A key barrier is the lack of mainstream capabilities/ infrastructure to separately collect 
textiles. Finance and investment are needed as well as guidance to achieve high levels of 
separate collection of textile waste. 
− Lack of mainstream capabilities for sorting and preparation for recycling of textiles.  
Maturing fiber sorting and pre -processing is critical to scale the recycling of post -
consumer waste. 
− Research and funding for innovation is needed for both automatic fiber composition 
sorting and textile to textile recycling technologies. 
− Limited data availability on collection, reusing and recycling rates. 
− There are no common and harmonized rules of EPR for textiles in Europe and the 
European textile collection and sorting market is fragmented today. 
− Lack of scalability for circular business models, such as resale,  repair, rental. Today 
giving/donating garments as a consumer is easy but selling products for reuse is not as 
convenient yet as the industry is aiming for. Economic incentives to scale those are 
needed.

Regarding the statement “ I am in favour of legally binding waste generation reduction targets ” we 
would like to comment that we choose to answer neither agree nor disagree to express that targets 
alone will not achieve the objectives. In this context, business need rather clarification in the revision 
of the Waste Framework Directive what is underst ood as ‘waste prevention’, otherwise there is a 
risk of fragmentation of measures when Member States can implement differing measures as part of 
waste prevention. 
 
In Regard to the statement “I am in favour of legally binding quantitative targets to increase reuse of 
products”, we recommend neither agree nor disagree to express that reuse of products is very 
product-specific and there is no one -size-fits all solution. Establishing legally binding quantitative 
targets for reuse can therefore risk creating un intended behaviours or contradicting targets. For 
example, reusability relies mostly on consumer behaviour. Engagement with and incentive for 
consumers are required to foster consumer behaviour  that facilitates reuse. Binding targets will not 
necessarily lead to a change in consumer behaviour.  
 
In general, we view EPR schemes are a good way to implement economic incentives for waste 
management. They have the potential to offer cost -effective ways to fulfil the obligations, but for 
them to work  properly we need effective implementation and enforcement. Harmonization of EPR 
schemes is being supported by retailers and wholesalers as well as better enforcement and 
cooperation between authorities like networks for authorities and Producer Responsibi lity 
Organizations to exchange information and best practices, more controls and a central website like 
the single digital gateway (one -stop-shop-approach) to simply compliance for companies. In cases 
where eco-modulation of fees is considered, to gain the  environmental benefit of such, criteria for 
eco-modulation must be harmonised on EU level – and aligned with the anticipated product design 
criteria under the Eco-design for Sustainable Product’s Regulation. 
 
On the broader topic of circular economy and w aste prevention we would like to highlight that the 
revision of the Waste Framework Directive must be aligned with EU’s ambition in the Circular 
Economy Action Plan and not least the Eco -design for Sustainable Products Regulation Proposal. 
Circularity and waste reduction takes place already in the design phase of a product and therefore 
coherence between different legislation in this area is needed, as this is not alone a waste -related 
issue.  
 
On binding targets for food waste reduction, we  want to have a harmonized approach that is 
keeping the flexibility to adopt to local needs, includes the whole supply chain and aligns with the 
baseline of the UN SDGs.    
 
On question i on food waste 
Regarding the challenge “Ensuring no compromise on foo d safety” that is listed as part of question i 
we would like to comment that it is very important to ensure food safety. For retailers and 
wholesalers offering food products to consumers this is not a challenge , as they always ensure food 
safety in their operations. We would like to highlight that there should be no trade-off between food 
safety and food waste reduction measures, there should be a consistent approach to both important 
issues and no prioritisation of one above the other.

Regarding the statement “Extended producer responsibility should also cover waste prevention 
activities” in addition to waste management costs, we request further clarity. What exactly is meant 
with waste prevention activities in this context and how would they interact with the core task of 
Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) schemes, the fees and the financing of the system? Retailers 
and wholesalers support EPR as an effective instrument to ensure the management  of the end -of-
life of a product. While waste prevention can be one of the benefits of an EPR scheme, usually EPR 
schemes focus on waste collection, recycling capacity and infrastructure – thus dealing with 
products that are discarded and considered waste.  
 
In addition, in some cases, like for EPR for textiles and garments there could be a conflict of 
objectives. Some textile waste collected through EPR schemes that is in the condition to be fit for 
reuse, should be sorted out and resold for these purposes . For the non-reusable fraction only fit for 
recycling, today the needed recycling capacity is not sufficiently available yet. To be able to be 
recycled, a large effort on recycling capacity is urgently needed. While in this case the EPR should 
not dismiss the opportunity to contribute to facilitate reuse of collected textiles, the main focus of 
EPR fees should be to finance the needed sorting and textile -to -textile recycling innovations and 
capacities in order to treat the large volumes of textile waste available in the EU in the next years.

Revision of the Waste Framework Directive  
Position Paper of Environmental Action 
Germany 
DUH welcomes the possibility to further comment on the revision of the Waste Framework Directive (WFD) as an 
opportunity to promote waste prevention and re-use over waste recovery and disposal. In addition to our answers 
provided within the questionnaire, we would like to further point out some of the areas where we see the need for 
reform in the upcoming revision.  
Those include, among others: 
- The need to introduce ambitious targets along the waste hierarchy, regarding waste reduction, reuse and 
recycling 
- Expansion of the role of Extended Producer Responsibility  (EPR) schemes, to further product categories  
and improvements regarding their enforcement 
- Introducing minimum requirements for source segregation and separate collection of waste 
- Improving the circularity of crucial waste streams, such as construction & demolition waste (CDW) and bio 
waste 
In this position paper, we would like to comment on several policy options and make further suggestions regard-
ing changes in the WFD. A small number of propositions also concern other legislative acts, such as the Packaging 
and Packaging Waste Directive (PPWD), but were added for reasons of comprehensiveness.

1. Ambitious targets along the waste hierarchy 
1.1. Targets on waste reduction 
To minimize the ecological impact of products and waste overall, the generation of waste has to be reduced. There-
fore, the definition of verifiable, ambitious quantitative and binding waste reduction targets and concrete deadlines 
for their transposition is inevitable. 
Therefore, an overarching waste reduction  target (or waste prevention target) is necessary, as well as reduction 
targets specific to key waste streams. 
1.1.1. Waste reduction targets for residual waste 
 150 kg per capita and year by 2027  
 120 kg per capita and year by 2030  
Furthermore, the bio-waste content in residual waste should be capped: 
o Max. 25% bio-waste content in residual waste by 2030  
o Max. 15% bio-waste content in residual waste by 2035 
1.1.2. Waste reduction targets for packaging waste 
 120 kg per capita and year by 2027  
 90 kg per capita and year by 2030  
1.1.3. Waste reduction targets for bulky waste 
 140 kg per capita and year by 2027  
 90 kg per capita and year by 2030 
 
In order to measure waste reduction regarding bulky waste, a legal definition of bulky waste 
needs to be introduced within the WFD. 
1.1.4. Waste reduction in the construction and demolition sector 
Construction and demolition waste is the largest waste stream by mass and is responsible for a 
high level of resource consumption and greenhouse gas emissions. It is therefore necessary to 
ensure that waste is avoided as much as possible, for example, to reduce unnecessary transport 
emissions. One possibility is to prioritize renovation over demolition and building of new build-
ings, or to use excavated soil directly on site before it reaches waste status. Furthermore, an obli-
gation of a pre-demolition audit to identify reusable components prevents those from becoming 
waste would be an effective measure to reduce waste and use valuable materials. The Commis-
sion should call for and promote such and similar measures, if possible, within the framework of 
the WFD.

1.1.5. Waste reduction targets for food waste 
 Legally binding targets for EU member states to achieve an aggregated 50% food loss and 
waste reduction by 2030  
 Covering all food loss and waste from farm to fork (including primary production, processing, 
hospitality, retail and household sectors) 
DUH calls upon the European Commission to establish a legally binding target from farm to fork for EU 
member states to jointly achieve a 50% food loss and waste reduction by 2030. According to the F2F strat-
egy, the European Commission is committed to achie ving food waste reduction levels in line with Sustain-
able Development Goal (SDG) 12.3. A target of less than 50% would violate EU commitments to meet SDG 
12.3. 
Champions 12.3, an international coalition of key governments, businesses, and civil society actors leading 
global food waste action, suggests that committed parties to the SDGs should interpret the target as a 50% 
reduction of all food loss and waste from farm to fork, starting with the primary production level. Indeed, 
we believe it is essential for the targets to cover the whole food value chain, not just retail and consumers, 
as food waste indeed occurs at all stages of production, distribution and consumption. 
Binding reduction targets should thus be set for all sectors along the value chain, covering primary produc-
tion, processing, hospitality, retail and household sectors. At the primary production level, this will require 
an inclusion also of unharvested food as well as animal deaths during rearing. Currently, the suppleme nt 
legislation to Directive 2008/93/EC by definition excludes much of the food waste that occurs at the primary 
production level.  
Covering only consumer and retail food waste would exclude most food waste in the EU from the scope of 
the binding targets. A ccording to a WWF report (2021), 60 percent of global food waste and loss is esti-
mated to already occur during primary producing. If targets were set merely at the end of the value chain, 
end consumers would be falsely held accountable for food waste, even though large food companies have 
a much larger impact. It may also lead to food waste being shifted from one level to another.  
In order to successfully monitor the target achievement, data availability on food waste and loss at the 
primary production level needs to be improved. Currently, there is no comprehensive database available in 
the EU, which however will be necessary to make the correct baseline assumptions.  
Binding reduction targets are urgently needed, as voluntary agreements have limited effec tiveness. The 
experience in Germany shows, that even when responsible companies sign agreements, a lack of control 
and accountability mechanisms for compliance hinders the process. Often, company data on food waste is 
not published, which makes monitoring even more difficult. This slows down the reduction of food waste.

1.2. Mandatory reuse targets 
While the introduction of mandatory targets  for the ‘preparation for reuse and recycling’  did prove effective, a 
specific mandatory reuse target is lacking up to this point. Seeing that the already implemented targets do have a 
strong focus on recycling and therefore do not effectively limit waste production at its source  and do not stop a 
product from becoming waste , it is important to introduce quantitative targe ts regarding reuse. This could also 
reduce CO2 emissions and support job creation. 
To better implement the waste hierarchy and its higher stages, in addition to mandatory waste reduction targets, 
mandatory reuse targets are necessary  and should be introduc ed for certain waste streams . Some of these could 
be regulated in other directives, such as the PPWD.  
1.2.1. Reuse targets for textiles  
 at least 10% by 2027  
 at least 15% by 2030  
1.2.2. Reuse targets for bulky waste  
 at least 10% by 2027  
 at least 15% by 2030  
If further, more longterm reuse targets for textiles and bulky waste are introduced, they should aim  even higher 
than the targets proposed here, since the reuse potential within these waste streams is high and massively under-
exploited. 
1.2.3. Reuse targets for packaging 
 Reuse targets for sales/shipment/transport and beverage should increase by 
1)  At least 15% by 2025 and 
2) 30% by 2030 in relation to 2018 
 In Germany, there is a reuse target of 70% for beverage packaging. The long-term goal should 
be to make this the norm in the EU. An ambitious goal regarding a long timeframe would con-
tribute to a steady switch to refillable beverage packaging. At the same time, this would mini-
mize any economic strain being placed upon the industry since single-use machinery (manu-
facturing and bottling) could mostly be replaced once it reaches the end of its lifetime after 
approximately 15-20 years. 
1) 70% reuse target for beverage packaging in the EU, long-term goal, allowing for more 
ambitious goals within the member states 
 Beyond beverage packaging, a reuse target of 25% by 2025 and 40% by 2030 should be set for 
sales packaging 
 For transport packaging, a reuse rate of 70% should apply from 2025 
1.2.4. Reuse targets for construction material 
Furthermore, an assessment of a potential reuse targets for construction material, construction 
and demolition waste should take place.

1.3. Recycling targets and definitions 
The introduction of targets for the preparation of re-use and recycling with in the WFD has been a necessary and 
valuable step towards a more circular Europe. The revised WFD should further strengthen this success by improving 
the targets themselves as well as the definitions used to calculate the recycling rates in the member states. 
1.3.1. Preparing for re-use and the recycling of municipal waste 
 At least 70% by 2030 
1.3.2. Preparing for re-use and the recycling of packaging waste 
 At least 80% by 2030 
Furthermore, the Commission shall propose a separate and ambitious recycling target for the following 
waste streams: 
 Plastic 
Specific recycling quotas for plastics are necessary, because there is great potential for im-
provement in terms of recycling not only in the area of packaging, but also for other products 
made of plastic, for example in construction, mobility or electronics 
 Material-specific recycling targets a for construction material, construction and demolition 
waste (CDW) 
Furthermore, backfilling should be excluded from the recycling rate calculation of construc-
tion and demolition waste Article 11 (2. (b)). Backfilling of construction and demolition waste 
should not be implemented in applications without the CDW going through recycling treat-
ment first. 
The definitions in Article 3 of the WFD need to be addressed in order to further ensure that recycling does repre-
sent technologies that lead to reduced environmental harm. 
» A revised definition of “recycling” must ensure that only usable and safe materials with sufficient quality 
that originates demonstrably from a post-consumer waste fraction is defined as “recycled”. Calculation 
methodologies must account for all additives, solvents, losses and chemical reactions within recycling 
processes and process products that are incinerated must be clearly expelled from these calculation. In 
the case of plastic recycling, only plastic-to-plastic recycling should contribute to recycling targets to avoid 
downcycling and truly closing the loop. 
» Chemical “recycling” should be defined as chemical “recovery”. Only mechanical recycling and chemical 
recycling in which plastic is not broken down to molecular level is acceptable as “recycling”. In this sense, 
thermal decomposition such as pyrolysis or gasification must not contribute to recycling targets or recy-
cled content. See here a position paper and analysis of environmental outcomes. 
» DUH opposes allowing the calculation of “recycled waste” via mass balance approach. The current calcu-
lation method proposed techniques such as chemical recycling/recovery, which is not reliable and may 
promote false developments and greenwashing. It allows the marketing of products as “recycled” even 
though they do not contain any recyclates. Traceability of recycled materials is important for the credibil-
ity of recycling systems, especially when virtual trade between plastic types is allowed. Instead DUH pro-
poses a batch level mass balance approach. 
» Composting and fermentation of packaging made from bioplastics should be considered as disposal, 
since mainly carbon dioxide, methane and water are generated. To fulfil the waste hierarchy, plastics, 
should preferably be designed for long durability and (mechanical) recycling. If bioplastics are recycled, 
recycled products must be usable polymers for common plastic applications.

2. Expansion of the role of EPR schemes 
To further strengthen waste prevention, it is necessary to implement regulation into existing or new EPR schemes, 
that provide incentives to prevent waste generation, be  it by influencing product design for better reusability, re-
pairability or recyclability, or by setting economic incentives to reduce use of resources.  
To ensure the effectiveness of EPR schemes regarding waste reduction and reuse, it is important that their applica-
tion is not limited to the later stages of the waste hierarchy, focusing on waste management and recycling. This can 
be done by introducing an ecomodulation of fees, as well as setting fees high enough to not only allow for adequate 
funding of separate collection and high-quality recycling processes, but to also raise costs on the use of virgin ma-
terials, which gives recycled materials a relative advantage. Furthermore, the acquired funds can be used to fund 
the development of measures of waste prevention or support (the development of) reuse systems. 
Also, a duty of care should be introduced into the WFD. Duty of care is a form of product responsibility and has 
recently been introduced to German law (“Obhutspflicht”). It applies to all products,  covers their distribution - in-
cluding their transport and storage - and requires the maintenance of the usability of the product. It prioritizes 
waste avoidance over disposal as waste. This includes the obligation for the responsible party, such as a prod ucer 
of electronic goods or textiles, to not turn a product into waste by a volitional decision without necessity. One 
exemplary application of this principle is a ban on the destruction of returned goods in online retailing. 
To further incentivize manufac turers to respect the waste hierarchy and reflect the ecological follow -up costs of 
many products, EPR schemes need to be expanded to further product categories. 
This includes: 
 Textiles 
Consumption of textiles, especially clothing, is ever increasing,  hugely contributing to raw material use 
and greenhouse gas emissions, chemical and water pollution. Specific targets regarding reuse of these 
products will not suffice to incentivize manufacturers to produce in a more environmentally friendly way. 
Hence, the introduction of EPR schemes is necessary. 
 Products that become bulky waste, such as furniture, mattresses, fixtures, upholstery and carpets 
According to URBANREC, about 60% of bulky waste is sent to landfills across the EU. Most of the remain-
ing waste is either burned or recycled, although the potential for reuse is rather high. EPR schemes placed 
upon important products within this waste stream in combination with the aforementioned measures 
(such as ecomodulation of fees) , could lead to a significant reduction of waste generation, greenhouse 
gas emissions and resource consumption. 
 Building materials  
The introduction of a  mandatory EPR scheme for construction materials is more than overdue, due to 
the environmental impacts of such materials on the climate and environment. We suggest an introduc-
tion of EPR for construction material similar to the EU-wide approaches for batteries, packaging and elec-
trical appliances or the French EPR scheme for construction materials starting 2023. Extended producer 
responsibilities for construction materials would increase collection rates and ensure a disposal free of 
charge for building owners. Thus, building owners are not drawn to low -cost landfilling or incineration. 
In addition, effective measures such as a mandatory take-back system for properly collected construction 
cut-offs can be introduced, as such materials can often be included directly into the production process. 
An EPR scheme could therefore ensure that essential measures are taken to create a sufficient circularity 
for construction materials.  
Additional measures on waste prevention for construction material could be to oblige manufacturers of 
building materials to provide evidence of recyclability and their pollutant-free status, specifying the per-
centage of products that can be recycled (a) as clean offcuts or construction site waste and (b) from 
dismantling or refurbishment. 
To improve the enforcement of EPR requirements, it is especially important to consider the role online -market-
places play in distributing products . It is important to require online -marketplaces to assume responsibility in 
meeting the obligations of EPR schemes.

3. Introducing minimum requirements for source segregation and separate collection 
The separate collection of waste is a pre requisite for high-quality recycling, preparation for reuse and reuse  itself. 
It also prevents hazardous substances from contaminating oth er waste streams as well as com munities and the 
environment. 
3.1. Collection points/ ‘recycling centres’  
In order t o improve the prevention and collection of municipal waste, DUH proposes to extend re gulations for 
collection points (e.g. recycling centres) to ensure a certain level of quality. Collection points must be obliged to 
separate certain amounts of delivered goods (e.g. bulky waste,  waste of electrical and electronic equipment  
(WEEE)) for reuse. For furniture, textiles, shoes, household goods, WEEE, toys, books, bikes etc., collection points 
must form co -operations with reuse initiatives or offer on -site sharing spaces for free takeaway. They must also 
care for appropriate collection and storag e of reusable goods. When arriving at collection points, the consumer 
must be given the chance to separate intact goods for reuse. In addition, employees at collection points must collect 
reusables from the delivered goods on their own initiative. 
3.2. Close-to-home sharing systems and promotion of reuse business models 
Close-to-home sharing systems (free drop-off and takeaway) for small reusables should be established legally pro-
moting the reuse of textiles, household goods, b ooks, shoes, toys etc. These may be reuse boxes, shelves in town 
halls, public buildings, shops etc. These systems must be supervised by professionals, so that they remain clean and 
are protected from theft.  Also, municipalities must be supported and encou raged to operate second hand stores 
where collected goods, e.g. from collection points, can be examined, repaired and sold. Overall, the EU should set 
incentives to business models to promote reuse, sharing or lending. This can be applied to many material streams. 
3.3. Bulky waste 
Despite a universal agreement on the waste hierarchy, the principle of reuse is still not implemented sufficiently, 
especially in the case of bulky waste. To ensure that more products are used and reused for a longer time period, 
non-destructive collection of bulky waste must be mandatory. Collected end -of-life products must be refurbished 
or repaired. Mandatory tests to determine whether it is technically possible and economically reasonable (including 
if there is a market) must be put  in place. Exceptions to the obligation to collect and reuse non -destructively may 
only be permitted if reuse can be ruled out for technical reasons and this can be documented and recorded by 
authorities. Best-practices already show how preparing for reuse and subsequent sale of still-functioning goods in 
second-hand stores can succeed and, in addition to climate savings, also contributes to the creation of green jobs.  
3.4. Separate collection of demolition waste 
To enable recycling in the first place, material s need to be separated sufficiently in the demolition process. Thus, 
the controls of obligations for separation of demolition waste must be increased and enforced. Furthermore, it is 
important to expand the separation obligations for relevant product groups according to material classes, such as 
insulation materials. , in order to allow recycling of par ticular insulation materials, e.g., stone  wool, EPS, XPS, PU 
etc.

4. Improving the circularity of crucial waste streams 
4.1. Construction & Demolition Waste 
The construction industry is booming, which is creating great challenges in the area of environmental and resource 
protection. This is increasingly catching the attention of policy -makers, who have so far concentrated primarily on 
energy-efficiency in buildings. Measures in the construction sector, however, need to be addressed along the whole 
life cycle to lift the full potential. Thus, we would like to suggest the following: 
» Mandatory pre-demolition audit to every deconstruction and major renovation 
A compulsory inspection of the building and components prior to every deconstruction and major renova-
tion project should be introduced. Before deconstruction can begin, hazardous material s should be re-
moved. Thus, an effective deconstruction can take place to avoid preventable waste and enable reuse and 
high quality recycling in the first place. Common EU guidelines such as the “Guidelines for the waste audits 
before demolition and renovation works of buildings” should be set as minimal standard.  
» Specify End-of-Life criteria 
A detailed specification of the End-of-Life criteria for construction materials is important to clarify managing 
reusable and recycled materials for every actor in the  supply chain. It will also increase the acceptance of 
secondary or reusable products, due to applicable definitions as “products” and not as “waste”. 
 
 
4.2. Bio-Waste 
With a share of 34% - 86 million tons in absolute terms in the EU-28 in 2017 – bio-waste makes up the largest single 
fraction in municipal waste in the EU ( European Environment Agency (2020): Bio -waste in Europe ). High-quality 
recycling of bio-waste is therefore key to the transition to a circular economy and to achieving the recycling target 
for municipal waste of 65 % by 2035. If the amount of residual municipal waste is to be halved by 2030, according 
to CEAP, this can only be achieved with consistent separate collection of bio-waste. 
Recent developments regarding the war in Ukraine have also shown, that the EU has to become independent from 
fossil gas as quickly as possible. Treating bio-waste (not crops planted intentionally for this measure) as a source of 
bio-gas could alleviate some of the need to use foreign gas. Recent estimations from Germany’s waste management 
industry indicate a fossil gas saving potential of 2% if bio-waste is collected and fermented properly in Germany 
(Source: https://www.biogas.org/edcom/webfvb.nsf/id/DE-Pressemitteilung-zur-IFAT-2022/$file/22-05-
30__PM_Ifat.pdf). 
» Implementing separate collection, strict interpretation of exemptions  
Separate collection obligations from 2024 must be implemented consistently in order to leverage climate protec-
tion potentials of high-quality fermentation and composting of bio-waste. Exemptions according to Article 10 (3 c), 
d)) regarding technical feasibility and economic costs of separate collection should only be a rarity. Technical feasi-
bility has been tested and established with existing composting and fermentation processes for years. Likewise, 
economic feasibility is given as confirmed by numerous studies (Bourka et al., 2015; Rodrigues et al., 2015; Niskanen 
and Kemppi, 2019). Derogations should require an independent third-party scientific assessment. 
» Mandatory kerbside collection system for higher collection rates  
Consumer-friendly design of bio -waste collection is crucial for high collection rates. Experience from Germany 
clearly shows that only the provision of bio -bins achieves significant collection volumes (over 20  kg per capita). If 
consumers have to take their bio -waste to collection points , e.g far off, only a small fraction of the bio -waste is 
collected. In addition, it has been proven that consumers predominantly use their car for those disposal ways, which 
contradicts climate protection mea sures. This is why, a kerbside collection system at least for kitchen and food 
waste shall be made mandatory within the WFD, certain derogations as outlined in Art icle 10 (3) can be applied in 
exceptional cases. Additional collection poin ts can be a useful addition for  biodegradable garden and park waste, 
but do not act as a replacement.

» Exclusion of biodegradable plastics from bio-waste  
The second sentence of Article 22 (1) should be deleted to ensure that no bioplastics are composted or fermented 
during organic waste processing. Composting bioplastics a) may rise problems in treatment facilities b) promote 
release of particulate plastics and pollutants into the environment and c) is not environmentally preferable. 
» Requirements for home composting  
The encouragement of home composting (Article 22 (2 b)) must be subject to certain preconditions, e.g. proof of a 
minimum size of the garden, which needs to be controlled by municipal waste disposal companies to avoid over-
fertilization. Th e Federal Environment Agency in Germany recommends a necessary minimum size of seventy 
square meters. A relevant part of kitchen waste is not suitable for home composting (e.g. meat, citrus fruits, bones), 
making bio-waste bins also necessary for home composting households. 
» Capping the bio-waste content in residual waste  
In addition to mandatory separate collection, further incentives are needed to increase bio -waste collection vol-
umes and to prevent incineration and landfilling. We propose the introduction of targets (see above, 1.1.1). Mem-
ber states need to report these figures together with the figures on recycling rates to the EU Commission.

MTK’s feedback on the proposal for a regulation on nature restoration 
 
 
The Central Union of Agricultural Producers and Forest Owners (MTK) thanks for  the opportunity 
to give feedback on the proposal for a regulation on nature restoration. 
 
As stated at the time of publication of the EU Biodiversity Strategy for 2030, MTK supports the 
main goal of the Strategy, that is halting the loss of biodiversity. MTK considers it important to solve 
biodiversity challenges also at the EU level and thus, MTK has welcomed the Strategy. The Strat-
egy highlights the need to widen the network of protected areas and to step up efforts to restore 
nature. MTK shares the opinion that these two actions are central in safeguarding and enhancing 
biodiversity: best practices in agriculture and forest management, well -targeted nature manage-
ment and restoration, and landowner -based voluntary nature conservation are the key elements 
in MTK’s own Biodiversity Programme. 
 
However, since the adoption of the European Green Deal, MTK has been concerned about the 
narrowness of aspects related to agriculture, forestry and bioeconomy  in the different EU pro-
cesses. Concern is also justified regarding the proposal for a regulation on restoration, as agricul-
ture and forestry are mainly seen as activities that must be controlled and restricted by strict legis-
lation. This is problematic since agriculture and forestry play a crucial role in achieving many of the 
goals of sustainable development and the Green Deal. Food production and s ustainable use of 
renewable natural resources  are a central source of  well-being, and from this point of view the 
restrictive legislation is neither realistic nor acceptable. 
 
Agriculture and forestry are subject to a wide range of needs and demands, from the point of view 
of both nature and society. The promotion of biodiversity – including also restoration – must be 
balanced with other environmental goals (e.g. climate change mitigation) and societal needs (e.g. 
food security, energy availability), especially during these uncertain times. In addition, MTK con-
tinues to emphasise that measures aiming at improving the condition of nature must also be eco-
nomically and socially sustainable. For this reason, MTK has constantly demanded that the differ-
ent implementation actions that are needed to achieve the objectives of the EU Biodiversity Strat-
egy must take place at the level of the Member States to ensure proper targeting and prioritisation, 
cost-effectiveness,  and full consideration of the rights of landowners. If restoration was based on 
a regulation, there would not be sufficient possibilities for the Member States to tailor the principles 
(e.g. restoration based on voluntariness vs. restoration required by law) or the actual restoration 
measures based on their national needs. Decision-making at the level of Member States is clearly 
a justified principle in the context of  restoration since it also reflects the principles of subsidiarity 
and proportionality. 
 
The European Commission has proposed a regulation that would have very significant impacts on 
areas currently used for sustainable agriculture and forestry. It is certain that implementation of the 
legislation would not happen without consequences on e.g.  food and energy security and rural 
livelihoods. The vast areal coverage, the very high ambition level in the targets from the viewpoint 
of both quantity and quality, and very optimistic timeframes all put together reflect the oversized 
setting of the proposal.

It is undeniable that r eaching the targets would demand unprecedented  effort  both in practice on 
the field  and financially. According to the impact assessment, the total costs for ecosystem resto-
ration in the Member States would be around 7.4 billion euros per year. The third largest absolute 
costs would be incurred in Finland (around 0.9 billion euros annually), and when annual costs are 
expressed as a share of GDP and per citizen, Finland is the one with the biggest burden (0.39 % 
and 168 euros / citizen). However, according to the impact assessment, the benefits of restoration 
would significantly outweigh the costs. Based on the information given in the impact assessment 
documents, MTK takes a cautious approach to the estimates presented by the Commission. MTK 
emphasises that achieving a considered and justified outcome requires that all impacts of the pro-
posal are evaluated thoroughly based on reliable information, taking into account all possible direct 
and indirect effects. So far, the estimation of costs and benefits seems to be based on assumptions 
and averages, and especially regarding the benefits  of restoration, the analysis seems to be quite 
theoretical.  
 
MTK identifies several articles that would be problematic from the point of view of Finnish agricul-
ture and forestry. When it comes to restoring and rewetting of drained peatland, MTK emphasises 
that peatlands are a significant and central part of the production potential of Finnish agriculture 
as they safeguard farming security at the farm level. Peatlands are unevenly distributed within the 
country, so regionally their importance for food pro duction is much greater than what could be 
thought based on the total area only. Financially, it’s going to result in significant economic losses 
or cost increase if a substantial amount of peatland is taken out of production or if the alternative 
production uses are unsuitable for Finnish conditions.  
 
Based on the general remarks mentioned above, MTK states that the proposal for a regulation on 
nature restoration is unacceptable. Significant amendments must be made not only to the articles 
but also to the principles expressed in the recitals. Any new legislation adopted must be realistic 
and balanced from the viewpoint of the three pillars of sustainability and the fulfilment of the diverse 
societal needs. The operating conditions of agriculture and forestry as well as the rights of land-
owners must be better acknowledged. To give concrete suggestions for improving the proposal, 
MTK points out the following: 
 
• Based on the content of the proposed regulation, the term "restoration" seems to refer  not 
only to actions aiming at natural state without human intervention , but also to other actions 
aimed at improving the ecological condition in different ecosystems . In Finland, g razing on 
semi-natural grasslands and leaving retention trees at the time of  harvesting operations in 
managed forests are examples of nature management that enables fulfilling  the needs of hu-
mans and nature  simultaneously. Multifunctional use in which diff erent objectives are taken 
into consideration on a case-by-case basis must be possible also in the future  since it is a 
precondition for  win-win solutions. EU legislation cannot lead to a situation where safeguard-
ing biodiversity would be considered possible only by restricting human activities. 
 
• Strengthening the possibilities for sustainable and profitable agriculture and forestry and re-
specting the rights of landowners must be central goals when discussing and deciding on any 
policy tools related to biodiversity . In its proposal the Commission is claiming that activities 
based on voluntariness are not sufficient or functional, and that is not true at least for Finland: 
The Finnish Helmi Habitats Programme focusing on voluntary-based nature management and 
restoration is an example of a national restoration plan that combines a landowner-respective 
approach and ecological functionality. Thus, from the very beginning of the discussions con-
cerning the EU Nature Restoration Plan, MTK has been of the opinion that the focus should 
be on strengthening different enabling measures instead of setting detailed legal obligations 
for r estoration. MTK has pointed out, and continues to do so, that there are possibilities to 
increase the EU added value through supporting the work done in the Member States by e.g.

providing better EU funding opportunities and enhancing knowledge sharing. Instead of legally 
binding restrictions and demands, the focus should be on positive incentives intensifying vol-
untary bottom-up participatory approaches, supporting the expansion of existing best prac-
tices, and encouraging the development of new business op portunities. These constructive 
approaches based on cooperation of multiple actors would create engagement leading to suc-
cessful outcomes. When looking at the proposal prepared by the Commission, the focus is 
surely not on supportive capacity building, but on strict control based on top-down thinking. 
 
• The EU's nature legislation is based on directives . Flexibility that comes with n ational imple-
mentation is a central element in a directive. This is not the case with a regulation since it must 
be applied in its entirety in all Member States . A regulation is not a suitable legislative act in 
the context of restoration since flexibility is needed to allow the recognition of every country’s 
precise needs, conditions, opportunities and starting points. Despite one element of the  pro-
posed regulation is a national restoration plan, the legislation does not leave enough room for 
national decisions for prioritising actions. Choosing a regulation over a directive cannot be 
justified by paying attention to the speed of the process only.  
 
• The EU does not have a common forest policy. Any new legislation must fully respect the 
Member States’ national competence related to forest policy.  
 
• Based on the principles of subsidiarity and proportionality , the proposed regulation can be 
questioned. It seems that in the end, very little is left for the Member States to decide despite 
national and local decision-making is highly necessary in the context of restoration. Targeting 
and prioritisation of restoration measures requires step by step thinkin g but due to the strict 
approach regarding the legally binding targets and the wide scope of the proposed regulation 
there is very little room for flexibility . When it comes to proportionality, the Commission's pro-
posal focuses on quantity instead of quality, regardless of costs and needs.  
 
• Reliable data is needed to achieve successful outcomes cost-effectively. However, the Com-
mission proposes that unknown condition shall be considered as not being in good condition. 
This is not acceptable as restoration must be based on real needs. Thus, filling knowledge 
gaps must be a priority.  
 
• Sites located within the existing protected area network should be prioritised in restoration to 
unlock the full potential of those areas. Targeting restoration measures to protected areas 
would help to avoid possible conflicting interests related to land use, and it would ensure that 
the benefits of nature restoration are lasting. In addition to protected areas, any new restora-
tion legislation must be limited to areas that are already covered by the current EU nature 
legislation. Areas used for productive agriculture and forestry must not be subject to any new 
restoration obligations or other requirements based on EU legislation.  
 
• Outside protected areas, integrated approaches combining both sustainable use of renewable 
natural resources and biodiversity protection are needed. However, it is not reasonable to set 
requirements related to precisely defined indicators for agricultural and forest ecosystems  at 
the EU level. The same indicators do not fit everywhere, and there is not yet the capacity to 
monitor at least some of the proposed indicators. In addition, the development of the indicator 
values would in many respects depend on the decisions taken by individual farmers and forest 
owners, and this decision-making power must remain with them in the future. From biodiver-
sity point of view, strengthening key structural features is very important, and to allow for mul-
tifunctional use of areas, it is necessary that the intensity of biodiversity protection varies be-
tween areas. Thus, variation is needed regarding the target levels of different indicators  not 
only between Member States but also between areas. This flexibility is best achieved when  
nature management activities are supported by incentive-based national policy tools.

• Peatland in sustainable agricultural and forestry use should not be categorically classified as 
needing restoration, even though the characteristics have often changed compared to a nat-
ural state. It must be possible to continue sustainable food, animal feed and wood production 
in high-yielding farmlands with a thin peat layer, and in peatland forests with good wood pro-
duction capacity. 
 
• According to the Commission's proposal, restored and rewetted peatlands can continue to be 
used productively in alternative ways. Cultivation of berries , sphagnum farming, and grazing 
with water buffaloes are mentioned as examples. Economic viability is a central prerequisite 
for production, so the options presented by the Commission are not necessarily realistic in 
practice. 
 
• Tendency to further strengthen the Commission's power over other EU institutions and Mem-
ber States is evident in the proposal. The Commission must not be given the power to adopt 
delegated acts. What is more, the Commission must not be given a dominant position regard-
ing the national restoration plans either. If there was a requirement to prepare national resto-
ration plans, the process must be such that the decision -making power on the content of the 
plan (what, where, when and how to restore) really rests with the Member States, not with the 
Commission. To fully embrace the country-specific character of the national restoration plans, 
it is highly necessary that the contents of the plans are not restricted by any EU level attributes 
based on top-down thinking. National restoration plans hold great potential for success if ver-
satility is understood as an asset. 
 
• The burden -sharing must be fair between Member States, sectors (agriculture and forestry 
vs. others , ecosystems (natural environments vs. urban environments) and different  groups 
of people (landowners vs. others).  
 
• When evaluating costs and benefits in monetary values and making decisions based on them, 
it must be recognised that the costs resulting from restrictions  are certain and inevitable, 
whereas the benefits are mostly calculatory or at least they do not necessarily turn into actual 
cash flow. The fact that there is no market for many of the benefits highlighted by the Com-
mission means that the benefits will not be realised in practice. In addition, the temporal dif-
ference between benefits and costs can be significant. Also, the individuals or groups of peo-
ple who receive benefits and those  who suffer costs can be at least partially different.  It is 
unfair and harmful in terms of the acceptability of biodiversity protection general, if farmers or 
forest owner s must bear the financial loss caused by restricting their livelihoods  while the 
potential benefit s go to others without their contribution. 
 
• The impact of all proposals must be thoroughly assessed based on reliable information, taking 
into account both direct and indirect effects. Special attention should be paid to the fact that  
there are currently multiple EU processes impacting especially forests, and it is difficult  to have 
an overall picture of the possible cumulative effects. It is highly necessary that the Commission 
analyses its proposal in a way that connections between different EU matters are considered.  
 
• If the EU sets new legal obligations for  the Member States, the EU must also arrange ade-
quate funding. Based on the proposal, the appropriateness and adequacy of the funding 
sources available have not been considered carefully enough. For example, the Commission's 
idea of the significant role of CAP funding as a fundin g source is not functional, because the 
CAP funding is intended to support active food and raw material production. In order to reach 
the EU's biodiversity goals, there could be a need for a separate financial mechanism.

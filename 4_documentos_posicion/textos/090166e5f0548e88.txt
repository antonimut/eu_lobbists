Biodiversity and mining 
Raw-material supply makes use of geological anomalies that cannot be moved from where they are found. 
Deposits may be situated in remote (and relatively pristine) areas. On a European scale, mining r elatively 
occupies a very small fraction of land (coverage of less than 0,016% of EU’s surfaces, according to EEA Report 
No 10/2017 Landscapes in transition - An account of 25 years of land cover change in Europe 2017). 
Europe’s long history of mining has taught us that if it is not responsibly and sustainably managed, its potential 
environmental impacts could include a loss of biodiversity, the formation of sinkholes, erosion, or the 
contamination of groundwater, surface water and soil. Today, by promoting  or adopting sustainable land 
management practices, supporting the conservation of biodiversity and reducing environmental harm, the 
mining sector and environmental conservation co-exist in Europe. 
Metal ores and industrial minerals from the mining and recycling industrial ecosystem are crucial drivers for 
transition to a sustainable low carbon economy. In these geopolitically challenging times, the safeguarding of 
metal/mineral supply is essential for strategic autonomy of the EU economy. Europe does not fully depend on 
providers outside of Europe and has the potential to sustainably extract more of the needed metals ores and 
industrial minerals from deposits within our borders.  
Looking ahead, considering increasing pressures on land use in and around cities, protection and restoration of 
biodiversity is to become increasingly important for all sectors, including for the mining industry. 
Euromines supports the protection and habitat restoration, and as stakeholder, Euromines participates in the 
EU Biodiversity and Soil Platform. The European mining industry can contribute to the EU Biodiversity Strategy 
with her best practices for biodiversity conservation at our mining sites. In general , it is import ant to stop 
biodiversity loss, and to mitigate the impacts of climate change, notably by restoring degraded wetlands and 
rivers, forest and agricultural ecosystems. Our sector comprehends in general  the goals of the Nature 
Restoration. Euromines also agree s with the Commission’s planning to provide more support for national and 
regional authorities for their preparation of national biodiversity restoration plans, biodiversity monitoring and 
monitoring (as described in the Nature Restoration Law proposal under Chapter III and IV.

Our reflection on the proposed Nature Restoration Targets (COM 2022/0195) 
In June 2022, the Commission proposed its legally binding targets to restore degraded EU ecosystems, in 
particular those with the most potential to remove and  store carbon and to prevent and reduce the impact of 
natural disasters. Some considerations from our sector’s point of view: 
▪ We would question the need for legally binding targets under the proposed Nature Restoration Law. If 
the Nature Restoration proj ect is implemented, it would be best through a Directive instead of a 
Regulation so that the member states have the flexibility for implementation. Also, we doubt whether 
setting fixed time frames for achieving nature restoration goals would be purposeful; nature has its own 
time frames of self-development.  
 
▪ In the explanatory memorandum of the proposal, it says that the aim is to complement existing 
environmental policy. Inter alia the Water Framework Directive and the Birds and Habitats Directives 
are m entioned. Indeed, in several environmental areas specific and detailed European Legislation 
already exists. i.e. the  Natura 2000 -Directive and the Water Framework Directive  already regulate in 
depth the protection of nature and biodiversity as well as th e protection of surface and groundwater 
bodies. To avoid misunderstandings or misinterpretations, it would be helpful to clarify in the 
Commission´s proposal that the existing environmental legislation – i.e. Water Framework Directive – 
should not be touched or contradicted by the new proposal. 
 
▪ According to Article 1 of the proposed Nature Restoration Regulation, by 2030 20% of EU land areas will 
be protected and implemented on a binding basis. This represents an increase of at least 4% of protected 
land and 19% of protected sea areas compared to today. According to Article 4 for each member state 
at least 30 % of the area of each group of habitat types listed in Annex I that is not in good condition 
shall be restored, as quantified in the national restoration plans. We cannot agree with simply applying 
an arbitrary coverage target for this purpose (and consequently set more potential no -go areas for 
mining activities). For instance, already nowadays, there is no possibility to make derogations for mines 
under the Birds Directive, and this applies even if the mining project is motivated by imperative reasons 
of overriding public interests (IROPI) (Article 9.1 in the Birds Directive versus Article 16.1 in the Habitats 
Directive). This means that -if a mining project conflicts with certain provisions in the Birds Directive - 
there is no opportunity to make a derogation and a permit cannot be issued. Besides this, the fixed 
coverage percentages for all member states, do not consider the population density or clim atic 
conditions within the MS territories. 
In environmentally and economically feasible mineral deposits first a well -balanced consideration 
between environmental and economic aspects should be made before labelling mineral -rich lands as 
non-extractable territory. One of the prevailing methods to determine environmental impacts is an EIA 
(Environmental Impact Assessment), whose implementation is already common practice for new mining 
projects. Besides, appropriate assessment under Natura 2000 will take plac e as described in the EU 
Commission Guidance for the Non -Energy European mineral raw materials industry 
(https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/69b6d6c1-bfc1-4fe5-9252-08af20a95cfe/).

▪ Article 4 of the proposal seems to establish a new protection and restoration regime in order to restore 
and improve habitat types to reach a good condition; re-establish habitat types; and prevent no 
deterioration (in analogy to the EU Water Framework Directive). Moreover, the proposal foresees only 
two exemptions clauses (in article 4 para 8 and 9) – applying for Natura 2000 areas as well as for areas 
located outside of Natura 2000. These clauses would de facto not only lead to a tightening of the 
regulations in Natura 2000 sites, but also to an extension of the Natura 2000 exemption mechanism to 
habitat types located also outside protected areas. This is neither justified nor proportionate. Moreover, 
in practice the new Commission´s proposal would further restrict other forms of land use, i.e., for social 
and economic purposes. Potential areas for extraction of raw materials would further be reduced, in 
particular because the extraction of raw materials is bound to a fixed raw material deposit. In this sense 
the Commission´s proposal is too one -dimensional focusing on the nature restoration. The proposal 
would indeed need a more balanced consideration of environmental, economic and social aspects.  
It has not been considered that there are changes in climatic development, and therefore habitat types 
and spe cies cannot be preserved or restored as they existed decades ago. The same applies to 
settlement in areas where these habitat types and species existed. The Directive must take this aspect 
into account in the dynamics of nature for justified cases. 
According to Article 4 - paragraph 8c, for projects outside Natura 2000 areas, the occurrence of 
overriding public interest is to be determined on a case-by-case basis. In practice, this would mean that 
a decision whether a project has an overriding public interest, may be very strongly politically influenced. 
The decision tree whether a project has an overriding public interest may be another burden in the -
already very challenging- trajectory of obtaining a permit.  
Investments in EU mining will be further jeopardized while, especially in these challenging times, Europe 
needs to have access to its own mineral resources.  We would further urge to keep the possibility at all 
times for the preparation and review by authorities of an EIA or EIA preliminary examination (according 
to the EIA Directive, 85/337/EEC and 2014/52/EU) for the assessment of new mineral extraction projects 
in or near protected areas. 
▪ Although we understand and support the need for restoring free flowing rivers in Europe (Article 7 of 
the Natu re Restoration proposal), we would like to stress that in some (mountainous) regions free 
flowing conditions might create critical high or low flow rates. Ensuring safe up - and downstream 
conditions in those regions that interfere with mining areas by cont rolling the water flow is crucial to 
prevent flooding of (potential) mine sites and villages. A constant river water flow is also needed for 
constant generation of hydro power and as condition for reliable water abstraction for drinking water 
production an d for many other productive sectors, such as mining. Therefore, before any decision -
making, proper hydrological assessment and modelling on (sub)catchment scale is needed to predict 
any effects on existing industrial activities. 
 
▪ Chapter IV of the proposa l (Monitoring and reporting) mentions under Article 17 (paragraph 1) which 
biodiversity related trends and indicators shall be monitored by the Member States. As biodiversity 
indicators are often still under development, several indicators may not be fully  fit for use at an 
(comparable) EU scale. More assessment is still needed for specifying pragmatic methods for monitoring 
(which is confirmed under Article 17 paragraph 9). In our view any underdeveloped indicators might 
become a bottle neck for correctly assessing and comparing changing biodiversity. Our sector wishes to

▪ The European Union still has not reached a “no -net-loss” objective. Different initiatives, such as ALIGN 
are underway, to determine a way to account for such an objective and case-studies exist demonstrating 
that achieving no-net-loss is already an approach that goes beyond state of the art. At the same time a 
growing body of research proves that compensations measures have contributed positively to 
biodiversity. In addition to the implementation of the measures themselves, a scientific underpinning of 
a positive effect of compensation measures requires a dutiful follow-up and guarantee of permanence. 
 
▪ The mining sector is almost predestined to promote “temporary nature”. Typical temporary nature 
features on extraction sites are, for instance, new ponds (benefiting amphibians and dragonflies), open 
ground, sand and gravel areas (attracting insects and birds), pioneer grasslands (attracting insects and 
birds), loose cliffs (benefiting birds and solitary bees), and the creation of areas providing shelter (f or 
reptiles, amphibians and insects). Although according to the Habitat Directive “temporary nature” can 
positively influence the conservation status of species, it has not been seen yet as an instrument of the 
EU-Biodiversity Strategy. Temporary natural a reas should be promoted as a voluntary measure to 
improve biodiversity to eventually contribute to the maintenance or restoration of favourable 
conservation status of a given species or habitat. 
 
▪ We suggest that during the review of the draft Nature Resto ration Law in Parliament and Council, the 
following issues will be better embedded:  
o New EU deposits should be made exploitable inside and outside Natura2000 areas.  
o Application of a “no-net-loss” objective, compensation measures (“offsetting”). 
o “Temporary nature” should be defined, recognised and included in the Nature Restoration Law.

Feedback: Nature Restoration Law Proposal 
 
Currently, Europe as whole is in a state of crisis due to the suffering and 
destruction coming from the Ukraine-Russia war. And, in times of war the focus 
on food security becomes an aspect of critical importance. It is essential to take the 
necessary steps to ensure that food supplies continue reaching those most affected, in Ukraine 
and globally. This is the moment to further strengthen united European and international 
cooperation. The cumulative impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, the energy price hikes, and the 
Russian invasion of Ukraine have profound and long lasting effects not only in the EU but also 
elsewhere in the World, particularly in Africa and in the Middle East. Food security and food 
affordability have always been a core part of the CAP, from its foundation 60 years ago. With 
COVID-19 and the war between Russia and Ukraine; this issue has re-emerged as a threat that 
can radically shake our entire society. Given the current politically precarious situation (war in 
the Ukraine; risks of food shortages and unaffordable prices for food in numerous countries), 
the EU should - at least temporarily - put the objective of food security at the forefront.  
Copa and Cogeca has continuously supported the EU’s objectives of protecting, 
conserving, and restoring our environment for the future of our planet, especially 
due to the role that farmers and foresters can and already play for nature. However, for us to 
play our part in these ambitions, targets must be realistic and achievable, supports must be 
sufficient, Member States must be allowed to tailor the measures to their needs, and property 
rights must be respected. All activities aimed at promoting biodiversity must fully acknowledge 
the economic and social perspective. Following the three pillars of sustainability in a balanced 
way is a prerequisite for the acceptability of the proposal and for its successful implementation. 
The EU is imposing unrealistic targets on farmers & foresters. The targets of 
restoration must be achievable and realistic in order for the process to be accomplished fairly 
and concretely in the coming years and decades. It is essential that the EU is ambitious in such a 
process of restoring Europe’s degraded ecosystems; however, it is likewise essential that they are 
not fantasists when expecting results for arbitrary deadlines every decade until 2050. The 
targets also do not make specific acknowledgement of bio-geographical sensitivities in the 
setting of targets in the proposal; such as the presence of standing and lying deadwood in forest 
land. The presence of deadwood, either standing or lying, can in most southern EU countries be 
a serious forest fire risk, due to increasing temperatures and unpredictable weather conditions. 
When these targets are not accomplished, either for lack of support or for safety reasons, it will 
be the farmers and foresters who will be targeted as the reason for failure; not the unrealistic 
and unsympathetic European Commission proposals. Aiming at successful outcomes cost-
effectively through prioritisation and allocation based on reliable data on the current state of 
play should be the key. However, the Commission seems to think that quantity is more 
important than quality. 
The alternative production uses are not realistic for a fully food and wood-fibre 
secure European economy. The plan for restoring and rewetting European peatlands that 
are under agricultural, forestry, and peat extraction zones, and other, is extremely ambitious and 
does not take into account the implications that will come from this proposal. Agricultural land 
and forestry land across Europe being removed from active production of food and wood-fibre 
products is not in keeping with the Commissions expectation to not reduce production but 
redeploy production to other forms of land-use. Paludiculture, water buffalo grazing, and

cranberry cultivation, as proposed by the Commission as alternatives to active food and timber 
production do not show a full comprehension of the differences in climate and conditions for 
agriculture in the different member states. And most of all, the lack of acknowledgement of the 
necessity of food and resource production in a time that is facing a food and resource price and 
availability crisis.  
Horticultural fruit, vegetable and plant production is not ready for the transition 
to a fully peat free environment. The ambition to rewet and restore peat extraction zones, 
will have dire consequences for the horticultural food and plant production sector. Horticultural 
peat represents 70% of growing media currently used in Europe, with less than 10% of growing 
media being fully peat free. Horticulture represents 18% of the total value of agricultural 
production in the EU while it uses only 3% of the EU’s cultivated land. At the same time, it 
enhances the already highly sustainable EU fruit and vegetable production that is fully 
dependent on horticulture and has a yearly turnover of over 120 billion EUR, with as many as 
550,000 employees across the Union. The benefits that horticultural production, ornamentals, 
fruits and vegetables, and related sectors bring to the overall well-being and food security of the 
European Union, greatly outweigh the risks associated with continued horticultural peat 
extraction. This is especially so in the absence of suitable, affordable, and available alternatives, 
also taking into account that the worldwide demand for growing media overall is expected to 
rapidly increase by 400% by 2040.  
The EU lacks clear and long-term financial support for nature restoration. 
Restoration is set to cost approximately 7 billion euro annually, which the Commission expects 
to cover from the 14 billion euro annual biodiversity spending under the MFF. With the majority 
of this expected to come from the CAP funding; but in the given contexts, the CAP funds are, in 
some cases, insufficient for achieving their main purpose..  
We would like to remind the Commission that these funds are already reserved for 
compensatory payments under the first pillar of the CAP and for voluntary measures under the 
second pillar. There is no full pot of money waiting to be tapped. We are talking about 
conversions of management, restrictions of use, and tasks of use in this draft; and for that fresh 
money is a prerequisite before you even discuss the content specifications. 
The financial dependency of the Commission on the CAP to restore land which is no longer 
agricultural or forestry land is not feasible, or implementable on a short-, medium-, or long-term 
basis. Restoration can only be achieved if it is not predominantly dependent on the CAP for the 
exercise of Member State’s Restoration Plans; it is absolutely necessary that restoration is 
funded through a dedicated financial mechanism for restoration. Long-term restoration requires 
long-term resources and coherent actions, not short-term measures , farmers and foresters 
require fairness and certainty into the future if their consent is to be factored into any new 
nature restoration support schemes.  
It is imperative that a further assessment of the availability of the intended finance mechanisms 
for farmers and forests, including data on previous rates of EU fund consumption by Member 
States to estimate the effectivity of the proposed financial proposal. Likewise, it is clear that a 
clear delineated plan for the amount each Member State will need to financially contribute from 
their own treasuries be made and circulated for the full-disclosure.  
The EU continues to expand its power over Member States. The Commission is  
proposing a regulation, which is being pushed forward specifically because there is no need to 
transpose it into Member State laws, would result in the measures being taken on the ground 
sooner. If this the case, Commission neglects that a regulation is applied as such regardless of 
the current natural conditions or the measures that each state can take without endangering the 
smooth running of agriculture or any other economic sectors. This is in contrast to a directive, 
which is more democratic and bio-geographically inclusive. Alongside this, the Commission’s 
wish to use delegated acts allows the never-ending expansion of EU control over EU Member 
State’s ability to govern their own natural spaces. Freely amending and extending the indicators 
and habitats to which the law refers, without proper consultation with stakeholders, will cause 
the continuous growth of an already enormous piece of legislation. 
The Commission has proposed a regulation which determines a wide variety of 
extraordinarily specific targets and indicators for nature restoration purposes.

Setting targets and choosing indicators without a sufficient knowledge base will cause 
uncertainty and in the worst case make it unnecessarily costly or impossible to reach the 
targets.. There are countless unknowns when it comes to our natural environment and it is 
essential we have the data before we set legally binding targets.  
There a variety of local, regional, and national reporting styles and indicators which have been 
developed and are in development already which aid farmers and foresters to take measures for 
restoration and nature conservation, such as LEVA. This is a system built to decrease 
eutrophication and uses local action coordinators to build the measures and actions based on 
entirely local settings and conditions. It is essential that all and any Commission move forward 
towards indicators for restoration include and make room for subsidiarity for such programmes 
as these, and not lock Member States into reporting indicators which are one size fits all system.  
The Commission and Member States must make a sincere assessment of the area, 
economic impact, and social impact that this will have, and not just focus on the 1 
to 8 benefit ratio. The proposed law focuses on six main groups of habitat types which overall 
comprise a variety of terrestrial, coastal and freshwater, wetlands, grasslands, riparian, forested 
and steppe and rocky habitats. However, it is unclear as to the estimated or confirmed range of 
the tens of millions of hectares which will be covered by restoration measures in both minor 
measures, or long-term rewetting programmes. Nor is there a sincere estimation of the 
significant impact economic and social burden that can come when restoration measures are 
placed on land.  
The removal of actively productive land from circulation will only exacerbate the access to land 
of current farmers and future farmers; only enhancing the difficulties young and new entrant 
farmers have in joining the sector. By actively removing land from production, and actively 
reducing the amount of land available for agriculture and forestry purposes; the price of land 
will only continue to increase. Access to land is one of the biggest issues that inhibit the highly 
educated and highly motivated farmers and foresters who are looking to join or expand their 
family farm into a more financially independent enterprise. However, it is unclear and as a 
result is worrying, what status these areas will have, what the impact of the connections will be 
on the agricultural sector, and what the relationship will be to the carefully established habitat 
targets in the member states. 
It is essential that the European Commission and the Member States carry out a prior 
assessment of exactly how many hectares of agricultural land, forestry, and watercourses will be 
covered by this proposed regulation. Once this assessment is complete, the land concerned 
should be further assessed in terms of its exact soil type, prevailing average rainfall, current 
drainage conditions and type and level of farming/forestry/other activity conducted thereon. 
Some member states are still in the process of gathering information in this regard and this data 
should be taken into account when setting rewetting targets. A further thorough analyses should 
also cover the effects on the direct and indirect costs, impacts on food and energy security, and 
the continuing supply of forest products and services.  
The fulfilment of the satisfactory levels in the proposal, should as a result include a solid 
assessment of the social, environmental, and economic implications that restoration level setting 
shall have. It is only with clear insight into the trade-offs that that will come from removing land 
from active production, and the impact this can and will have on local rural economies both 
financially and socially; that informed and transparent decision-making can take place with 
restoration.  
Definitions are ill-defined and inappropriate for the fulfilment of targets for now 
and in the long-term. The proposal raises a number of major issues with regard to the 
definition of “good condition”, “favourable reference area”, “ecologically sensitive area”, and 
“sufficient quality and quantity of a habitat or species”. While these terms can be considered 
scientifically correct, their application within the practical implementation of setting targets, 
and making the choices between restoration areas and measures, will be overly difficult, if not 
impossible to achieve. For example, Art 11 par 2: For each habitat type the favourable reference 
area should take into account the documented loss over the last 70 years. Looking at that it 
becomes clear that the EC wants to reestablish a nature which doesn´t consider that there was a 
huge development since then and there is mostly no possibility to chance the landscape into the

1950´s again. This sounds difficult but has to be recognized. Also, due to the lack of adequate 
and reliable data in order to assess condition, the lack of previous thresholds for each aspect, 
and the weighting of the identified characteristics on the ground. The data is simply not 
available to establish what is needed in the short-, medium-, and long-term viability of

all; “resilience”, “reference condition”, “satisfactory level”, “forest connectivity”, “sufficient

The protection of property rights must be a priority. It is imperative that the property 
rights of those affected by these targets are not adversely affected. To be able to achieve the goals 
in the proposal, Member States need to step over the boundaries of property rights All measures 
must be voluntary and with the full-knowledge and agreement of the landowner. Restoration 
will primarily take place on private land, which member states do not have the power to control 
and can only be achieved with the full-backing of those who own and whose livelihoods depend 
on the land. Further, there is an essential need to ensure acceptance on the ground-level, when it 
comes to promoting restoration. There are many good examples in member states that needs 
support from the EU. EU farmers have the will to increase biodiversity and restore nature. That 
will should not be crushed with strict legislation and enforcement. We want to and need to 
increase EU biodiversity and restore nature – together.

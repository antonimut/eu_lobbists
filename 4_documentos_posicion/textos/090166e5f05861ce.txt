 
 
 
I. Introduction 
As the draft regulation has already been published and submitted to the other EU institutions 
we lack the understanding of this feedback process. Never before the European Commission 
(EC) has asked for a public feedback in this way  after the proposal being already published. 
Therefore, we do not understand how the EC will make use of it. Nevertheless, we put efforts 
and thoughts in this feedback and urge the EC not to just write recommendations and 
guidelines but go back to reassess the published draft instead. 
In times of war, the focus on assured energy supply and food security becomes a crucial issue. 
It is impe rative that the necessary steps are taken to ensure that Europe strives for self-
sufficiency in food and energy and that the supply of  food remains affordable not only for 
people in EU Member States, but also for other countries outside the European Union and 
outside Europe. 
One of the main challenges is to reduce the inflation rate. Therefore, energy supply from 
renewable sources is one of the key elements to fight inflation. Landowners play a crucial role 
in providing renewable energy. Society and farmers are still struggling with the consequences 
of the COVID -19 pandemic and there was no time to breathe until the war began and 
production prices rose enormously.  
Under these circumstances, farmers in Austria as well as farmers in Europe are not prepared 
to accept such measures as contained in this proposal. As we have already mentioned several 
times to the EC, we are not only willing to continue to contribute to the improvement of the 
environment as before, but also to do more. However, the proposal does not intend to help us 
in this matter, but it will force  us to set-aside land, unable the way to generate income and 
finally abandon agriculture and forestry activity. Consequently, the EC does not believe in any 
of the good work we have done so far  and does not admit that agriculture has and still is 
contributing to maintain the diverse cultu ral landscape that society perceives and demands 
when talking about ‘nature’.  
Looking more closely at the obligations for other sectors, agriculture and forestry are the only 
sectors that are deprived of the economic basis for their production. Considering that the 
agricultural sector 
- needs a vital environment as a livelihood, 
- has provided for the vitality of the land up to now,  
- has provided additional environmental services within the framework of 
conditionality and the agri-environmental programmes,  
and that the forestry sector has delivered exemplary sink effects and additional climate 
measures, it is not reasonable that in Europe these sectors are forced to stop producing. In 
this regard, the suggestion to cultivate spices or rare potatoes on rewetted sites is a mockery 
of producing  agriculture. W e all know it neither can contribute to food security nor to a 
relevant income in the needed scale and it only relativizes the concept of a producing 
agriculture. 
We know thriving nature, environment and biodiversity is important for everyone, and the 
enhancement of our degraded habitats and endangered species can lead to a more vibrant 
and richer Union to live in. However, for us to make our contribution to achieving these goals, 
the proposal must be  
- realistic,  
- achievable,  
- funding and support must be additional and sufficient,  
- Member States must be allowed to tailor the measures to their needs, and  
- fundamental rights must be respected.  
All activities aimed at restoring the nature and promoting biodiversity must fully 
acknowledge the economic and social perspective.

II. The EU is imposing unrealistic targets on farmers & foresters 
The EC prepared a proposal with targets that are unrealistic in implementation and unfair to 
those on the ground who will be irreparable affected.  
Usually we go through a legislative proposal article by article and give our suggestions but 
that will not work with this piece of legislation. 
We cannot suggest anything realistic that will help  
- to bring the nature back in a condition of the 1950’s 
- to rewet peatlands in the proposed amount 
- to restore habitat types by at least 90 % until 2050 
- to put in place restoration measures of 60 % of the area of Austria 
- to implement high diversity landscape features in such an extent where no farming  
activities, forestry or hunting is allowed  
- to stop harvesting to achieve the required amount of deadwood, and even more so 
when it is not yet foreseeable what value the EC envisions here  
- remove barriers in such way that all surface waters are connected 
- allow the EC  to give it a free pass on what ranges of indicators to be achieved in 
restauration are acceptable from its point of view, which would lead to further 
uncertainties in the future with us. 
Reading this proposal makes one think of utopia, because it has nothing to do with realistic 
targets and measures , nor with the world we live in . It becomes clear that on charts, 
dashboards and some grids the input of some data might show a result, but reading these 
lines above, it becomes clear that these are just charts  and will not change our environment 
for good. There is  
- a huge lack of evidence - Before setting targets there must be a scientific based setup 
for defining baselines and securing monitoring,  
- several definitions are ill -defined and inappropriate for the fulfilment of targets for 
now and in the long-term. The proposal raises a number of major issues with regard 
to the definition of ‘good condition’, ‘favourable reference area’, and ‘sufficient quality 
and quantity of a habitat or species’,  
- the necessary funding is not ensured,  
- the impact is widely unclear  (a clear impact of every measure is missing. To beware 
proportionality this is an essential), 
- the timeframe is not realistic because it takes time to restore nature (butterflies won’t 
appear tomorrow when the regulation gets in force today),  
- several indices do not reflect reality sufficiently  (e.g. woodland and farm land bird 
index),  
- FFH-reporting is diverse in member states and the Austrian report is tendenti ous 
(EU-methodology is missing  as EC already knows because it is mentioned in the 
impact assessment), 
- some of the indicators are still not available (pollinators),

III. The lack of evidenced-based legislation 
The impact assessment clearly states that a valid database is missing for most of the different 
ecosystems. The EC admits that there a re too different methods in creating the FFH -report 
and the state of nature report. Despite this lack of evidence , the EC will implement targets 
and measures, which prohibit farmers and foresters to continue their work for the society and 
the environment. In no other sector of the economy or society would such concrete targets 
and interventions be prescribed on the basis of such insufficient data. 
 
IV. Violation of fundamental rights 
With this proposal, the EC ignores that there are several fundamental rights  of landowners 
and managers that are seriously violated by these measures and targets.  The fundamental 
right of property, the fundamental right of trade and the fundamental right of equality cannot 
been reconciled with the design of this proposal. 
The fundamental right to property laid down in Art 17 Charter of Fundamental Rights of the 
European Union (CFR) lays down a frame when property may be encroached. In our view, it 
cannot be reconciled with this fundamental right that a farmer for example has to  
- rewet his peat land up to 50 % until 2050, 
- create yield with a high ecological impact on which no farming and hunting is allowed, 
- set-aside a part (EC wants to set up reference values) of his property and 
- implement similar recovery measures. 
The freedom to conduct a business according Art 16 CFR cannot be reconciled with the overall 
targets that the EC wants a farmer to 
- give up growing plants and fruits, 
- reduce livestock farming and 
- manage his property in the proposed manner. 
The fundamental rights of equality according Art 20 et seqq. CFR is violated when the EC  
- burdens just farmers and foresters, 
- treats agro industry the same way like small farmers and finally 
- when a cross-sectoral and globally problem shall be solved by a 4 % part for 96 % of 
the population in European Union.

The EC responds to these arguments by stating that the requirements under this proposal are 
‘only’ binding on member states, thus seeking to disguise the fact that it is ultimately farmers 
and foresters who will be adversely affected.  
Restrictions provided for by law must be suitable, necessary and proportionate to be lawful. 
The encroachments on the fundamental rights to property, freedom to conduct a business  
and equality foreseen in this draft can thus only be restricted if they do not constitute a 
disproportionate, unacceptable encroachment with regard to the purpose pursued. 
However, the proposed measures constitute precisely such an encro achment if agriculture 
and forestry are de facto prohibited from cultivating entire areas or even regions  and earing 
a fair standard of living from agricultural activity as it is written down in Article 39 of the 
TFEU. Whether an intervention serves the co mmon good would also have to be clearly 
worked out and examined in advance within the framework of an impact assessment. This 
requirement is also not apparent on the part of the EC.  
With this type of proposed measure, a human rights lawyer would consider whether there is 
some proportionality. Considering that the EU wants to finance these measures with money 
from the CAP that is already entirely budgeted to fulfil various other demands from society , 
the answer is no. 
 
V. The EU lacks clear and long -term finan cial support for nature 
restoration 
The EC aims for the majority of the financing of this l egislation and its implementation to 
come from the budget of the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP), and the future eco-schemes.  
The funds under CAP shall compensate the loss of income so that society gets high quality 
and at the same time affordable agricultural goods. The funds for future eco -schemes are 
foreseen to compensate environmental measures that farmers already take. 
We urge the EC that the funds they refer to for financing are already reserved  for the 
compensation of revenues that cannot be generated on the market. T here is no full pot of 
money waiting to be tapped within the CAP . Nature restoration and protection is an 
additional service demanded by society and its provision is connected with additional 
management expenses and costs accruing for those who answer this demand. Therefore 
provision of additional funding for delivering meeting additional demands is a necessary and 
logically prerequisite to even start an discussion. 
 
VI. The EU continues to expand its power over Member States 
1. wrong means 
This proposed legislation aims to ensure a high level  environmental standard across the EU 
and in the opinion of the EU the best way to reach these targets is a regulation. Considering 
the wide range of measures and the high targets , there has to be some leeway  for 
implementation and proportionality.  
Consequently, the EC takes the Member States ability to tailor the law precisely to their 
particular requirements. Flexibility is needed to allow the recognition of every country ’s 
precise needs, conditions, opportunities and starting points. Therefore, more det ailed 
planning and implementation of the Strategy in relation to restoration must take place at the 
level of the Member States. The key is to choose the correct measures for the right places, 
which underlines the need for case -by-case decisions. Planning a nd implementation of 
restoration measures have highly local ly characteristics. While it may be argued that this is

the purpose of the Nature Restoration Plans, the enshrinement of properly adjudicated self -

A directive, that is more democratic, bio-geographically inclusive and local

2. missing proportionality due to lack of predictability 
As a result, the Nature Restoration Law subverts the principles of subsidiarity and

the indicators and habitats to which the law refers, by committee. While this is to enable the 
creation of additional targets that are not backed up by the necessary data, such as for 
pollinators, it enables the European Commission to constantly change the legally binding 
targets imposed on Member States and leads to an endless growth of this law.  
 
VII. The EC must be careful about how it moves forward with the Nature 
Restoration Law  
The current EU Nature Restoration Law proposal has ambitions, but they will not live up to 
reality when implemented on the ground. Achieving these targets by the dates and with the 
percentages proposed is impossible, and this must be acknowledged. If the EC really believes 
that it can put ‘nature’ in a ‘good state’ with this legislation, it is promising citi zens a silver 
bullet that does not exist in reality. Likewise, these targets disregard totally the role that 
farmers and foresters play in maintaining and managing the natural ecosystems and species 
in and on their lands as they are already today the main facilitators and experts in providing 
high quality food and biogenic materials as well as diverse, structures landscape, highly 
appreciated by society, at the same time.  
The EC ignores the people, the regions, and the sectors being impacted by these targets, and 
deliberately forget the role that agriculture and forestry play in the vitality of rural areas and 
society as a whole.  
To this end, we ask the EC to assess the proposal again and to take our suggestions and 
concerns into account oth erwise it will lead to a lex imperfecta or an example of abuse that 
will be rejected by the European Court of Justice.

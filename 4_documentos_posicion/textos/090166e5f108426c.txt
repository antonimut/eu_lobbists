Article 2(2) concerning the scope states that the Regulation is fully applicable in the case of 
autologous application where SoHOs are processed and stored before use. We do not believe 
this article follows the risk-based approach outlined in paragraph 10 of the preamble: 
When autologous SoHOs are collected and processed before being re-used in the same person, 
risks appear that should be mitigated. Thus, there needs to be an assessment and authorisation 
of the processes applied to ensure that they are demonstrated to be safe and effective for the 
recipient. 
This introduces risks of both disproportionate legislation and obstacles to patient autonomy. 
We propose the following amendment:  
In the case of autologous use of SoHOs where SoHO is processed and stored before use, the 
Regulation shall apply to the processes used as defined in this Regulation. 
 
Furthermore, it is unclear if SoHOs prepared for autologous use are also to be included in the 
scope. 
 
Comments on Article 3 
 
SoHO and SoHO Preparation – all SoHOs should not be SoHO preparations; Art. 3[12]) -  
 
Article 3(12), concerning the definition of SoHO preparations, states that a SoHO preparation 
(a) is a specific type of SoHO that has undergone one or more SoHO activities, including 
processing, (b) meets a predefined specification and (c) is intended for use in a recipient for a 
particular clinical indication. Furthermore, "processing" in paragraph 15 is defined as any 
activity related to the handling of SoHOs. Taken together, we find that the proposed definition 
leaves very limited possibilities for a SoHO to be excluded from entering being preparations. 
For example, human oocytes that are not used directly after removal but are frozen and 
packaged will be included in the definition. As a result, there is a high risk of disproportionate 
Regulation in connection with Articles 20, 21, 22, 23, 40 and 41 in particular. In paragraph 9 
of the preamble, the Commission describes how  
SoHOs can be prepared and stored in a variety of ways, becoming SoHO preparations, which can 
be applied to recipients. Here, we note the wording "becoming" as indicative of the intention of 
the introduction of SoHO preparations as a concept: that the processing of a SoHO must result 
in a real change in the biological material before it is considered to be a SoHO preparation. 
This is not the case with frozen gametes, where the processes of purification, preservation or 
packaging do not alter the individual gametes or their effectiveness. 
 
We propose that the Regulation clarifies what the intention is with the introduction of SoHO 
preparations and that examples are given of how specific materials are defined as either 
SoHOs or SoHOs preparations. 
 
If the current definition is maintained, we consider it necessary to introduce a risk-based 
implementation for all related acts, as is already the case for Article 41(3).

SoHO Entity and SoHO Establishment– Art. 3(24) and 40 
 
The Regulation introduces two new concepts: SoHO Entity and SoHO Establishment 
(paragraphs 24 and 40, Art. 3, respectively). The preamble describes how both a SoHO 
establishment and a SoHO entity are legal entities that can consist of separate locations. 
Further, we note that SoHO establishments are a special type of SoHO entity that is defined by 
their activities or risk level. When implementing these two types of SoHO companies, it 
becomes possible to make differentiated legislation that is precisely adapted to activities and 
risk. However, we find that these new concepts do not cover companies that have multiple 
SoHO establishments and SoHO entities in different Member States. The lack of inclusion of 
organisations of this type is particularly evident in Articles 50 and 51, which note that all 
SoHO entities must possess a quality management system, as well as appoint a physician 
responsible for developing, reviewing and approving policies and procedures for establishing 
and applying SoHO donor qualifying criteria and criteria for allocating SoHOs and SoHO 
preparations. In larger organisations, the above systems and tasks will typically be 
centralised, which strengthens the quality and uniformity of SoHO activities. Since the 
preamble explicitly mentions that both SoHO establishment and SoHO entities may consist of 
several locations, it should be clarified that centralisation of resources is permitted and that it 
is not the intention of the Regulation that a legal group with SoHO entities in several member 
countries is expected to operate autonomous quality management systems and employ 
doctors in all member countries where SoHO activity is carried out (Art. 51 [1]).   
 
The possibility of operating in group form across several Member States in the union could be 
regulated by the following various proposals for solutions. 
 
• A SoHO establishment is defined as: 
 "SoHO establishment" means a SoHO entity whose activities include both the 
processing and storing of SoHOs. A SoHO establishment may also be a group with 
several locations (legal entities) in one or more Member States where responsibility 
for SoHO activities is shared and/or centralised.  
 
• A third type of SoHO company is introduced and defined: a SoHO organisation covering 
larger organisations/legal groups with locations/companies in several Member States. 
This would allow the requirements of Articles 50 and 51 to be waived for SoHO 
establishments that are part of a SoHO organisation in which the requirements have 
already been managed centrally.  
 
• Article 51(1) is amended to waive the requirement that the designated doctor must 
live and carry out their duties in the same Member State and instead state that all 
SoHO entities must: have access to a doctor licensed to practice medicine in the Union. 
Furthermore, Article 50 should be amended to include the clause that a SoHO entity 
may use the quality management system from another entity in the interests of 
cooperation and/or sharing SoHO activities between the entities.

Art. 3, (62) and (63) 
 
Regarding the matter of gamete donation, Articles 3 (62) and (63) establish a differentiation 
between whether the donor and the recipient are in an intimate physical relationship or not 
(third party donation and within couple use, respectively).  However, in the field of fertility 
there are many situations that are not covered by these proposed definitions, including: 
 
• Known donation is a type of donation of gametes in which two people are familiar with 
each other's identities and one party wishes to donate gametes to the other. These two 
people do not have a physically intimate relationship but wish to make a gamete 
transfer for other reasons. In these cases, the general requirements for donor 
suitability should be significantly reduced to infectious diseases that can be 
transmitted between donor and recipient during treatment. If infectious diseases are 
found that can be transmitted through treatment with the gametes, the recipient must 
be allowed, on an informed basis, to choose whether to receive the donation 
regardless. This must be done to ensure patients' autonomy and the right to make 
decisions for/about their own treatment. If the possibility of known donation is not 
included in the Regulation, or a full donor suitability assessment is required in line 
with a third party donation, the costs to individuals will be so disproportionate that 
patients seek unsafe and untested methods of obtaining treatment outside the system.   
We recommend that known donation is included as a definition in the Regulation and 
is subject to proportional regulation. 
 
• Paragraph 63 on within couple use notes that this only applies in cases "where one 
person supplies their own oocytes, and the other person supplies their own sperm cells".  
This means that couples in an intimate relationship using donor eggs do not fall under 
the exceptions for within couple use. Gametes from a person’s intimate partner should 
always be classified as a partner use, regardless of whether donor eggs or own eggs are 
used. Donated eggs should, of course, be considered a separate third party donation.  
We recommend that the definition of partner use be extended so that couples who use 
donor eggs, but their own sperm are not subjected to disproportionate legislation.

Recommendation for definitionin Art. 3.  
We recommend that the term "ID release" be defined in the Regulation to ensure 
transparency in relation to the possibilities of data exchange for all involved in the process, i.e. 
the donor, the recipient and donor-conceived offspring. 
 
Paragraph 44 of the preamble states that the Regulation respects fundamental rights and the 
principles recognised by the EU Charters, including the rights of the children. It is specified 
that all SoHO activities should always be carried out in such a way that said rights and 
principles are respected. However, we believe that this intention has not been implemented in 
the Regulation in relation to donor-conceived offsprings’rights. This view is supported by 
paragraph 44, where the Commission recommends that activities be carried out in a 
transparent manner that prioritises the safety of both donors and recipients, without 
mentioning transperency towards donor-conceived offspring. 
 
 
The need is supported by the principle of proportionality, which recommends that the 
Regulation be limited to aspects that Member States cannot achieve satisfactorily on their own, 
and where there is clear EU added value. As gamete donors, recipients and donor-conceived 
offspring are scattered throughout the EU, we see clear EU added value and a necessary need 
for ID release to be defined as a concept so that transperency is ensured even during 
distributions between member states. Each member state can independently legislate on 
whether ID release is allowed, required or prohibited within their own state.  
 
We propose the following wording:  
"ID release": Disclosure of personal indentifying information about a gamete donor to donor-
conceivedoffspring that has been confirmed to have been conceived with gametes from that 
donor.

Comments on Article 53 
 
European recognition and protection of a donor's consent (Art. 53[1a]) 
 
Under the current Directive on cells and tissues, situations often arise where a donor's 
consent in the recruitment country is not recognised in the recipient country. This means that 
a donor whose cells are transported to another Member State must complete national consent 
declarations. This practice discourages donors and leads to a reduction in both understanding 
and transparency. We propose that a separate provision be added under Chapter VI, stating 
that a donor's protection be regulated according to the country in which the donor is 
recruited. Thereafter, a donor cannot be subject to national requirements from the recipient 
country.  
………………………………….. 
 
Donor's right to privacy (Art. 53[1d]) 
 
Gamete donation does not only involve a donor and a recipient, but also any offspring 
conceived under such circumstances. In many countries, donor-conceived offspring have the 
opportunity to take advantage of the ID release process, where the identity of the donor used 
is disclosed. As the Regulation neglects to regulate such data disclosure, the donor’s right to 
privacy is not adequately protected.  
 
We recommend that Chapter VI is amended to state that the disclosure of a gamete donor’s 
identity must be conditional on the donor's specific consent to do so. This consent must 
 
specifically define what information may be disclosed to whom and under what conditions.   
For example, a donor can consent to the name, date of birth, and nationality being passed on 
to any offspring conceived with their gamete when the donor-conceived offspring are of legal 
age.  
This will create transparency for both recipients and donor-conceived offspring, as well as 
give gamete donors the autonomy to protect their privacy and decide over their own personal 
data following the rules of GDPR. 
 
Donation frequency (Art. 53, [1j]) 
 
It should be considered that several forms of donation, including sperm donation by the 
means of ejaculation does not carry a health risk. Therefore, it should be stated that Article 
53(1j) only applies to donations that may endanger the donor's health.

Paragraph 1 states that SoHO units must ensure that acquired pathogens, toxins or genetic

Comments on Chapter XI 
 
 
The introduction of an EU SoHO platform is, in many ways, a sensible measure to ensure both 
transparency and access to data. However, clarification is needed on the following:  
 
• It is requested that clarification be made of what information the SoHO platform must 
contain, as well as how it is collected and possibly disclosed.  Articles 44 and 47 state 
that activity data and SAO reports must be recorded, but the type of data is unclear.  
• Paragraph 45 of the preamble states that data on donors, recipients and donor-
conceived offspring should be pseudonymised and kept to the minimum necessary 
requirements.  However, paragraph 76 (76) of the Treaty follows from article 76 (2) 
that personal data, including health data, may be processed as  necessary for 
compliance with Articles 74 and 75.  In these articles, we find that it is  not specified 
when it is necessary to process personal data in the SoHO platform.  
• Paragraph46 of the preamble states that Member States should "entrust the 
appropriate competent authorities with the role of controller".  This need is not 
elaborated and we do not consider it to be either necessary or appropriate. 
Furthermore, it will hamper transparency towards donors and recipients, as well as 
hinder their both their access to and control over their own data.  
• Please note that any disclosure of data relating to donors should be affiliated with sec 
code, and not personally identifiable data. The latter should only be stored by the SoHO 
unit to which the donor is affiliated.  
 
In conclusion, we would again recommend the creation of a central register of gamete donors 
to replace national registers. This could be in continuation of the EU's SoHO platform.

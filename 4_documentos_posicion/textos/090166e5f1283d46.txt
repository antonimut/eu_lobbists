12 September 2022
Feedback to the EU Commission’s call for evidence 
Reform of the Union customs 
legislation 
In the light of globalization and growing pressure on supply chains, customs authorities are facing 
particular challenges in the course of their tasks. Not only does the number of traded goods continuously 
increase, but equally does the number of non-compliant and unsafe goods placed on the EU Single 
Market. This trend is further exacerbated by a rapidly growing e-commerce market, in particular during 
the pandemic.  
Against this background, the TÜV Association welcomes the intention of the European Commission to 
evaluate EU custom legislation and possibly adapting it to new developments. From the view of the TÜV 
organizations, the objective to ensure that imported products comply with EU product safety legislation 
and (future) due diligence requirements along the value chain is of particular importance. This equally 
applies to the identification of counterfeit products at the borders. These objectives require a close 
coordination and cooperation between customs authorities and market surveillance authorities. 
However, given the vast amount of products, both authorities are only able to carry out sample checks 
thus identifying only the tip of the iceberg of non-compliant products entering the EU Single Market. 
The services of independent assessment organisations can help to somewhat bridge this gap. By 
assessing the conformity of products before they are placed on the market, organisations such as the 
TÜV do effectively support the work of national authorities, which do not need to identify non-compliant 
or counterfeit products at a later stage. In doing so, they help to implement the precautionary principle as 
a cornerstone of EU product legislation. Moreover, this preventive approach is completely financed by the 
manufacturer, importer or distributor and thus reduces the need for further tax-financed intervention by 
national authorities. 
Independently issued certificates (e.g. test marks) create more transparency for consumers, other 
economic actors and national authorities alike. A certificate summarizes and documents a product’s 
compliance with safety, environmental or due diligence requirements in a transparent manner. By doing 
so, information asymmetries between manufacturers and customers are reduced, and consumers can 
better compare the functional and environmental characteristics of different products. Hence, the trust 
in product claims made by the manufacturer is substantially enhanced. This reduction of complexity 
equally facilitates that work of customs (and market surveillance) authorities, as they can immediately 
recognize that the product has been independently assessed thus being able to focus their limited 
resources to other products.

There are a number of existing services by independent assessment organizations that can support 
customs authorities in performing their current and future tasks. Here are two examples:  
1) Supplier qualification: traders can register as “trusted traders” with regard to their economic 
activities by having the completeness and correctness of relevant information (e.g. location, 
availability of stock, information of supply chain) independently assessed.  
2) Pre- & post-shipment inspection:  shipped goods (e.g. feedstock or commodities) are 
independently assessed with regard to the correct type, amount, quality class etc. at the port of 
departure or arrival on behalf of the importer or trader. A positive attestation/inspection report is 
only issued if there is no divergence between the declared data and the actual goods.   
As regards data provision, the Digital Product Passport as envisaged by the European Commission can be 
an effective instrument to reduce complexities for customs authorities. However, the prerequisite is that 
economic actors and authorities can fully rely on the validity of the data stored therein. Consequently, the 
correctness and completeness of the information to be incorporated in the Digital Product Passport 
should be independently assessed, for example by making use of the new and lean conformity instrument 
of validation and verification (ISO/IEC 17029). It is used to validate information that have been declared 
(i.e. declarations, claims, reports). Validation is applied to claims regarding an intended future use or a 
projected outcome (confirmation of plausibility). Verification is applied to claims regarding events that 
have already occurred or results that have already been obtained (confirmation of truthfulness). 
In summary, the services of independent assessment organizations do significantly contribute to the 
well-functioning of the EU Single Market (establishment of a level-playing field and creation of trust for 
market actors in the safety of products) and function in a complementary way to the checks and 
inspections of customs and market surveillance authorities and should be made use of.

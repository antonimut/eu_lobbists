 
 
 
 
 Response to Public Consultation August 2022 
 
EBC response to the public consultation  
on the revision of the Waste Framework Directive 
About EBC 
Established in 1990, the European Builders Confederation (EBC) is the European professional organisation 
representing national associations of construction crafts and micro, small and medium-sized enterprises. 
EBC is a member and partner of SMEunited, the European association of SMEs, and Small Business 
Standards (SBS), the European association representing SMEs in standardisation. 
The construction sector is of vital importance to the European economy. With 3 million enterprises and a 
total direct workforce of 18 million, the construction sector contributes at around 9% to the GDP of the 
European Union. 99.9% of the European construction sector is composed of micro, small and medium -
sized enterprises. 
 
 
General remarks 
For more than three deca des, EBC has been a fervent advocate of making the construction sector more 
sustainable, with rational ambitions to concretely improve its environmental footprint. In this context, 
EBC fully supports the hierarchy enshrined in the Waste Framework Directive 2008/98/EC (WFD), which 
gives priority to prevention, reuse, recycling and other types of recovery over disposal and landfill for 
all types of waste. This is also the case for construction and demolition waste (CDW), which is addressed 
hereunder. 
CDW accounts for around 35% of the overall waste generated in the EU. Aware of their environmental 
footprint, our SMEs therefore support the Commission in further augmenting the ambition for a more 
circular construction  sector, as inscribed in the European Green Deal  strategy, the Circular Economy 
Action Plan, and the Renovation Wave strategy. In this sense EBC welcomes the Commission’s intention 
to revise the WFD, with the aim to secure a coherent, realistic, and efficient waste management 
framework for construction and demolition waste. 
The WFD had established a target of 70% of CDW to be re cycled by 2020. During the past decade, the 
recovery rates of (non-hazardous) construction and demolition waste, namely the waste prepared for re-
use, recycling or subject to material recovery, has significantly improved across most Member States 1.

or recycling of construction and demolition waste, there are already good practices to learn from. 
In that vein, EBC supports the progressive ban on landfilling as detailed in the Landfill Waste Directive  
1999/31/EC, as long as suitable alternatives are accessible  within a close geographical area for 
construction companies and workers. 
However, among the most prominent hurdles to recycling and re -using CDW in the EU are the lack of 
confidence in the quality of secondary raw  materials and their price in comparison to new /virgin 
materials. This lack of confidence discourages the demand for secondary raw materials, which inhibits the 
development in the EU of industrial manufacturing processes allowing the valorisation and reintroduction 
of CDW.  
In addition, the application of overall circularity principles and methods to usual construction activities is 
a challenge that also requires an update of vocational education and training. Indeed, activities such as 
sorting and reuse require a continuous training that should be made available with adapted methods and 
tools for SMEs. 
 
Specific remarks 
Proximity principle for waste disposal 
Construction SMEs usually work in a small radius around their undertaking, with at periodic intervals a 
need to collect and transport their waste to specific facilities. However, too often they do not easily find 
collection or treatment facilities where to deposit their waste close to the construction site s or their 
businesses. This situation not only disproportionally affects their budget in terms of transportation costs, 
especially for companies managing small quantities of waste coming from disparate locations, but it also 
has a dissuasive impact on prices for customers . Additionally, long, and frequent journeys also have a 
significant ecological impact, which undermines the sector’s ambitions towards sustainability.  
Indeed, the lack of adequate facilities of proximity allowing companies t o deposit their construction 
waste remains a problem. These facilities must be adapted to the practices of the companies (e.g., 
specific opening hours, reception of construction vehicles) and be located near construction sites . For 
decades, this has been an important challenge faced by construction SMEs and crafts, who are too often 
obligated to drive long distances to dispose CDW safely and appropriately. Initiatives such as the EU 
Construction and Demolition Waste Protocol2 have been helpful for SMEs thereof, but more needs to be 
done. 
At the same time, it is essential to facilitate the establishment of new  complementary facilities adapted 
to transit, waste regrouping/storage platforms or waste collection/treatment centres. These new facilities

Alignment with standards for circular construction 
According to the WFD currently in force, “‘re-use’ means any operation by which products or components 
that are not waste are used again for the same purpose for which they were conceived .” Manufacturing 
re-usable products or preparing products for re-use requires human and financial resources, which leads 
to the necessity to ensure a stable market for these re-used products, by making sure that both inherent 
(e.g., performance characteristics) and external factors (e.g., higher prices of recycled materials, also due 
to transportation and processing costs) do not influence their attractivity. 
The reuse of construction products highlights the problem of their performance, their traceability, the 
conditions of their full placing again on the market, the liability of user companies and their compliance 
with the legal obligations arising from the revised Construction Products Regulation (CPR). If reusability 
of products gets overregulated  and overpriced, if it poses risks to business owners , this will dissuade 
construction companies from preferring them in relation to new products.  
Similar challenges arise when it comes to secondary raw materials derived from recycling of CDW. The 
vast majority of CDW is fit to be reused, recycled, or recovered in other ways. For instance, the waste 
generated from demolished concrete can be fully recycled locally and used again as an aggregate, for 
which there is usually a high demand on the market3. In that sense, the availability of a clear framework 
for the reuse of secondary raw materials  (paired with the local availability of appropriate waste 
management infrastructure) is key.  
In this sense, the revision of the CPR should be the instrument used to achieve the WFD targets related 
to secondary raw materials. The introduction of the use of secondary materials and life cycle assessment 
characteristics under basic requirements for construction works provide an opportunity to enable a solid

Extended Producer Responsibility 
The 2018 revision of the WFD provided a European definition of Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR). 
According to the Directive, EPR consists in a “set of measures taken by Member States to ensure that 
producers of produc ts bear financial responsibility or financial and organisational responsibility for the 
management of the waste stage of a product’s life cycle.” This is a clear EPR definition that provides for 
a stable EU framework to be maintained.  
EPR schemes have shown that they can be useful for certain waste streams (e.g., waste flows difficult to 
collect separately or complex to valorise) but their effectiveness is not to be taken as a given for all types 
of waste. Witnessing the introduction of  EPR schemes for different waste streams by certain Member 
States, including for CDW in certain countries (e.g., France), construction SMEs and crafts fear to face 
administrative and financial burdens stemming from the implementation of those EPR schemes. 
Therefore, the Commission should ensure a close monitoring of the EPR schemes for CDW implemented 
by Member States so that they are not detrimental to SMEs. 
Additionally, expanding EPR schemes inherently leads to the adverse effect of duplicating financing 
circuits at the expense of citizens, as the collection of some waste flows is financed from EPR systems, 
without any decrease of municipal taxes. To cope with th is issue, operational and financial 
responsibilities should be borne not only by producers, but also by consumers and municipalities, which 
also play a significant role in disposing waste. Due to the nature of the construction sector , notably the 
specific treatment, re-use or recycling leading to secondary construction materials, a shared responsibility 
system would be more adapted  to reality and would lead to a more efficient , circular, and sustainable 
construction. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Recommendations: 
• Retain the existing definition of Extended Producer Responsibility and the relevant provisions in 
Article 8 and 8A in the WFD. 
• Avoid t he application of EPR schemes resulting in additional administrative constraints 
practically inapplicable for construction SMEs, particularly in terms of the required traceability. 
Any potential measures relating to improving enforcement of existing EPR requirements must 
be proportionate.  
• Better consider the existing virtuous models  of waste management set up by the craft 
companies of the building sector in any new EPR organisations or Producer Responsibility 
Organisations. 
• Guarantee a territorial network of collection points  for building waste  throughout Member 
States’ territories (see “Proximity principle for waste disposal” section of this paper). 
• Promote the creation of  national observator ies of the impact of the EPR on the prices of 
materials (and therefore of costs impacting construction and renovation works), where 
applicable. This is especially important in the current inflationary state of the EU economy.  
 
Preparing for Re-use and Recycling 
Construction SMEs are fully aware of the role they must play to reduce the impact of their renovation and 
demolition activities on the environment. They thus support better waste management, which starts with 
proper sorting, despite technical challenges (e.g., when the building materials are entangled or glued to 
each other) due to the nature of construction works. Indeed, without sorting, no recycling or recovery is 
possible. 
During the previous revision that took place in 2018, Article 11 of the Directive gave a very specific 
framework for the re-use and recycling of CDW, stipulating that “Member States shall take measures to 
promote selective demolition in order to enable removal and safe handling of hazardous substances and 
facilitate re -use and high -quality recycling by selective removal of materials, and to ensure the 
establishment of sorting systems for construction  and demolition waste at least for wood, mineral 
fractions (concrete, bricks, tiles and ceramics, stones), metal, glass, plastic and plaster.” 
This framework is exhaustive, coherent and up to date, with no need of further changes. In fact, with the 
last revision taking place only a few years ago, several Member States are still in the process of creating  
or implementing the appropriate national tools to transpose those requirements. It is therefore crucial to 
maintain the existing framework, to ensure efficiency and stability for the market, especially during such 
troubled times. 
Recommendations: 
• Retain the existing provisions on preparing CDW for re-use and recycling. 
 
 
 
 
• Closely monitor the correct implementation of sorting systems by Member States following the 
2018 WFD revision and make sure that national provisions on sorting are adapted to the needs 
and practices of SMEs, notably microenterprises 
 
Supporting pioneering companies & promoting the sharing of best practices 
The best kind of waste is the waste that we never created. Article 9 of the Directive mandates Member 
States to  take measures to “ reduce waste generation in processes related to industrial production, 
extraction of minerals and construction and demolition, taking into account best available techniques”. 
In that spirit, construction companies that prevent and reduce the generation of waste on the ir 
construction sites should be actively supported and encouraged. The EU Construction and Demolition 
Waste Protocol and Guidelines issued by the European Commission offer a useful set of tools in this 
regard. As advised in the Protocol, t he companies that demonstrate their dedication to making a 
difference in the direction of preventing waste generation and applying the best available practices in 
managing CDW, should be highlighted, supported and promoted. This would not only reward companies 
that already act to reduce the waste generated on their construction sites, but also positively incentivize 
others to follow suit by leading by example. 
Although efficient practices and systems to manage construction and demolition waste do exist across 
Member States, construction SMEs – especially microenterprises - do not always have access to them  or 
have to put up with practices that are not adapted to them. Especially when it comes to renovation works, 
it is challenging to estimate the potential  quantity of waste that might result from the construction site. 
Fomenting the exchange of good practices on waste prevention and waste management and bringing 
together the se best practices  in a single database  would facilitate access to this knowledge  for 
construction professionals, especially SMEs, across the EU. 
Recommendations: 
• Establish an online platform available in all official EU languages where best-practice examples 
on deconstruction, renovation and construction waste recovery activities are explained and 
shared. Additionally, manufacturers c ould inform about the eco -design parameters of their 
materials/equipment and technology innovators could inform on modern technologies and tools 
to reduce waste. 
• Provide adequate European, national and regional funding for awareness -raising and training 
programmes or projects  on deconstruction works and waste prevention  activities in the 
buildings sector for construction professionals of all levels (white and blue collars; apprentices ; 
trades representatives), and in particular for on-site staff and small entrepreneurs. 
• Mandate Member States to financially support construction SMEs that prevent and reduce 
waste generation, notably by applying strict sorting methods or implementing re-use. 
 
 
 
 
 
Guarantee accurate and dependable CDW data across the EU 
The availability and reliability of data is becoming an ever more relevant element  not just for better 
European policy making for the built environment, but also for the work of our construction SMEs on the 
ground. Oftentimes, different definitions and/or methodologies are applied throughout the EU, which can 
make cross-country comparisons challenging or even impossible.  
For instance,  Eurostat publishes data every two years on the “Recovery rate of construction and 
demolition waste” as defined in Regulation (EC) No 2150/2002 on waste statistics. This data is derived 
from data gathered by national authorities, with methodologies for data collection that are not identical. 
To address this situation, t he Commission should provide guidance to streamline the data landscape of 
CDW across Member States , while also  calling upon Member States to carefully monitor the data 
collection process in order to ensure accuracy. 
Recommendations: 
• The Commission should issue Guidelines for a harmonised CDW data collection methodology, to 
ensure further reliability and comparability across the EU. 
• The Directive should mandate Member States to closely monitor CDW data collection to ensure 
data accuracy.

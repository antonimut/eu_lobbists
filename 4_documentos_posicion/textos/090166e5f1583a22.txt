Comments on the Call for Evidence for an Impact Assessment on the 
Reform of the Union Customs Legislation 
 
Intellectual property right holders rely on customs officers to target, control and detain shipments of illegal 
- including counterfeit, sub -standard and non -compliant - goods at the EU’s borders. The AIM Anti -
Counterfeiting Committee thanks the EU’s Customs Authorities for their excellent cooperation in fighting this 
global illegal trade over many years and is grateful to the Commission for consulting European industry on 
the future of the underlying customs legislation. Please note that we have restricted our comments to the 
fields of IP infringement and IP crime, both here and in our responses to the e-survey. 
 
The fight against counterfeiting is far from being only the protection of private rights. Due its proven links 
with cross-border organised crime, IP crime has been reinstated by the Member States as a priority under 
the European Multidisciplinary Platform Against Criminal Threats (EMPACT) for 2022-25. Europol’s IP Crime 
Co-ordination Coalition, with the EUIPO’s support, works with Member States, Law Enforcement Authorities 
(“LEAs”), OLAF and right holders to organise cross -border operations and intelligence gathering; in some 
circumstances it can also now receive intelligence directly from industry. 
 
Counterfeit goods are estimated by the OECD and EUIPO 1 to account for almost 6% of all imports by value 
into the EU, translating directly into tax and duties evasion. Multiple studies2 prove the high socio-economic 
damage of this illicit trade, in direct consumer harm (financial and physical), depletion of national revenues 
and legal EU employment and the use of illegally-gained profits to fund many other criminal activities (“poly-
criminality”)3. By default, no counterfeit product complies with EU legislation or standards.  As such we are 
collectively engaged in the fight against counterfeits to protect EU citizens. 
 
The EU’s innovators and creators, on whom we rely for European competi tiveness and to rebuild our 
economies after the current crises, do not only suffer reputational and financial loss, but are forced to divert 
ever more of their resources away from their core businesses into trying to clean on - and offline markets 
over whic h they have no control. Many micro and smaller companies simply cannot do so. Even our 
environment is endangered with the production and transportation of goods that should never have been 
made and cannot be sustainably destroyed or recycled as we do not know their raw materials. 
 
As such, we are rather concerned about the terminology in this consultation, which may be misinterpreted 
as downgrading the priority given by EU Customs to the fight against counterfeiting, despite repeated high-
level recognition of the importance of IP as one of Europe’s most important intangible assets.  
 
1. Concerns as to the omission of “illegal” and/or “counterfeit” goods in the call for evidence 
 
“Intellectual property” is mentioned only once in the call for evidence, and in a negative context as one of 
the “new tasks” that customs struggle to enforce. “Illegal” products are again mentioned only once. 
References to “counterfeit” goods are an obvious omission throughout the text.

2. Refocussing customs’ resources 
 
We fully recognise that customs, indeed all LEAs, are over -worked and under-resourced, and that customs 
officers cannot physically control more than 1-2% of shipments. However, we also know that stopping illegal 
goods at the border is far more efficient than tracking them down once dispersed throughout the internal 
market. Further, the official reports published by the Commission and EUIPO show that detentions of 
counterfeit items at our borders continue to fall, which seems to be due to three main reasons: (1) the focus 
on small, rather than volume, consignments; (2) the lack of pre-arrival data; and (3) the low political priority 
afforded to training and deploying IPR-expert officers. 
 
In 20204, 85% of customs’ IPR cases were in the post/courier channels, rendering only 5% of detained articles, 
while sea/road transport accounted for 87% of articles but only 2.8% of cases. Rebalancing towards controls 
on large volume cargo is essential; many containers are filled with small items, e.g. stock for fulfilment 
centres, parcels pre -affranchised by the EU’s post offices in third countries or small packages for onward 
distribution. To be able to target effectively suspect containers, customs nee d pre-arrival data to feed into 
their risk analysis.  
 
Such targeted controls would maximise their success, reducing the burden on all LEAs and prevent the 
evasion of tax and duties, thus supporting customs’ main role as collectors of public revenue. More data dots 
could then be joined together as customs should log and store data on all their detentions, including small 
consignments, so everyone in the wider law enforcement families could update their own risk analysis and 
targeting methods. 
 
Right holders do not have visibility into illicit supply and trading channels; instead, this essential pre -arrival 
data is owned by, or at least visible to, the supply chain intermediaries, including: shipping and other 
transport and logistics oper ators; export, import and freight forwarding agents; customs intermediaries; 
postal and express courier operators; and online intermediaries, including platforms. Such businesses know, 
or should know, their business customers, with whom they have contracts . They should be able to verify 
shipping documentation, have details of repeat offenders and, if they have fulfilment centres and distribution 
networks, also have manifests and picking lists. The proactive, pre -arrival sharing of certain shipping and 
customer data with law enforcement should be standard commercial practice. Please see AIM’s submission 
to your customs risk management consultation from 2021 for further detail. 
 
Information shared by customs with right holders is also a challenge. In some Member States there is zero 
visibility on illicit goods being seized; for example, some customs will send co unterfeit medicines to the 
Ministry of Health without reference to pharma companies. These products are then written off as 
unregistered medicines without ever informing the companies. If the detention procedure were to be more 
streamlined, with detention notices administered via IPEP, customs could rapidly send to the right holder the

We fully appreciate the complexities of data sharing, thus we - all - urgently need official guidance from the 
Commission on how and when data can be shared in the IP infringement and crime context, with and 
between law enforcement, and when appropriate from and with the private sector. The GDPR does not apply 
to legal persons’ data and, like all data privacy laws, it was never intended to act as a shield for illegal activity.  
 
With more effective risk management and targeting, as well as greater attention given to training all customs 
officers in the holistic effects of  counterfeiting as described above, we believe that scarce resources would 
be better deployed by both public and private sectors. Importers of illegal products are at present 
deliberately selecting entry points in the Customs Union where they know the loca l authorities are 
insufficiently equipped both in terms of knowledge and resources.  
 
We respectfully call upon the Commission to impress on the Member States the need for appreciation of the 
importance of IP enforcement by their Customs Authorities and to  support any relevant capacity building; 
right holders of course stand ready to assist in any way that we can. 
 
3. Specific comments on the call for evidence 
 
3.1 Problem the initiative aims to tackle / objectives and policy options 
• Customs do not yet function “as one”: Despite EU legislation, especially the Customs IPR Regulation 
608/2013 and the Trade Mark Regulation 2017/1001, there are many differences between Member 
States in terms of IPR enforcement with different policies, stra tegic and operational approaches and 
appreciation for, and priority given to, IPR.  
 
There are vastly different (often non -transparent) expenses and processes for identification and 
detention of products (tallying, storage , haulage, transportation to destruc tion facilit ies, destruction 
attendance and processes  etc.). There are also clear contradictions between Article 23 of Regulation 
608/2013 and Articles 197 and 198(1)(b)(iv) of Regulation 952/2013 ( Union Customs Code). The latter 
provides: “The costs of the destruction shall be borne by the holder of the good s” and “ The customs 
authorities shall take any necessary measures, including confiscation and sale, or destruction, to dispose 
of goods… where the goods cannot be released for any of th e following reasons… (iv) the goods are 
subject to prohibitions or restrictions”. For clarity, the latter does not place responsibility for these costs 
on Customs Authorities, or on the right holders, but on the holder of the goods.  Commission guidance 
on this conflict would be appreciated. 
 
There are differences in the numbers of times customs detain products and/or contact right holders, 
methods of communication, disposal o r return o f samples and practices in providing photographs of 
detentions (quality, quantity and responsiveness to requests for additional images). There are also many 
differences between ports/airports/border posts in each country, often dependent on expertise and 
resources.  
 
In those countries where customs have a wider appreciation of IPR, collaborate closer with the other 
local LEAs and actively cooperate with right holders, e.g. in requesting intelligence to create risk profiles, 
there is a far better use of resources on all sides.

We would be grateful if the Commissi on could issue Guidelines on the harmonised application of 
Regulation 608/2013, given these disparate practices. As to strengthening common risk management, 
please see our comments above and our response to your 2021 customs risk management consultation. 
• Customs work has shifted from the traditional revenue collection: While we fully appreciate the 
workload and lack of resources of customs, we respectfully note the amount of revenue that is lost due 
to the counterfeit trade. In 2020, the EUIPO’s Status Report on  IPR Infringement5 estimated that each 
year, EU governments lose EUR 15 billion in direct and indirect taxes and social security contributions 
due to the presence of counterfeit goods in the EU marketplace in just 11 industry sectors. Please see 
the comments above concerning the inter-relation of IPR with other key policies, including security and 
crime. 
• Cooperation between customs and other sectoral authorities : We would strongly support better 
cooperation between all LEAs.  
• Information and data management: We fully agree that these need to be improved. One key tool is IPEP, 
which should allow all operational officers to have secure access to right holders, both for information 
and data as well as two -way communications. Neither right holders nor customs ha ve the resources to 
duplicate keying in to, or consulting, multiple databases. IPEP should also allow customs’ notifications 
and feedback to right holders, so no data is lost and trends/alerts can be logged . Customs can also log 
and share all their detention data, including for small consignments, which can then feed into wider LEA 
risk management. Unfortunately, as yet right holders see little use of IPE P by customs, especially the 
operational officers who would most benefit from this collaborative tool. We strongly support its wider 
deployment to all of the EU’s customs officers. Please see further comments above. 
• The rise of e -commerce: Please see the comments above re small consignments, as well as online 
intermediaries and postal and express courier operators (etc.) providing pre-arrival data. 
• We are pleased to see the (one) reference to “ protecting the single market from illegal products”, but 
kindly note our concerns that this is sadly missing elsewhere in the text. Counterfeits are illegal. 
• We trust that in any “EU layer which could, for example: Better implement the “risk appetite” or risk 
priorities identified at policy and political level ” the fight against counterfeiting will be recognised for 
its multiple socio-economic negative effects as outlined above, and its high priority at political level. 
• We would fully support “ strengthening supervision of business -to-consumer flows and liability of 
involved actors for all fiscal and non-fiscal rules” in the e-commerce field. 
• The “ green agenda in the customs agenda and traders’ behaviours ” must include  addressing the 
difficulties in destroying counterfeit goods when we do not know their composition. A network of 
approved and audited destruction/recycling facilities is needed. 
 
3.2 Likely impacts and consultation 
While we agree with the positive likely impac ts, we must once again stress that the fight against 
counterfeiting would contribute to all of your bullet points. We also regret that there is no specific mention 
of right holders in the consultation strategy, but trust that both this document and our response to the 
questionnaire will be of practical benefit. 
 
Once again, we thank all EU Customs Authorities for their long -standing cooperation with right holders and 
look forward to reinforcing this relationship and the fight against counterfeiting going forward.

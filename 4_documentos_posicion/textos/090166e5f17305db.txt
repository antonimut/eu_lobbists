Background 
As the institutional representative of over 45 million businesses, reaching more than 170 countries, 
the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) operates with a mission to make business work for 
everyone, every day, everywhere.  
We are the primary voice of the real economy in a range of intergovernmental organisations—from 
the World Trade Organisation to the UN climate process—championing the needs of local business 
in global decision making. 
The convening power of our global network enables us to set rules and standards that facilitate over 
US$10 trillion dollars in trade each year— in addition to providing tailored products and digital 
services that directly address the real challenges faced by businesses operating internationally.  
As critical stakeholders in supply chains and international trade, c ustoms and its operations are 
what connects the flow of goods across borders and thus facilitate international trade.  
As observers to the World Customs Organization (WCO), ICC regularly provides input to the  
development of customs policies and guidance.  
With many of our members operating within the EU or importing/exporting from outside of the EU, 
we are delighted to provide input to the call for evidence for the upcoming reform of the Union 
customs legislation.   
The growing scope of customs – e.g., covering sustainability, forced labour  – combined with 
significant changes in trade and technology – such as the rise of e -commerce leading to higher 
volumes of imports and exports – have compounded pre- existing challenges that customs are 
facing. Geopolitical tensions are also playing a role in many of the changes we are witnessing today 
and have highlighted the urgency of an effective customs union that is “fit-for-purpose”.1   
While challenges have grown, there are also more opportunities to leverage technology and simplify 
processes that will facilitate more trade – enabling economic growth and prosperity. 
1. Stakeholder engagement matters 
While we appreciate the opportunity to provide input to the call for evidence for an impact 
assessment in  relation to the reform of the EU Customs Union legislation, we regret the short 
timeframe that was chosen over a holiday period. This applies in particular for entities, such as ICC, 
that are functioning on a consensus basis.

2. The gold standard: effective and coherent implementation + capacities 
Right now, the EU Customs Union (still) signifies 27 countries with 27 customs, operating in different 
judicial systems – this has led to varying levels of implementation of EU customs regulations across 
Member States. While the principle of subsidiarity is important for the Union, the strong differences 
in implementation and application of EU customs legislation – even when it comes to the treatment 
of non-compliance and sanctions – are negatively impacting the business environment and inviting 
anti-competitive practices to take hold on the market. 
According to the wise persons report, today, more than 350 pieces of EU legislation treat prohibitions 
and restrictions across a wide range of policy areas. The tasks of cus toms authorities continue to 
grow, in the EU there are environmental and social legislations  coming up ( e.g., Carbon Border 
Adjustment Mechanism, forced labour regulation), thus  it critical that adequate investments in 
capacities and capabilities of customs are done.  
ICC is in strong support of sustainability and climate policies that address the emergency the planet 
is facing, and customs are playing a role in the support of the green agenda. But at the same time, 
there is also significant potential for trade facilitation to address climate and sustainability issues, 
e.g., effective digitalisation and effective clearance processes reduce port waiting times. “Smarter 
controls” save customs’ resources that can be used for other purposes, but also help with the green 
agenda.  
Overall, customs can contribute to green objectives via the change of customs processes by using 
less paper-based procedures and increased use of modern technology. 
Further, customs controls could be carried out in a more sustainable way , e.g., efficient and risk -
based planning of inspections to reduce emissions, as well as carrying out as much as possible 
remote inspections. As scanning technology is continuously advancing, physical checks could often 
be replaced by scanning, reducing the need for travel. Customs may install scanners in their facilities 
to avoid moving trucks for controls and to present the declaration for exports. 
Investments are needed, not only in equipment, but also in capacity-building for customs authorities 
to ensure a more coherent implementation and application of legislation. The planned widening of 
scope for customs, further stretching their capacities, must be accompanied by appropriate 
investments. 
Ensure achievable timeframes  
The trade community, at the national and international level, is supporting customs modernisation 
efforts, and it is critical that adequate timeframes for the implementation of reforms are set to allow 
for continuous input from stakeholders. Establishing adequate platforms/opportunities and 
timeframes allows for trade stakeholders to provide critical inputs into the development process, 
which will contribute to a more meaningful outcome. 
In addition to providing adequate review and comment on proposed reforms, importers and 
exporters are challenged by the need to, sometimes simultaneously, implement and “go‐live” on new 
IT systems and/or IT systems changes at the Member S tate (MS) level. Today, M ember States  
operate on different timeframes,  and simultaneous cross MS  IT system implementation and “go ‐
live” is of critical interest for the trade stakeholders. 
Building an effective EU Customs Authority  
As provided in the Wise Person Group report, “Under existing arrangements, responsibility for 
customs policy is at the level of the European Union whereas implementation and controls on the

ground are the responsibility of Member States.”2 Both EU and international companies struggle with 
differences in treatment at the MS level on critical topics like classification and valuation.  
Without adding more requirements and bureaucracy on top of existing national processes, ICC 
recommends the cr eation of an EU level customs agency to assure uniform interpretation and 
implementation of regulations, operational processes, systems rollouts, and customs controls across 
all MS ports of entry. It would enable a more uniform implementation across MS, and further enable 
the use of economies of scale in procurement.  
The current approach of having multiple MS customs authorities operating with differing 
interpretations of laws and regulations and using different IT systems results in increased complexity 
and fragmentation, potentially doubling (or more) the cost of conducting business in the EU for 
importers and exporters. 
An EU customs authority has the potential to help streamline processes, and thus simplifying 
administrative processes required for the c ross-border movement of goods, thereby unlocking the 
potential for economic prosperity, while limiting the movement of illicit goods through more effective 
risk-management at borders.  
ICC strongly believes that the competences of an EU Customs Authority need to be  defined very 
clearly – to help solve problems and help – without adding another administrative process to cross-
border trade (need to be a “helping” authority instead of being an additional step). 
Improved coordination at borders 
The large increase in non-fiscal customs tasks requires improved coordination at the border. Today, 
border agencies often work in silos, using unaligned systems and processes, leading to inefficient 
handling of cross -border trade and procedures. This complex regulatory setting generates 
burdensome reporting obligations for trade.  
It is increasingly important to make a clear distinction between the various customs non-fiscal tasks 
and carry out customs controls on risk-based methodologies inland. To legislate under a Regulation 
instead of a Directive could also help to achieve the same controls and prohibitions, and even more 
so for cases where the goods are in free circulation. 
3. Harness the potential of data with the right frameworks in place 
The digitalisation of customs and trade processes is of vital importance  to the global business 
community, which is why ICC has set up the ICC Digital Standards Initiative (DSI) to drive  the 
establishment of a globally harmonised digital trade environment.  
Currently, there are burdensome administrative requirements, and duplications in relation to data 
entry and management – a fragmented landscape with 27 different and unconnected IT systems – 
without any benefit to any of the stakeholders. Further, it creates an environment in which the ability 
to control risks is very limited, due to the poor quality of data  and inefficient processes that must  
handle growing volumes of moving goods.  
Businesses and customs authorities alike would benefit from digitalised processes that make use of 
digital trade documents, simplified processes  that due to improved  data management, enable 
better tracking of the cross -border movement of goo ds. For example, for recurrent business 
transactions, periodic collective declarations, e.g., monthly, could be envisioned and would result in 
reduced data sets  – a lower administrative burden  and more effective risk management, freeing 
valuable customs resources to focus on other areas.

While ICC is a strong supporter of the digitalisation of trade, the issue of appropriate data and 
appropriate data sharing must be considered. It is critical, from an economic security and fair 
competition perspective, that confidential business information (CBI) is protected.  
ICC supports the sharing of data for law enforcement purposes under appropriate legal authorities 
and mechanisms, however, there is also a need to establish necessary guardrails  and frameworks 
to prohibit the unauthorised use or access of data.  
Single Window  
One single entry point for data and information – a single window (SW) –  with all border agencies 
connected would offer a real benefit for businesses and authorities alike in tackling the 
fragmentation of data management and further improving inter-agency cooperation for a more 
effective border management. 
The current vision of the SW in the EU  could potentially result in 27 different, separate SW 
applications by the MS. If each MS is solely responsible for programming its own SW application, the 
Commission should provide clear guidelines to ensure comparable functioning and more 
importantly interoperability. 
However, it is critical to establish the necessary legal structures , linked to for example SW  
interoperability, to protect confidential commercial data. That is why, data protection must be 
addressed during the initial stages of developing the legal agreements and governance applied to 
interoperability of MS customs and other government agency systems.  
In circumstances, where an international development agency provides SW support to either an EC 
MS and/or another country, at the end of the development process, the system must be returned to 
the government. Further, it is important to prepare for ongoing maintenance /operation of the SW 
to remain up-to-date and function effectively.  
Single Windows can help reduce errors that risk occurring with more manual data entries  and 
processing and because of the use of customs brokers or similar services, many companies lack the 
visibility over their import/export data and thus errors are more difficult to identify and correct. An 
effective SW will enable companies to have a better overview of their data, reduce duplications, 
and in the long-term, enable a more effective risk management process with less errors. 
Information management 
Improved data management should also extend to how information is shared with stakeholders, for 
example, ongoing changes of classification and related tariff codes may occur in the EU . These 
changes can be due to anti-dumping measures or other reasons, but the main issue for companies 
is that these changes are communicated at very short notice, sometimes literally over night . 
Additionally, there is no clear channel/notification system to receive the information, it is mostly only 
published in the EU official journal, within a flood of other information, basically hidden and not easily 
accessible – often leading to bad surprises at import.  
Many companies are depending on external information sources to learn about these changes, 
which they see as contrary to the TFA Art. 1.1 that envisions all information and documents to be 
easily accessible for everyone.  With the upcoming reform, the re is the opportunity for improved 
information sharing that would lead to real improvements to the business environment – and it could 
be done by a newly established EU Customs Authority.  
4. Better understanding e-commerce 
With e -commerce having grown exponentially these last years, it is critical to understand its 
functioning in order to design the most adequate policies that protect consumers from harmful 
products, while also shielding the market and businesses from market-distorting  practices that risk

taking hold. In this context, ICC stands ready to bring stakeholders together to facilitate discussions 
and we recommend further stakeholder engagement and discussions.  
ICC encourages the EC to undertake a concerted effort to understand who has what data, and 
what data is actually necessary to effectively regulate e-commerce transactions. Efforts at the U.S. 
domestic level and as part of ongoing pilots (i.e., entry type 8 6 and 321) are intended to inform 
international efforts. We encourage consideration of U.S. pilot developments and final outcomes as

5. Safety and security – illicit trade 
The “best” regulations can be adopted and ratified, but they are only as good as their 
implementation. A stronger focus should  be put on proper implementation of existing standards, 
regulations, and agreements  because the poor capacities and implementation will lead to the 
persistence and increase in risks.  
There is a gap between policy objectives and the reality on the ground/deliverables. For example, it 
is estimated that more than 90% of chemicals in consumer products that are non-compliant with 
the EU regulation concerning the Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of 
Chemicals (REACH) come from outside the EU/European Economic Area (EEA) and at 25% restricted 
phthalates are the most frequent case of non compliance, and mostly found in children’s toys.3   
An ineffective Customs Union means that the weakest link can be exploited for the trade of illicit 
goods, that can range from counterfeit medicine to children’s toys or strollers that are made of toxic 
materials not in compliance with EU standards. A functioning and effective EU Customs Union is not 
only in the interest of businesses, but it is crucial for consumers and their protection.  
At the same time, it must be ensured that legitimate traders are not burdened by ever increasing 
requirements in (the very important) effort to close those weak links/loopholes. The facilitation of 
legitimate trade and thus simplification, especially for AEOs, must also be a focus. 
The EU Commission in collaboration with the private sector should evaluate and determine whether 
new and emerging technologies can support the Customs Union in controlling the safety and 
security risks associated with the growth of e -commerce. These new and emerging technologies 
may include blockchain and artificial intelligence (AI) solutions.  As new technologies are considered, 
engagement with the private sector is critical and tests or pilots must take place. 
In addition, the EU customs authorities should strengthen their cooperation with other enforcement 
agencies (e.g., OLAF) to develop a comprehensive customs and regulatory framework for reducing 
the growth of illicit trade in the EU (e.g., illicit tobacco). 
6. Unlocking the potential of Authorized Economic Operator (AEO) programmes 
There is a need for improving AEO programmes to ensure more harmonisation in relation to the 
handling of AEO assessments, monitoring and the granting of waivers –  as well as the benefits for 
certified companies. Currently, there is a gap between the expected benefits for AEOs and the  
reality, making many companies question the burdensome endeavour of becoming or renewing 
their AEO status.  
It is urgent for the European Commission to ensure ongoing process improvements and noticeable 
simplifications that lead to trade facilitation for AEOs that have put in the significant efforts for their

7. Unified Customs Code (UCC) and ATA Carnet system 
The UCC was amended to include a decentralised process for digital ATA Carnets. ICC, through its 
special body, the World Chambers Federation (WCF), administers the international guarantee 
chain, while the WCO monitors the international conventions that govern ATA Carnets and the 
temporary admission scheme. ICC further established a World ATA Carnet Council as the meeting 
and administrative platform for all National Guaranteeing Associations (NGAs) around the world. 
We appreciate the support received by DG TAXUD to focus all efforts on the ongoing WCO 
supported ICC WCF eATA pilot and project. 
8. Going forward 
Currently, the modus operandi for customs differs across Member States – similar cases will receive 
different treatment i n terms of rules, times of issu ing of authorisations, compliance, control 
management – which is contrary to the idea of a Customs Union. While still respecting the 
subsidiarity principle, there is a real opportunity to create the “EU Customs Union of the future” that 
is adapted to respond to today’s challenges.  
ICC stands ready to continue engaging with the European Commission to provide the voice and 
expertise of the real economy, drawing from our diverse network that covers all sectors and regions 
in the world.

The Commission opened this inquiry seeking suggestions on how to build a more efficient and 
coordinated compulsory licensing scheme in the EU, reduce current fragmentation and improve 
Europe's resilience in managing crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic. 
 
Intellectual property has been a major positive contributor to the creation of health solutions to 
the crisis. Supply or availability issues resulting from the recent COVID -19 pandemic were 
questions of supply chain and other logistical readiness. We believe that the role of the 
Commission should b e to develop and implement policies which increase investment in 
research and development of innovative therapeutics in the EU. Introducing a unified approach 
to compulsory licensing will not achieve the stated goals of the Commission and will discourage 
future investment.   
 
Dealing with the Covid pandemic has required the rapid development, production and 
distribution of new vaccines, therapeutics and diagnostics (and other products such as PPE).  
Many of these products are entirely new; others are older products which have been repurposed 
for use in the treatment of Covid. 
 
There are now 47 v accines1 and 37 therapeutics approved and distributed in the EU (and 
globally) and 569 vaccines2 and 1776 therapeutics are in the development pipeline.  Although 
the non-profit sector has played an important role, most of these vaccines and therapeutics 
would not have been developed without significant contributions by the innovative 
pharmaceutical industry over the past years and decades.3   
 
The development, manufacture and distribution of Covid countermeasures has been 
characterized and enabled by: 
1. A foundation of scientific and technical knowledge, know-how and logistical expertise, 
built up by years of previous investment. 
2. Rapid commitment and deployment of significant resources (manpower and financial) 
by the private sector.4  
3. Collaboration between all stakeholders involved in the development and manufacturing 
chains. A very large number of the se collaborations are between private sector

Without the existence of robust IP (particularly patents and know-how protection), these critical 
countermeasures would not have been available. IP is critical to enabling the private sector to 
commit resources to very risky development and manufacturing.  When a product  is 
commercialized, the IP system enables  the generation of commercial returns on investment. 
These returns on investment are needed to fund further innovation.  
  
In the biopharmaceutical sector, various factors are considered in deciding wh ether to commit 
funds to research and development projects, from commencement of a project through 
development of a product. These factors naturally include anticipated benefits to patients in 
need and the scientific risks of being able to develop a product which will meet safety and 
efficacy requirements for regulatory approval.  They also include recurring financial 
considerations such as the ability to make an adequate commercial return on R&D investment, 
should a product reach the market.  That commercial return is what fuels the innovative 
biopharmaceutical sectors ongoing and future research efforts. Many factors impact the ability 
to make a commercial return, including the actual and likely presence of competitive innovative 
products on the market and, in the EU, pricing and reimbursement rules and procedures.   
  
Compulsory licences expropriate the value of patents (and any other IP that may be subject to 
a compulsory licence). They remove the ability to make commercial returns based on market 
factors and, in effect, replace those returns with a sum deemed by the state granting authority 
to be appropriate. They essentially replace market value with a royalty and these determinations 
are divorced from the underlying reality of the costs of innovative research and development. 
Any increased risk of compulsory licensing in a major market such as the EU, increases the risk 
of R&D investments becoming unsustainable.  
 
Compulsory licences therefore inherently undermine the incentive effect of patents (and  other 
IP). The extent to which they do so will depend on the frequency with which they may be 
granted and the commercial impact of each granted compulsory licence (which will be 
influenced by such factors as territorial scope, duration and the number of r ights at risk).  As 
the risk of a compulsory licence being granted and its impact on commercial returns increases, 
particularly in a major market, so the incentive to invest in biopharmaceutical innovation will 
decrease. Further, because collaborations are  largely based on the “sharing” of IP, increasing 
risks associated with compulsory licences will also undermine collaboration.  
 
Member States already have extensive powers to grant compulsory licences. 5 With one 
exception6, these were not used or threatened to be used during the Covid crisis.

When considering changes to existing compulsory licensing regimes, it is important to consider 
both any potential benefits vs the risk to innovation, as well as whether these benefits could be 
achieved without the extreme measure of compulsory licensing. It is also important to 
appreciate that a compulsory licence can only possibly deal with a specific situation; for 
example, it might allow production and sale of a specific product, in a specific market, for a 
defined period of time. However, a legislative change that increases the risk and/or impact of a 
compulsory licence has a broader, systemic effect, in that it will influence  decisions on 
investment in innovation more generally; the increased possibility of a compulsory licence will 
disincentivise all investment decisions 7, even those that would never in fact lead to grant of 
compulsory licence. An increased risk or impact of  a compulsory licence will therefore be 
detrimental to the aim of building or maintaining an innovative environment.   
 
Although Member States have different laws and procedures for granting compulsory licences, 
there is no evidence that these laws or their differences have been an impediment to expanding 
treatment during the Covid crisis or at any other time. Although hypothetical problems have 
been raised during discussions, there is no evidence that these have caused real world problems 
or are likely to do so. Any changes to compulsory licensing laws which increase the risk or 
impact of compulsory licensing in the EU will adversely affect biopharmaceutical innovation. 
If the Commission proceeds with this initiative, it is critical to ensure that specific real-world 
problems with existing laws are identified  and to objectively assess how any proposed CL 
changes would improve the outcomes. Any changes should be specifically targeted to deal with 
those problems. Furthermore, those changes should be the only w ay to deal with those 
problems; if there are other ways of dealing with these problems, they should generally be used, 
instead of increasing the risk of impact of compulsory licensing. 
 
It is also notable that the Commission has published its proposal for a Regulation on 
countermeasures in the event of a public health emergency at Union level (the HERA 
Regulation).  That proposal, which draws on lessons learned from the Covid crisis, is intended 
to provide a “framework for ensuring the supply of crisis-relevant medical countermeasures of 
measures in the event of a public health emergency, in order to enable the Union to take 
measures that are necessary to ensure the sufficient and timely availability and supply of crisis-
relevant medical countermeasures in t he event of a public health emergency where that is 
appropriate to the economic situation.”8

The proposed HERA Regulation contains provisions relating to intellectual property, including 
facilitation of voluntary licensing of IP relevant to medical countermeasures. However, there is 
no reference to compulsory licensing in the Explanatory Memorandum, or the summary report 
of the consultation undertaken before the HERA proposal was published or , with a limited 
exception9, the HERA proposal itself. That is an indication that changes to existing compulsory 
licence laws are not needed for at least public health crises as they are defined in the proposal. 
Further, unless the Commission believes that its proposal is inadequate, the proposal (which we 
largely support) takes away any need to revise the emergency framework for public health 
crises.  
 
The intellectual property system is one factor taken into account, particularly by large 
companies, in deciding where to place innovation investment.  The EU competes fo r 
biopharmaceutical research investment with other countries, most notably the United States 
(US-based innovation has led to more innovative molecules than the EU  between 2014 and 
201810) and more recently China. It is disappointing that in the last few years, the Commission, 
instead of championing European innovation , has introduced, promoted or is considering 
introducing a series of measures which have undermined incentives for biopharmaceutical 
incentives in the EU and globally. This possible initiative on compulsory licensing is a further 
example of what appears to be a developing trend. Taken together, these initiatives may put 
pressure on future investment in innovation in the EU. Not only would this adversely affect the 
economy of the EU, b ut it would also undermine EU public health in crisis and non -crisis 
situations. 
 
Instead of pursuing this initiative, we urge that the Commission continues to engage with all 
relevant stakeholders to consider how best to  
 
• facilitate and incentivise R&D and collaborations to develop countermeasures to future 
health crises, 
• promote EU p roduction of countermeasures (particularly by facilitating scale up of 
production by innovators and others already involved in production of 
countermeasures), 
• strengthen supply chains.   
In contrast to focusing on compulsory licensing, which can only have harmful long-term effects, 
appropriate measures taken to achieve these objectives (such as, potentially, HERA and 
EUFAB) will protect EU citizens and more generally promote innovation and manufacture in 
the EU.

We strongly support this initiative to develop a common framework for measuring GHG 
emissions from freight and passenger transport services, provided that the common 
framework accommodates for: 
 Accounting of emissions based on primary data as opposed to modelling 
(“calculating”) emissions based on general emission intensity factors. 
 Allocation of emissions to the value added by the transportation and storage 
activities, being the translocation of goods and/or persons from origin to 
destination. 
 Avoiding carbon leakage by allocating the emissions from the full Energy lifecycle 
(well-to-wheel) to the transport activities. 
 The aggregation of data from subcontracted segments (legs) in supply chains or 
transport chains to integrated outputs. 
 Support for carbon accounting (e.g., as part of the Corporate Sustainability 
Reporting Directive (CSRD)), carbon pricing and carbon trading, which means 
there is a verification system is needed. 
 Integration of recording and accounting for CO2 in standard IT systems used in 
the industry to minimize the increase in costs and administrative efforts. 
 Support for a “ladder” of participation and data quality, accommodating the wide 
variety in IT-maturity of companies in Member States, especially SME’s. This 
makes a mandatory standard of GHG emission possible on short notice.  
 KPI’s that acknowledge the shared responsibility of shippers and transporters in 
logistics for the results via their collaboration in a supply chain. 
 KPI’s that do not disturb or can be abused in the a-symmetrical power relationship 
in purchasing decisions of transport 
 
For the last 6 years Topsector Logistics has been active in testing and developing the 
practice of applying the methodology first described by the COFRET working group, and 
lately incorporated in the ISO14083 standard.  
 
The result of our efforts, including running implementation projects with more than 600 
logistics companies, is a complete package of: 
 A GHG emission calculation and allocation methodology that fulfills of the 
demands listed above and has been applied in practice thoroughly. 
 A complete set of implementation guidelines in multiple languages. 
 An ISO compliance check for carbon footprinting software, including test datasets. 
 Auditing guidelines for SME accountants. 
 
All free of charge available at www.carbonfootprinting.org.

Based on our extensive practical experience we advise the committee to take the 
ISO14038 as the base for a CE norm.  
 
We are confident about this advice because the methodology is successfully being used 
by more than 600 companies for 90 different types of supply chains: 
 All modalities, including maritime, air, rail inland shipping and road 
 International, continental, and local distribution up to last mile delivery 
 All kinds of goods (bulk, container, distribution, e-commerce, food, plants, 
chemicals etc.) 
 All kinds or roles (producer, retailer, 4PL, forwarder, transporter, platform, etc.) 
 All size of companies, form small SME to multinational 
 
Implementation projects range from initial insights based on primary data (150 
companies) to benchmarking with communities of peers (450 companies). This covers 
the large majority of what is the current practice. 
 
We believe the success of the COFRET methodology is not only that it is fair, but that it is 
also easy to start with and provides valuable insight to the organization for more 
advanced users. This is crucial for large scale adoption by commercial organizations. 
 
In our experience the main reasons for transport service providers to not measure and/or 
disclose GHG emission are: 
1. Effort and costs involved 
2. No added value due to lack of demand

3. Data restrictions 
4. Unclarity about what GHG calculation and allocation method will be the

5. Lack of trust in supply chain partners 
 
The methodology is designed such that the cost involved are initially low and ultimate 
potential business value are very high. Key element is removing the threshold to start 
due to imperfect data. We have found a solution to the practical problem by introducing 
and testing an easy-to-use data quality framework that adds meta-data to 
measurements. The meta-data percolates through the aggregated accounting of the 
results, and gives a quality assessment of the resulting number: what percentage is 
derived from high quality data (data quality level ‘Gold’), and what percentage from 
lower quality sources (data quality level ‘Silver’ and ‘Bronze’)? This makes its quality very 
transparent and comparable to others. It allows organizations to start and continuously 
improve. Additionally, it allows auditors to certify the results.

Eventually, organizations can use information from FMS and TMS systems and generate a 
wealth of (digital) data. Not only does this allow GHG emission allocation to be performed 
with great precision, but it also gives very valuable insight in a company’s efficiency. It is 
quite common that organizations improve their financially performance by analyzing their 
GHG emissions in detail.  
 
By using primary data, even on an initially high aggregation level, makes the 
methodology more accurate opposed to modelling (“calculating”) emissions based on 
general emission intensity factors. Additionally, it is very common practice that gaps of 
information occur due to ad-hoc sub-sub-contracting of activities. Collecting primary data 
from these SME’s or self-employed contractors is currently cumbersome. Using the so 
called “emission intensity factors” as promoted by many to fill the gap renders misleading 
or even very wrong results. We have developed the so called COFRET performance

Indicator (CPI) which does not have these drawbacks and can be applied to even 
individual shipments. 
 
In addition to bringing value to companies, the methodology brings maximum value to 
society since it is fair. It allocates all emissions from the energy lifecycle (well-to-wheel) 
to the value added by the transportation and storage activities: being the translocation of 
goods and/or persons from origin to destination. Key aspect is the right distance metric. 
Research by TNO concluded that Great Circle Distance (distance as the crow flies) is the 
only fundamentally correct option for determining the transport activity, transport 
performance and for emission allocation as well as for the goals of sharing emission 
intensity data, minimum data requirements and auditing. SFD metric is a second-best 
choice, when used for calculating and comparing carbon footprints within one transport 
modality. The SFD metric is however mutable, software-dependent and can lead to 
directionally incorrect decisions.  
 
Additionally, we believe fair is seeing performance of transportation companies in the 
right context of the supply chains they are part of. Shippers design the supply chain and 
dictate constraints and planning demand. The other way around shippers are dependent 
on transportation companies for efficient execution and using the best possible 
equipment. Both share the responsibility for the resulting CO2-emissions caused by the 
transportation of a good in a supply chain. We have developed interacting but slightly 
different KPI’s for transporters and shippers to take this interaction into account. In 
practice we have seen 50% reduction due to better alignment between shipper and 
transportation company after investigating these KPI’s. 
 
To further reduce the cost, we believe in highly automated and verified for accounting IT 
systems. It makes the GHG emission accounting process more efficient for transport 
service providers and makes the results auditable by regular auditors: specialized 
auditing would be prohibitively expensive and limiting. Currently two IT systems, BigMile 
and Loginex, are the living proof that the methodology and the application in tools is 
easily integrated into IT-systems, and that auditors can certify results during their 
normal auditing work. 
 
The last point we want to emphasize it that a common framework for measuring GHG 
emissions is a very important step, but not the end goal. Sustainability is more than only 
GHG emission. Since the creating of a common methodology for GHG emission is nearing 
its completion, we are developing the next steps: 
- Frameworks to measure NOx, particulate matter, and scarce resources. 
- A framework to do GHG emission accounting in supply chains: Carbon Added 
Accounting. 
 
For NOx, particulate matter, and scarce resources methodologies are being developed 
and tested in practice. First results show that it is possible to allocation NOx emissions in 
a comparable way as GHG emissions with just a little bit of additional data. The same 
holds for the use of scarce resources.

The Carbon Added Accounting methodology creates verifiable GHG emission accounting 
claims in supply chains. We announcement on ‘embedded carbon’ legislations worldwide, 
like the Carbon Boarder Adjustment Mechanism in the EU and the Inflation Reduction Act 
in the USA, the need for such a methodology is stronger than ever.  
 
Since publishing the concept in 2021, testing in practice proved that the Value Added Tax 
(VAT) inspired methodology works in all steps of the supply chains: for production 
companies, transportation companies and trading companies. A Harvard Business Review 
article by Robert S. Kaplan advocates more or less the same concept. Except that, like 
for GHG emissions for transportation, data quality meta-data should be added to create a 
methodology that works in practice.

As a starting point, our industry would appreciate to have a better understanding of the meaning of 
“degradation” in the context of the EU Soil Health Strategy and therefore suggest the European 
Commission to come up with a definition in the upcoming proposal. In particular, we wonder whether 
the Commission intends to use the material provided by the European Environment Agency1 in order 
to establish a definition: 
 1. Soil degradation is not well monitored, and often hidden, but it is widespread and 
diverse. Intensive land management leads to negative impacts on soil biodiversity, which is the 
key driver of terrestrial ecosystems’ carbon and nutrient cycling. There is increasing evidence 
that land and soil degradation have major economic consequences, whereas the cost of 
preventing damage is significantly lower.  
 
2. Europe’s soils suffer from sealing, erosion, compaction, pollution, salinisation and 
carbon loss.

3. Soils deliver key ecosystem services such as nutrient provision, water purification, 
filtering of pollutants and a habitat for soil organisms. Non -degraded soils provide these 
functions simultaneously and to a level needed for ecosystem performance (Chapter 3). Two 
closely connected indicators are the basis of soil multifunctionality, the soil organic carbon 
(SOC) pool and soil biodiversity. Carbon is one of the primary sources of energy in food webs; 
losses of carbon (through erosion, climate change, drainage of otherwise waterlogged soils) 
impact the supply of ecosystem services and reduce biodiversity (Stolte et al.,  2016). 
Biologically mediated composition of organic material is the fundamental process for building 
the soil carbon stock, which, together with clay minerals, are important for nutrient retention 
and cycling. Different forms of soil degradation (SOC loss , tillage, pollution, compaction and 
erosion) negatively impact the habitat available for soil organisms. In all regions across 
Europe, the species richness of earthworms, springtails and mites has been negatively affected 
by increased intensity of land use (Tsiafouli et al., 2015). Healthy soils contain active microbial 
(bacteria and fungi) and animal (micro to macro fauna) communities (Orgiazzi et al., 2016), of 
which bacteria and fungi are mainly responsible for nutrient cycling, which is essential for plant 
growth. 
With regard to the different drivers of the degradation of soils mentioned in the question, FuelsEurope 
would like to bring to the attention of the European Commission the limited footprint of its industrial 
activities: in particular, refining footprint in the context of soil degradation is smaller than other 
activities contributing to it. 
Moreover, the EU refining industry has reduced its environmental footprint by continually increasing 
its energy integration and investments in effici ency. For example, European refiners have made 
significant investments to ensure their compliance with some of the world’s most stringent air quality 
(SO₂, NOx and particulate matter emissions), water quality and soil protection rules. This has played a 
large role in reducing their environmental footprint. In fact, the amount of sulphur emitted by EU 
refineries has been halved since 1998. The quality of effluents has improved greatly and, over the past 
30 years, refineries have decreased the amount of oil discharged into water tenfold2. 
Q3. In your opinion, are the causes of soil and land degradation sufficiently addressed at EU level?  
& In your opinion, are the causes of soil and land degradation sufficiently addressed at national

We replied “not enough” to this question. 
From an industrial perspective, the need for harmonised EU level regulations is well recognised, and 
all parties benefit when the less complex the process is to manage. The use of a framework which 
recognizes that sound principles of science, technology, engineering and mathematics can be used to 
derive an actionable approach which can protect human health  and the environment. However, 
setting standards at a non-science derived standard may introduce unnecessary ‘compliance’ actions 
or additional control/remediation where a more science-based, sustainability driven framework is in 
place. 
When it comes to Member States, we believe that soil and land degradation are addressed with a 
variety of different legislations ( due to the variety of soils and differentiation due to geographic al

Q4. Which types of soil use should be addressed by the initiative.  
We replied “we totally agree” that “all types of soil uses should be addressed” to this question.  
We believe that, at this stage, it is difficult to state which types of soils should be addressed by the 
initiative: different soil types provide different functions to mankind and the environment  (e.g. 
industrial, residential, commercial, and agricultural use) and their respective risks and need should be 
assessed and addressed separately.  
Q5. In your view, should the future EU Soil Health Law set legal obligations for Member States to 
achieve healthy soils by 2050? 
We replied “I don’t know/no opinion/not applicable”  to this question . Please note that the below 
explanation also applies to answers given to Q13 (Please rank the importance you attribute to the 
different provisions for achieving healthy soils in the EU by 2050 described in the questions 5-12).  
FuelsEurope acknowledge that European soil targets do not exist and therefore they should be built 
from the beginning, while national targets are extremely variable. 
Fixing absolute targets for soil health is not the same as actually improving it: an eventual EU Soil 
Health Law could, for example, set general European expectations for Member States to meet them 
with their own tools (different legal framework, different geographical  and geological conditions for 
soil restoration and different uses - e.g. Industry vs. Agriculture). 
Finally, FuelsEurope believes that before setting any target , there should be a clear definition of 
“healthy soil”. However, the eventuality of having such definition in a legal framework should be 
carefully assessed, especially considering that soils amongst Europe are extremely different between 
MS, susceptible for evolutions and unlikely to be strictly and rigidly framed. 
Q8. Do you agree that there should be legal obligations for Member States to identify contaminated 
sites that pose a significant risk to human health and the environment & Do you agree that there  
should be legal obligations for Member States to remediate those contaminated sites? 
We replied “totally agree” to the first question and “I don’t know/no opinion/not applicable” to the 
second question.  
FuelsEurope support a risk-based framework: should there be genuine risks to the environment, then 
appropriate action should be taken. In particular, for permitted installations there are already 
legislative provisions under the I ndustrial Emissions Directive (IED)  for ensuring much of the 
protections sought here (see below bullets). 
▪ IED article 22 (2) requirements to prepare and submit to the competent authority a baseline report 
before starting operation of an installation that determine the state of soil and ground water 
contamination so as to make a quantified comparison with the state upon definitive cessation of 
activities .

IED article 22 (3) to take the necessary measures to, where the installation has caused significant 
pollution of soil or groundwater by relevant hazardous substances compared to the state established 
in the baseline report, return the site to that state upon definitive cessation of the activities.   
In particular circumstances, where the contamination of soil and groundwater at the site poses a  
significant risk to human health or the environment, the operator shall take the necessary actions 
aimed at the removal, control, containment or reduction of relevant hazardous substances, so that 
the site, taking into account its current or approved future use, ceases to pose such a risk. 
▪ IED article 14 (1) and the BREF BAT conclusions, which actually mandate the application of measures 
that aim to prevent / avoid accidental contamination of the soil.  
As responsible operators, we believe that the industry would rather prevent on a voluntary basis any 
significant risk to human health and the environment. 
Q11. Do you agree that there should be legal obligations for Member States to set mechanism 
informing the buyer about the health of the soil when land is sold? 
We replied “totally agree” to this question.  
FuelsEurope believe that responsibilities of land users are limited until the use of the land and no 
additional binding obligations are needed. 
When the land is sold, the buyer must be given the opportunity to inspect the site and to consider the 
price, which usually account s as indicator of the land’s conditions. In case of lack of disclosure or 
truthfulness applied by the seller, then the usual national existing litigation rules shall be applie d, 
therefore we discourage legal binding obligations at EU level. 
Such obligations would imply standards to which a seller should strictly stick with the risk of impeding 
a transaction, even with full awareness of the actual state of the land from the buyer.  
To make an analogy, the same logic of a costumer buying a house should apply: right before the 
transaction, the buyer has the opportunity to fully inspect and scrutiny the state of the property on 
sale. During such inspection, the buyer judges the cond itions of the house on the basis of evidence, 
his own judgment, information provided by the seller and the price. Once the transaction done, the 
responsibility of the state of the house is upon the new owner.

PART 2 – Key points for the Commission to consider when identifying the final scope of the 
proposed Soil Health Law 
FuelsEurope comments are principally focussed on the application of a new EU Soil Health Law in the 
sense of identification, management, remediation, and disposition / beneficial reuse of Contaminated 
Land, and we would like to see a framework which evolves to be: 
BASED ON CLEAR DEFINITIONS OF 
 Healthy soils; 
 Significant harm; 
 Degradation of soil. 
RISK BASED 
 Founded on risk-based management principles, which requires development of a conceptual 
site model (e.g., ISO 21365:2019) that identifies source-pathway-receptor linkages. 
PROPORTIONAL 
 Requires remediation actions proportional to the risk(s) to human health / environment, and 
does not require remediation where there is no unacceptable risk. 
 Allows remedy selection in line with impact significance – recognising that high risk, 
recalcitrant chemicals may require high energy, destructive technologies to reduce loading to 
acceptable levels. 
SUSTAINABLE 
 Incorporates sustainability assessment (e.g. ISO 18504:2017, Sustainable Remediation) into 
remedy selection and implementation. 
 Encourages re-use of contaminated land for future beneficial uses, and does not place new 
barriers to the successful regeneration of sites. 
ALIGNED WITH STAKEHOLDERS 
 Recognizes that voluntary remediation (e.g. through a Land-use Planning System) can deliver 
land regeneration/ remediation. 
 Enforcement or register identification (e.g. through a contaminated land system) should focus 
on high risk sites where risk is not acceptable & for which no voluntary action is forthcoming. 
 Allows the transfer of land ownership and liabilities in a manner that facilitates both 
responsible business and brownfield regeneration. S hould consider ‘polluter pays principles’ 
as valuable – but not eternal obligations after contractual transfer.

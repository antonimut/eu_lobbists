 
 
Soil Health - Protecting, sustainably managing, and restoring EU 
soils 
The Industrieverband Agrar recognizes the importance of ensuring healthy soils across the 
European Union and supports the ambition to protect, restore and promote a sustainable use 
of soils, tackling the issue of soil fertility. 
Soil represents the primary resource for agriculture, therefore ensuring soil health has a 
strategic importance to guarantee European strategic autonomy and food security; productive 
soils allow farmers to obtain high  yields, and good quality  food, while a loss of soil fertility 
threatens food security and causes biodiversity loss and pollution.  
The fertilizer industry represents a crucial actor as it provides soils with the essential nutrients 
needed to grow healthy crops, and support soil fertility. The fertilizer industry  is aware of its 
responsibility of providing food for a growing population and it  is committed to continuing to 
contribute to healthy soils. 
To provide an effective framework for soil health, the following must be considered: 
• The importance of defining healthy soils with a use-specific and site-specific approach 
(regulated at a regional level) 
• The need for scientifically backed definitions for all terminology 
• Harmonized legislation to reduce bureaucracy and unnecessary overlap at the EU-level 
I. Definition of healthy soil 
To effectively deliver on soil protection and tackle the causes of land degradation, the Soil 
Health Law should primarily provide a clear and detailed definition of healthy soils specifically 
tailored to the soil use (recreational, natural, forestry, agriculture, etc.)  
The EU Soil Strategy 2030 defines healthy soils as soils in good chemical, biological and 
physical condition, and thus able to continuously provide as many ecosystem services as 
possible.  
Such an approach provides a horizontal definition that does not take into account the specific 
soil functions and ecosystem services provided. An effective definition of soil health should not 
be based on fixed parameters but should take into consideration the variability of natural soil 
types as well as in soil  use and composition. In other words, the definition of soil health 
depends on the “use” of the soil: the definition of soil health differs between a soil in a natural 
ecosystem (e.g. natural forest or meadows) and a soil for growing crops for food and feed. 
Agricultural soils can be considered healthy when they are able to support healthy crop growth 
and production at appropriate levels while encouraging improvement in nutrient use efficiency 
(NUE). 
NUE describes the difference between the amount of nut rients applied to the crops and the 
percentage of nutrients retained within the harvested crops. When paired with proper 
management Soil health is a precondition for nutrient use efficiency.  
When it comes to fertilization practices, balanced fertilization  shall combine mineral and 
organic sources as the best way to improve and maintain good chemical, physical and 
biological conditions, and to provide crops with the nutrients needed in an efficient way. The 
EU Farm to Fork strategy itself puts forward the objective of maintaining soil fertility.

The upcoming Soil Health Law should avoid one-size-fit-all approaches and should encourage 
Member States to develop national fertilization plans to maximize soil health for their differing 
ecosystems and soil uses. These fertilization plans should be based around the EU research 
program Horizon Europe. The indicators for soil health should be further defined by an expert 
group and include precise soil assessment methods to serve as an advisory document for 
farmers. 
Even among agricultural soils, different pedo-climatic conditions, compositions, and land-use 
history makes it impossible to provide a single set of parameters. 
II. Site specific governance 
The European territory is characterized by significant differences in terms of territory, climate 
and soil conditions, farm structure and farming systems. 
Due to this heterogeneity, it will require an enormous effort to provide an overarching 
standardized framework. The EU Soil Health Law should therefore refr ain from adopting 
arbitrary targets or punctual parameters at the EU level. Rather, it should define the basic 
framework and guidelines to promote site-specific solutions at the local level. 
It would be preferable to encourage EU Member States to develop national soil health plans 
where threats can be identified on a national and/or regional level, and actions implemented 
to remedy those via existing legislation. National soil health plans should be designed in close 
consultation with relevant stakeholders, including the scientific community, fertilizer industry 
and distributors, the food industry, farmers and agronomists, to ensure ownership of the 
actions and science-based indicators.  
This would provide necessary scientific governance in order to identify suitable management 
options and to focus on agronomic practices supporting soil health, while enabling sufficient 
productivity and profitability of crop production. 
III. Land degradation causes 
Land degradation is often a reflection of local conditions, soil origin, geographic location, 
climatic conditions, implemented soil management practices and the intended land -use type. 
Degradation in agricultural soil refers to the loss of ability for the s oil to support the needs of 
healthy plants as well as the reduced ability to sustain production of nutritious food.  
Maintaining soil fertility is important not only to maintain crop production, but also for soil health 
itself. It is therefore necessary that the EU Soil Health Law focuses on the nutrient content of 
soils not only to reduce their losses to the environment, but also and most importantly to ensure 
soil fertility by promoting a balanced nutrient supply in the soil through the use of mineral 
fertilizer, organic materials, and soil amendments.  
The use of mineral fertilizer in combination with organic material has proven to be the most 
effective nutrient management practice to ensure productivity while preserving soil fertility and 
building up soil organic material. 
IV. Harmonized legislation 
Considering the complexity of the subject, many separate pieces of legislation are likely to 
affect the soil. It is important that the future EU Soil Health Law proposal ensures policy 
coherence and avoids double legislation and additional administrative burden. 
To promote coherence with existing and upcoming legislation initiatives at the EU level, 
(Integrated Nutrient Management Action Plan, Farm to Fork Strategy, Common Agricultural 
Policy, etc.) the Soil Health Law must focus on integration with existing EU environmental and 
 
agricultural legislation, and build upon the subsidiarity principle. In the agricultural domain, the 
Soil Health Law needs to avoid redundancy, and should prioritize setting up the framework , 
mechanisms and resources to ensure that there is no deterioration to soil fertility.  
The final objective must be maintaining the agricultural soil’s fertility through the promotion of 
balanced fertilization practices that combine mineral and organic inputs. 
V. Ensuring food security 
Today about 50 Percent of the world’s population has access to food as a result of the use of 
mineral nitrogen fertilizers. In other terms, with no mineral fertilizers our agricultural area would 
need to double to maintain the c urrent food production levels, causing dramatic effects on 
biodiversity and GHG emissions due to land use change.  
To put that into perspective, nearly 40 Percent of EU land is used for agricultural purposes. It 
is of utmost importance for food security, in both quality and quantity, to ensure the fertility of 
EU soils; while also considering the additional future growth in global population and the crucial 
role that Europe plays in contributing to global food security. The objectives of maintaining, 
improving, and restoring soil health in the European Union must be achieved while maintaining, 
or even increasing, the EU’s food production capacity. Food security in Europe has been, and 
must remain, a top priority especially considering the current geo-political situation. 
The Soil Health Law should ensure food security by promoting the effective use of mineral 
fertilizers to maximize the fertility, and longevity of European soils.

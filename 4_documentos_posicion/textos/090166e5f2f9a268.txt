PEPSICO’S POSITION ON SOIL HEALTH – PROTECTING, SUSTAINABLY MANAGING AND 
RESTORING EU SOILS  
24 October 2022 
 
PepsiCo welcomes the opportunity to provide input to the EU’s work to establish a regulatory framework 
for soil health.  
As one of the world’s leading food and beverage companies with a strong footprint in the EU, PepsiCo 
strongly supports the European Com mission’s Green Deal and wishes to play a key role in shaping an 
improved food system, aimed at bringing sustainable and long -term solutions for European consumers, 
agro-businesses and our planet more broadly. PepsiCo believes a resilient food system is essential to our 
business, and our Positive Agriculture agenda prioritizes investment, innovation, and robust collaboration 
with our farming partners to deliver impact around the world.  
In the spirit of providing constructive input, this paper lists several  elements we hope will be considered 
as the regulatory framework is developed.  
 
PEPSICO’S KEY RECOMMENDATIONS 
1. For this legal framework to be successful, it requires a clear definition of ‘healthy soil’ and must provide 
key parameters for improving soil health.  
2. There needs to be a harmonized, science-based measurement protocol for data collection to assist in 
baseline measurement and monitoring impact.   
3. A methodology should be introduced for a  standardized certification scheme for the adoption of 
sustainable farming practices which should also reward farmers that already apply regenerative 
agricultural practices.    
4. The framework should facilitate  a strong supportive framework for farmers which provides 
educational opportunities and financial incentives to invest in regenerative agriculture practices.

1. DEFINING HEALTHY SOIL 
A clear definition of healthy or clean soil is required for a Soil Health Law to be successful. However, this 
will be complex as the starting point to measure soil health may differ between Member States due to 
the variability and constant evolution of soil. We therefore suggest that the EU should not mandate 
numerical targets but focus on providing high level guidance to guarantee no further degradation of soils. 
It should be up to individual Member States to further define what is important and useful for soils in each 
country, in combination with national targets for improvement.  
We must recognize the significant timeframe required for restoration practices and soil improvement. It 
can take many years to measure results and these can also be impacted by external factors  like floods, 
extreme winds and droughts. In an agricultural context, short term goals should focus on encouraging and 
incentivizing regenerative practices that prevent further land degradation at local level which then will 
create a platform for improved soil health on a regional level as a longer-term outcome. 
A definition for healthy soil should be accompanied with key measurement parameters . Ideally, we 
would need a combination of physical (soil loss through water), chemical (nutrient losses), and biological 
(microbial mass and an abundance and diversity of earthworms) indicators to assess soil health.  
Lastly, for the  Soil Health Law  to be impactful and  lead to regenerative farming and sustainable food 
systems, alignment with other EU initiatives – especially in the Farm to Fork and Green Deal context – 
will be essential. 
 
2. A HARMONIZED METHODOLOGY FOR MONITORING  
This legislation offers the opportunity to establish a harmonized and science-based methodology across 
Member States to set baselines  and track benchmarks  which are necessary  to monitor change  
successfully. This methodology needs to provide transparency along the full value chain and allow all 
stakeholders to track progress of investments made on regenerative agriculture and their corresponding 
results. It will also create an opportunity to recognize best practices and identify restoration benchmarks.  
In addition , we urge that this monitoring process should not put any additional burden on farmers . 
Increasing the financial and/or time burden on farmers may risk non-action. Therefore, the upcoming 
monitoring system must be based on easily accessible and affordable technolog ies, associated with 
meaningful indicators that are feasible in rural contexts. 
 
3. ADDITIONALITY AND BASELINES THAT REWARD PREVIOUS EFFORTS  
A methodology for assessment of previous actions to restore soil health should be introduced to reward 
regenerative practices  executed in the past . It is crucial to include these early adopters in incentive 
programs so they can be come ‘soil restoration champions’ through peer -peer networks and can also 
provide best practices to other farmers new to the game.  
 
 
4. ENSURING A LEVEL PLAYING FIELD FOR FARMERS 
PepsiCo believes that methods for restoring soil health also require a strong supportive framework, both 
financial and educational.  This legislation offers an opportunity to establish a level playing field for those 
who are contributing to regenerate soil health, notably farmers, but also needs to include incentives for 
non-operating landowners to support farmers leasing or renting land to make the transition.  
Financial support will trigger investments by businesses and farmers to develop regenerative agriculture 
practices at scale  – including investments in technological capabilities . As previously mentioned, the 
starting point for measuring soil health can differ between locations and can be impacted by external 
factors. We therefore suggest that these incentives for farmers should consider both action-based and 
outcome-based results. 
Next to economic incentives, we believe that access to information about new technologies and farmer 
peer networks are important.  Leveraging existing and new farmer peer-peer networks would be a good 
first platform to start exchanging information and learn about best practices and upcoming technologies.

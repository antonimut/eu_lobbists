 
 
European Commission’s Public consultation on a possible EU Soil Health Law 
Moët Hennessy’s contribution 
 
As indicated in our response to the previous consultation on the matter, Moët Hennessy 
supports an EU soil health law, including ambitious targets, to help create the framework for 
national and local actions and to help reach the EU’s climate and biodiversity goals. Since the 
previous consultation we have gathered around 300 stakeholders to discuss  healthy soils 
through our World Living Soils Forum in June 2022 and plan on organizing further events to 
keep the topic high on the agenda.

About us 
Moët Hennessy is the wine and spirit division of LVMH. Located in France ( Champagne, 
Cognac, Provence), Spain, Poland and other illustrious wine  and spirits pro ducing countries 
around the world , each of our 2 6 Houses have a unique character and terroir  and in many 
cases centuries of history , and share a strong culture of heritage, excellence, authenticity, 
innovation and creativity. 
Given our field of work and expertise, we have chosen to focus our answer to the consultation 
on agricultural soils and to leave aside the topics of artificialization, industrial and built areas 
and soil contamination. 
Our work and achievements 
Over the past years, Moët Hennessy Houses have invested in environmentally friendly 
equipment, trained farmers and winemakers in new technologies, supported sustainable 
viticulture certifications and funded research. Moët Hennessy has  recently invested €20 
million in a research center  in the Champagne region devoted to scientific research around 
sustainable viticulture.  
Today 95% of our own vineyards are certified by the highest environmental standards for 
viticulture in France and worldwide. A key achievement was to reach 100% herbicide-free in 
our own vineyards in Champagne in 2020. We are now working with our winegrower partners 
to encourage and support their attempts to become certified sustainable. Hennessy , in 
Cognac, is committed to supporting its suppliers to reach zero herbicides by 2028.  
It is particularly important for us to engage with our partners, who are supplying our Maisons 
with grapes, wines, eaux -de-vies, cereals etc. It is only by sharing best practices a nd 
accompanying their daily activities on the transition path that we can encourage their 
commitment to sustainable viticulture and agriculture. 
Our Maisons have been working to find  solutions to improve soil  health and biodiversity 
through trials of several viticultural practices including: 
• 90% of Moët Hennessy Maisons are experiencing with natural grass and/or cover 
cropping between the rows of wines. For example , in Champagne, Moët & Chandon 
and Veuve Clicquot are testing specific cover crop mixes such as rye, clover, and faba 
bean, aiming to improve soil structure and biological activity.

We do recognize that those are only examples and that more systematic work is needed to 
help restore soils and combat climate change, by further reducing chemical inputs, restoring 
biodiversity and decreasing water consumption.  
While legislation can help to foster such actions , room should be left f or responsible 
companies experimenting initiatives responding to  the local needs and requirements 
depending on the type of culture and geography. 
The role of experts 
The enlarged expert group on soils, which gathers experts with very different background and 
knowledge - including of the champagne production (Comité Champagne)  - will be key to 
develop the set of efficient indicators to assess the health of the soils and its evolution over 
several years (monitoring framework) . Their expertise will hel p to develop a reference 
framework for land users. 
The role of indicators 
In terms of indicators  and thresholds for soil quality assessments , while the list proposed 
addresses the various themes that should be covered to assess soil status, some are easily 
 
measurable (e.g. physical indicators ), whi le others still require a better understand ing and 
approved methodology (e.g. biological conditions indicators).  
Above all there is no widely approved definition of “healthy soils” and while we believe an 
obligation of results  will help  accelerate efforts towards more sustainable agriculture,  the 
focus should be on improving current practices.  
To the best of our knowledge, precise indicators have not been agreed and additional work at 
European/international level is needed in this field to create a rob ust and efficient set of 
indicators, to help guide discussions. There is much to learn yet on some of these themes – in 
particular on how carbon captures in soils works or vegetal cover - where more research is 
needed. 
We believe that it is important to  engage on two types of indi cators which should be 
functioning together in order to better understand and achieve improved soil health: 
- practice-based indicators (i.e. : surface of cover cropping)  help set objectives for the 
implementation of practices on the fi eld, but need to be completed with the  
assessment of outcomes; 
- outcome-based indicators (i.e.: % of organic carbon)  are key to prove real impact of 
the practices on the ecosystem health. 
We want to highlight again that comparing a cultivated land to a forest soil is not appropriate 
and will systematically negatively reflect on the cultivated land. The right indicators for soil 
quality will also very much depend on the type of use. When it comes to agriculture (and 
viticulture), indicators, group of indicators and reference framework need to be adapted, to 
reflect what is considered optimal for different types of cultures.  
In addition, t he nature of soils  is very diverse  within countries and regions . The basis of 
comparison is crucial to compare similar soils structures and cultures when developing 
indicators. The diversity of viticultural practices according to regions must be factored in while 
trying to define sustainable best practices. This is why we support a measurement that allows 
a high level of granularity, down to a local level, where production basins could be considered.  
The role of the regional level 
While the EU should provide direction and support to Member States, the national, regional 
levels and even closer to the reality of the soils, the zone homogeneous for pedo -climatic 
conditions and use, can be administered through interprofessional organizations. Those must 
be empowered to decide how these indicators should be used and prioritized in the local 
context. This is key especially in wine -growing regions where the soil may have different 
properties and challenges from one region to another. 
Intermediate measures and benchmarks may  be needed. These indicators should help land 
users to improve their practices while taking into account their type of activity and area of 
production. 
Consistency with other legislation and systemic approach 
We also call for EU funding of research, a simplification of funding schemes (incl. through the 
CAP) and alignment with the objectives of other pieces of legislation (including the sustainable 
food labelling framework, sustainable use of pesticides , product envir onmental footprint ). 
 
When it comes to environmental issues, a systemic approach is necessary as different pieces 
cannot be taken in isolation. Moving one lever will necessarily impact other parameters.  
We thank the European Commission for the opportunity to contribute to this important topic 
and look forward to the publication of the legislative proposal.

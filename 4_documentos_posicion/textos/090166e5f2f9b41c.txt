The European Steel Association, EUROFER, besides to  a written response to the public 
consultation via the Questionnaire would like to share some considerations to be contemplated 
in view of the new Health Soil Law proposal.  Given that  the steel industry is committed to 
reducing the environmental impact and  supporting the Green Deal through the different on -
going Commissions initiatives, such as the Zero Pollution Ambition, the Circular Economy Action 
plan, the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability, we are sharing the following recommendations. 
 
 
➢ Coherence with other legislations 
Soil monitoring requirements, including monitoring frequencies are already set in Art. 16 of the 
Industrial Emissions Directive (IED). To this end, and in order to avoid do uble regulation and/or 
regulatory overlaps, we ask that a clear line is drawn between the IED and the new soil proposal. 
A possible EU Soil Health Law for protecting, 
sustainably managing and restoring EU soils 
 
EUROFER comments to the Public Consultation on a possible EU Soil Health Law for protecting,  
sustainability managing and restoring EU soils 
Publication date: 01 August 2022 
The EU Soil Health Law should be based on the following: 
➢ Coherence with other legislations  should be ensured to avoid duplication of 
requirements and additional burden for the industry. 
➢ Remediation measures  for contaminated soil have to be set by National /Regional 
Authorities, taking into account the local conditions. 
➢ Use the current scientific knowledge and a risk-based approach to identify when there 
is a real risk to human health and the environment. 
➢ The soil health certificate for land transactions has not to prevent the transfer of land 
that may not meet the definition of a ‘’healthy soil’’ along with the preservation of the 
right to conduct commercial transactions. 
➢ A passport for an excavated soil has to improve the safe recycling of healthy soils.

➢ Measures related to soil contamination 
Some MSs or local Authorities already apply remediation measures by requiring that only land 
that is likely to cause a Significant Possibility of Significant Harm (SPOSH) is remediated. Most 
other remediation interventions are addressed during planning and rely on a change in land use 
that triggers remediation to ensure future users are not affected. In other MSs, the Soil 
Protection legislation defines trigger and action values, which, if exceeded (e.g. due to the 
presence of a harmful soil change or site contamination), lead to an investigation and/or 
additional remediation measures. 
We believe that it is the resp onsibility of the National /Regional Authorities to identify the best 
procedure for contaminated land and set which are the priorities, taking into account the local 
conditions (e.g., some contaminants are naturally present in areas of the country and would  
almost certainly be higher than any trigger values, these may be different depending on the soils 
and their status). Other factors, which are part of the local geochemical background, like pH, for 
instance, are hugely variable, depending on the country an d it can vary from acid soils to very 
alkali soils, and these areas are very valuable for variation in biodiversity of both flora and fauna.

➢ Options for a soil health certificate for land transactions  
Soil and groundwater investigations are already conducted across the site in some MSs before 
sales. Some other MSs maintain registers of possible contaminated sites based on historical data 
only, which are not necessarily systematic investigations. However, according to Art. 21 of the 
IED, a requirement already exists to ‘’prepare and submit to the competent authority a baseline 
report before starting operation of an installation or before a permit for an installation is updated 
[…], which contains the information necessary to determine the state of soil and groundwater 
contamination”. 
We are of the opinion that this practice is already happening  under the IE D, even if is not 
harmonised yet in all MSs, and we fear that an additional binding ‘’soil health certificate’’ which 
reports the soil status would add an extra administrative burden on operators and overlap with 
the baseline report.  
However, should be the intention to consider a soil health certificate for land transactions in the 
business impact statement, such certificate should be made on a voluntary basis and not prevent 
for transfer of land that may not meet the definition of a ‘’healthy soil’’ and preserve the right to 
conduct commercial transactions.  
 
➢ Options for a passport for excavated soil 
Anything that stops soil  from being a waste should be welcomed. A site should be required to 
adequately characterise any soil that it wish es to gi ve to another site whether this is free of 
charge or a monetary transaction.   As long as it meets the requirements of the receiver site, it 
should be able to be moved and used as a product and not a waste.  For example, if a company’s 
business is to excava te and sell top soil, they are allowed to do so without any 
impediment.  However, if a site has an excess of soil, which it wishes to sell to another site even 
if it is chemically identical, it is treated as a waste and there are numerous regulatory hurdle s 
that must be overcome.  This prevents the sustainable use of soil and leads to some soils being 
landfilled even though they are perfectly suitable for sustainable re-use. The intended “passport

CONCLUSIONS 
EUROFER agrees with the purpose of a Healthy Soil Law  as considers it as  an opportunity that 
helps to achieve the goals of a zero-pollution ambition under the Green Deal. The steel industry 
is open to share further  ideas and explore potential solutions within the European Commission 
and other stakeholders.

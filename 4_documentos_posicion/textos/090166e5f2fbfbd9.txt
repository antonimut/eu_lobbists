 
 
EUROBAT feedback to the public consultation on the Soil Health Initiative (24 
October 2022) 
 
EUROBAT, the Association of European Automotive and Industrial Battery Manufacturers, welcomes the 
opportunity to provide further input for the impact assessment underpinning the drafting of a legislative initiative 
on soil health.   
EUROBAT notes that soil contamination does not have a cross-border dimension. While EU-wide targets related to 
land use or the establishment of common definitions and methodologies for the identification of contaminated 
sites could be considered, a strict application of the subsidiary principle should prevail when it comes to 
safeguarding or remediation of contaminated sites.  
Overlaps with the Industrial Emissions Directive (IED) should be avoided, in particular when considering 
monitoring and reporting requirements on the condition of soils. The impact assessment must acknowledge the 
central and successful role of EU rules on hazardous chemicals management (REACH) and industrial emissions in 
ensuring that emissions of pollutants to soils are controlled.  
 
Possible monitoring and reporting requirements on the conditions of soil 
Articles 14(1)(b), (c), (e) and 16 of the IED already specify soil health monitoring requirements for operators of 
industrial sites, including as regards monitoring frequency.  
The permitting regime includes soil protection and prevention of contamination during the length of a permit. 
Such provisions include background information on the current state of the soil, such as details on former 
activities and potential contamination. Accordingly, the IED specifies that Best Available Techniques Conclusions 
(BAT-C) may include relevant site remediation measures.  
Any measure on monitoring and reporting requirements should complement and not overlap with the provisions 
of the IED. Any new indicators for soil health characterisation should align with the content of BAT-Cs of relevance 
for soil health.  
 
Identification, registration and remediation of contaminated sites  
A proportionate and risk-based approach should be prioritised to enable full re(use) of degraded soil lands.  
Rules on the extent to which contaminated soils should be remediated or simply secured to preserve human 
health and environment protection should be based on risk assessments. The risk-based approach should also 
consider environmental and technical aspects.  
In any case, soil contamination is not a cross-border threat. In line with the subsidiary principle, EUROBAT 
considers it the responsibility of the Member State Competent Authority to identify the best procedure for dealing 
with contaminated sites, based on local conditions, land use and human health impact.

The core legislation on soil health must stay in the remit of Member States. There is a wide variety of soil types in 
Europe, characterised by geographic specificities. Policies and rules on soil health should therefore be prioritised 
and developed at the regional level. 
 
For example, Germany benefits from the German protection law and the guarantees brought by the technical 
regulations on preventive soil protection legislation, such the Ordinance on Installations for the Handling of 
Substances Hazardous to Water) for the prevention of soil pollution or restoration.  
Several other Member States benefit from long-established regimes to tackle legacy damages with important 
financial penalties again soil contamination. This should be reflected in the impact assessment.  
 
Development of EU-wide indicators for soil health 
The mere presence of pollutants in soils should not justify its classification as contaminated site. The identification 
of soils as contaminated sites should be based on background values and rigorous risk assessment.  
For example, the UK Soil Observatory showed that naturally occurring lead concentrations in England can be 
surprisingly high and diverse across the country1.  
It is also important to ensure proper stakeholder participation in the drawing up of any EU priority list for 
pollutants in soils as well as in the definition of soil health indicators and their value range, for example within a 
new or an existing expert group.  
 
Coherence and articulation with EU initiatives affecting soil health and state 
 
With regards to polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs), 5 European countries have been developing a restriction 
proposal to restrict the manufacture, use and placing on the market as substances, in mixtures, or in articles of 
non-essential uses of such substances under the EU’s Chemicals Regulation “REACH”.  
 
We call on the Commission to consider the central role played by the REACH Regulation in addressing the 
environmental risks posed by hazardous chemicals, including, but not limited to, PFAS, as part of the impact 
assessment.  
 
Section 2.2.3. of the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability already notes that the restriction on PFAS under REACH 
should be the primary vehicle for addressing soil pollution posed by PFAS.  
 
Regarding the impact of lead-acid battery value chains, a recent study showed that emissions of lead to soil via 
natural processes are 1.7 times higher than total emissions to soil from anthropogenic sources once ammunitions 
are removed2.  
 
On soil emissions via the air and the environment, current IED provisions and the related best available techniques 
reference document (BREF) process on non-ferrous metals have been extremely successful in reducing emissions 
of lead to air and water.

Recent data from the European Pollutant Release and Transfer Register (E-PRTR) showed that there has been an

It should also be noted that Article 22 (site closure) of the IED provides an extremely efficient instrument to 
prevent damage to soil.

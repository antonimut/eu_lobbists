OCP’s position on the EU Soil Health Law 
Protecting, sustainably managing, and restoring EU soils 
Why healthy soils are important to achieve a sustainable way of living 
The EU Soil Strategy for 2030 aims to restore soil health in Europe to ensure its benefits for people, 
food, nature, and climate.  A big driver of this effort comes from the large Horizon Europe funding 
program for research and innovation – almost €100 billion – in which one of the five missions is 
dedicated to soils – Caring for Soil is  Caring for Life. The aim of this mission is to ensure that 75% of 
European soils are healthy  by 2030 and able to provide essential ecosystem services. This is a very 
important step towards achieving a more sustainable way of living.  
Bringing soils to the centre of our most important societal challenges is  vital. Most life on earth is in 
Earth’s Critical Zone, a thin surface layer of our planet which extends from the top of the vegetation 
to the bottom of drinking water aquifers . Here, rock ( lithosphere), water (hydrosphere), air 
(atmosphere), and living organisms (biosphere) interact and shape earth’s surfac e. In the central 
component of Earth’s Critical Zone soils are created. Soils are an even thinner and more active and 
complex interface which perform several critical tasks: 
- Soils are the main source of our food and nutrients; 
- Soils filter, store and control the water flow; 
- Soils hold a big part of Earth’s biodiversity;  
- Soils provide biomass that can be used as clean energy source; 
- Soils are one of the biggest compartments of carbon in its global cycle; 
- Soils control/provide a bunch of ecosystem services that sustain life on earth. 
Soils are the connector of our major societal challenges ( see Figure 1). Securing them and ensuring 
healthy soils is the best way of maintaining the ecosystem services that sustain our lives.

Key points of OCP’s position on the EU Soil Health Law 
OCP Group welcomes and supports the European Union’s overall ambitions to restore and improve 
the quality of European soils. As a company that produces fertilisers, we will be directly impacted by 
legislation impacting soils. Building on our research, extensive exp erience and knowledge on soil 
health, we would like to express our recommendations on how to protect, manage and restore EU 
soils to continue pursuing our mission to “feed the soil to feed the world”. 
OCP’s recommendations: 
• A need for a clear definition  or key performance indicators (KPIs)  of soil health : The KPIs 
should be linked to life supporting ecosystem services provided by the soils, namely: water 
security, climate change abatement, biodiversity protection, and food security (including 
nutritious security) , and these KPIs should be different for differe nt land uses.  It should 
consider the different reference state and the healthiest condition a given soil can reach for 
a given land use. This would allow us to compare and gauge other soils. 
• Nutrients should be managed following the 4R principle s – applying fertilisers at the Right 
dose, Right source, Right time, and Right placement. The Soil Health Law should rely on, and 
develop further , the 4R framework to improve soil fertility and health throughout Europe. 
These 4R principles are the proven solution to improve nutrients use efficiency and optimise 
fertilisers recommendation, solving the issue of excess nutrients in Europe and closing yield 
gaps all around the continent where soil fertility is low and nutrients are required to improve 
crop yields and soil health. 
• Regenerative agriculture practices must be incorporated into our farming systems to use 
nutrients as a source of soil health , increasing soil organic matter and improving all the soil 
health indicators proposed by the “Caring for Soils is Caring for Life” mission. Sufficient funding 
to make the shift to regenerative agriculture should be made available. 
• Paying farmers for their environmental services, including the correct use of nutrients, should 
be part of the Soil Health Law . However, the different conditions of the land must be 
considered.  
• There is an urgent need for standards (used to issue carbon credits) that take into 
consideration how the correct use of fertilisers improves carbon sequestration (e.g., biomass 
production and soil organic matter) and reduces nitrogen GHG emissions. This is a big support 
for farmers to improve the nutrition management of their land.  
• The European Commission should develop/establish the value of its soils so it can be included 
in the measurement of the GDP. Only when people understand how much soils are worth, will 
they start to see soils in a different way.

OCP’S VIEW ON THE EU SOIL HEALTH LAW 
Understanding what soil health entails 
Soil Health is a rather new  and very appealing concept. It is increasingly used and fits  well into other 
new and more integrative and holistic concepts like the One Health approach. As it has been correctly 
advocated by the World Health Organization (WHO), the only way of having healthy people is by having 
a healthy environment (including healthy animals). This is a concept in which healthy soils fit perfectly. 
However, while the concept of soil health is very appealing, many misunderstandings persist in the 
scientific world and even more in society.  
Soil health is defined by the NRCS-USDA as “the continued capacity of soil to function as a vital living 
ecosystem that sustains plants, animals, and humans ”, while the European Commission’s Caring for 
Soils is Caring for Life Mission identifies the indicators of soil health that would ensure the provisioning 
of our needed ecosystem services. In its communication regarding the EU Soil Strategy for 2030, the 
European Commission defined soil health in a si milar way – “soils are healthy when they are in good 
chemical, biological, and physical condition, and thus able to continuously provide as many of the 
following ecosystem services: food and biomass production; absorb, store and filter water and 
transform nutrients and substances; pool biodiversity; carbon reservoir; physical platform for human 
activities; source of raw materials; archive of geological and archaeological heritage”. 
While expressed in different ways, these concepts define soil as a living organism that provide s life-
supporting ecosystem services. Ensuring healthy soil means considering all aspects of the soil, not just 
a few. Because the soil interacts as one and the same complex system. This is a game-changing concept 
for soils, which were usually seen as a rather physical and not very proactive body in terms of their 
functioning. It is very important for the EU Soil Health Law to make this concept as clear as possible. 
This would avoid any confusion around the definition of  more sustainable agricultural systems , such 
as agroecology, organic farming, sustainable farming, regenerative farming, etc. 
Specially in respect to the public and stakeholders, this confusion can lead to catastrophic events as 
was recently the case in Sri Lanka 2. While most of the Sri Lankan farmers were open to the transition 
to organic farming, many did not fully understand what it was nor its practices. The fact that the 
farmers misunderstood the concept and its implications, had catastrophic consequences. In Europe, 
an in -depth analysis 3 published by the European Parliament also shows that switching from 
conventional systems to organic ones would increase food prices and push lower income classes 
towards unhealthier diets. 
The concept of soil health is  somewhat nebulous and difficult  to define. The health of soils from the 
same environment with similar properties and characteristics might be easy to compare. But if we take 
different agricultural systems, from different regions, how can we determine which soil is healthier?

The concept of h ealthy soils gains traction as it holds the key to ensure a healthy and sustainable 
environment. Currently, soil degradation is one of our most important environmental issues, as it leads 
to a decreasing amount of land available for food production each year and contributes massively to 
climate change. However, while it represents one of our most important environmental challenges, it 
also represents our biggest opportunity. The solution to stop soil degradation and build healthy soils  
is the development of a sustainable way of farming (see Figure 2). As 95% of our food comes from the 
soil, establishing sustainable agricultural systems will allow us to break the cycle of food production 
and land degradation and start the virtuous cycle of food production and environmental protection.

Biomass production as an integral part of soil health 
Biomass production is one of the most important ecosystem services soils provide to us. Not only is 
it responsible for the food we eat, but it also represents a solution for  green bioenergy; carbon 
sequestration (as plant biomass and soil organic matter); and feed for soil organisms, increasing soil 
biodiversity. The more biomass  a given soil produces, the more root system it has and  the more 
protected it is against water erosion, leading to increased water infiltration and storage in the soil. The 
more biomass a soil produces in a sustainable way, the better we can optimise the use of land, water, 
and other resources. It means that we need less land to produce food, sparing more natural land, and 
producing more per drop of water. 
The optimisation of biomass production depends on a balanced availability of nutrients in the soil to 
optimise yield and to ensure nutritious food  (see Figure 3). This new concept of soil fertility still 
represents the ability of the soils to provide nutrients to sustain plant growth, but now integrating the 
soil’s physical, chemical, and biological properties  and characteristics. In the past, soil fertility was 
always more related to chemical and physical properties , with soils usually see n as a rather passive 
body. This new concept focusses on the living part of the soil (biological properties), incorporating the 
concept of soil health, and stating that the living organisms influence the availability of nutrients to the 
plants.

A delicate balance of nutrients for a healthy soil 
Healthy soils with sufficient levels of available nutrients are the foundation of nutritious food  (see 
Figure 4). Nutrient-overloaded soils  release more greenhouse gases (N 2O) and can affect soil 
biodiversity. The same way, nutrient-depleted soils bring a series of problems, including less nutritious 
food and lo w yields. Thus, a balanced nutrition that will keep critical levels of soil fertility is a 
keystone to ensure healthy soils . Without nutrients, there can be no life on earth, including human 
life. Plants absorb mineral nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, zinc, or iron from the 
environment. Mineral nu trients are essential to plants and animals and are major drivers of food 
production. Therefore, not surprisingly, providing these nutrients as fertili ser to agricultural systems 
was one of the pillars of the Green Revolution, responsible for saving billions of people from starvation 
and for allowing us to feed the current nearly 8 billion people on earth. Nitrogen – N, potash – K, and 
phosphorous – P, are the three most frequently used nutrients to boost plant biomass production. 
However, the continuous application of these nutrients, and lack of knowledge on the appropriate way 
of managing them, has led to changes in their natural cycle and to problems of excess of these nutrients 
in the environment, which is historically linked to excess of N and P in the  environment (safe 
environmental limits), particularly in Europe. 
While biogeochemical cycles of nutrients are usually at climax in nature, this is not the case with 
agricultural systems. In nature, there is no removal of nutrients  (or very little) as there is no harvest. 
On the other hand, the goal of any agricultural system is to produce something (e.g., fruits, grains, 
meat, biomass, etc.), and when the harvest leaves the farm there is a natural exportation of nutrients, 
no matter if the system is conventional or organic . This brings a negative balance of nutrients to the 
soils, which need to be replenished to maintain soil health and potential biomass production. Except 
for nitrogen, which can be fixed by microorganisms in symbiosis with certain legume crops, all the 
other nutrients need to be added as organic or mineral fertili sers. Even the most fertile soils will at 
some point lose their natural fertility if there is only the removal of nutrients and no input.  
This leads to a second challenge, which is the balanced nutrition. Applying the needed nutrients at the 
right rate s to keep critical levels in the soil is mandatory for soil health.  This requires a good 
understanding of the soil and cropping system used . For nutrients recommendation, soil and plant 
analysis are essential, as well as fertiliser recommendation guidelines.  Nutrients should be 
recommended following the 4R principle – applying fertilisers at the Right dose, Right source, Right 
time, and Right placement . This 4R principle is the proven solution to improve nutrients use 
efficiency and optimise fertilisers recommendation.

A recent report (2022)6 published by the European Environment Agency (EEA) brings the most updated 
assessment of the Impacts of nutrients (N and P) and heavy metals (Pb, Cd, Zn, and Cu)  in European 
agriculture. The report clearly stated the problems regarding N and P in Europe, showing the issues 
with their overuse, but also indicating when they are needed to ensure food production and to close 
yield gaps. While Europe is one of the most food secure regions in the world, it still needs to increase 
its food production, for domestic demand  and to ensure food security globally.  The EEA report 
highlights that several regions in Europe still have substantial crop yield gaps induced by nutrient 
limitations, demanding a higher level of fertilisation. 
This report correctly state s the differences between N and P  biogeochemical cycles and their 
environmental issues. While P has a very low mobility in the soil and does not possess a gas phase, N 
shows different oxidation states, is very mobile, and shows a gas phase (e.g., NH3 and N2O). While P is 
associated with surface water eutrophication, N is  related to surface water eutrophication, soil 
acidification, groundwater contamination, and greenhouse gas emissions.  Understanding their 
differences is necessary to bring the right solutions for each one of these very important nutrients. 
As the latest and most comprehensive report on the impacts of N and P in European agriculture, it 
shows that there is a large spatial discrepancy in terms of the use of N and P in Europe (see Figure 5). 
On average, N and P are used in excess in the EU, with t he surplus of N reaching 53.0 kg/ha/yr and P 
2.3 kg/ha/yr. Even though these are average numbers, the reality is that the places where they are 
applied in excess are rather localized, particularly in the case of P. If the EU reaches the target yields 
proposed in this report, P removal by harvest would increase by 25%. Keeping the same level of P input 
would turn this surplus into a deficit and lead to an overall mining of the European soils for P. In views 
of crop demand (P removal),  there is a need to reduce P inputs in Western Europe (Ireland, the 
Netherlands, Belgium, and Luxembourg and Bretagne in France), while increased P inputs are needed 
in the remaining parts of Europe.

The Soil Health Law should reflect the 4R principles – Right dose, Right source, Right time, and Right 
placement 
The foundation of any efficient soil fertility management is to provide farmers with adequate tools to 
measure the bioavailability of nutrients  in the soil (soil analysis) and to recommend the appropriate 
amount of nutrients (fertiliser recommendation guides), for each specific soil and crop condition. The 
4R principle – applying fertilisers at the Right dose, Right source, Right time, and Right placement, is 
the most effective tool that EU can use to reach the target of reducing nutrient losses by 2030.  The 
4R principle provides the framework to increase nutrients use efficiency and improve farmers’ 
profitability, sustainability, and sustain (or even increase) crop production. The Soil Health Law should 
rely on and develop further the 4R framework to improve soil fertility and health throughout Europe. 
This ra ises other overlooked issues in agriculture worldwide, not only in Europe. While new 
technologies based on satellites, drones, spectroscopic techniques, and others are very important 
farming management tools, best way of recommending the right amount of nutrients (fertilisers) for a 
given crop remains based on soil analysis and understanding crop needs. If we use (and recommend) 
more nutrients than what is necessary , it is because we are missing this basic information. A good 
understanding of nutrients availability in the soil (e.g., LUCAS soil) and up to date fertili ser 
recommendation guidelines is of paramount importance to recommend the right amount of nutrients 
crops need to reach a desired yield. This would allow farmers and extension agents to recommend less 
fertilisers where soils are rich (above a critical threshold) and more nutrien ts where the soils are 
deficient. Once this is done, policies and technologies that would allow the transportation of recycled 
fertilisers (manure) to the areas where soils are deficient could close the gap of circular economy 
regarding nutrients in Europe. 
Taking the 4R principle to the extreme means that each nutrient should be managed in a separate way, 
which is also known as precision agriculture. Nutrients behave differently in the soil, and plants require 
different amounts of each one of them in different stages of their cycle. Applying fertiliser technologies 
that improve nutrients use efficiency by plants and that meet plants requirements for each nutrient 
(improve plants uptake and decrease losses to the environment) is important to cease nutrient losses 
and improve soil health . Having a balanced fertili sation that includes other nutrients (e.g., calcium, 
sulphur, zinc, and boron) is important to improve plants biomass production and optimi se the use of 
all the other resources, including N and P. On top of that, developing optimi sed plant varieties, 
cropping systems (including rotation, cover crops and mixed crops), and bringing the right 
microorganisms (e.g., plant growth promoters and nitrogen fixers) would close the gaps towards 
achieving more sustainable, optimised, and productive agricultural systems. 
Advancing with the 4Rs for specific nutrients has already been pushed  for N 9, which they called the 
4R+. This 4R+ concept shows that the solution to improve nutrients use efficiency , and to help solve 
the issues with misuse of nutrients and environmental contamination , passes through the separate 
and unique management of each nutrient . Integrating, not only the 4R components , but additional 
management practices best suited to each nutrient is needed. This allows us to achieve more precise 
site-specific recommendations that would optimise yields and nutrients use efficiency.

The need to map soil variability in the EU 
In this sense, OCP has been developing a strong agronomy program in Europe with partners including 
universities and research and extension institutions. This program is currently developed in nine 
European countries (including 6 EU Member States)  – Bulgaria, Greece, Romania , Serbia, Bosnia,  
Poland, Turkey, Germany, and France, and focus on bringing solutions to the farmers that would allow 
them to produce more and optimi se the amount of nutrients they apply. The main target of  this 
program is to understand the status of P, K, S, and micronutrients in the soil (Figure 6) and the response 
of cereals and oil seeds to the fertili sation with these nutrients. Mapping soil variability and bringing 
solutions that would allow farmers to apply what is needed at each square meter of their land , is the 
foundation of the 4R concept . It is also  the solution that will allow us to optimi se our fertili ser 
recommendations and decrease nutrient losses to the environment while ensuring food production.

reduces nitrogen GHG emissions . This is a big support for farmers to improve the nutrition

The EU’s CRM Act 
Comments and suggestions 
• An overall framework for 2030 EU strategic autonomy 
Introduction 
Europe’s accelerated REPowerEU clean energy goals but also some its other policies (such as 
agraculture, construction and renovation, health care) require a considerable increase of strategic 
metals and minerals for new technologies, and a number of raw materials are central in several strategic 
areas.  
The EU’s urgent priority in the next decades is to secure sufficient primary and secondary raw materials 
from its own resources and constrained global markets whilst ensuring that environmental and social 
impacts are minmised. For many commodities only after 2040, meaningful volumes of recycled material 
for economy of scale recycling will start to be available from Europe’s first-generation of clean energy 
technologies, beginning to balance the need for primary raw materials. 
Europe’s Critical Raw Materials Act should be at least as bold and decisive as the US’s Inflation 
Reduction Act and onserve intensely the strategic approach of other countries that are major suppliers 
of these strategic materials in order to drive forward and protect European investments and secure 
responsible global supply. 
 This requires the Commission to set headline goals and 2030 targets that will catalyse real change once 
implemented, avoiding the risk of disjointed policy actions that do not shift the status quo. 
1) Establishing a priority list of strategic raw materials 
We support the European Commission’s approach to identify which strategic metals and minerals 
Europe will require in higher volumes for supplying its twin transitions and other strategic areas. A 
prioritisation of “strategic raw materials” will provide clarity to Member States to focus on national raw 
materials projects most linked with the Green Deal needs. 
- It is crucial that this prioritisation is not limited to today’s Critical Raw Materials list. 
A first step: codifying “strategic raw materials” within an overall coherent approach 
• The current criticality assessment has a specific methodology providing a time-limited view on 
today’s risk of supply disruption and past developments. It does not reflect on future demand 
increases, supply risks, and investment needs linked to the twin transition. A substantial number

2) Supporting investments in new and current operations 
• The Act should aim to keep all EU raw materials production economically viable (re. growing 
energy/geopolitical challenges) and address hindrances and obstacles. 
• The Act should recognise that even some fossil resources are used in chemical transformation 
and not as a fuel and will therefore also be reuqired in the future. 
• The Act should recognised that many raw materials are essential to Europe: All metals and 
minerals have an essential role to play in strategic areas of the European economy even when 
major future demand increase isn’t expected, and producers will equally require strong 
framework conditions to prevent new dependencies in the future. Many raw materials and 
minerals are intermediates for others in their production chains and therefore cannot be 
considered in isolation.  
• The Acgt should recoginse that some of tomorrow’s strategic materials are unknown: Metals 
and minerals not defined as strategic today may have new strategic applications in the future 
and so a regular assessment is required (for example on new emerging battery materials and 
technologies). 
• In some cases, the complementarity of not only primary and secondary but also of natural and 
synthetically produced materials such as graphite should be taken into consideration. 
• The Act should commit to reviewing Europe’s strategic needs on a five-year basis. 
• Investment potential for raw materials and across Member States differs due to availability and 
degree of exploration and known deposits as well as expansion possibilities in built up areas: 
Europe has some level of a project pipeline for most of the strategic materials needed in its 
energy transition, stimulated by the rising demand from downstream sectors. However, 
investments need to be fostered and protected in particular where China has a very dominant 
global position and countries or companies in other parts of the world use unfair trade 
practices. 
• The planned regulation on foreign subsidies should be applied where appropriate. 
 
3) Putting the legislation in place: Setting a 2030 headline goal for the right strategic autonomy 
To catalyse real action, the Critical Raw Materials Act must formalise an ambitious 2030 headline goal 
targeting a minimum level of strategic autonomy and diversification for strategic raw materials. This will 
confirm the overall strategic ambition for Member States to follow with their EU projects and strategies. 
The US’s Inflation Reduction Act has demonstrated how a strong headline measure can immediately 
drive forward domestic investment in minerals supply chains. The European Union must define targets

• Ensuring a minimum level of strategic autonomy 
• Europe should set a minimum but realistic level of strategic autonomy to achieve across all its 
strategic materials, rather than only setting targets for a few of them. 
• Where there are difficulties to set these targets a deep analysis of the reasons should be carried 
out.  
• It should be noted that Europe does not have potential to source some specific raw materials 
domestically in meaningful minimum levels by 2030, due to insufficient geological resources and 
scrap availability. Only where it is necessary, supply from defined preferential  partners – based 
on a formal strategic agreement – may also be identified for qualifying towards the minimum 
target. 
• Ensuring a minimum level of diversification 
• Europe should set a minimum level of diversification to achieve across all its strategic materials, 
which prevents an over-dependence on single suppliers as their demand increases 
• Targets should be set at extracted and smelted/refined raw materials levels. Both are important, 
to guard against China’s dominance of imported raw and processed materials as well as next 
stage products, and single suppliers for extracted and processed materials as well as next level 
products. 
• For raw materials, there may be some cases where single suppliers will still provide a significant 
share of the EU’s imports in 2030 due to concentration of resources. This could be permitted for 
Europe’s formal strategic partners, but should be notified as an exception to the overall rule. 
• Europe’s mining and processing capacity has already declined progressively in the last 10-20 
years. Europe should set a formal goal that it will maintain and expand its existing capacity – at 
least to grow its domestic contribution in line with projected demand growth

• Actions to bring new European projects online this decade and prevent EU 
capacity curtailments 
Introduction 
The Commission’s ambition to identify priority projects, accelerate permitting procedures and unlock 
new finance is a very welcome step to improve the speed and viability of new projects. It is just as 
important that the Commission takes action to ensure competitive operating conditions and a level 
playing field (i.e. energy costs, trade defence, joined-up environmental policies).  
o New EU legislation should prioritise projects for faster permitting and finance 
There are numerous European projects at intermediate permitting stages, which could be brought 
online this decade for strategic metals and minerals (e.g. for graphite) and provide between 5 and 50% 
of Europe’s needs. But most suffer from permitting delays, local opposition, or operational uncertainty, 
and there is a risk that too few are successful. The Critical Raw Materials Act can make a measurable 
difference to taking strategic European raw materials projects forwards, including mining and 
processing. In our view, EU action requires three steps. 
Within a year, the Commission should introduce an EU-level list which formally identifies the priority 
projects that will improve Europe’s strategic autonomy for the metals and minerals in scope, allowing 
for accelerated permitting and easier financing. This list should cover the full raw materials value chain: 
mining, refining, and recycling operations. All viable projects should be added in a longlist, before 
identifying the priority shortlist for taking forward urgently. 
An EU strategic raw materials project list could take inspiration from the EUs Projects of Common 
Interest (PCIs) list for interconnecting energy system infrastructure, i.e. 
o A project list that is reviewed and updated every two years 
Identification of priority projects for Europe 
o One of the priority projects for all Member States should be the exploration 
programme for new deposits and the reassessment of existing ones in light of 
changed circumstances. 
o Existing projects at a permitting stage should be given priority to allow for quick 
access to new resources.  
o Research into the recyclability of end of life products where this has not been 
established yet should be given priority.  
o Priority financing from EU and national tools

3. Competitive OpEx & level playing field 
High OpEx costs are a significant barrier to entry for European companies, which will face 
intensive competition from China and other regions with favourable conditions and/or state 
support. Unless the EU’s Critical Raw Materials Act catalyses a broader rethink of industrial 
policy in other areas, Europe will remain a less attractive investment destination compared with 
other regions. 
• Competitive electricity and energy: synthetic graphite processing activities are highly 
electricity-intensive, with power comprising up to 40% of total production costs (in normal 
functioning electricity markets). Natural graphite mining operations also need to electrify on the 
path to decarbonisation. Both existing and new operations face a critical challenge to access 
competitive low-carbon electricity, during today’s power crisis and in the medium-term. 
The industry has put forward concrete proposals for tools which can facilitate access to 
competitively priced low/zero-carbon electricity. The Critical Raw Materials Act should catalyse 
urgent action on these proposals in the respective policy areas: 
• Trade defence: China completely dominates numerous global markets. There is a high risk of 
China dumping or distorting prices in its dominant markets once Europe commits to bringing 
new projects online, to retain its position. New EU projects for these commodities may require 
pre-emptive and assertive trade defence measures, in cases where a risk is identified.

The EU critical raw materials act should encourage an assertive application of the EU’s trade 
defence framework: 
o Commit to speed up the process for imposition of provisional trade defence measures. 
This does not require a law change, but speeding up the process through possibilities 
allowed in the EU’s existing trade defence framework (following the US practice)  
o Reaffirm the importance of keeping existing trade defence measures in place for 
graphite at proven risk of dumping from China. 
• Environmental coherence: New raw materials projects face unintended investment 
bottlenecks from chemicals and environmental legislation. For example, major investment 
decisions are being delayed by uncertain REACH requirements. It’s crucial that the Commission 
takes this opportunity to address incoherencies that are delaying investments, while 
maintaining environmental ambition levels. 
o The EU critical raw materials act should align with the REACH review and other 
environmental policies:  
o Call for predictability and coherence in the ongoing REACH review, including a new focus 
on “strategic raw materials” and their production/recycling routes, which allows the 
Commission to make earlier decisions on risk management measures that are effective 
while also coherent with the Green Deal investment objectives.  
o Recognise the „essentiality“ of a series of precursors for processing and production since 
otherwise the resource base for the industrial redevelopment is curtailed even further. 
o Commit to developing new Member State Guidance for applying the Water Framework 
Directive to mining projects as well as any other notified incoherence in the application 
of environmental policies, i.e. enforce the already acknowleged compatibility of 
extractive operations and Natura 2000 sites where this has been proven on a case by 
case basis. 
 
• Maintaining the level-playing field 
Europe will remain reliant on imports for a range of strategic materials to supply its medium-
term growth in the next 15 years, even with a successful domestic investment strategy to create 
a level of strategic autonomy .  
The Critical Raw Materials Act must include an global strategy for securing responsible and 
diversified imports, avoiding an overdependence on unsustainable single suppliers while 
safeguarding the EU industrial base.

Trade Defence instruments 
Europe’s strategic raw materials supply is threatened by unfair trade practices - especially from 
China. The EU’s Critical Raw Materials strategy should commit to maintaining a level playing 
field and fair trade conditions with all global partners.  
China has massively subsidised its energy and processing industry and now has a dominant 
global position for most strategic metals and minerals (from 35% to 95% of global production). 
The EU has already needed to implement anti-dumping measures for several sectors including 
the graohite industry to safeguard against proven dumping and subisdisation. 
If the EU looks to establish new domestic capacity for natural graphite where China has a very 
dominat  position, then it must safeguard against the likelihood of dumping or other trade 
distortions which could hinder and derail projects. This requires speeding up the process for 
imposing provisional trade defence measures, within the existing framework. Existing anti-
dumping measures must also be maintained on synthetic graphite electrodes with established 
production capacity in Europe. 
Free trade agreements 
All the EU’s Free Trade Agreements should contain strong, enforceable provisions on raw 
materials and the next processing steps in the supply chain, to diversify sourcing, reduce 
dependency on single or limited suppliers, and ensure access at more competitive prices, but 
not only on the raw materials but also their respective downstream products. Otherwise the 
development of EU resources will just be undercut again. 
Free trade agreements should also include chapters on regulatory cooperation and 
sustainability, with powerful enforcement mechanisms to ensure that non-European partners 
also meet expected environmental and regulatory standards.  
Rules of origin 
We support the application of stricter Rules of Origin for the graphite sector, with the PEM 
convention’s revised list offering the right blueprint for future FTAs. Strict rules are needed to 
prevent products from third countries gaining preferential origin and exported to the EU market 
duty-free and circumventing the applicable tariffs.

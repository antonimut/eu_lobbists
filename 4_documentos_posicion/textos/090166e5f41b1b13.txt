 
 
EU Critical Raw Materials Act  
Response to Call for Evidence 
 
 
Stora Enso contributes to making the EU and world carbon neutral in following ways:  
• by capturing, or sequestrating, carbon dioxide in growing trees 
• by storage of carbon in harvested wood products 
• by substitution, when bio-based products and bioenergy replace other products that are fossil-based 
• by becoming the first forest products company to have its climate targets approved by the Science Based Targets initiative in 2017  
• In 2019, our total climate impact, including avoided emissions by product substitution and forest sequestration, was 12 million tonnes of CO2 
• In addition to that we also operate at the heart of the bioeconomy and contributes to a circular economy with our renewable and recyclable products 
Stora Enso is a renewable materials company that employs 
more than 18 000 people in the EU. We deliver renewable 
and circular products that store carbon and provide low -
carbon alternatives to non-renewable materials. We are one 
of the largest private fores t owners in the world and are 
working to replace fossil -based materials with sustainable, 
renewable alternatives based on wood.  
 
We fully agree that to achieve the green transition, the EU 
must significantly increase and diversify its critical raw 
materials supply, strengthen circularity, and support 
research and innovation.  
 
To achieve these objectives, it is worth noting that such 
materials are not only available through extractive 
industries, as new, renewable alternatives are emerging. In 
this context , Stora Enso believes that the Critical Raw 
Materials Act must strengthen and support European 
renewable raw materials and solutions that can replace 
fossil materials imported from outside Europe .  This 
would help to boost Europe’s strategic autonomy and 
resilience, in line with the Green Deal’s objectives.  We 
note that the Call for Evidence refers to a parallel objective 
focusing on the substitution of critical raw materials.  This is 
welcome, but we would recommend going further  by 
adding a fifth pillar to the regulatory dimension outlined 
in the Call for Evidence. 
 
Stora Enso recommends that the European Commission 
therefore also focus on: 
1. projects and solutions where renewable material can 
replace fossil material to help speed up the European 
green transition; and  
2. scaling up solutions where European raw materials can 
replace materials sourced outside Europe to shore up 
European self-sufficiency and resilience.

The definition of critical raw materials is tied to their high 
economic importance and the hig h risks linked to their 
supply.1  The new EU policy framework for critical raw 
materials should, therefore, consider the significance of 
emerging alternatives to critical raw materials with sufficient 
supply and production capacity in the EU.  
 
One such a lternative is our wood-based hard carbon, 
called Lignode®, which can replace fossil -based 
graphite (synthetic or natural) in battery anodes. Lignode is 
derived from lignin, a side stream of the pulping process, 
and is readily available in Europe. Trees consist of 20-30% 
lignin, which acts as a binder and gives wood its stiffness. 
As such, lignin is one of the largest renewable sources of 
carbon anywhere.  
 
Traditionally, lignin has been burnt to enable the recovery 
of pulping chemicals and to supply energy to the industrial 
process. However, we can now take some of it and turn it 
into a value-added product, namely battery anode material. 
We are thus ensuring the material is used for a more long -
lived anode product. As only about 2% of the world’s 
graphite production takes place in the EU, Lignode offers 
an essential substitution for a non-EU-produced critical raw 
material,2   and supports the transition towa rds a 
sustainable EU battery value chain.  
 
The Commission’s Critical Raw Material Act is thus not only 
a key opportunity to secure the traditional materials needed 
for the EU’s green and digital transitions, in light of the 
different geopolitical instabil ities affecting supply chains; it 
is also a chance for the EU to accelerate the transition 
towards the substitution of critical raw materials, 
wherever possible.

Whilst Lignode is a direct substitute for graphite, non-fossil 
solutions in general open up more widely for new battery 
technologies using, for example, sodium-ion instead of 
lithium-ion technology3. This would relieve pressure on 
other critical raw materials such as cobalt, nickel, copper 
and, of course, lithium itself. 
 
As stated in the Call for Evidence, the global demand for 
critical raw materials is projected to soon exceed supply.  
This means that the creation of a level playing field for 
innovative, sustainable alternatives is vital for the EU . 
This requires not only  investments in research and 
innovation, but also the creation of market conditions that 
allow renewable substitutions to compete with traditional 
materials. We believe that the EU should prioritise funding 
for strategic projects, which not only have the potential to 
help secure the EU’s diversified access to critical materials 
but also propose renewable European raw materials. 
 
We have produced lignin since 2015 and our plant at Sunila 
Mill in Finland currently has an annual capacity of 50,000 
tonnes. This production could be further scaled up to more 
than 300 kilotonnes per year in Europe to help meet the 
increasing demand of the battery market, which is 
estimated to be ca. 1 million tonnes annually by 2030. In 
fact, if all relevant pulp and paper mills 4 in the EU were to 
produce anode materials  from lignin , European demand 
could be met by European production .  And this would not 
increase the consumption of forests, because lignin is an 
existing by -product. This provides us with the perfect 
opportunity to increase European resilience in a key 
industrial value chain.

1. Overview of the cobalt industry  
 
Cobalt, listed as a critical raw material in the EU , plays a central role in the EU ’s security, future 
prosperity and transition to a low carbon economy.  
 
• As of 2021, 65% of all cobalt used globally goes into lithium -ion batteries1. Over half of this 
number (34% of all cobalt)  goes into batteries for electric vehicle s, with the remainder going 
principally into batteries for consumer electronics (31%)2.  
• Cobalt is also critical for a range of other sectors, including superalloys used in aerospace and 
defence applications (11% of cobalt use). It is also used for cemented carbide tools, alloys and 
magnets, which represented about 14% of cobalt usage in 2021. 
 
In 2021, cobalt-containing batteries accounted for three quarters of the global electric vehicle battery 
market and EVs are expected to drive 70% of medium-term cobalt market growth3. As the transition to

Cobalt demand is likely to grow significantly as Europe transitions to a green economy. It is a critical 
mineral in this context and likely to be strategically important in the future. We make the following 
recommendations, which are elaborated on later in this submission: 
 
• The Communication  accompanying the Critical Raw Materials Act should set out a plan to 
deepen relationships with partner countries that will be strategically important for the supply of 
cobalt. 
 
• The Critical Raw Materials Act  should establish a mechanism to ensure that strategic critical 
minerals have special political consideration under chemicals management rules  like REACH 
and OSH. 
 
• Consideration should be given to the technology neutrality of recycling efficiency and recycled 
content proposals in the Critical Raw Materials Act. It should be designed to apply commensurate 
requirements to all critical minerals.

The European cobalt market has the potential to grow to 30 - 50 kt in 2030 and 80 -100 kt in 2050. 
Europe’s success rate in developing battery cathode manufacturing capacity will determine the growth 
in cobalt demand4. Employment in the Europe’s cobalt value chain reached 48,000 jobs per annum on 
average over the 2010-2021 period. The end-use sector was by far the largest contributor, generating 
approximately 18,000 jobs. Direct applications followed (15,000) and smelting & refining accounted for 
8,000 jobs. Of total global employment, Europe was the third largest region5.  
 
Tax contributions in Europe amounted to $673 million on average p.a. over the 2010 -2021 period. A 
large proportion  (43%, or  $276 million ), came from end manufacturing, followed by first -use 
manufacturing ($183 million). After China, Europe was the second largest contributor.6 
 
Cobalt is a common metal, but only occurs in quantities that are economically viable to extract in certain 
conditions. This is typically as a by-product of copper and nickel mining. The  
Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) and Zambia sit atop the “copperbelt” region, parts if which have 
significant cobalt reserves. This constitutes about 46% 7 of known economically extractable cobalt 
reserves. Australia has the second largest reserves, representing about 18.5%8. Other potential major 
sources include Indonesia (~8%), Cuba (6.5%), the Philippines (~3.5%) and Canada (~3%)9. 
 
While terrestrial mining is an established source of cobalt, there will be other options in the future.  
Recycling be a major one , particularly once a critical mass  of cobalt is in circulation . It’s possible to 
recycle the vast majority of the cobalt in a relevant spent battery and cobalt – as an element – can be 
reused infinitely. Additionally, while th ere are 7.6m tonnes of land -based cobalt reserves , there is a 
predicted 120m tonnes of sea bed cobalt resources 10 (albeit there needs to be work done to identify 
what proportion are “reserves” and develop a better understanding of related environmental risks). 
 
Battery production requires cobalt substances that are refined to a battery-grade level of purity, which 
requires specialist facilities. Once cobalt is mined, it is typically turned into an intermediate material like 
cobalt hydroxide (in the case of copper -cobalt ores) for transport, and then needs to be further 
processed into substances that can be used in products, for example cobalt sulphate (of battery grade 
purity). This is subsequently integrated within precursor and cathode active materials that will ultimately 
be incorporated within a battery. In an annex to this submission is a more detailed flowchart of the cobalt 
supply chain. 
 
Currently China does most of the processing and refining of cobalt, especially of battery grade purity. 
China was the world’s leading consumer of cobalt, with more than 80% of its consumption being used 
by the rechargeable battery industry11. Processing and refining of cobalt can be (and is) done outside 
China, Finland being the largest cobalt refiner outside of China , with about 10% of global supply 12. 
Making batteries requires cobalt substances that are refined to a battery -grade level of purity, which 
requires specialist facilities . There would need to be a considerable increase in European domestic 
cobalt refining and processing capacity for Europe to achieve self-sufficiency. The EU does not provide 
commensurate financial and political support to its industry when compared with countries like China.

There is enough cobalt for Europe to achieve a resilient long-term supply of cobalt, albeit Europe also 
needs to take steps to ensure it can continue to access these resources. Globally, new cobalt streams 
can be brought online in a number of ways, including undeveloped mining assets, extracting cobalt from 
tailings sites, recycling, and – assuming it can be done sustainably – nascent exploration of deep-sea 
resources (where there is estimated to be 120m tonnes of cobal t, compared to 7.6m on land 13).  
However, Europe does not have abundant domestic terrestrial reserves of cobalt. Inevitably, therefore, 
open strategic autonomy for cobalt will depend on a number of factors. 
 
We would recommend any strategy to supply of raw cobalt be based on the following pillars: 
 
1. Resilient international supply – the Copperbelt region, Australia, Cuba and Indonesia will be 
important future sources of cobalt and so the Commission must recognise that there will be a 
geopolitical element to global security of supply. This plays to Europe’s strengths. The current 
government of the DRC is pro-European and looking to grow its Western facing infrastructure. 
Zambia is a democratic nation with close ties to both the DRC and the West. Australia, likewise, 
is an ally of the European Union , with close investment and trade relations, including the 
Australia-European Union Framework Agreement which recently entered into force. 
 
2. Recycling – if cobalt can be collected from spent batteries, it can be recycled in large amounts. 
By 2040, it is possible that Europe’s use of cobalt will start declining, driven by high volumes of 
recycled content14. This will effectively allow Europe to leverage its position as a consumer of 
cobalt products to allow it to “supply” itself, whilst lessening its environmental footprint. By 2050, 
secondary supply from end -of-life batteries has the theoretical potential to provide 65% of 
Europe’s total cobalt demand. This requires that Europe is successful in building up sufficient 
battery recycling capacity to match its electric vehicle market15. 
 
3. Refining and processing capacity – in an annex to this paper we include a Sankey diagram 
that provides a simplified flow of cobalt from mine to products. It is worth noting that access to 
cobalt – whether from primary or secondary sources – cannot guarantee availability of coba lt 
for end products without commensurate processing and refining capacity. Cobalt also has a 
complex value chain that means a range of facilities are needed. It is not possible to achieve 
a resilient cobalt supply chain  without a strategy for the creation of refining and 
processing capacity. 
 
Based on these three pillars we would make three recommendations for action to the European 
Commission. 
 
3. Recommendation 1: Sourcing of cobalt 
 
Given its global nature, sourcing cobalt for European industry will inherently be a geopolitical endeavour. 
As mentioned above, the European Union is also uniquely well situated to capitalise on this.  
 
The DRC will be an essential partner for the EU on cobalt supply. Diversification is possible for Europe 
to achieve, but DRC will remain an important source in all scenarios. The current DRC government has 
indicated its desire to work with Western partners and is seeking investment from European companies 
in both mining and the processing and refining of cobalt.  It is also in the DRC’s interest to ensure it s 
exports are diversified. 
 
There is a “win -win-win” scenario here. Firstly, if the EU and its industry supports the DRC , then it 
justifies the government’s pro-European stance and turns from them a potential partner into a regional 
ally. Secondly, this helps secure European access to cobalt and other critical minerals from the DRC.  
Thirdly, it ensures cobalt is being sourced responsibly and in accordance with the highest standards, 
as mandated under EU law and in line with the expectations of many European citizens, and will create

4. Recommendation 2: Alignment of European ambitions 
 
Two areas that will be crucial to European resilience for cobalt will be recycling, and processing and 
refining. As mentioned earlier, without access to appropriate processing and refining facilities, there 
can be no open strategic autonomy for cobalt , as the raw material cannot be turned into substances 
used in end products. Additionally, recycling is the most likely candidate for European “domestic” 
sourcing of cobalt. 
 
Both recycling, and processing and refining will be subject to the European Union’s rigorous chemicals 
management laws, REACH and occupational safety and health (OSH). Our industry supports measures 
to protect workers and we have long advocated for an Occupational Exposure Limit (OEL) for cobalt, 
under OSH. However, it is important to note that there is a balance to be stru ck between the extent to 
which cobalt use is controlled in Europe and creating a globally-competitive environment in Europe for 
these crucial industries.  
 
If decisions are made within technical committees to effectively ban the use of substances (either 
because the restrictions are not technically  feasible, or European manufacturing ceases to be 
economically viable or desirable) then it will not be possible to achieve open strategic autonomy for 
these in Europe.  
 
The Cobalt Institute has long advocated in favour of an OEL for cobalt, but the proposed values under 
the previous REACH Restriction (and possibly the future OEL) were so low that it would have made 
working with cobalt in Europe impossible – ending the possibility of recycling, processing and refining 
in Europe and most likely making the EU reliant on imports from jurisdictions with much lower standards 
of worker protection. This level was established on the theoretical basis of one potential cancer case in 
the EU every 50 years (based on rat studies), and despite there being no evidence of carcinogenicity in 
humans. 
 
We recommend that the Communication, which we understand will accompany the 
Critical Raw Materials Act, set in place a plan to engage with strategic partners. 
 
This could include: 
• the development of Strategic Partnerships with mineral-rich countries; and 
• plans relating  to how Eu ropean investments (like the Global Gateway ) can 
support EU soft power in relation to mineral-producing countries.

If the Commission believes the risk a substance poses is  so great that it means that the loss of open 
strategic autonomy is a price worth paying, then it should of course be able to do so. But that is ultimately 
a political decision, which should be made actively and at an appropriate political level. It should not be 
possible that it happens “by accident” because two parts of the Commission are acting without 
coordination. For the sake of clarity, this does not mean substances with risks should not be managed 
– the end result may in fact be the same – rather it is to say decisions should be made in a way that 
accounts for the full range of impacts and risks. 
 
5. Recommendation 3: Technology neutrality 
 
The Cobalt Institute has previously expressed its concern that applying recycling efficiency and recycled 
content requirements for cobalt in batteries under the Batteries Regulation, could have the unintended 
consequence of incentivising battery chemistries that are not contained within the Act.  We proposed 
that a mechanism be included for the addition of commensurate  requirements for all industrial -scale 
battery chemistries.  This would ensure high levels of recycling for battery metals, but without the 
unintended consequence of incentivising chemistries like lithium iron phosphate that are not 
economically recyclable16 and that use minerals deemed even more critical like phosphorus17. 
 
Any proposal should apply commensurate requirements t o all critical minerals , in order to avoid 
regrettable substitution to other critical minerals, that might have unintended impacts on recyclability. 
 
Additionally, we would reiterate the urgent need to include lithium iron phosphate batteries under the 
scope of the Battery Regulation, given that there are numerous estimates that this chemistry is already 
on course to comprise 40% of the global battery market by 2030 18. These are not currently covered, 
which will make them comparatively cheaper and therefore act as in incentive to switch to batteries that 
are not economically recyclable.

6. About the Cobalt Institute 
The Cobalt Institute represents the global cobalt industry and value chain, our members account for 
over 75% of global the cobalt market. It is a non-profit trade association composed of producers, users, 
recyclers, and traders of cobalt. We promote the sustainable and responsible production and use of 
cobalt in all its forms.  
We recommend that c onsideration should be given to the technology neutrality of 
recycling efficiency and recycled content proposals  in the Critical Raw Materials Act. It 
should be designed to apply commensurate requirements to all critical minerals. 
 
Additionally, the Commission urgently needs to include lithium iron phosphate batteries 
under the scope of these requirements in the Batteries Regulation , to avoid creating 
perverse incentives that will worsen the EU’s open strategic autonomy.

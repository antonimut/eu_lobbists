ACEA VIEWS ON THE CRITICAL RAW 
MATERIALS ACT 
 
GENERAL REMARKS 
ACEA welcomes the announced initiative of the European Commission for a European Critical 
Raw Materials Act (CRMA). 
The COVID -19 pandemic together with the semiconductor crisis and Russia’s invasion of 
Ukraine made obvious some inherent weaknesses in global supply chains and the EU’s high 
dependency on third countries for certain key materials. 
From ACEA’s perspective, the CRMA initiative is timely and essential to enable the necessary 
conditions to ensure a smooth and resilient green and digital transition while at the same time 
strengthening the open strategic autonomy  and supply chain diversification  of key EU 
industries, such as the automotive sector and its battery value chain. 
The European automobile industry is fully committed to  the goal of decarbonizing road 
transport. Following the setting of a zero-CO2 target for 2035, that de-facto means the phase-
out of traditional internal combustion engine for passenger cars and vans , and future CO2 
reduction targets  for the heavy -duty vehicles  coming soon , the need to ensure a smo oth 
change towards the green transition has never been more evident. Currently, almost 40% of 
new car sales in the EU are electrified1, and this is expected to increase  further in the next 
years. Medium-duty electric trucks, for example for city distribution and refuse collection have 
been in serial production since 2019, and 2022 saw the first series production of pure electric 
heavy-duty vehicles. The nature of the market in both segments will be completely transformed 
over the next decade. 
E-mobility is clearly the driving force of transport decarbonization. That also means that: 
• Battery technology will be dominant for decarbonizing cars and vans, and Europe 
cannot electrify the sector without having reliable access to the necessary critical 
raw materials and battery co mponents e.g. batter y cell s (coming mostly from 
China). 
• The battery, fuel cells and hydrogen value chains must be developed in order to 
reduce strategic dependence from a small number of third countries for a critical 
sectors (especially for the transport as well as energy sector). Critical raw materials 
play a crucial role.

• When it comes to bilateral trade agreements with third countries, a more pragmatic

Nevertheless, the key materials in batteries for these vehicles are  largely of non-EU origin, 
making our EU industry reliant on other countries and external factors for their sourcing. These 
supply chains are vulnerable to external shocks, to dominance by a very small number of third 
countries and to supply/demand imbalances that create significant price volatility. To reduce 
these risk dependencies and advance the transition to e -mobility, it is essential  to secure 
enhanced access to critical raw materials.  
ACEA calls on the European Commission to centre its strategy for raw materials  (in general 
needed in the automotive sector, not only for e -mobility purposes)  around the following 
principles: 
1. Enhance - through industrial policy  measures - domestic capacity to extract, refine 
and process critical raw material s as identified in the Commission’s critical raw 
materials list, which should be updated in 2023. 
2. Improve security of supply by diversifying external access to critical raw materials 
coming from third countries and helping companies to diversify sources to those raw 
materials.  
3. Strengthen the role of the circular economy to increase the availability of secondary 
raw materials. 
4. Ensure that access to critical raw materials - whether sourced domestically, externally 
or through recycling methods  - respect international standards and adheres to 
sustainable practices that fully respect the human rights and the environment. 
 
As underlined earlier , the CRMA initiative is of key importance in the context of the whole 
transformation pathway of the sector  and will impact the entire automotive value chain, 
covering the biggest OEMs to the smallest SMEs. In this regard, ACEA - together with other 
associations in the automotive sector - has already drawn up some recommendations2. 
 
SPECIFIC REMARKS 
Access to critical raw materials is essential for the  set-up and proper functioning of the  
European battery value chain and grid management (as well as the renewable energy use in 
the energy sector) . This is valid to support the transition towards e -mobility and for new or 
second/re-used batteries for the energy sector transformation. The EU should clearly focus on 
ensuring resilience throughout the battery value chain, through diversifying domestic and 
international raw material supply sources and recyclin g (including supportive measures for

investments in domestic raw material supply). The fact that most of the key raw materials are 
insufficiently available on the European market reinforces the need for a trade and industrial 
policy on the basis of both openness and strategic autonomy. 
On the basis of a preliminary analysis, ACEA has identified - in a non-exhaustive manner - that 
the following critical raw materials are of u tmost importance to the green t ransition of our 
sector: 
• Lithium, nickel, manganese, cobalt and graphite, used in the production of lithium -ion 
batteries. 
• Rare earths such as dysprosium, neodymium and praseodymium, which are essential 
to the production of permanent magnets in the traction motors for electric vehicles.  
Access to these materials will therefore have to be significantly enhanced to help ensure the 
green transition of the automobile industry. 
 
1. Domestic capacity and industrial policy  
 
For the automobile sector to deliver the targets set under the EU’s climate policy with regards 
to the reduction of greenhouse emissions, the CRMA will have to strongly rely on the industrial 
policy of the EU in its implementation.  
The European Commission should make sure that the CRMA initiativ e is in line with the key 
priorities of the EU on strengthening its industrial base, ensuring its strategic autonomy, 
enhancing manufacturing investment and creating favourable framework conditions to support 
the global competitiveness of the European industry. 
ACEA therefore sees high value in having an open discussion with all relevant stakeholders 
on the raw material needs throughout the entire automotive value chain to identify existing 
gaps and analyse potential solutions.  
 
2. External dimension 
 
Since there are geological, economic and political limitations to the availability of certain raw 
materials in the EU, imports will continue to play a key role in the future in the EU’s transition 
to a green and digital economy. 
In order to ensure se curity of supply, the EU must avoid  to e xcessively concentrate the 
sourcing of raw materials from  only a few third countries.  The recent invasion of Russia in 
Ukraine exposed the EU’s vulnerability in this respect . S everal industries across Europe - 
including automobile - were severely affected for several months due to shortages in key 
components and critical raw materials until alternative source markets were found. 
The EU’s action in the trade policy field is therefore key and should complement the CRAM 
by: 
 
www.acea.auto 4 
• Ensuring that FTAs concluded with resource-rich countries  are ratified as soon as 
possible after negotiations are finalised. Currently, agreements with Mexico, Mercosur 
- which were concluded over three years ago - and Chile are still waiting to be submitted 
for ratification. 
• Finalizing the FTA negotiations with Australia – one of the world´s largest producers of 
lithium and rare earth – and speed ing up FTA negotiations with Indonesia is also 
extremely important. 
• Exploring options on how to enhance the provisions of the energy and raw materials 
chapters already contained in EU FTAs to ensure that EU companies can maximise 
their benefits. 
• Establishing strategic partnerships with resource-rich countries beyond the framework 
of FTAs to deepen cooperation in the field of critical raw materials , including R&D, 
mining and exploration, processing etc., notably with neighbouring countries.  
• Ensuring coherence between the raw materials act and trade policy in relation to issues 
such as rules of origin for batteries and for recycled materials in the batteries used in 
EVs that would facilitate the export of those vehicles under EU FTAs.  
• Ensuring a level-playing field with third countries. EU partnerships should be 
established on clear basis and fair rules.  
 
3. Circular economy and recycling  
 
Given the heavy dependency on imports, it is essential to ensure that critical raw materials are 
used to their full potential. European companies across the automotive value chain are already 
applying t he principles of circularity in their business models. Vehicle manufacturers are 
already using recycled materials, despite numerous technical limitations and considering that 
vehicles are long-lasting products with an extremely complex supply chain. 
However recycled materials do not necessarily represent the best option to reduce 
environmental impacts over the vehicle’s whole life cycle. The use of recycled material 
sometime increases the weight of a part because the part needs to be reinforced to reach the 
same properties. In these cases, the ecological effect of recycled material s can be offset 
through an increase in CO2 emissions during  the use phase. For the reuse of  critical raw 
materials to be effective on a large scale, a number of key preconditions have to be in place , 
for example: 
• Recycled material quality must be guaranteed. Recycled materials can only be used if 
there is a guarantee that they have the exact same properties as the virgin material. 
• The availability of recycled content must be ensured.  
• The cost of recycled raw materials has must be competitive. 
While recycling and re -using critical raw materials are an attractive alternative to import 
dependency, some measures currently being discussed might result in problematic 
 
www.acea.auto 5 
bottlenecks for the automotive transition towards cleaner mobility solutions. For example, the 
EU is trying to safeguard the reuse of recycled material through provisions such as the ones 
contained in Article 8 of the Battery Regulation. Setting mandatory recycled content targets 
should be done with guarantees on the quality, availability or cost of such re-used material in 
order to avoid disrupting the plans to make cleaner and cheaper vehicles in the future. 
The CRAM should therefore focus on: 
• Encouraging and strengthening the European recycling sector and promoting 
investment in technologies that will yield quality recycled content. 
• Monitoring the availability of the materials considering the growing needs of many 
industrial sectors, including automotive and recycling. 
• Promoting fair pricing policies . In this regard, the E U should avoid any measure that 
might artificially distort the markets such as  mandatory recycled content targets to 
ensure that key material prices remain competitive vis-à-vis the raw materials. 
The automotive industry already has systems in place today that may be used for the tracking 
of critical raw materials contained in vehicles. These systems, tools and processes are well 
established throughout our full global supply chain and may only require minor modifications 
and improvements. In addition, other upcoming legislation such as the Eco design for 
Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR) or the Battery Regulation are elaborating tools to 
collect and provide the relevant information to and within the automotive value chain ( ie. the 
digital product Passport / Battery Passport). 
In order to av oid unnecessary administrative burden  - ie. by duplications of requirements  – 
policies in the pipeline need to be taken into consideration. For instance, waste policy should 
not be discussed in isolation to other policy areas such as climate and energy pol icies. Any 
further initiative should complement and not undermine existing legislation. Furthermore, the 
risk of failing to meet sector-specific recycling targets should be carefully considered. There is 
a considerable legal overlapping among different legislative acts which should be avoided. 
Moreover, any measure must be introduced in a harmonized - standardised - way across the 
EU. 
For this reason, ACEA welcomes the idea of looking into recycling raw materials but warns 
against a one-size-fits-all approach which may lead to more expensive and harder to produce 
batteries in the future. 
 
4. Standardisation, ethical, social, environmental and other issues.  
 
There are number of issues that the CRMA has to tackle with respect to standardi sation, as 
well as voluntary standards /best practices promoting responsible behavio ur in mining, 
processing and use of critical raw materials. This is also valid for a number of social items that 
are related to the extraction of the raw materials (not only the critical ones):

• Assess IRMA (Home - IRMA - The Initiative for Responsible Mining Assurance) for 
mining as the recommended/adopted industr y standard  to ensure le vel-playing 
field. 
• Promote/support/require RMI Environmental, Social & Governance ( ESG) and/or 
RMI All Minerals ( Responsible Minerals Initiative Releases ESG Standard for 
Mineral Supply Chains) for processing companies as the recommended/adopted 
industry standard. 
• Assess RBA ( https://www.responsiblebusiness.org/) for all companies as the 
recommended/adopted industry standard on Code of Conduct. 
• Promote access to raw materials with net-zero carbon footprint. 
• With respect to social and environmental issues, the following suggestions should 
be taken on board by the European Commission when drafting recommendations 
for corporate behaviour in the area of raw materials mining and processing: 
• Ensuring the creation of opportunities for the indigenous community that 
reside in the geographic region , including s upport of worthy community 
institutions, programs and endeavours by actively contributing to charitable, 
educational, environmental, human rights and c ivic initiatives in the local 
community. 
• Protection of the environment that includes protection of rare, sensitive, 
endangered and threatened species  and ensure biodiversity management 
and safe management of operations.  Specific attention to be given to th e 
protection of water that includes addressing water allocation, water quality, 
and the impact of water usage on the local environment/community  and 
mitigating those impacts, as well as mitigation of waste, waste disposal, and 
product transportation impacts across the company and value chain. 
• Ensuring transparency on ESG items along the entire sourcing/value chain. 
• Adoption of globally acceptable assurance certificates (qualifications) related 
to the initiative to “certify” that operations (and entire value  chain) are 
completed in a responsible manner. 
• Avoid that any measures taken would constrain the shift away from critical 
raw material to alternatives due to innovations of the new materials and 
technologies.  
NEXT STEPS AND RECOMMENDATIONS: 
 
ACEA and its members welcome the CRMA initiative and will provide full support to the 
Commission in deeper and further work on this initiative. The automobile sector would like to 
suggest the following recommendations: 
 
www.acea.auto 7 
1. The European Commission - in co -operation with industry stakeholders - should 
continue its work on the update of the critical raw materials list and  associated future 
policy measures. The screening and updating of the list should be based on needs of 
all industry sectors since different industrial sectors have different raw materials needs 
and varying levels of urgency in terms of their green transition and electrification of the 
transport sector. This review process should be initiated as soon as possible to ensure 
that it feeds all the legislative work that will follow in the coming months.  
2. E-mobility is the driving force in the decarboni sation of road transport. Therefore, all 
critical raw materials for the European battery value chain and EV application must be 
assessed and included in the list and reviewed periodically.  
3. A thorough impact assessment is critical ahead of the adoption of any policy measure 
taken and it must include all relevant parts of the e-mobility value chain.  
4. Industry asks for a concrete and rapid action plan to be associated with the proposal 
of the CRMA to provide clear guidance and predictability for the industry and to the 
decision-making process as well as.  
5. With respect to the issue of stock piling, there are a number of examples of good 
practice across the globe that third parties are currently performing and practicing with 
respect to the emergency reserves of raw materials. Updating best practices studies of 
such systems across the globe  could be a good starting point for the European 
Commission. 
6. The European Commission should evaluate and assess the practical implementation 
of the CRAM and, in particular, of those projects aimed at strengthening  domestic 
capacity throughout the entire value chain of raw materials (extraction, refining , 
processing and recycling).

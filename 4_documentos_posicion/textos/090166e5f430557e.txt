 
 
 
Representing 7 million manufacturing, mining  and energy sector workers across Europe, 
industriAll European Trade Union is the voice of Europe’s industrial workforce at European Level. 
IndustriAll Europe is an organisation of independent and democratic trade unions representing 
manual and non-manual workers in the metal, chemical, energy, mining, textile, clothing and footwear 
sectors and related industries and activities.  
As the EU Critical Raw Materials Act will have impacts on the international level, we have 
prepared this contribution with our sister organisation, industriAll Global Union which represents 50 
million workers across the globe in 140 countries in the mining, energy and manufacturing sectors and 
is a force in global solidarity taking up the fight for better working conditions and trade union rights 
around the world. 
Trade unions have long been calling for a stronger EU strategy on raw materials to move 
towards greater independence through the sustainable exploitation of their own resources, as well as 
security of supply through a joint and fair external agend a. The EU raw materials strategy must be in 
line with the UN SDGs and the 2030 Agenda, domestically as wel l as internationally. Increasing 
sustainable activities in the domestic extractive industries and optimising secondary raw materials via 
the completion of a circular economy will create industrial growth, jobs, tax revenue and export 
income. Furthermore, d elivering on environmental, labour and human rights, as well as a gender -
sensitive approach for production and recycling, import and export, should lead to a more socially 
responsible supply.  
Securing the sustainable supply of raw materials in Europe is a  strategic priority and will be 
key to achieve climate neutrality. It is also a question of strategic autonomy  which is more than ever 
important in the current geopolitical context. We thus welcome  that more initiatives, supports and 
policies are going to be developed on the EU level  as lay down in the call for evidence for the  critical 
raw materials act.  
In order to strengthen its strategic autonomy, while ensuring the sustainability of raw 
materials, Europe will need to have a strong and global strategy resting on four pillars:  sustainable 
domestic extraction, circular economy, improving research and innovation to reduce demand and 
dependency, and ensuring international standards and binding human rights worldwide . It is 
important that human rights and en vironmental impacts are given equal importance under the 
Commission’s proposal.

The objectives and policy options of the Critical Raw Materials Act as described in the call for evidence 
for an impact assessment should follow these recommendations:  
1) On pillar 1: defining priorities and objectives for EU actions 
• On setting EU objectives for increasing capacity to guide national and EU efforts in mining, 
refining and recycling, these objectives must be  in line with industrial policies and 
underpinned by a commitment and enforcement of hight environmental and social 
legislation and standards.  
• Any objectives should be in line with a Just Transition agenda , accompanying the 
transitions which will occur in these sectors  of mining, refining, processing and recycling  
(decarbonisation, operations in new sites, exploitation of new raw materials…). The 
involvement of trade unions and workers is key. 
• Any objectives should  be in line with a mandatory human rights and environmental due 
diligence. 
2) On pillar 2: Improving the EU’s monitoring, risk management and governance in the field of 
CRM 
• Mapping the mineral resources in the EU and EEA countries for raw materials must be 
addressed with a coordinated EU effort to ensure a better understanding, that is regularly 
updated. This is why industriAll Europe supports the creation of an (EU) European  
Network or Agency  for Geological Survey, as there is a need to collect data in a uniform 
way, and having an institution dealing with resources in the ground and in our economy  
(secondary raw materials) . As access to minerals is also a strategic security i ssue, the 
agency should also assess imports and exports, as well as global supply and demand. 
• Europe needs to be a frontrunner in research, development and innovation: on ways to 
reduce the demand, more incentive and public support towards substitution, recycling 
possibilities and eco-design. 
3) On pillar 3: Strengthening the EU’s critical raw materials value chain (mining, refining, 
processing, recycling) in a global context 
• Supporting projects and developing capacities to enable the development of the supply  
chain will be key but should comply with environmental and social standards as listed 
above and below (pillar 4).  
• We absolutely need more and fair er circular economy and re-use/recycle of CRM from 
waste in Europe: 
o We need more investments, actions and support from the European Union, 
Member States and companies for this to happen effectively. 
o We need consistent circular policies which include social, economic and 
environmental aspects. A silo approach, focusing on one aspect only will miss the 
objective of a more circular economy. The socioeconomic  dimensions should be 
fully integrated in circular economy policies. 
o These policies, actions and supports must be at the heart of the EU’s long -term 
industrial strategy and include all regions and territories. 
o Investments, stronger incentives and improved policy framework in collection 
and waste treatment systems, as well as a boost for recycling infrastructures, with 
targets set by the Member States, should be at  the heart of national circular 
policies 
o Affordable and sustainable supply of clean energy, coherent with the 
development of a circular industry is absolutely key to develop recycling activities 
o Strong support for research and development is needed to tackle current 
quantitative and qualitative limit of  re-use and recycling of CRM. Public support 
and investment (from local to European level) will be needed in  order to find 
solutions to improve the possibility of recycling and re -use of mineral raw 
materials, as some of them are currently very difficult to recycle or re-use. 
o The sector of waste processing and of materials recycling should guarantee good 
working conditions. 
o A review of the current exportation of waste. The current revision of the Waste 
Shipment regulatio n must  ensure that exports are better regulated, and more 
specifically, exportation must only be allowed when the  country of destination 
demonstrates its commitment towards high climate and environment standards 
but also to workers’ rights and ILO fundamen tal rights. Exports must be 
authorised only when the destination  country can undergo reliable and effective 
audit procedures and can demonstrate that it has implemented, and is following, 
equivalent standards on environment, social and human health. 
 
4) On pillar 4: Ensuring a sustainable level playing field across the single market: 
• Level playing field must also applied to imported materials. 
• Sustainable raw materials (primary and secondary) should be based on a clear 
definition which include workers’ perspective. The presence of strong and independent 
trade unions and collective bargaining will ensure sustainable raw materials extraction, 
refining, processing and recycling with good working conditions, a safer and healthier 
environment, job satisfaction and decent wages. As stated in the EP’s resolution on 
CRM: “Responsible sourcing in the EU can only be based on an effective social dialogue 
promoting the health and safety of workers, securing decent jobs and working 
conditions and protecting workers’ rights by promoting gender equality” 
• The CRM Act must effectively secure decent work and good working conditions, protect 
workers’ rights, support effective social dialogue on the sector and company level, and 
involve trade unions in the planification of the dev elopment of the sector. Social 
dimension must also cover the needs for improving skills, education and training, 
including the support or creation of appropriate training courses and making these 
professions more attractive  by ensuring good working conditi ons. Protecting workers in 
the extractive in dustries and recycling activities  will require solid commitment to 
occupational health and safety  (identification of new H&S risks  linked to CRM will be 
needed urgently). A strong gender equality perspective will have to drive these issues. 
• High-level environmental standards for domestic extraction harmonised on the European 
level via a common regulatory base, preserving the environment, preventing pollution, 
preserving biodiversity and water resou rces, as much as possible, and in complete 
coherence with the reduction of CO2 emissions agreed on the European level. This also 
means accompanying the decarbonisation of the extractives industries. 
• A dialogue with local communities ensuring that local communities are involved in new 
projects. Current EU legislation creates a series of obligations in terms of access to 
information and consultation of local communities. This must be promoted and better 
enforced. 
• Setting and following high social and environm ental standards for mine sites is the only 
way to build trust in mining projects and to secure supplies of critical raw materials. The 
IRMA Standard for Responsible Mining is a “tried and tested”, highly respected, and 
holistic set of best practices that can be implemented to proactively prevent conflict and 
aid in the continuous building of trust between governments, companies, workers, and 
communities: 
o The IRMA Standard requires meaningful stakeholder engagement at all phases of 
the life cycle of a mine, from exploration through closure.   
o IRMA’s unique equal multi -stakeholder governance model incorporates buy -in 
from human rights and environmental NGOs and is far more effective in building 
trust than industry-led schemes. The standards set under the CRM Act should be 
defined through a multi-stakeholder process such as IRMA’s.  
o The IRMA Standard can inform both the foreign and domestic sourcing elements 
of the legislation as the IRMA Standard is not limited to a particular jurisdiction.  
o IRMA’s audit proces s is inclusive, providing a holistic assessment of mining 
operations by considering perspectives from affected communities, workers, 
government, and civil society stakeholders and rights holders, in addition to mine 
management. 
o IRMA’s detailed and comprehensive audit reports are publicly and freely available 
to any stakeholder who wishes to review a mine site’s performance. 
o IRMA covers all mined materials making it possible for members’ sustainability 
and purchasing teams to reduce duplication and create ef ficiencies by learning 
the details of only one comprehensive standard. 
• Any “streamlined and quicker permitting procedures” must focus on strengthening and 
improving administrative capacity, for example through the digitalisation of permitting, 
increased wo rkforce or one -stop shop, and not on by -passing environmental and social 
standards or safeguards.  
• IndustriAll Europe also wants to address raw material extraction which, if well planned 
and respectful of a strong social and sustainable framework, could be an opportunity for 
coal and carbon intensive regions in transition.  They offer a potential for div ersification 
and opportunities for today’s and future skilled miners in these regions. It could be a good 
alternative to deindustrialisation, and this must be promoted in a coherent way at 
European level. 
  
On the Non-regulatory aspects of the Critical Raw Material Act: 
1) EU external action 
Europe has available on its soil mineral raw materials, but some of the most critical raw materials are 
only located in a few countries outside Europe. IndustriAll Europe is working closely with IndustriALL 
Global Union in  this matter, to obtain decent work across the supply chain all around the world 
regarding the supply of mineral raw materials. In addition, industriAll Europe asks the European 
Commission to ensure that any raw materials strategies should also take into consideration the needs 
and interests of workers and people in the countries from which raw materials are to be exported to 
Europe. This has to be done in total commitment to the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, ILO 
labour standards, UN Guiding Principles for Business and Human Rights, as well as the UN Sustainable 
Development Goals.  Trade agreements and policy have a role to play in moving raw material 
extraction towards a more social and sustainable path globally. Trade agreements must integrate a 
strong, binding and enforceable commitment to the protection of the environment and fundamental 
labour standards and human rights. A more inclusive approach to trade and the sustainable 
development chapter should address these concerns. 
On the international level, there are multiple voluntary initiatives defining principles for sustainable 
raw materials. This is why IndustriAll Europe demands the adherence to due diligence throughout the 
global supply chain, notably regarding the sourcing of critical raw mate rials. We demand a legally 
binding EU instrument in the form of a European Directive on mandatory human rights (including 
workers’ and trade un-ion rights) and environmental obligations due diligence as close as possible to 
the requirements for Euro-pean domestic extraction.  
We demand: 
• That the European Commission takes into consideration the needs and interests of 
workers and people in the countries from which raw materials are to be exported to 
Europe, in its policy regarding raw materials, in total comm itment to the Universal 
Declaration of Human Rights, ILO labour standards, UN Guiding Principles for Business and 
Human Rights, as well as the UN Sustainable Development Goals. 
• An EU strategy regarding access to critical raw materials through socially resp onsible 
investments, partnership and alliances with countries outside the EU. 
• the use of trade agreements as a key element in moving the extraction of raw materials 
towards a more social and sustainable path globally. 
• A legally binding EU instrument in the form of a European Directive on mandatory human 
rights (including workers’ and trade union rights) and environmental obligations due 
diligence. 
 
2) On skills 
Workers must be equipped to face the major transformation of our industries so that no one is left 
behind. Vocational education and training (VET) must play a key role, though on our terms: 
• Everyone should have access to training, re- and upskilling, irrespective of age, occupation, 
employment status : we demand an individual right to training 
• We want 1st class VET, i.e. high quality training leading to validation/recognition of acquired skills 
(incl. through non-formal/informal learning) 
• Anticipation is key, and efforts on skills forecast must be stepped up at sectoral (local skills 
observatories) and company (strategic skills planning, incl. Career guidance) levels 
• Public and private investment on VET must be scaled up. Work-related VET (to adapt to one’s 
occupation) must be cost-free for workers, and provided during working hours 
• Governance of VET must rely on social partners and social dialogue, incl. collective bargaining 
But it’s not just about Skills. What would be the point of training workers if there’s no job in the end, 
or jobs in a dying industry? So any initiative on Skills must be integrated in a broader approach which 
pay equal attention to employment and social policies as well as long-term industrial strategy. 
A strong focus on skills and competences must be included in European and national circular economy 
policies. These policies must encompass the development of circular economy education and 
competences, as well as upskilling and reskilling within the lifelong learning framework. 
Educational institutions are also key to enable the transition to a circular mindset. The transition into 
circular societies is dependent on a mindset change and the continued education of future circular 
economy professionals. Public funding for educational institutions’ research, development and 
innovation activities could be focused on circularity and building networks and sustainable ecosystems 
with businesses and the public sector.

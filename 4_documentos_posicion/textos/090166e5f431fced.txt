IMA-Europe position on the IA of Critical Raw Material (CRM) Act 
FINAL 
 
Industrial Minerals Association Europe (IMA -Europe) representing the interest of Industrial minerals 
producers in Europe, welcomes, the Call for evidence the European Critical raw Material Act presented by 
the Commission ( 30 September 2022). While we understand the rationale to move forward with this 
initiative, as minerals sector we have som e considerations to share with the Commission for 
reflection/inclusion in the final content of the initiative:  
 
 
 
Problem statement: 
1. EU sources a very small fraction of the critical raw materials that needs for its industry and societal 
needs. The key reasons for this pattern are:  
1. The lack of these raw materials deposits in EU (e.g. Borates)  
2. The lengthy permitting process in EU *  
3. Even though the raw materials sourced and processed in EU do this is in a sustainable way, 
there is a l ack of social acceptance of mining to occur in EU. Examples: Not in My back Yard 
(NIMBY); Not In My Nature (NIMN); Build Absolutely Nothing Nearby Anything (BANANA).  
*Most part of Minerals are sourced domestically within EU and used in EU, however the permitting 
for new projects of extension of existing ones, can last from 2 – 10 years and exceptionally can take 
even more time. This causes major delays in the planning and the relation with the value chain actors.  
 
 
Raw Material Occurrence and Critical Raw Materials assessment:  
Raw Material deposits occur where geological process have placed them. They can not be moved . They 
need to be sourced where they are located. These geological deposits can be developed to source one or 
multiple raw materials.  
Critical Raw Materials in the EU and in most global regions are defined as materials that have a high 
economic importance and can be confronted with a supply risk (by a third country; supply disruptions (i.e. 
due to war), trade restrictions; etc).

Addressing the CRM supply shortages:  
The shortage of CRM’s can be addressed by putting in place  an effective system that  addresses existing 
bottlenecks and combines multiple solutions to develop / access the CRM in EU and/for EU:  
1. Waste valorization paths to be valorized:  
➢ The Critical Raw Materials (CRM’s) of today were by-products of past mining operations. In 
the past, there was no market demand and nor a business case for today CRM’s. Mining waste 
valorization: Consistent mapping of former mining waste and assess legislative bottlenecks 
to valorize this flow and its potential for EU industry/society.  
➢ Two examples of Li, being part of Boron or Kaolin extraction (example: Jadar project in Serbia; 
Emily project in France).  
➢ Urban mining: the recycling of end -of life products containing CRM is very low and efforts 
should be put in place to boost the recovery and recycling rate of this resource flow.  
 
2. Boost mining in the EU, by permitting new projects that provide EU sovereignty of CRM sectors that 
need them.  
➢ All raw materials are critical if the conditions to access them are not there.  
➢ The EU cannot put the pressure for CRM’s to other global regions. Effor ts should be put in 
place to boost EU projects that source these CRM’s sustainably from EU and for EU.  
➢ CRM’s are generally a by -product of another raw material. Most raw material deposits are 
multiple raw materials. Efforts should be made to valorize most of these raw materials and 
technically to minimize the volume of mining waste that is not valorized.  
➢ Clear requirements as part of the permitting should be set at the beginning (between the 
developer; authority and the directly concerned stakeholders) and not be changed during the 
process. As inspiration, to set Best Available Technique (BAT), the process of agreeing on 
conclusions agreed by all stakeholders duri ng the Kick -off-Meeting is a transparent way 
forward.  
➢ In line with all exiting EU legislations in place, the streamlined permitting of all raw materials 
(Critical or not) for the EU market should be a top priority of the CRM Act initiative. The CRM 
Act, like the renewable sector provisions, should foresee conditions to permit strategic 
projects with wide public interest and overcome the difficulties set by e.g. Habitat Directive 
or the new proposal of Nature Restoration Regulation.  
 
3. Only after addressing the above two options, the EU can identify the existing CRM/Raw materials gaps 
and set trade agreements with countries where the CRM are sourced and/or manufactured.  
➢ EU is not the only country looking for CRM’s. Other regions (i.e. USA; Canada; Australia; Japan, 
UK) are competing to access these CRM’s.  
➢ The trade agreements in the chapter dedicated to raw materials should address the 
sustainable sourcing of these CRM/raw materials outside of EU and for the EU market.  
➢ The same requirements as the EU standards of sourcing and processing these raw materials 
should apply.  
 
4. Stockpilling of CRM for strategic sectors can be another solution to minimize CRM shortages and the 
potential shock on sectors needing them the most.

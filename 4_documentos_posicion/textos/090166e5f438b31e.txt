CRITICAL RAW MATERIALS ACT 
Companion paper to ECI Response to Open 
Public Consultation 
November 2022  
 
ECI welcomes the initiative of the EU Commission for a Critical Raw Materials package with 
legislative and non-legislative measures. This paper wants to summarise our views about which 
essential elements the package should address and to  give the rationale of and expand on our 
responses to the open public consultation questionnaire. 
In particular, ECI wants to stress the following crucial elements: 
▪ The package should be broad in scope and consider all the raw materials that are 
strategic for reaching the goal of the  transition to clean energy and climate neutrality in 
the EU, such as copper ; not just those classified as critical by the EU metho dology on 
criticality. 
▪ The package should foresee measures to grant easier and more predictable permitting 
procedures to those projects involving strategic raw materials in the different steps of the 
supply chain: mining, smelting and refining, and recycling. 
▪ The package should ensure the competitiveness of the EU industry  via the supply of 
green electricity and energy at competitive prices compared to other regions. 
▪ The EU legislative framework on waste should be adapted to ensure the availability and 
quality of secondary raw materials  for the European operators and should improve its 
legislative provisions to allow a wider collection of end-of-life products and complex waste 
streams and more efficient recovery of their content of strategic raw materials. 
▪ The new framework should guarantee a harmonised approach  with existing and 
upcoming legislative files and that there are no contradicting provisions targeting strategic 
raw materials needed for the EU’s twin transition (e.g., ban of Lead). 
▪ Strategic raw material  value-chains, including mining, refining and recycling,  should be 
eligible in the context of sustainable finance, by development of ambitious but operational

1. Introduction 
The European Copper Institute (ECI) welcomes the intention of the European Commission to release a 
package on Raw Materials. The preservation, strengthening and possible extension of the different raw 
materials supply chains are an essential element for a resilient and sustainable EU economy and 
society. It is undoubtful that there will be a massive increase in the demand for raw materials, and, many 
of those materials will be essential for ensuring the  essential conditions – e.g., electrification, green 
technologies, and renewables – for reaching the targets fixed by the EU.  In particular, the package 
should foster a multiple set of elements, all of which are aimed at improving and reinforcing the open 
strategic autonomy of the European Economic Area ; for instance : EU availability of primary and 
secondary raw materials; improving quality of secondary raw materials; securing effective and 
sustainable international strategic partnership on raw materials; s etting conditioning for securing an 
industrial presence in the EU and fostering investments; introducing easier permitting process for mining 
and industrial sites producing and processing key raw materials. 
2. Relevant elements of the package for the European Copper Institute 
Scope of the legislative Act on Critical Raw Materials 
During the last years, the EU and different stakeholders widely used to term ‘critical’ with different 
connotations. For some, the term critical means a material that needs to be substituted, while for others 
it means a material that is necessary for the EU economy because it’s part of many key supply chains. 
In addition, others understand the term as  a material that is or will be in short supply. For instance, the 
IEA Critical Minerals policy tracker1 stresses the importance of ensuring supply reliability and resiliency, 
irrespectively of the fact that different regions or countries can classify the minerals as critical or 
strategic. It is not easy to have a clear definition, but it is also understandable that policymakers – EU 
and Member States – want to have a definition to have a cle ar scope of the future legislative act.  
However, the definition actually available at the EU level and given by the methodology to assess the 
criticality of the raw materials developed by the Joint Research Centre is not fit for this package and 
should not be used for its foundation. 
This EU criticality methodology: 
▪ utilises past data to derive an assessment of how much a raw material is critical, and 
▪ does not consider future needs of investments  in the supply chains that will experience the 
largest increments of raw materials demand. 
Moreover, the past revisions of the EU list of critical raw materials were carried out more like a screening 
exercise rather than a tool to help the strategic planning of the EU  open and strategic autonomy . For 
instance, coking coal and rubber are still on  the list, but it is questionable how much they are linked to 
future investments in green technologies. Finally, it is important to consider that although the EU 
methodology on criticality does not flag a mineral, this does not mean that the material is not strategic 
for the EU and that its availability and quality cannot be subjected to bottlenecks or disruption in the 
short and medium term. Thus, ECI wants to propose to define the scope of the legislative action based 
on the raw materials that will be nec essary for the supply and value chains, relevant to the digital and 
green technologies and electrification of the EU economy.

The relevance of copper by-products and recycling 
Copper mining , processing, and recycling p roduce multiple benefits beyond making refined copper 
available for the EU market.  The production of copper , from primary ores,  also generates other non -
ferrous metals that, depending on the ore type and deposit characteristics, can be gold, silver, zinc, lead 
and molybdenum, among others. 
Moreover, copper can be produced via a secondary production route that mainly uses secondary raw 
materials, such as Waste Electric and Electronic Equipment (WEEE), Printed Computer Boards (PCBs), 
industrial residues containing copper and Incinerated Bottom Ashes (IBAs) containing copper. The 
peculiarity of copper metallurgy is to allow the recovery and then recycling of other non -ferrous metals 
that are components of the waste stream. Figure 1  shows the indus trial eco -system of non -ferrous 
metals, where the ability to recover base and technology metals from natural resources, residues and 
end-of-life products is visualised.

In particular, copper metallurgy can recover a wide range of non -ferrous metals , most of which are 
classified as critical by the EU and send them to other industrial sectors . Moreover, the production of 
copper also generates other industrial by-products, e.g. iron silicates that are industrially manufactured 
aggregates or raw materials that can replace the use of virgin natural resources, like limestone for 
cement production or natural aggregates for concret e fabrication and engineering and construction 
works. The exchange of such industrial by-products – e.g., iron silicates and other non-ferrous metals -

Therefore, the new legislative act on critical raw materials must be a pivotal instrument to put projects 
on raw materials forward and to ensure certainty and predictability through the following actions: 
(a) the identification of strategic projects to secure raw materials supply to the copper industry;  
(b) the definition of a clear and easier framework for permitting and financing the new projects;  
(c) the preservation of the competitiveness of the copper industry operating in the EU within the future 
global context.

▪ Easier permitting procedures and financing eligibility should be granted when the project

impacting on the ability to plan investments. Structural changes are needed to electricity

About the European Copper Institute  
Based in Brussels, the European Copper Institute (ECI) is the leading advocate for the copper industry in Europe and is 
the European arm of the International Copper Association (ICA). Through a team of policy, industry and scientific experts, 
ECI acts to support copper’s role in achieving the EU’s policy goals. Our members mine, smelt, refine and recycle copper 
for use across the economy, in the electricity system, buildings, transport and industry.

Critical Raw Materials Act: 
An agenda for Europe to get ahead in the global race 
Introduction 
The Critical Raw Materials Act is Europe’s unmissable opportunity to catch- up in the global race for metals and 
minerals supply. Europe’s energy and mobility systems will completely transform over the next decade, supercharged 
by the REPowerEU plan and 2035 internal combustion engine ban. EU demand for base metals, battery materials, 
rare earths, and more will rise strongly as it builds-up local production of batteries, solar panels, permanent magnets, 
and other clean tech. But Europe will lose out to China and the US without a decisive plan for securing its own supply. 
Europe’s metals industry calls on the European Commission to deliver a tr uly ambitious Critical Raw 
Materials Act that kickstarts a rethink of the EU’s industrial strategy for its clean energy supply chains:  
1. Equal the ambition of Europe’s international competitors
Europe is now in competition with the US Inflation Reduction Act and its subsidies and tax incentives for local supply 
chains. The Critical Raw Materials Act must deliver 2030 headline goals and targeted market measures that will offer 
at least a comparable incentive for new investments building up sustainable European raw materials supply chains.  
• Focus on the right strategic materials: Put at the centre the metals & minerals most needed for the energy
transition – as agreed by international authorities - including aluminium, copper, silicon, nickel, lithium, cobalt,
graphite, high-purity manganese, rare earths - plus others the EU identifies through a detailed analysis

3. Secure responsible and fair imports from prioritised resource-rich partners  
Europe will remain reliant on imports to supply its medium-term growth in the next 15 years, even with a successful 
domestic investment strategy. The Critical Raw Materials Act must also include a much stronger global strategy for 
securing responsible imports, which avoids an overdependence on single suppliers and addresses trade distortions.  
• Strengthen Europe’s raw materials partnerships: Prioritise new partnerships with regions that can deliver 
supply in the next 5+ years when Europe’s demand pressure is highest, i.e. Latin America (copper & lithium), 
Democratic Republic of Congo (cobalt & copper), Indonesia (nickel & cobalt), South Africa (PGMs)  – plus 
likeminded partners like Norway, Canada, & Australia and through the Minerals Security Partnership.  
• Mainstream raw materials in EU free trade agreements:  Commit to strong raw materials chapters in all 
EU Free Trade Agreements, as well as regulatory cooperation & sustainability chapters to encourage shared 
standards. Strict Rules of Origin should be used to manage goods flowing through non- partner countries.  
• Finance European mining projects abroad: Learn from China and financially support European 
companies with ownership or part-ownership of competitive global resources, especially for those materials 
with a lower domestic supply potential. Projects should meet European sustainability expectations.  
• Combat unfair trading practices: Speed up the process for the EU imposing provisional trade defence 
measures, to safeguard new European investments against likely dumping from China in its dominant global 
markets, e.g. magnesium, rare earths. Keep the existing anti-dumping measures for aluminium and silicon.  
4. Make Europe the global leader for high-quality metals recycling  
Recycling will be Europe’s key long-term opportunity to establish metals strategic autonomy, especially after 2040 
once the first generation of clean energy technologies reach end- of-life in significant  volumes. The EU must act 
now to grow its recycling industry into a leadership position, facing high global competition from other regions.   
• Make targeted amendments to existing waste legislation: Take immediate action to 1) require minimum 
treatment standards for recyclers of European electronics waste, 2) strengthen controls on battery “black 
mass” through confirming its status as a hazardous waste not a product, 3) harmonise waste classification 
systems across Member States to prevent shipment delays  
• Prioritise raw materials circularity in upcoming waste legislation : Commit to follow the Batteries 
Regulation’s integrated raw materials focus in upcoming reviews of the End-of-Life Vehicles Directive, Waste 
Electronics & Electrical Equipment Directive, and others. These reviews should include material-specific end-
of-life recycling targets, collection/sorting improvements, and action on scrap leakage.  
• Check whether new legislation is needed for emerging waste streams: Confirm whether emerging waste 
streams like permanent magnets and solar panels need new  or strengthened waste legislation to ensure 
high quality recycling of their metals and minerals.   
Eurometaux also supports that the Critical Raw Materials Act: 
• Coordinates national agency efforts through an EU Raw Materials Agency or other cooperation, providing a 
overall vision and helping direct financial support plus carry out monitoring activities .  
• Prioritises that the European market is supplied by environmentally & socially responsible metals and minerals:  
o Coherent implementation of the EU’s due diligence legislation, Batteries Regulation,  taxonomy, and 
future provisions for other product groups – applied to the entire value chain in an inclusive way  
o Starting a process to benchmark the different mining certification schemes available on a global level 
to identify those that meet the EU’s environmental and social expectations, without preferencing one 
single scheme nor slowing down new responsible European investments.

Critical Raw Materials Act recommendation 1  
Equal the ambition of Europe’s international competitors  
Introduction 
Europe’s accelerated REPowerEU clean energy goals require a massive increase of strategic metals and minerals 
in replacement of fossil fuels, and raw materials are central in several other strategic areas. The EU’s urgent priority 
in the next 15+ years is to secure primary raw materials from constrained global markets, with a minimised 
environmental and social impact. After 2040, meaningful volumes of recycled material will start to be available from 
Europe’s first-generation clean energy technologies, beginning to stabilise primary raw materials growth.  
Europe’s Critical Raw Materials Act must be at least as bold and decisive as the US’s Inflation Reduction Act to drive 
forward European investment and secure responsible global supply (while fitted to the EU’s specific governance 
model). This requires the Commission to set headline goals, 2030 targets, and market incentives that will catalyse 
real change once implemented, avoiding the risk of disjointed policy actions that do not shift the status quo.  
In this paper, we provide our recommendations for the legislation’s overall framework, which should 1) codify 
a priority list of strategic raw materials, 2) set an ambitious headline 2030 goal for the EU’s strategic autonomy  
A first step: codifying “strategic raw materials” within an overall coherent approach  
We support the European Commission’s approach to codify which strategic metals and minerals Europe requires in 
higher volumes for supplying its twin transitions and other strategic areas. A prioritisation of “strategic raw materials” 
will provide clarity to Member States to focus on national raw materials projects most linked with Green Deal needs.  
It is crucial that this prioritisation is not limited to today’s Critical Raw Materials list. The list has a specific methodo logy 
providing a time-limited view on today’s risk of supply disruption and past developments. It does not reflect on future 
demand increases, supply risks, and investment needs linked to the twin transition. A substantial number of minerals 
on the list do not have an increasing demand from the twin transitions, and are not the focus of investment plans.  
Recommendation: The European Commission’s priority list of ‘strategic raw materials” should:  
• Put at the centre those minerals agreed as “critical” for the energy transition by international authorities: 
Aluminium, copper, nickel, lithium, cobalt, graphite, silicon, high-purity manganese, and rare earths1  
• Expand to include other critical raw materials linked to defined strategic value chains (see Annex 1 for 
examples), while remaining manageable in size and reflecting integration of metals production routes  
• G ive clarity on the specific time-limited use of the EU Critical Raw Materials list compared with the Act’s future-
looking perspective.  
Aluminium, copper, nickel, and high-purity manganese are not on today’s EU Critical Raw Materials list (except 
bauxite), as there is not a present risk of supply disruption. However, they are all required in significantly higher 
volumes by the EU’s clean energy goals, and have different supply concerns by 2030 if no EU regulatory  action and/or 
investment is taken (see Annex 5 for detailed profiles). They are all included in the International Energy Agency’s 
critical minerals work, and are on Critical Raw Materials lists of other regions (e.g. US, Canada, or Japan).  
Alongside this prioritisation, the Critical Raw Materials Act should be clear that:

2. Some of tomorrow’s strategic materials are unknown: Metals and minerals not defined as strategic today 
may have new strategic applications in the future and so a regular assessment is required (for example on 
new emerging battery materials and technologies).   
 The Act should commit to reviewing Europe’s strategic needs on a five-year basis.  
3. Production routes are integrated: Most strategic raw materials are produced in an integrated metallurgical 
system which is impossible to separate. For example, investing into zinc metallurgy will be the only way for 
Europe to access primary critical metals like germanium or indium, and using lead in electronics waste 
recycling is essential for critical material recycling.  
 The Act should be clear on which strategic materials rely on integrated production routes, to avoid silos  
4. Investment potential differs: Europe has some level of a project pipeline for most of the strategic materials 
needed in its energy transition, stimulated by the rising demand from downstream sectors. Thi s is not the case 
for all critical raw materials. New investment is especially challenging where the raw material is only produced 
as a by-product of other industrial processes, or where China has a very dominant global position.  
 The Act should focus its investment goals on strategic materials with a realistic project pipeline  
Ensuring the legislation has weight: Setting a 2030 headline goal for the right strategic autonomy  
To catalyse real action, the Critical Raw Materials Act must formalise an ambitious 2030 headline goal targeting a 
minimum level of strategic autonomy and diversification for strategic raw materials. This will confirm the overall 
strategic ambition for Member States to follow with their EU and non-EU projects and strategies.  
The US’s Inflation Reduction Act has demonstrated how a strong headline measure can immediately drive forward 
domestic investment in minerals supply chains. The European Union must define targets and accompanying 
measures that catalyse at least the same level of concrete action and ambition, fitted to its governance competences 
and avoiding the US’s overly prejudicial approach.  
Without a strong headline goal, we are concerned that the EU will repeat the pattern of previous raw materials 
initiatives, which have been well-intentioned by all stakeholders but not resulted in major steps forward.   
Our first proposal for a headline goal is provided and explained below, addressing the three key elements of criticality: 
Europe’s autonomy, diversification, and production erosion.  We recommend that the numbers are set after an im pact 
assessment of project pipelines and other factors. The industry can also provide its own proposal.  
Suggestions for a 2030 headline goal (to be fully included in the legislative text):  
The EU aims that none of the strategic raw materials prioritised for its Green Deal qualify as “critical” in 2030. In 
practice, this means: 
• A minimum level of autonomy: Ensure at least X% of extracted/recycled raw materials and Y% of 
smelted/refined raw materials are supplied from the EU or defined partners.   
• A diversified global supply: Ensure that no single third country has an EU import share higher than X% 
for extracted raw material* and Y% for smelted/refined raw material  
*Unless formally listed as a preferential partner for a specific raw material  
• A maintenance and capacity expansion of today’s industrial base: Avoid a contraction of the EU’s 
existing industrial base for each strategic material and increase supply contribution by a minimum of X% 
 
   
The Commission should target having an easy to communicate headline target on what Europe aims to achieve by 
2030 for its raw materials supply (comparable to the “Fit for 55” approach applied for extracted, smelted/refined, 
recycled material), which can then be backed-up by the material specific approaches that will be needed to reflect 
each varied raw material profile.  
The Act should partner this goal with well-designed market incentives that encourage downstream sectors to buy from 
European or preferred partner sources. The Commission could take inspiration from the Inflation Reduction Act’s tax 
credit and evaluate options for proposing incentives or public procurement requirements that are possible at EU level. 
It will be important to reflect the EU’s specific competences, and to ensure a model that is more WTO compliant.  
Explanations: 
• A minimum level of strategic autonomy  
o Europe should set a minimum level of strategic autonomy to achieve across all its strategic materials, 
rather than only setting targets for a few of them  
o Europe should set a realistic lower minimum target for its ext racted raw materials to reflect geological 
limits, also combined with secondary inputs (i.e. scrap).  
o Europe should set a higher target for its smelted/refined raw materials (e.g. at least 50% of supply from 
European sources). Its ambition here is not limi ted by geology. Processing capacity in Europe should 
be built up and/or maintained for strategic materials.  
o Supply from Europe’s neighbourhood should count to targets (e.g. Norway, Iceland, Serbia, Ukraine)  
o Supply from EU-owned or part-owned operations in third countries should count towards targets 
o It should be noted that Europe does not have potential to source some specific raw materials  
domestically in meaningful minimum levels by 2030, due to insufficient geological resources and scrap 
availability (i.e. cobalt) . Only where it is necessary, supply from defined preferential partners – based 
on a formal strategic agreement – may also be identified for qualifying towards the minimum target 
  
• A minimum level of diversification  
o Europe should set a minimum level of diversification to achieve across all its strategic materials, which 
prevents an over-dependence on single suppliers as their demand increases  
o Targets should be set at extracted and smelted/refined raw materials levels. Both are important, to 
guard against China’s dominance of processed materials, and single suppliers for extracted materials  
o Diversification targets should be more ambitious for processed materials, to reflect that supply is less 
tied to geological resources  
o For raw materials, there may be some cases where single suppliers will still provide a significant share 
of the EU’s imports in 2030 due to concentration of resources. This could be permitted for Europe’s 
formal strategic partners, but should be notified as an exception to the overall rule.  
 
• A maintenance and capacity expansion of today’s industrial base 
o Setting the two “minimum level” targets will not guard against Europe losing autonomy for the strategic 
materials where it already has production. Europe should set a formal goal that it will maintain and 
expand its existing capacity – at least to grow its domestic contribution in line with demand growth    
o Future production losses are unfortunately a high potential scenario.  
 Europe’s mining and processing capacity has already declined progressively in the last 10- 20 
years (i.e. depletion of operating mines, closure of 1/3 aluminium production)  
 Depletion of mining resources will continue, with Europe scheduled to lose up to 20% of its 
current copper/nickel/cobalt mining capacity by 2030, and up to 50% by 2040 without projects  
 The energy crisis is putting European processers under further severe pressure, and dumping 
from China is a major issue specifically for the aluminium and silicon sectors

Critical Raw Materials Act recommendation 2 
Catalyse new investment through permits, finance, & frameworks 
Introduction 
Europe must act urgently to push forward its European mining, refining, and future recycling projects to establish a 
minimum level of strategic autonomy and sustainability in critical areas. This must be accomplished while avoiding a 
contraction of Europe’s existing industrial capacity in the wake of the energy crisis.  
The Commission’s ambition to identify priority projects, accelerate permitting procedures and unlock new finance is 
a very welcome step to improve the speed and viability of new projects. It is just as important that the Commission  
takes action to ensure competitive operating conditions and a level playing field (i.e. energy costs, trade defence, 
joined-up environmental policies). The Critical Raw Materials Act will only be effective if catalysing a rethink of EU 
industrial strategy, as the US has recently done with its Inflation Reduction Act.  
This paper proposes a framework through which  the EU’s planned raw materials legislation can accelerate 
strategic projects for the Green Deal and establish a level playing field for all operations   
New EU legislation should prioritise projects for faster permitting and finance  
There are numerous European projects at intermediate permitting stages, which could be brought online this decade 
for strategic metals and minerals (e.g. for lithium, rare earths, nickel, copper, graphite) and provide between 5 and 
50% of Europe’s needs (see Annex 1). But most suffer from permitting delays, local opposition, or operational 
uncertainty, and there is a risk that too few are successful.  
The Critical Raw Materials Act can make a measurable difference to taking strategic European raw materials projects 
forwards, including mining, refining, and recycling. In our view, EU action requires three steps. 
1. Identification of priority projects for improving security of supply to European industries  
Within a year, the Commission should introduce an EU-level list which formally identifies the priority projects that will 
improve Europe’s strategic autonomy for the metals and minerals in scope, allowing for accelerated permitting and 
easier financing. This list should cover the full raw materials value chain: mining, refining, and recycling operations. 
All viable projects should be added in a longlist, before identifying the priority shortlist for taking forward urgently.  
An EU strategic raw materials project list could take inspiration from the EUs Projects of Common Interest (PCIs) list 
for interconnecting energy system infrastructure, i.e.  
o A projects list that is reviewed and updated every two years 
o Allowing for accelerated permitting processes and easier finance eligibility 
o Subject to a sustainability assessment during project selection (e.g. linked to the EU principles for 
sustainable raw materials and associated benchmarks)

Both domestic and non- EU projects should be eligible if their supply is tied to the European market  and relevant 
environmental and social conditions are met  (particularly for mining, when the most competitive deposits for certain 
metals are outside Europe).  
2. Permitting and Finance: New rules for faster permitting and easier finance 
The EU’s identified list of strategic raw materials projects should then be made eligible for:  
• An accelerated permitting procedure for applying at national level 
o Require local and national authorities to prioritise strategic projects in their overall workload with 
prescribed timelines for reaching a decision  
o Speed up the permitting process while retaining rigorousness, by increasing local resources, and allowing 
parallel processes (rather than sequential as now)   
o Take inspiration from REPowerEU  permitting rules for “overriding public interest” renewable energy 
projects, while reflecting the different profile  of raw materials project s and the importance of keeping 
environmental protection in place.   
• Priority financing from EU and national tools  
o Ensure that all available EU and regional finance tools are open for strategic raw materials projects, as 
well as allowing State Aid flexibility for Member States . The IPCEI should be revisited as an important 
part of EU raw materials financing.  
o Introduce a dedicated Raw Materials public/private investment fund, combining equity, loans and 
guarantees - or with a dedicated focus in the announced Sovereignty Fund. 
o Introduce a dedicated thematic debt lending facility under the European Investment Bank (EIB) and 
European Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD) , encouraging them to take a more active 
role in de-risking priority projects. The credit policy of these institutions must be adapted to the specificities 
of the sector, especially to undertake the country risk that is inherent to most of these projects.  
o Investigate other financial support, for example treating exploration expenditure as tax refundable and/or 
grand refundable; allowing R&D rebates for process test work on new ore bodies; provide corporate tax 
holidays on early-stage developments that intend to build scale  
 
3. Framework: Measures for competitive operational expenditure and level playing field  
Just as important but less in the discussion until now, new projects and existing operations need long-term certainty 
for competitive operating conditions (investments take 5-10 years to come online and operate for decades after). High 
OpEx costs are a significant barrier to entry for European companies, which will face intensive competition from China 
and other regions with favourable conditions and/or state support.  
Unless the EU’s Critical Raw Materials Act catalyses a broader rethink of industrial policy in other areas, Europe will 
remain a less attractive investment destination compared with other regions.  
• Competitive electricity and energy : Metals processing activities are highly electricity -intensive, with power 
comprising 15-40% of total production costs (in normal functioning electricity markets). Mining operations also 
need to electrify on the path to decarbonisation. Both existing and new operations face a critical challenge to 
access competitive low-carbon electricity, during today’s power crisis and in the medium-term.  
The electricity crisis has taken 50% of EU aluminium and zinc capacity offline and 30% for silicon, while 
threatening to decimate the investment climate for new processing operations planned in the next 2- 3 years (all 
metals and minerals are energy/electricity-intensive to produce).   
 
   
The industry has put forward concrete proposals for tools which can facilitate access to competitively priced 
low/zero-carbon electricity for metals producers1. The Critical Raw Materials Act should catalyse urgent action on 
these proposals in the respective policy areas:  
The EU critical raw materials act should make the link with EU electricity market discussions, and:  
o Commit to urgent action for removing barriers to power -intensive metals producers signing long- term 
power purchase agreements, especially for renewables  
o Confirm the need for an emergency recovery package to support the metals producers that have been 
forced to curtail or close production capacity during the energy crisis  
 
• Trade defence: China completely dominates numerous global metals markets (i.e. >90% share for rare earths, 
magnesium, graphite, and overcapacities for several metals). There is a high risk of China dumping or distorting 
prices in its dominant markets once Europe commits to bringing new projects online, to retain its position. New 
EU projects for these commodities may  require pre- emptive and assertive trade defence measures , in cases 
where a risk is identified. 
The EU critical raw materials act  should encourage an assertive application of the  EU’s trade defence 
framework: 
o Commit to speed up the process for imposition of provisional trade defence measures. This does not 
require a law change, but speeding up the process through possibilities allowed in the EU’s existing trade 
defence framework (following the US practice)
 2.  
o Reaffirm the importance of keeping existing trade defence measures in place for aluminium, silicon, and 
other strategic transition materials at proven risk of dumping from China.  
• Environmental coherence: New raw materials projects face unintended investment bottlenecks from chemicals 
and environmental legislation. For example, major investment decisions are being delayed by uncertain REACH 
requirements (i.e. see cases of lithium
 or cobalt compounds for batteries, and lead in aluminium or precious 
metals recycling 3).  It’s crucial that the Commission takes this opportunity to address incoherencies that are 
delaying investments, while maintaining environmental ambition levels.  
The EU critical raw materials act should align with the REACH review and other environmental policies: 
o Call for predictability and coherence in the  ongoing REACH review, including a new focus on “strategic raw 
materials” and their production/recycling routes, which allows the Commission to make earlier decisions on 
risk management measures that are effective while also coherent with Green Deal  investment objectives. 
o Commit to developing new Member State Guidance for applying the Water Framework Directive to m ining 
projects, as well as any other notified areas of incoherence in the application of environmental policies.  
 
 
1 Energy - The EU’s wider climate policy will also make industrial electricity prices more expensive, at a time when other regions are promising cheap 
power sources to encourage local investment (e.g. Canada) or subsidising their producers’ energy costs (China). Examples include the ever-
increasing CO2 prices (combined with marginal pricing system where fossil fuels set the power price), and the inclusion of indirect emissions in CBAM 
(removing compensation for European producers). Today, plants are already paying EUR 30/MWh in carbon costs, compared with wholesale 
electricity prices of 30-60 MWh across the EU just two years ago. This is a major disadvantage to European producers even outside of today’s crisis. 
2 Trade defence - AEGIS Europe has completed a recent assessment of the EU’s Trade Defence Instrument regime with precise recommendations 
for improvement, including the conclusion that “earlier relief is needed and is possible under existing law” through earlier registration and earlier 
provisional measures – particularly for example if officials change their work practice to impose measures based on submitted data, but prior to 
verification.  
3   Chemicals - Most strategic transition materials have hazardous properties, whether directly or alloyed in their applications with other hazardous 
materials. The EU’s chemicals framework is identified by companies as one of the major investment deterrents into Europe. Strategic materials such 
as lithium, cobalt and silver threaten to be caught up in long and uncertain regulatory processes, with a threat of disproportionate measures that would 
make new investments impossible without environmental benefit. Recycling operations are also impacted, for example the precious metals recyclers 
relying on lead in their metallurgical processes, or aluminium recyclers with lead an unavoidable impurity in their scrap intake. See more in Annex 3.  
 
   
As well as focussing on new investment, we strongly recommend a section in the Critical Raw Materials Act 
Communication on “Preventing further criticality for strategic raw materials”.  
Europe’s existing metals capacity is under severe pressure from a combination of high energy costs and unfair global 
competition. A realistic scenario in the next decade is that Europe loses further capacity for  strategic materials (as also 
seen historically with the loss of magnesium production capacity and before then rare earths).  
In the last 10-20 years, Europe has lost 1/3 of its primary aluminium production and had a depletion of mining 
resources. Today’s energy crisis threatens to continue this trend. 50% of the EU’s aluminium and zinc capacity is offline 
since October 2021, and 30% for silicon. 
By 2030, Europe’s mining resources for copper, nickel, and zinc will also deplete by up to 20% without new capac ity. By 
2030, depletion will reach up to 50%. 
The Critical Raw Materials Act should make clear that competitive electricity, assertive trade policies, and coherent 
environmental policies are the must-have framework conditions both for new projects and existing installations to be 
viable in the future – also reflecting the impacts of the energy crisis and need for recovery of industrial conditions.  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
   
Critical Raw Materials Act recommendation 3: 
Secure responsible and fair imports from prioritised partners  
Introduction 
Europe will remain reliant on imports for a range of strategic materials to supply its medium- term growth in the next 
15 years, even with a successful domestic investment strategy to ensure the highest possible level of strategic 
autonomy throughout the whole value chain 1. The Critical Raw Materials Act must include an ambitious global 
strategy for securing and expanding the EU industrial base, and for ensuring responsible and diversified imports, to 
an overdependence on unsustainable single suppliers.  
This requires balancing the EU’s two interests: offensive (establishing new raw materials supply sources abroad and 
gaining access to foreign partners as well as expanding the EU industrial base) and defensive (ensuring fair trade 
conditions for production capacity already existing in Europe).  
This paper proposes five key action areas for the Critical Raw Materials Act’s global strategy:  
1. Expanding and strengthening EU raw materials partnerships 
The Critical Raw Materials Act should expand and strengthen the EU’s raw materials partnership model, prioritising:  
• Like-minded regions with defined project pipelines: e.g. Norway, Canada, Australia 
• Regions best equipped to deliver short-term supply of strategic minerals: i.e. Chile/Argentina for copper 
and lithium, Indonesia for nickel and cobalt, Democratic Republic of Congo for cobalt and copper  
• For medium-term supply, the EU’s neighbourhood and other parts of Africa  
The EU’s priority in its raw materials partnerships should be linking to an operational pipeline of identified responsible 
projects that can be brought online in very short term when demand requirements will rise most steeply (i.e. by 2030). 
This is of most important to European businesses, with longer-term cooperation around minerals exploration in other 
regions to be pursued as a secondary priority. Secondary raw materials should also be in the focus of partnerships.  
The EU’s challenge will be offering new models of cooperation to regions that are today already dominated by Chinese 
or other foreign investment (Indonesia, DRC, parts of Latin America). A horizontal business forum could be 
established where European companies provide early overall feedback on the highest priorities for their operations 
and where the European Commission should focus in its resource diplomacy with each priority region.  
The Minerals Security Partnership is also of great interest as a forum for multinational cooperation. It should focus on 
developing alternatives to the supply chains where China has a near monopoly and high levels of vertical integration, 
like rare earths and permanent magnets. Europe faces a significant challenge to compete in these supply chains alone 
and here would benefit from pooling resources and combining markets. Agreeing on shared environmental and social 
standards would also be beneficial to create a large market for responsibly produced materials.   
- The Critical Raw Materials act should commit to expanding the EU’s raw materials partnerships with a priorit y 
focus on the regions that can deliver short term and responsible materials supply to the European market  
- The Critical Raw Materials Act should establish a horizontal business forum for informing the Commission’s 
overall partnerships strategy (or empowering one of the existing raw materials groups)  
 
1 If Europe succeeds in opening new domestic refineries, then if will need to import metal ores in higher volumes from resource-rich countries, instead 
of the processed metals (where China dominates). Opening new mines in Europe will soften demand for imported ores but not replace demand. 
 
   
2. Combatting unfair trade practices and keeping a level playing field 
Europe’s strategic raw materials supply is threatened by unfair trade practices - especially from (but not limited to) 
China. The EU’s Critical Raw Materials strategy should commit to assertiveness in maintaining a level playing field and 
fair trade conditions with all global partners.  
China has massively subsidised its metals processing industry against WTO rules and now has a dominant global 
position for most strategic metals and minerals (from 35% to 95% of global production). The EU has already needed to 
implement anti-dumping measures for namely its under-threat aluminium and silicon sectors to safeguard against 
proven dumping. 
If the EU looks to establish new or expand domestic capacity for metals like magnesium and rare earths where China 
has a near-monopoly position, then it must pre-emptively safeguard against the likelihood of dumping or other trade 
distortions which could derail projects. This requires speeding up the investigating process by imposing earlier 
provisional trade defence measures within the existing framework, or by imposing at the initial stage registration of 
imports as a deterrent measure. Existing anti-dumping measures must also be maintained on metals with established 
production capacity in Europe (i.e. aluminium, silicon).  
We also support the Commission’s proposal in the Waste Shipments Regulation to require that exported European 
scrap is recycled under equivalent conditions to our standards here.  
- The Critical Raw Materials act should commit to speeding-up the EU’s investigating trade defence process by  
imposing early provisional anti-dumping measures and more generally using possibilities allowed in the existing 
trade defence framework.  
- The Critical Raw Materials act should commit the EU to challenging global raw materials trade distortions that 
contravene WTO rules  
3. Financing European raw materials projects abroad 
Europe should financially support its companies with ownership or part-ownership of competitive global resources, 
especially for those strategic materials with a lower domestic supply potential. This is a necessary accompaniment to 
the EU’s domestic investment strategy, where local mining resources will only secure between 5- 50% of supply.  
China has long subsidised its companies in purchasing competitive global mining assets. Chinese firms now have a 
stake in 15 out of 17 cobalt mines in the Democratic Republic of Congo, most Indonesian nickel operations, and 
growing lithium assets in South America. This locks-in supply to China’s processing industry and lowers raw materials 
availability for Europe and other regions – with concerns over environmental and social standards.  
Europe must develop its own global investment and finance strategy to support its companies in raising capital for 
securing supply sources from resource-rich areas of the world, which also meet sustainability expectations:   
- The Critical Raw Materials Act could define specific areas where Europe would aim to secure strategic materials 
directly through part-owned operations in third countries, in complement to its domestic investment targets.  
- The EU’s financial support, including from EBRD and EIB, should be applicable to strategic projects in third 
countries, assuming off-take commitments for the European market and environmental conditionality  
 
 
 
   
4. Mainstreaming raw materials in EU Free Trade Agreements 
The EU should include strong raw materials chapters in all its Free Trade Agreements to help secure a sustainable 
raw materials supply, while using rules of origin to manage goods flowing through non- partner countries.  
Raw materials chapters: All the EU’s Free Trade Agreements should contain strong, enforceable provisions on raw 
materials, to diversify sourcing, reduce dependency on single or limited suppliers, and ensure access at more 
competitive prices. This should be added consistently alongside provisions on energy and non- energy raw materials.  
Free trade agreements should also include chapters on regulatory cooperation and sustainability, with powerful 
enforcement mechanisms to ensure that European partners meet expected environmental and regulatory standards. 
Our industry would also benefit from harmonisation and simplification in the regulatory field (i.e. by aligning 
approaches towards industry’s responsible sourcing certification schemes), aiming towards regulatory convergence 
and mutual recognition on compatible regimes.  
Rules of origin: We support the application of stricter Rules of Origin for metals sectors, with the PEM convention’s 
revised list offering the right blueprint for future FTAs. Strict rules and efficient controls are needed to prevent products  
from third countries gaining preferential origin and exported to the EU market duty -free and circumventing the 
applicable tariffs.  
- The Critical Raw Materials Act should confirm the EU’s prioritisation of raw materials in its Free Trade 
Agreements, committing to strong and enforceable chapters on raw materials, regulatory cooperation and 
sustainability concerning raw materials in all FTAs; as well as strict rules of origin to avoid circumvention.  
5. Using the Global Gateway for sustainable Africa development (and other regions) 
The EU should implement a new model of sustainable raw materials development with Africa, while accepting the 
associated challenges and risks. Priority countries include the DRC, Zambia, Mozambique, Tanzania, Malawi, 
Namibia. The time is ripe for an alternative to China’s Belt and Road Initiative, which has lacked ESG considerations 
and not delivered the promised benefits to local communities.  
The EU Global Gateway initiative already commits to working with partners to invest in infrastructure for  developing 
sustainable and resilient raw materials value chains, and overall includes €150bn worth of grants and loans  into Africa 
for spurring private investment. The Critical Raw Materials Act should focus this ambition and develop a set of 
concrete priorities for raw materials investment into Africa (as well as other resource- rich developing regions).  
The EU must also reinforce the rule of law in its raw materials partnerships with developing countries , to help prevent 
conflict and guarantee an equitable distribution of resources. This should include a requirement that national mining 
laws be well developed and applied at local level, with focus on ensuring economic and social development and 
protecting human rights. Involvement of local communities in decision making will be key . 
Case study – Cobalt: Over 60% of the world’s cobalt supply will come from the Democratic Republic of Congo for the 
foreseeable future. The EU’s Global Gateway could support the region in developing local processing capacity to add 
additional value and take Chinese processing out of Europe’s cobalt supply chains. It could also support the DRC in 
developing the wider infrastructure needed for sustainable metals supply, from lower cost/capex projects (e.g. using 
solar/wind to produce hydrogen) to larger scale products (e.g. the 40 GW Great Inga Dam, which has the potential to 
be the largest hydroelectric power station in the world).  
- The Critical Raw Materials act should commit to using the Global Gateway for raw materials sustainable 
development in Africa and other priority regions, defining its use in future partnerships. 
 
   
Critical Raw Materials Act recommendation 4: 
Make Europe the global leader for high-quality metals recycling 
 
Introduction 
Europe’s Critical Raw Materials Act should improve recycling rates for targeted material and introduce measures to 
make Europe the global leader for high-quality metals recycling. The Commission can achieve this through prioritising 
raw materials circularity in upcoming EU waste legislation reviews, committing to new legislation for emerging product 
groups (e.g. rare earth permanent magnets), and making direct improvements to today’s waste framework.  
Recycling will be Europe’s key long-term opportunity to establish strategic autonomy for its metals supply, especially 
after 2040 once the first gener ation of clean energy technologies reach end- of-life in meaningful volumes. The EU 
must act now to grow its recycling industry and its value- chain into a leadership position, amidst global competition 
with other regions. 
Of particular importance, the Europ ean Commission should introduce mandatory treatment standards for recyclers 
of complex product groups (e.g. e-waste, batteries, PV panels), and act to prevent uncontrolled leakage of key waste 
materials like e.g. battery black mass. 
This paper  proposes tha t the Raw Materials Act prioritises metals and raw materials circularity for key 
upcoming EU waste legislation, while already making direct targeted amendments to today’s framework.  
1. Prioritise metals/raw materials circularity in upcoming waste legislation reviews  
The EU’s Batteries Regulation is a blueprint for uniting all sustainability dimensions of a product’s raw materials supply 
together, including responsible sourcing, chemicals management, recycling, and carbon footprint. The Critical Raw 
Materials Act should commit to an integrated prioritisation of metals and raw materials in the planned waste legislation 
reviewsEnd-of-Life Vehicles Directive (ELV) and Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment (WEEE) Directive,  as well 
as the Waste Framework Directive and implementation of Waste Shipment Regulation (WSR)  review.  
The Commission’s communication could highlight several priority actions to be carried forward across waste legislation 
reviews for delivering higher circularity of strategic and critical raw materials:  
• Calling for achievable end-of-life recycling targets to be set per relevant metal for each product group 
considering the entire materials mix used
1 
• Confirming that the recycling value chain ends at the point when the secondary materials go to new 
applications allowing to avoid using primary materials  
• Requiring improvements to collection and sorting systems, e.g.: 
o Strengthening collection systems for portable electronics 
o Advancing the dismantling & sorting technologies in vehicles recycling chain (like for aluminium alloys) 
• Acting to better control the leakage of waste outside of Europe, e.g.: 
o Addressing the high volumes of illegally exported and other unreported end-of-life vehicles & WEEE  
o Ensuring a strong implementation of the Waste Shipments Regulation’s likely new measures requiring 
proof of equivalent conditions if European waste is treated outside the EU. 
 
1 It is important to consider the entire materials mix in a product including value drivers such as precious metals or copper and prioritise the most 
relevant materials/metals to be recycled. Singling out certain CRMs for recovery without considering the mix can lead to severe losses of other 
valuable metals that could be recovered and form the economic backbone of recycling activities. For example there is no need to separate nickel from 
the stainless steel scrap as nickel is an essential constituent of many stainless steels anyway and can be used directly to produce new stainless steel. 
 
   
• Improving the recyclability of products at their design stage creating a link to the future legislation on the 
ecodesign requirements for sustainable products (ESPR) 
NB. The European Commission should apply caution when applying the recycled content measure more widely than 
the Batteries Regulation. Recycled content is not a necessary tool for raw materials that already have a high uptake of 
supply from secondary material, but could be explored in challenging areas (e.g. rare earths & magnets recycling – 
see ERMA Action Plan). A product-by-product verification and review procedure whether an assigned recycled 
obligation continues to apply is key.  
 
2. Check whether new waste legislation is required for emerging waste streams  
Europe’s waste flows of clean energy technologies like batteries, solar panels and permanent magnets will only  reach a 
meaningful size from 2030 onwards. The European Commission should use the Critical Raw Materials Act to assess 
which other of these emerging groups will require dedicated new legislation in addition to the Batteries Regulation.  
For example, Europe’s end-of-life permanent magnets will provide a major rare earths supply source in the decades 
ahead and may require a dedicated waste policy. By 2050, they could provide volumes of rare earths twice as high as 
the EU’s ambition for setting-up a domestic magnets supply chain for 20% of its needs. But the EU must overcome 
major challenges, including the economic viability of recycling processes and the risk of scrap leakage to China’s 
globally dominant magnets supply chain.  
Other emerging waste streams are already covered by existing legislation. For example, waste Solar PV systems are 
covered under the WEEE Regulation. But given their significant market growth after 2030, the Commission should 
assess whether stronger measures or a product-specific response will be required to maximise recycling opportunities. 
Innovation will also remain important to continue developing new recycling processes, and the Critical Raw Materials 
Act should confirm this as a continued focus of EU research and innovation programmes. Across existing and emerging 
waste streams, there remain several critical raw materials where end-of-life recycling in products is not technically or 
economically feasible
1. 
The Critical Raw Materials Act should: 
• identify where new waste and products legislation for emerging product groups will be required for development in 
the next European Commission term. 
• reconfirm metals circularity as a priority for EU R&I programmes. 
 
3. Make direct and targeted amendments to the existing waste legislation  
The Critical Raw Materials Act should also make important targeted amendments to the EU’s existing waste 
legislation. We have identified three four key actions areas that would immediately help establish a level playing field 
for high-quality European recyclers and help them in accessing European scrap.  
• Mandatory minimum standards for electronics waste and other complex products  
The Critical Raw Materials Act should make it mandatory for all recyclers of European electronics waste (and other 
identified complex product groups) to fulfil minimum standards on the efficiency and safety of their processes. This one 
action would massively raise the EU recycling rates of strategic raw materials  from electronics waste.  
 
1 At a certain point there will be a trade-off for the energy required for recovering/separating low-volume materials from complex material mixes. 
 
   
Laptops, phones, and circuit boards are complex products containing over 40 different metals, often in very low 
volumes but too many are lost to informal processes. They must be recycled by state- of-the art operators which are 
equipped to recover the maximum range of materials while safely handling hazardous substances. The series of 
standards EN 50625 and related technical specifications has been developed to provide this accreditation 1.  
• The Critical Raw Materials Act should confirm a procedure to make the CENELEC 50625 standard and 
technical specifications mandatory for all recyclers of EU electronics waste 2 
 
• New rules on intermediate waste such as black mass 
The Critical Raw Materials Act should strengthen the EU rules on waste and its transportation for key value chains, 
especially focussing on black mass, the intermediate waste stream in lithium-ion battery recycling that contains 
critical/strategic metals such as lithium, cobalt, nickel and manganese, recovered from end- of-life batteries or battery 
production waste. 
Europe’s battery production waste volumes will start increasing in the second part of this decade as electric vehicle 
battery gigafactories open, with the battery production waste rate relatively high in the start  up phase due to strict 
qualification procedures for new batteries. However, China and South Korea’s battery production waste markets and 
recycling industries are already scaled up, and their operators (supported by strong industrial policies) are working to 
consolidate a global leadership position through acquiring battery production waste from Europe and other regions.  
Operators in Europe can easily recover black mass, containing valuable metals such as lithium, cobalt and nickel, either 
from end-of-life batteries or battery production waste, and then export it to other regions for final treatment and 
recycling, in order to make them ready to be added into new battery materials
3. This makes it harder for European 
recyclers to establish a competitive position, jeopardizes Europe’s raw materials security of supply and creates a risk 
that European black mass is treated by operators that do not meet the same high environmental and social standards.  
The EU’s regulatory framework on waste should therefore clarify that black mass is an intermediate waste stream within 
the full battery recycling value chain, and not a product that could be more easily shipped outside of the EU. This aligns 
with the Waste Framework Directive’s definition of recycling.  
- The Critical Raw Materials Act should classify black mass and any other intermediate battery waste stream as a 
‘hazardous waste’ and not as a ‘product’ to ensure that the intermediates of battery recycling are bound by the 
correct shipment rules.  
 
• Harmonisation of waste classification systems across Member States  
Electronics waste, batteries, and other key product groups will need to be shipped across multiple European borders 
for recycling as they must be treated in specialised facilities not present in every Member State.  
 
1 EN 50625 series on the collection, logistics and treatment of WEEE together with the related Technical Specifications, i.e. TS50625-5 for the end-
processing of WEEE fractions- copper and precious metals. 
2 The EU-funded CEWASTE project – led by a consortia of partners across the electronics waste value chain – has the same key conclusion that the 
EU should implement mandatory treatment standards for electronics waste. Moreover, so far eight Member States (France, the Netherlands, Czech 
Republic, Ireland, Luxembourg, Belgium, Slovenia, Lithuania) have incorporated the standards into their national legislation, making compliance 
against the EN 50625 mandatory. 
3 As a recent example, the Korean company POSCO has recently opened an operation in Poland that will recover black mass from European 
batteries and ship it to South Korea for final recycling and input into new batteries  
 
 
   
Shipments are regularly disrupted by different Member State interpretations of whether waste is hazardous or non-
hazardous, leading to delays of several weeks or months. For example, shipments of green- listed metals-containing 
waste have been stopped and disputed in transit Member States. Although different batteries have different waste 
codes, some Member States treat all batteries as hazardous waste. 
The recently proposed Waste Shipments Regulation review process has started to improve this situation but much still 
needs to be clarified in the Regulation itself and/or the secondary legislation. The Critical Raw Materials Act should 
confirm a direction for further steps forward.  
The Critical Raw Materials Act should: 
• Request a harmonisation of waste classification across Member States to facilitate intra- EU shipments (e.g. for 
waste batteries, electronic scrap or anode slimes), including mutual recognition of national decisions in relation 
to the hazardous nature of wastes for the purpose of waste shipments. 
• Confirm the Waste Shipment Regulation’s proposal to establish specific criteria for distinguishing used good and 
waste for products like electronics or vehicles, where exports are a problem.  
• Confirm that the Commission will abstain from setting contamination thresholds for the Green List of Waste when 
implementing Annex III of the revised Waste Shipments Regulation, as this would greatly complicate the 
shipments resulting in applying so called notification procedure.  
• Encourage addition of new waste codes in the Green List of the Waste Shipments Regulation, to improve recycling 
for non- hazardous waste streams (an example being windows, doors, curtain walls, and framing profiles for 
aluminium). 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
   
Annex 3: Ensuring high environmental and social standards for Europe’s raw materials  
The metals industry supports that the European market is supplied by environmentally and socially responsible 
materials as a differentiator. Certification schemes and due diligence requirements are important to 
guaranteeing this, and we reflect considerations by the Commission and Member States in this area.   
• The EU should start by benchmarking the different certification schemes available on a global level, identifying 
those meeting its own environmental and social ambition and the gaps with existing legislative requirements. It 
should avoid preferencing one single scheme above others.  
• It should be reflected that EU environmental legislation already offers a guarantee of environmental protection 
going further than most other world regions in terms of site emissions, biodiversity etc.  
• The EU should avoid an “EU-only standard” given the global nature of raw materials and metals markets. 
Linking to the available global standards is a more efficient and coherent approach.  
• It’s important that any requirements in the Critical Raw Materials Act are coherent with the Batteries Regulation, 
Corporate due diligence directive, and taxonomy discussions, building on them rather than taking a completely 
new approach. Requirements should be inclusive for the full value chain 
• The CRM Act’s focus must be on bringing responsible projects online quickly. Any new requ irements should be 
developed relatively quickly in-line with existing legislations to give certainty, and should be designed not to 
overburden new investments in Europe versus other regions. Care is also needed not to overly limit Europe’s 
supply options in any specific areas.   
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
   
Annex 4: Recommendations for ensuring a coherent raw materials and REACH agenda 
The Critical Raw Materials Act should confirm the need for an aligned approach to achieving the Europe’s 
environmental goals for sustainable chemicals.  
It's crucial that the European Commission uses the REACH revision to give itself pre -emptive tools for alleviating 
the risk of uncoordinated chemicals policies clashing with its Green Deal raw materials goals, while ensuring 
high environment and health protection.  
The problem to be solved 
• Metals will make up most of the “critical chemicals” being newly defined under the European Commission’s 
forthcoming Chem4EU foresight analysis, due to their increased demand in the energy and digital transitions. 
A majority have direct or indirect hazardous properties. Batteries, solar PV, wind turbines, hydrogen fuel cells, 
power cables, digital technologies all contain metals with a defined hazard.  
 
• The REACH Revision is an opportunity for the European Commission to take more early control of its 
chemicals regulatory processes and their links with other Green Deal priorities: including to ensure that metals 
in the EU raw materials agenda are not caught up in long unpredictable regulatory  processes not fitted to their 
risk profile (in parallel to the EU’s stated objective of controlling harmful chemicals in consumer products).  
 
• Companies making long-term investments into new European refining & recycling activities require regulatory 
certainty. The risk of unwarranted REACH bans or unpredictable extra compliance costs is proving a real 
disincentive to investors choosing the EU over competing markets. Recent examples impacting key green 
technologies have included on EV battery cobalt salts, the potential lithium salts
 classification, and decisions 
on lead impacting on aluminium, copper alloys and precious metals recycling (interdependent with lead). 
These examples will inevitably be followed by others as the regulatory process intensifies (see Annex 2).  
 
• In the current REACH Regulation, the European Commission does not have effective oversight on the 
chemicals that get prioritized for risk management. There is no legal reference to a methodology that would 
allow it to identify up-front which specific risk management measures will be the most appropriate for each 
situation1. This means that strategic metals get caught up in long regulatory processes which remove certainty 
for investors and threaten disproportionate bans or control measures not faced elsewhere in the world – while 
lacking a risk to human health or the environment.    
Solution:  
The Critical Raw Materials accompanying communication should confirm the need for coherent and effective 
chemicals legislation, for carrying forward in the REACH Review.  
The European Commission should use the REACH Review to strengthen the existing procedure for initiating risk 
management on targeted substances. It can increase its oversight by building on the current process and introducing 
a formal screening step at an appropriate stage of risk management, which allows it t o agree in advance direction for 
the materials that underpin the green and digital transition or other strategic areas.  
 
 
1 Most metals have a very low potential for substitution, and are refined and recycled in integrated processes linked to several other metals. The only 
way for Europe to achieve its raw materials and chemicals goals together is to focus on preventing harmful health and environmental exposure from 
metals - in circular loops that reduce the need for new primary extraction. Occupational Health and Exposure legislation, Industrial Emissions legislation 
and targeted restrictions can all be more effective than substitution-focussed authorization.   
 
   
Examples of strategic raw materials investments delayed by today’s EU Chemicals policy  
• Lithium refining: The EU’s proposal to classify lithium compounds used in electric vehicle batteries as a 
Category 1A hazard is contributing to major uncertainty for new lithium refining investments, with companies 
raising public concerns
 about the scientific basis and other countries (Australia, US, Chile) openly 
questioning the approach taken. If a lithium classification does ever go forward, then investing companies will 
be in limbo for several years while the REACH process is carried out to define control measures.  
 
Europe requires new lithium refining capacity as part of its battery supply chain, to process both primary and 
secondary raw material.  
 
• Case study of Cobalt Salts: Five cobalt salts used in electric vehicle batteries were evaluated and then 
under consideration first for authorisation under REACH and then for a REACH restriction procedure, before 
the European Commission eventually stopped it after six years in favour of setting binding occupational 
exposure limits (OELs).  
 
Because the Commission had no formal route for selecting this direction in advance, its decision was ad- hoc. 
It will be unsustainable and too slow to make similar ad-hoc decisions for all other strategic chemicals falling 
under the REACH process.  
 
• Aluminium recycling: European companies are delaying investments
 into new aluminium recycling plants and 
processes due to ongoing discussions on a potential strict classification/authorization of lead metal in its solid 
form. Aluminium alloys, like many others in the metals industry, contain lead at low impurity levels that would be 
targeted by the new rules, despite their being no evidence of harm.  
 
Europe needs to grow its aluminium recycling capacity as scrap volumes increase, to supply low -carbon material 
to electric vehicles, grid infrastructure, and solar panels, and to help in diversifying away from today’s primary 
aluminium imports from Russia (which comprise 20% of supply).  
 
This will be made much more difficult if aluminium alloys are defined as hazardous  mixtures despite having no 
environmental impact, and will encourage that Europe’s valuable scrap is exported to other regions for recycling.  
 
• Precious metals recycling: Recycling processes to recover critical raw materials from electronics waste also rel y 
on lead, which acts like a magnet to bring out low volumes of metals like silver, platinum, and various critical raw 
materials like indium and germanium.  
 
New EU investments into electronics waste recycling will require a guarantee of future market for lead in this 
necessary Circular Economy application (opposite to the Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability’s ambition to 
minimize and substitute hazardous substances in recycling streams).   
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
   
Annex 5: Rationale for prioritisation of selected strategic materials not on the EU CRM list   
Aluminium, copper, nickel, and high-purity manganese are not included in the EU’s critical raw materials list (except 
bauxite which is used as the main raw material to produce primary aluminium) as they  do not face supply risks today. 
However, they are all central for the energy transition with rising demand profiles and specific supply challenges in the 
next decade, and this should be reflected in the Critical Raw Materials Act (+ other carrier metals l ike zinc).   
Aluminium  
1. Relevance for energy transition  
KU Leuven estimates that Europe’s clean energy production goals will require an extra 4.6 Mt of aluminium in the next 
30 years, predominantly for grid infrastructure, electric vehicles, and solar panels. This is a 33% increase to today’s 
levels, which is very significant when considering how widespread aluminium already is in today’s economy. The 
tonnages of aluminium required are far higher than for any other non-ferrous metal.  
On a global level: 
• The International Energy Agency confirms aluminium’s strategic role: “Clean energy transitions will impact 
aluminium demand, with potential for upward pressure from technology shifts that require greater use of 
aluminium, e.g. for lightweight vehicles and solar energy”.  
• The World Bank identifies aluminium as a “high impact, cross-cutting mineral” without substitution options: 
“Aluminium is used widely for both energy generation and storage technologies. The demand for aluminium is 
therefore expected to be critical regardless of which technology-based mitigation scenario is achieved”.  
 
2. Reasons for concern to Europe in the next 10-15 years  
• Europe’s primary aluminium production is under severe pressure. 1/3 of the EU’s primary capacity has already 
been lost in the last 15 years due to increasing low-cost imports from China, the Middle East and Russia, and 
the energy crisis has driven 50% of the remaining capacity offline. There is risk of permanent closures, 
threatening a situation like the loss of Europe’s magnesium industry.   
• Russia is today a major importer of aluminium to the European market (11% share), and alternative sources 
will need to be found after the invasion of Ukraine in case of self-sanctioning by aluminium users in Europe. 
China’s proven dumping practices put pressure onto the EU value chain, and there is a risk that China 
supplies Europe’s extra demand due to its 60% global production share (mostly coal-powered).  
• European aluminium production has one of the lowest carbon footprints in the world. With the significant 
deindustrialisation of the sector in Europe, there is no guarantee that sufficient low-carbon aluminium will be 
available to meet growing demand. This is important due to the high electricity -intensiveness of production. 
o The hypothetical scenario of supplying the extra 4.6 Mt of aluminium completely from coal -powered 
primary sources would be equivalent to adding 96 Mt CO2 (or roughly the same as Belgium’s entire CO2 
emissions), compared with only 2.3 Mt if met completely from recycled aluminium ( own calculations).   
• An early warning sign of potential supply challenges in a carbon-constrained world came last year, when 
China was forced to shut down segments of its coal-powered aluminium production to meet energy targets. 
 Europe’s priorities: 1) Raise recycling rates from Europe’s growing scrap pool, 2) Prevent fur ther EU plants 
from closing and support transition to affordable carbon-free electricity sources & direct CO2 reduction, 3) 
Diversify away from high-carbon imports (at the same time as finding alternative sources to Russia)  
 
3. Supply and demand profile today  
Aluminium is produced in Europe (including Norway and Iceland) today from primary and secondary sources, making 
up 29% and 42% of supply respectively, together with 30% from imports (mainly from Russia, Mozambique, and 
China). Europe’s total demand for aluminium was 14 Mt in 2019. Only 3% of Europe’s bauxite needs are met 
domestically, with most supplied from Guinea (70%) & Brazil 
 
   
Copper 
 
1. Relevance for energy transition  
KU Leuven estimates that Europe’s clean energy production goals will require an extra 1.5 Mt of copper in the next 30 
years, predominantly for electric vehicles, grid infrastructure, electric vehicles, and wind turbines. This is a 35% 
increase to today’s levels, which is very significant when considering how widespread copper is in today’s economy.  
Copper has been nicknamed the “new oil
” of the global energy transition. Because of its conductive properties and 
cost-effectiveness, it “sits at the heart of capturing, storing, and transporting these new sources of energy”, without 
substitutes of comparable performance. It also has a number of critical metal by -products in production.  
• International Energy Agency: “Electricity networks need a huge amount of copper and aluminium, with 
copper being a cornerstone for all electricity-related technologies”  
• World Bank: Copper is “used in more than eight clean energy generation and storage technologies; thus, 
even if technological improvements, costs reductions and deployment of new emerging technologies were 
to take place, these changes would have little impact on the overall demand for th em”.  
 
2. Reasons for concern to Europe in the next 10-15 years  
• Europe’s own copper mining capacity has depleted by 17% in the last 5 years, and will deplete a further 13% 
by 2030. Depletion will then accelerate and reach almost 50% by 2040. Without new mining capacity, Europe 
will become significantly more dependent on global markets at the time when global supply is most at risk.   
• Experts are warning of a major global supply gap between 2030 and 2040 for copper ore.  
o Bloomberg New Energy Finance quantifies that annual copper demand will expand 53% to 2040 
mainly due to electrification. Supply is growing a lot more slowly, risking a shortfall of between 5 and 14 
million tonnes by 2040.  
o S&P Global states that “the chronic gap between worldwide copper supply and demand projected to

3. Supply and demand profile today  
Copper metal is produced in Europe today from primary and secondary sources, making up 55% and 39% of supply 
respectively, together with 13% from imported metal mainly (from Chile, Russia, and DRC). Europe’s total demand for 
copper was 4.3 Mt in 2018. Europe mines 15% of its copper needs directly, importing primary raw materials mainly 
from Chile, Peru, and Brazil (roughly a 20% import share each).  
Chile and Peru are the main global suppliers of copper ore (together with a 40% global market share), and China is 
the main global supplier of copper metal (with a 40% global market share).  
 
   
Nickel 
1. Relevance for energy transition  
KU Leuven estimates that Europe’s clean energy production goals will require an extra 395 Mt of nickel in the next 30 
years for its lithium-ion battery production goals. This is a more than doubling of today’s consumption, with new supply 
required at high-purity “battery grade”.  
All international organisations agree that nickel will be a critical mineral in the energy transition:  
• International Energy Agency: “Electric vehicles and battery storage…are set to take over from stainless 
steel as the largest end user of nickel by 2040. Nickel plays a central role in giving batteries greater 
performance, longevity, and higher energy density”  
• World Bank: Nickel is “Nickel is a crucial metal for the move to a green energy future as it is needed in 
energy storage, for use in Li-ion batteries, and is also used in a wide range of generation technologies. 
Nickel is a cross cutting mineral: “increase in demand in either absolute or relative numbers is significant”  
 
2. Reasons for concern to Europe in the next 10-15 years  
• KU Leuven estimates that the world will have a 2030 nickel shortfall of 19% in an aggressive decarbonisation 
scenario, even if all projects announced globally today are brought online (including those seen as unlikely). 
This can only be solved through a rapid expansion of global mining capacity.  
• Europe’s existing nickel mining capacity will deplete by 15% by 2030 and by almost 50% by 2040 without new 
mines being brought online. Three unlikely projects in Finland and Spain could partially compensate for 
depletion, but they require sufficient incentives to be viable. In the longer-term, the Sakatti project in Finland 
could offer more significant supply. 
• While nickel is abundant, the production of high-purity Class 1 nickel for batteries has supply constraints.  
o The sulphide ores used for producing Class 1 nickel make up only 45% of the world’s nickel production 
today, and most planned capacity for the next decade is based on lower-quality laterite ores.  
o Laterite ores are usually converted into Class 2 nickel products such as ferronickel and nickel pig iron 
(used in stainless steel production). They are more available but have traditionally not been suitable as 
input for EV batteries. Processes have been developed for converting Class 2 nickel into a suitable 
quality (HPAL – high pressure acid leaching), but the CO2 emissions are very high and currently not 
compatible with EU sustainability goals (e.g. 69tCO2/t for class 2 nickel vs. 18tCO2/t class 1 nickel)  
• Russia is Europe’s major supplier of Class 1 nickel today. It produces 17% of the world’s class 1 nickel, and 
makes up 30% of Europe’s nickel imports. Europe will be challenged to find alternative sources of battery -grade 
nickel supply if it decides to diversify away from Russia, especially if supply from Indonesia or China is defined 
as too carbon intensive or environmentally impactful. 
• Europe’s priorities: 1) Open new mining and refining capacity, 2) Find alternative sustainable sources of 
global supply to diversify from Russia, 3) Invest into battery recycling capacity

High-purity manganese 
Manganese is the “forgotten battery metal”, and frequently missed off critical minerals analyses. This is because its 
major mass-market application is to alloy steel from abundant resources, and demand from batteries will continue to 
make up a smaller percentage of its overall market (today 90% goes to steel production while only 1% goes to 
batteries).  
But batteries require specialised high-purity manganese chemicals that are available in much lower volumes and must 
be produced in completely different factories to other manganese refineries. China produces 90% of the world’s high-
purity battery grade manganese today.  
Manganese demand from batteries is now rising more steeply due accelerating electric vehicle take- up and the 
decision of several OEMs and battery manufacturers to prioritise the development of high- Manganese cathodes to be 
commercialised at scale this decade (reducing costs while keeping range).This is because high-purity manganese is 
much more affordable than high-cost cobalt and nickel, and increasing the manganese content of the cathode will 
reduce the cost of the battery, and thus the electric vehicle.  
Cairn research estimates that the world’s high-purity manganese demand will increase tenfold in the next decade to 
almost 1,000,000 tonnes. But there will be a 433,000 tonnes supply deficit based on today’s announced projects, 
requiring significant new investment into speciality processing plants.  
Europe’s battery plans alone will need over 200,000 tonnes of high-grade manganese by 2030, but the EU today has 
no local supply sources. The Euro Manganese reclamation and processing project in Czech Republic is currently the 
only viable project for this timeframe and would add 50,000 tonnes of local supply if brought online.  
Europe’s priorities: 1) Invest into specialised refining capacity, 2) Open viable domestic mines/reclamation projects, 
3) Secure diversified global supply sources to avoid a new China dependency  
Additional note: Zinc & energy crisis showing the integration of Europe’s metals ecosystem  
Zinc does not have the future demand or supply challenges faced by “clean energy minerals”, but is the main 
metallurgical route for accessing critical raw materials needed in higher volumes like indium and germanium. But the 
energy crisis has created a meaningful risk of Europe permanently losing a large percentage of its zinc supply and 
having to increase its reliance on global markets for zinc and its by-products. This demonstrates why the European 
Commission must focus on an integrated raw materials supply alongside its prioritisation exercise.  
Further background  
Zinc is an abundant material, well supplied from Europe today. Europe meets 60% of its zinc metal needs from domestic 
primary production and 40% from recycling, without requiring major metal imports. 25% of its needs come from 
domestic mines and 35% from imported ores. These imports are quite diversified, 45% from Peru, US, Australia.  
The energy transition will not lead to a significant change in Europe’s zinc demand (although zinc is used across a wide 
range of technologies like electric vehicles, solar panels, and wind turbines), unless emerging technologies like zinc 
batteries take on a growing market share. KU Leuven projects that clean energy technologies will make up 10% of 
Europe’s overall zinc demand in 2050. 
But Europe’s production base today is still severely under pressure from the energy crisis. Zinc is one of the most 
electricity-intensive metals to produce, and Europe’s high-power prices have resulted in over 50% of EU capacity being 
taken offline by October 2022. A reduction in zinc capacity would directly reduce EU potential for accessing  critical raw 
materials like indium and germanium, as well as disadvantaging the end-users of zinc.

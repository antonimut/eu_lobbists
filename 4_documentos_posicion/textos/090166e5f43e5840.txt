Albemarle is an industry leader in battery, industrial, and specialty grade, high -quality lithium production. 
We operate across a diverse range of hard rock and brine lithium resources and conversion facilities in 
Europe, North and South America, Asia, and Australia, to develop and manufacture a broad range of lithium 
compounds, including lithium carbonate, lithium hydroxide, lithium chloride, and value -added lithium 
specialties and reagents. Albemarle employs approximately 5,600 p eople and serves customers in 
approximately 100 countries. 
Political context and EU Strategic autonomy  
Albemarle welcomes the European Critical Raw Materials Act as a needed initiative to ensure a stable and 
secure supply of strategic raw materials to the  EU. Lithium is a strategic raw material, a fundamental 
element for the battery technologies of today ( Lithium-Ferrous-Phosphate, LFP, or Nickel -Manganese-
Cobalt NMC) and tomorrow (solid-state). Lithium production is a specialty chemicals market more than a 
conventional mining market: the main sources of Lithium are hard rocks and brine, but these sources need 
to be further refined into Lithium compounds used in batteries (Li thium carbonate, hydroxide, chloride, 
and metal) and in other key applications including lubricants, specialty glass, cement, ceramics, pharma, air 
purification and more. Therefore, the value chain of Lithium has two possible bottlenecks, namely at mining 
and refining level. Additional mining capacity will not benefit the EU security of supply if not coupled with 
comparable refining capacity. Currently, the EU lacks significant refining capacity for Lithium: Albemarle 
operates the only Lithium conversion plant in Europe, based in Langelsheim (Germany) and active since the 
1920s. The Lange lsheim plant produces Lithium compounds used in specialty applications. Additional 
refining capacity in Europe will be crucial to serve the nascent EU battery industry.  
The need to support the growth of e-mobility in view of the 2035 Internal Combustion Engine (ICE) ban and 
the geopolitical situation call for bold initiatives: several analysts point out to a likely shortage of Lithium 
for the next decade. For instance, the KU Leuven study “Metals for Clean Energy ” commissioned by 
Eurometaux identified a 37% shortfall in Lithium supply by 2030, while m eeting the European Union’s 
Green Deal goal of climate neutrality by 2050 will require 35 times more lithium. Recycling is an important 
opportunity to ensure an efficient use of resources and contribute to the long-term supply of raw materials, 
and it should be supported by the regulators. However, we cannot expect a real contribution of secondary 
(i.e. recycled and/or recovered) Lithium to ease the shortage of materials of this decade.  
The EU should therefore use current resources as efficiently as possible, open new mining and refining 
capacity to support the growth of the EV industry, and sec ure stable supply of raw materials through 
incentive programs and trade diplomacy. Other countries and regions are already well -positioned: the 
President of the Commission Ursula Von der Leyen remarked that China controls 60% of the Lithium 
refining capacity, while in the USA the Inflation Reduction Act is likely to drive important investments in the 
entire value chain. To be successful, we believe that the European Raw Materials Act should focus on the

Policy recommendations 
Trade diplomacy  
Given that recycling and new resources are likely to contribute to the global supply only in the medium-
long term, the EU should work with like-minded partners such as Australia, Argentina and Chile to finalize 
an ambitious trade deal able to support  the Lithium flow towards Europe, including strong raw materials 
chapters in all its Free Trade Agreements. These agreements should establish a level -playing field for 
products and materials originating in the territories of said FTA partners. For instance , should the EU 
develop targets on local content for its products, the materials originating from the FTA partners should 
qualify as local content.  
Funding 
Relevant public-private funding should be made available to support strategic projects able to increase EU 
security of supply, both in the EU and outside the EU. This support should ideally match the ambition level 
of the Inflation Reduction Act in order to ensure EU competitiveness at a global level. Funding should be 
made available in a stable way dur ing the next decade, and not just as a one -off grant, so as to ensure 
continuous support to the development of the industry.  
Fast permitting, respecting communities and the environment  
Permits for strategic mining, refining, and recycling projects should be awarded following a fast -track 
procedure, developing different steps in parallel. However, this should not result in the approval of projects 
with lower environmental standards, or developed against the will of local communities. In Chile, Albemarle 
has cooperative agreements with several local indigenous groups, including with the Council of 
Atacameñan Peoples (CPA) . The CPA represents 18 indigenous communities and 6,000 people who  live 
around the Salar  de Atacama . Under these agreements, we meet with the CPA monthly to discuss 
community concerns, address changes that need to be made at our operations, and plan ahead for our 
monthly joint visits to monitor water levels together. We also contribute 3.5% of our Chilean revenue to 
the indigenous communities in the CPA. Over the years, these funds ha ve contributed to a variety of 
projects focused on improving the quality of life of local residents.  
ESG standards  
Ambitious ESG standards should be applied to these projects, coherently with the procedures identified in 
other initiatives such as the Batteries Regulation and the Corporate and Social Due Diligence Directive. The 
most ambitious schemes, such as IRMA (the Initiative for Responsible Mining Assurance), should be used 
as a reference to guarantee compliance with ESG standards. IRMA offers objective, independent third-party 
verification of industrial -scale mining sites using comprehensive standards agreed upon through a 
collaborative, multi -stakeholder process including material producers, customers, labor, NGOs, and 
communities. In 2022, our Salar de Atacama site was the first lithium mine in the world to complete a third-
 
 
party audit in accordance with the IRMA standards. In 2022, we also began IRMA self -assessments at our 
mining sites in Australia and will complete a third party audit in 2023. We are also p lanning to apply the 
standard for mineral processing facilities, currently in development of the draft, to La Negra Plant in Chile. 
Taxonomy  
The inclusion of Lithium extraction, refining and recycling in the EU taxonomy would be beneficial to 
correctly in form investors on which Lithium projects are more sustainable. In our recent sustainability 
report, Albemarle disclosed its Life -cycle Assessment results for rock-based lithium hydroxide and brine -
based lithium carbonate. Both studies were performed in accordance with ISO 14040/14044 standards. 
 
Consistency in chemicals management 
A stable and coherent legislative environment is a precondition for investments. Several substances 
fundamental for the energy transition are classified as hazardous, or are undergoing a classification process 
despite contradicting or weak s cientific evidence. This is the case of Lithium chloride, carbonate and 
hydroxide, for which the Risk Assessment Committee (RAC) of the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) 
proposed a classification as 1A reprotoxic, despite the negative opinions of several EU Member States and 
like-minded partners (Argentina, Australia, USA).  
Part of the problem of this classification is the uncertainty it would create on which regulatory measures 
will be implemented, and by when. This is making any investment decision very c omplicated, above all 

 
 
when other countries are not going to implement the same classification and measures. To solve th ese 
issues, we believe that the following initiatives should be undertaken:  
• Refrain from including the Lithium salts in the next  Adaptation to Technical Progress (ATP)  and 
ask the RAC to re-assess its opinion; 
• In absence of unambiguous, reliable and robust scientific evidence for the suspected hazard, RAC 
should refrain from adopting the proposed Harmonised Classification. Instead  they should advise 
for additional scientific information to be gathered to address scientific uncertainty or bias in 
available data to ensure that a decision is taken on basis of sound and robust scientific evidence.    
• Use the CLP revision as an opportunity to include a formal appeal procedure against RAC opinions,  
by an external body (e.g. ECHA Board Of Appeal); 
• Include a prioritization step before the start of the classification process, making sure that the 
different aspects and targets of the EU Gree n Deal and other key policy initiatives are fully taken 
into account; 
• Prior to the launch of the harmonised classification process for strategic raw materials (or related 
compounds), a holistic  impact assessment taking into account the impact on key policy initiatives 
and EU competitiveness, as well  a Risk Management Option analysis (RMOA) should be carried out 
, to ensure that the full impact of this action is known and  industry has clarity on which regulatory 
measure need to be complied with or could be triggered by the Classification.  
Conclusion 
In closing, we appreciate the opportunity to comment ahead of the publication of this ambitious Regulation 
proposal. As EU policymakers continue in this process, we encourage they find ways to harmonize 
overlapping provisions within different pieces of legislation.  
Uniform enforcement likewise will be a significant challenge. Furthermore, unlocking funding and fast-track 
permits would be key to achieve the EU strategic autonomy through mining, refining, and re cycling 
projects. 
We also ask that policymakers verify feasibility of timelines to ensure compliance with ESG standards. If 
rushed, certain provisions risk creating over -regulation and unnecessary overlaps and may have the 
unintentional consequence of stif ling growth and investment in an emerging sector, which is poised to 
make an unprecedented impact in the decarbonization of personal and industrial ground transportation 
and thus contribute significantly to achieving the goals of the European Green Deal.

Statement of the Austrian Federal Economic Chamber (WKÖ)  
European Critical Raw Materials Act 
 
 
In the current situation, it is essential to generate growth impetus through all channels. To 
achieve this goal, however, it is necessary to have unhindered access to essential raw 
materials both within the EU and in foreign markets. This is not only indispensable to 
preserve the European/Austrian business environment and thus European/Austrian 
production, but also to achieve a green and digital transition. 
 
We therefore welcome the intention of the European Commission to identify strategic 
projects along the entire supply chain and to build up strategic reserves where supply is at 
risk – if there is no mandatory quota for companies and the final decision about stockpiling 
remains with the companies through a European Critical Raw Materials Act. 
 
The Austrian Federal Economic Chamber (WKÖ) has been and still is a proponent and 
supporter of deepening EU trade relations, especially through EU trade agreements. EU 
trade agreements help to counteract the negative effects of recent global developments 
(such as the Corona crisis, the Russia-Ukraine war, etc.), build resilience and diversify 
supply chains. Therefore, in this context one of our main demands in connection with EU 
trade negotiations with third countries is that they must be primarily focused on securing 
access to raw materials. 
 
For this reason, we are very pleased that the President of the European Commission Ursula 
van der Leyen announced the pursuit of supply diversification through Trade Agreements 
and new partnerships. We hope that this announcement will soon be followed by deeds and 
that important agreements such as those with Mercosur and Australia will be swiftly signed 
and implemented. 
 
As far as the Raw Materials Act is concerned, based on our initial assessment, the following 
points should be highlighted: 
 
Think competition first. The new legislation must be in line with existing or planned EU 
initiatives and must not lead to further bureaucratic hurdles. The competitiveness of 
European/Austrian companies in international trade must be the top priority. DG Trade 
and DG Grow should work in close cooperation on this proposal/issue.

1. Defining priorities and objectives for EU actions 
The determination of strategic raw materials must not only be based on existing data. 
It must also consider current developments (shortage of wood, switch to electric cars, 
advancing the energy transition) as well as future developments, such as the ban on 
internal combustion engines from 2035 onwards. The available data are mostly data 
from the previous year and therefore do not reflect the current situation nor the future 
needs. Thus, the definition of “strategic raw materials” must be extensive.  
The EU list of critical raw materials must not be used as a basis. Raw materials that are 
important for production, such as aluminium, rubber, and magnesium, are not included 
in this list, nor is wood, which is no longer available to the same amount as it once 
was. In the same way, raw materials to produce food or animal feed must also be 
included in the considerations, e.g., grain from Ukraine is no longer available in the 
same way. Shortage situations can also occur, as different sectors of the economy 
compete in the processing of the same raw materials, e.g., the production of biofuel 
with food processing. The definition must be made according to key end products and 
after including the entire respective supply chains. For example, batteries require not 
only lithium and cobalt, but also aluminium for their shell.  
Due to rapidly changing conditions, a shortened review cycle for the critical raw 
materials list must be considered and a dynamic methodology must be found to be able 
to include – to the extent possible – considerations about future needs in raw materials. 
 
2. Improving the EU’s monitoring, risk management and governance in the field of CRM  
An important discussion addresses the question whether there is a need for a European 
Raw Materials Agency or if the (existing) agencies of the member states should need to 
coordinate more. Alongside this discussion (mandatory) collection and analysis of 
relevant data (e.g., for monitoring and crisis management) by an EU or national 
agencies is yet unsolved. For both, an answer must be found. 
Existing communication structures and existing data/databases must be built upon. If 
necessary, exiting databases should be revised and expands. The monitoring 
mechanisms to be developed must not lead to further and higher reporting obligations 
for companies. At the same time, company and business secrets must be protected. 
 
3. Strengthening the EU’s critical raw materials value chain (mining, refining, processing, 
recycling)  
The advancement of the development of projects relating to critical raw materials by 
streamlining procedures and by improving access to funding for selected projects is to 
be seen as positive.  
EU projects should build on and complement projects of EU member states. They must 
neither duplicate nor counteract them. Strategic projects must be selected in close 
consultation with the European business community. The focus must not only be on 
raw materials: It must be broader. For example, green energy from wind turbines is 
also an important raw material, but it hast to be transported from A to B by pipelines. 
Therefore, the expansion of energy sources, the necessary infrastructure and  means 
of transportation etc. should also play a role.  
When designing possible funding, care must be taken to ensure that it is as transparent 
and practical as possible and that it can be applied for without obstacles. If the scope 
of funding is too narrow, this can also put the brakes on development. Furthermore, 
easy access, especially for SMEs, to these projects must be ensured. 
With respect to recycling, the technologies to reach the necessary amounts of 
secondary raw materials are not fully available yet. Due to the prolongation of product

lifespan, many raw materials are just put on the market now and will only be available 
for recycling at the end of their lifespan – for some metals e.g., only in 10 – 15 years. 
Furthermore, raw materials necessary for recycling, e.g., scrap, must remain available 
in Europe. Therefore, one of the goals for scrap delivered to third countries must also 
be to create a level playing field in third countries based on the Waste Shipment 
Directive through incentive systems and intensive cooperation. In addition, not all 
mineral raw materials can be recycled, e.g., because they are used during the 
production of other products (e.g., graphite electrodes), or they become part of a 
high-tech material (e.g., iron ore in steel or bauxite in aluminum etc.). Finally, the 
required quality of the final product often limits the possibility to use recyclates. 
 
4. Ensuring a sustainable level playing field across the Single Market  
In general, it is important to strengthen the internal market also in this area, as 
Community regulations would contribute to legal certainty. However, care must be 
taken to ensure that trade with third countries rich in rare earths or other raw 
materials relevant to the European economy is not made more difficult as a result, but 
that trade channels remain open under a regulated approach. This is because it is 
questionable to what extent the EU can draw on primary resources within the EU, or 
whether these are available within the EU at all since Europe is known to be a 
continent poor in raw materials.  
It should also be noted that some European and Austrian companies have also been 
dependent on third countries to date and are likely to remain so in the future. 
Accordingly, it must be ensured here that these trade relations are not made 
disproportionately difficult. Joint trade agreements with third countries are considered 
desirable. In any case, it is important to make the European economic area stable to be 
able to counteract any price explosions. 
A sensible strengthening of the circular economy is to be supported in principle, as this 
can also achieve a certain independence. However, care must be taken to ensure that 
the legal framework is designed in a practical manner and that it is consistent with 
existing directives, regulations and laws, and to ensure that SMEs are not 
disproportionately disadvantaged by excessive bureaucratic hurdles.  
 It is important to ensure seamless transport of waste in the single market and that 
secondary raw materials are available in the EU. Care should be taken to ensure that 
waste is not exported to third countries with lower recycling standards than in the EU. 
However, this should not lead to the introduction of regulations that restrict trade in 
secondary raw materials with third countries. A ban on export of secondary raw 
materials must be rejected. Above all, European secondary raw materials producers 
and traders of secondary raw materials must not be subjected to further restrictions. It 
would be more important to create incentives and support for producers and traders to 
keep the required number of raw materials in the EU and to encourage third countries 
to raise their standards through more intensive cooperation. Research funding related 
to the extraction of critical raw materials from waste would also be welcome. 
It should also be noted that the decision whether to stockpile can only be made by the 
companies themselves based on business management or location policy 
considerations. The effort to make strategic raw materials available in the EU must not 
become a pretext for "managed trade". A potential stockpiling by the EU and / or 
member states needs to address a range of yet unsolved questions: Who will pay for 
the raw materials? Will there be a(n) (legal) obligation for EU / national companies to 
deliver raw materials to the stock, if needed? At which price will the companies be 
requested to deliver the raw materials? In case of a crisis, how will the available raw

materials be distributed to the companies that need these raw materials? We have also 
been made aware that there are many different qualities of raw materials and not each 
type of raw material can be used for every application / process. So, who decides,

5. Legal coherence and alignment are essential 
The current legal framework of the EU lacks consistency and consequently does not 
contribute to ensuring the necessary supply of raw materials. Therefore, the 
availability of raw materials must be considered in any legislative process and 
permitting procedures must be streamlined and accelerated. For instance, we firmly 
believe that including mining into the IED will not solve the current issues. 
Furthermore, current environmental regulations create legal bottlenecks. This comes 
on top of also high energy prices and a lack of green energy availability. Finally, a lack 
of coherence in chemicals, energy, climate, and trade policies will hinder growing 
security of supply. 
 
6. Sustainable mining in Europe is decisive to secure supply 
One key element to securing the raw materials for the industry is ramping up domestic 
supply. In this context, we support the European Parliament’s call for the promotion of 
sustainable domestic mining (see Resolution 2021/2011(INI) of November 2021). Several 
elements need to be streamlined to be able to do so, including accelerating permitting 
procedures, ensuring the right skillset in the workforce as well as enabling adequate 
funding. Legal coherence is also paramount in this regard. In addition, we would like to 
recall that mining in the EU already today complies with the highest safety and 
environmental standards worldwide. Nevertheless, “sustainable mining standards” are 
discussed now. If the Commission wants to benchmark these standards, we will ask to 
consider the high variety of raw materials mined and the respective variety of mining 
techniques. We would therefore urge the Commission to refrain from only focussing on 
one standard.

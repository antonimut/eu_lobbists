Recent international events, including the coronavirus pandemic and Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, 
have illustrated clearly that supply chains for critical goods are highly connected and carefully 
organized on a global scale. These supply chains count on cooperation on a broad scale, from 
sourcing raw materials to transporting them to final assembly or refinement. They involve a multitude 
of participants worldwide in countries of various stages of economic maturity and with a range of 
attitudes and actions regarding human rights. Disruptions to this carefully balanced supply chain can 
not only be inconvenient to European consumers, but national security risks for countries, especially 
when it comes to materials such as cobalt, graphite, copper,  nickel, lithium, and certain rare earth 
elements used in the manufacture of electronics, clean energy, and aerospace and automotive 
products.  
 
In response to the economic security threats these recent disruptions have posed, the European 
Commission is now appropriately considering a Proposed Directive to establish legislation that would 
secure the EU’s access to critica l raw materials (CRMs). The measure, called the EU Critical Raw 
Materials Act,1 could improve the stability for supply chains that are important on an economic level 
and generally address the significant threat that China poses to Europe’s economic well -
being. Among the objectives, the Act would support the EU’s green and digital transitions by 
assuring that the raw materials necessary for these transitions are available from a robust array of 
sources, not controlled by single actors that act as de facto monopolies. 
 
In addition to bolstering economic security, the measure would have meaningful impact on efforts to 
avoid economic relationships with countries that engage in the violation of human rights. This 
includes China, whose state-owned companies profit from Uyghur slave labour2 as Beijing engages 
in anti-competitive government intervention that undercuts competition. By combating China’s grip 
on a number of raw materials, including rare earth elements, cobalt, lithium, and graphite ,3 the EU 
can make important pr ogress in creating a values -based approach to sourcing materials from 
mineral-rich regions in a more ethical way. Focusing on cooperation with different countries and 
regions and p artnering with publicly traded multinational corporations to access secure s upply 
chains can help ensure that the EU is not supporting slave labour, all while weakening the general 
threat China poses.  
 
The European Foundation for Democracy cares deeply about these issues, which prompted our 
organisation to file comments in respon se to the European Commission's call for evidence on this 
issue.  For this reason, we support the European Commission’s initiative in this area.  
 
Broadening the Supply Chain Has Economic and Human Rights Benefits 
 
The Commission should be applauded for its work to recognize and mitigate the risk that China 
poses in terms of its dominance of certain raw materials. However, as the Commission and its 
partners contemplate the best approaches to improve the EU’s position  in this area, it should 
consider not only the security risks that China poses to the EU economy, but also the well -
documented human rights abuses that the country has committed, such as using Uyghur slave 
labour. Evidence from multiple international sourc es shows that The People’s Republic of China 
(PRC) uses Uyghur slave labourers to drive the economy, posing ethical dilemmas for any country 
doing business with the PRC.

The evidence for these human rights violations is abundant and disturbing. For example, the United 
States Department of Labor’s Bureau of International Labor Affairs reported in 2020 that the PRC 
“has arbitrarily detained more than one million Uyghurs and other mostly Muslim minorities in China's 
far western Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Regi on” and that “100,000 Uyghurs and other ethnic 
minority ex-detainees in China may be working in conditions of forced labor following detention in 
re-education camps.”4 In addition, the report details that the PRC has coerced large numbers of rural 
poor workers as part of an overall national strategy of creating competitive economic advantages by 
exploiting minority populations and other vulnerable groups through forced labour. 
  
An April of 2021, Human Rights Watch released findings of “numerous credible reports that Chinese 
authorities are subjecting Turkic Muslims to forced labor under the government’s idle labor transfer 
programs. This program involves sending those released from Xinjiang’s political education camps 
to forced labor camps where inmates al legedly provide low -cost or unpaid labor. 5 In some cases, 
forced laborers may be forcibly kept from returning home and are paid nothing for their work until 
they “complete their training.”  
 
The United Nations reinforced these findings in August 2022 in a  long-awaited report on human 
rights violations committed by the PRC. The group’s investigations found “allegations of patterns of 
torture, or ill-treatment, including forced medical treatment and adverse conditions of detention, are 
credible, as are allegations of individual incidents of sexual and gender-based violence.”6 A vast and 
condemning amount of evidence exists that the PRC has engaged in arbitrary detention, torture, 
involuntary medical treatment, and sexual violence as part of what the PRC refers to as “vocational 
education and training centres,” 7 an unconvincing euphemism for sites where the inhumane 
treatment of people regularly occurs as part of a government policy that values an economic edge 
over ethics. 
 
These reports have promoted strong reactions by some major economic partners, notably the United 
States, which has required companies to determine whether their supply chain benefits from PRC 
firms assisted by forced labour.8 France was the first country to regulate economic partnerships with 
Chinese-based firms, with the Netherlands and Germ any following suit. Belgium has its own 
legislative proposal on the matter and many multinational companies are weighing their own legal 
liabilities and ethical implications for buying goods made in part through labor forced or vastly 
underpaid by PRC policy. 
 
China’s Considerable Grip on Critical Raw Materials 
 
China is a nation rich with raw materials, but also controls corporations and subsidizes firms that 
mine, process, and market CRM s, actions that distort global markets with low -priced goods that 
undersell the rest of the international private sector. The central government also exerts tremendous 
pressure on Chinese companies to help it build competitive advantages, undercut competitors, and 
dominate the increasingly important global market for CRMs.  
 
Through its unethical practices and anti -competitive government subsidies and intervention, China 
has become the world’s largest developer and producer of many of the raw materials the EU relies 
on for manufacturing and technology. For example, the PRC has developed advanced infrastructure 
networks throughout mineral -rich regions, such as the Democratic Republic of Congo, which

China dominates other markets as well. According to 2019 data from the Intern ational Energy 
Agency, China held a 60% share of the extraction of rare earth elements among the top three 
producing countries and an 87% of processing using the same comparison. The same data shows 
that the Chinese held a 64% share of extraction in the gr aphite market and process 65% of the 
world’s cobalt, 58% of its lithium, 40% of its copper, and a little more than a third of the world’s 
nickel.11 By extracting and processing the lion’s share of these materials, China wields considerable 
influence over the future of electric vehicles, wind turbines, and solar panels, final products that rely 
heavily on massive amounts of CRMs. In addition to sheer supply, Chinese market dominance of 
these materials threatens the feasibility and affordabili ty of technology itself, as the imbalance in 
competitive ability that tilts in China’s favor works to stifle innovation and prevent broad competition 
from suppressing prices. 
 
Understanding its own advantages, China works hard to preserve them at all costs . For example, 
Bloomberg reported last year that Chinese “authorities have in recent weeks ordered state firms 
to curb their overseas commodities exposure. 12 In addition, the report details efforts by the PRC to 
force domestic banks to hold more foreign currencies13 and to consider a cap on thermal coal prices 
to undercut foreign competition. 14 The Chinese government censored searches for crypto 
exchanges15 and effectively banned brokers from publishing bullish equity-index targets.16 
 
The Chinese have other coercive tools, too. A new PRC rule will bar cash management products 
from holding riskier securities and limit their use of leverage.17 The Chinese have even stated publicly 
that China plans to sell metals from state reserves as a way of manipulating world markets. 18 Such 
strategies by the Chinese leave Europe dependent and vulnerable, forcing European manufacturers 
to do business with a country that engages in systematic human rights violations. With China having 
built the infrastructure necessary to mine and transport critical minerals, as well as extensive facilities 
to process the raw goods, others without available supply chain options must partner w ith PRC 
companies and implicitly support slave labour and anti-competitive behaviour. 
 
Diversifying the EU Supply Chain and Diminishing Chinese Control 
 
It is clear that the EU must diversify from China if it hopes to better control its own economic future  
and create a more ethical supply chain that refuses to support goods built on the backs of forced 
labour. From an economic security standpoint, a diversified supply chain helps render country -
specific disruptions less dangerous, prevents over-reliance on a country such as China, and fosters 
competition for raw materials and finished goods that places downward pressure on global prices.

We believe the Commission should implement values-based legislation addressing China’s security 
and human rights concern s. These policies would source directly from mineral -rich regions, as 
establishing reliable access to CRMs requires developing supply chains based on where these 
resources are accessible. For example, Africa and South America have significant deposits of 
CRMs.19 Mineral-rich countries, such as the Democratic Republic of Congo for cobalt and Chile for 
lithium, have already developed relationships with international companies to mine their vast natural 
resources. The Democratic Republic of Congo, in fact, pro duces more than 70% of the world's 
cobalt, holds 60% of the planet's coltan reserves, and is the world's fourth -biggest producer of 
copper,20 which Goldman Sachs is calling the "new oil”. 21 With its large supplies of cobalt, the 
Democratic Republic of Congo could become the Saudi Arabia of the electric vehicle age, w ith its 
commercial centre, Kinshasa, at the center of the cobalt trade.22 
 
Lithium, too, is a metal poised to create new marketplaces and generate new supply lines. 
The International Energy Agency is projecting a more than 40-fold increase in demand for lithium by 
204023 and prices for the silvery-white metal hit record highs this year.24 Such skyrocketing demand 
is increasing the importance of  mining companies in Chile, which is the second -largest lithium 
producer after Australia. Together with Argentina and Bolivia, Chile helps form the "lithium triangle,” 
an area that possesses more than half of the world's proven lithium reserves.”25 
 
To help cultivate more robust supply chains for CRMs, the EU needs regions with potential supplies 
to successfully develop them. 
 
Publicly Traded Multinational Corporations Can Diversify Supply Chains 
 
As EU member states look to diversify their supply chains, publicly traded multinational corporations 
are promising partners. Unlike the authoritarian regimes, such corporations are obliged to comply 
with human rights and ecological standards. The Commission must make sure that such standards 
are met in the supply chains.  
 
As it works to strengthen supply chains through the new Act, the Commission should maintain a 
strong focus on the value of multinational corporations that are already active in exploring, mining, 
developing, and processing CRMs, as those companies bring a wealth of experience and a 
knowledge of complex supply issues that could complement the perspective of governments. Recent 
global economic uncertainty has illustrated the value of casting a wide net for materials and the 
inherent danger of bottlenecked supply chains that rely on single sources.  
 
The Commission’s “Call for Evidence” correctly explains that the EU “often relies on highly 
concentrated supply sources in a few third countries. This increases the EU’s vulnerability to supply 
disruptions, leading to potential shortages and price hikes.” While governments may not be able to 
take these burdens along, publicly traded multinational corporations could be in a better position for 
developing secure and effective supply chains.

As the Brookings Institute has written, “Governments in the U.S. and Europe will need to build 
substantially on their existing commitments to build out their critical minerals supply chain. This 
would likely entail significant additional financial capital, ongoin g innovation, and improvements to 
regulatory and permitting regimes. This by itself would be challenging, but especially given the 
current global context. Governments globally are facing myriad economic challenges, including high 
inflation, a commodity pri ce shock and global energy crisis, and the looming threat of recession. 
Governments are not in the best position to be writing large checks…”26 
 
As the European Commission considers the Proposed Directive for the European Critical Raw 
Materials Act, the security and moral threat of China must be at the forefront of consideration. The 
PRC’s unethical practices, such as the continued coercion of Uyghur slave labour and anti -
competitive government market intervention, lends to lower operating costs that create  unfair 
competitive advantages, distort the marketplace, and undercut international competition. The EU 
must develop resilient supply chains through value-based considerations that link Europe directly to 
substantial and secure CRM deposits. Cooperation with different regions and countries and publicly 
traded multinational corporations can help diversify the supply chains for these materials and reduce 
Chinese dominance in CRM production and processing.  This should be done by ensuring the 
compliance with human rights and ecological standards. 
 
China poses an immediate and significant security threat to Europe. The Commission must act 
pragmatically in creating smart policy.

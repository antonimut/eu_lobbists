Introduction 
On 30 September 2022, the European Commission (“Commission”) published a call to evidence and a 
public consultation on the so-called “European Critical Raw Materials Act”. In addition to our reply to 
the survey, VNO-NCW & MKB-Nederland will use this cover letter to contribute to the consultation 
by sharing comments and suggestions on the envisaged package of legislative and non-legislative 
actions. 
General position 
VNO-NCW & MKB-Nederland welcome the announcement by the Commission to draft and propose 
a European Critical Raw Materials Act (“CRMA”). The proposal can improve the security of supply, 
reduce strategic dependencies on third countries and accelerate the twin transition towards a more 
sustainable and circular economy. As such, a well drafted CRMA contributes to the digital and green 
transition and to open strategic autonomy. In this regard, we would like to emphasise that the package 
should focus on three primary areas of action: 
1. Incentivise the sustainable extraction, refining and processing of critical raw materials in 
Europe. In the longer term (2035 – 2050), encouraging a minimum level of extraction, 
refining and processing in Europe can greatly reduce dependencies on third countries. At the 
same time, increasing the production within the EU allows for better oversight concerning 
sustainability and due diligence aspects. 
2. Apply circular strategies to improve the efficiency in relation to all stages of critical raw 
materials. In this regard, the Commission should consider applying all the circularity 
strategies: 1) refuse and rethink, 2) reduce, 3) reuse, 4) repair, refurbish, remanufacture, 5) 
recycle and 6) recover. As a result, the demand for critical raw materials from third countries 
can be reduced and the supply from European sources increased. 
3. Employ the EU’s trade and investment policies and instruments to increase access to 
critical raw materials in third countries. The EU should firstly negotiate and conclude trade 
agreements and partnerships with third countries to diversify supply. Additionally, the EU 
should conclude political understandings with third countries as second-best alternatives to 
trade agreements. Lastly, trade instruments such as import guarantees and loans should be 
considered to support companies, and with them the EU, in obtaining the necessary critical 
raw materials.

All these steps require secondary measures in terms of legislation, governance, financial support, 
public support, analyses, and monitoring in order to facilitate the transition. These building blocks are 
described in the subsequent paragraphs. 
In terms of governance, the proposal should facilitate access to sustainable raw materials by 
empowering economic operators. The proposal may not include a top-down governance model that 
picks winners, puts unnecessary obligations on economic operators and impedes the level playing 
field. To create successful raw materials policy governments and companies should work together to 
identify the main challenges and the viable solutions. Creation and implementation of industrial policy 
without guidance and expertise from industry is prone te be ineffective.  
Regarding the 2020 CRM list from the JRC we would like to stress the importance of anticipating 
future dependencies and to broaden the scope. It takes time to redirect supply chains, create refinement 
facilities and to secure the necessary raw materials. While the 2020 list consists of important raw 
materials it is important to note that this list is prone to change. Gas and fossil fuels for example, while 
they are not listed by the JRC they are currently critical due to the war in Ukraine. Therefore, it is 
important to analyse the dependencies on raw materials beyond the scope of the current CRM list. 
Position on the proposed legislative dimension 
VNO-NCW & MKB-Nederland would like to make the following key recommendations and 
suggestions, which can be applied to the objectives and policy options from the call for evidence from 
the European Commission.  
- Incentivise the sustainable extraction, refining, processing, and recycling of critical raw 
materials in Europe.  
o Setting up new mines, refining and processing installations takes time. For mines it can 
even take up to 12-15 years, but this does not negate the fact that in the longer term 
(2035 – 2050) it can greatly reduce European dependencies. It is important to start with 
these projects to create more stable supply chains on the long run. In addition, by 
bringing extraction, processing, and refining capacity to Europe it will be easier to 
safeguard ESG-criteria. Currently, sustainable methods are already being applied in 
Europe such as the geothermal extraction of lithium and the extraction of rare earths with 
biological acids. 
o An essential element to consider regarding mining is that there are sufficient raw 
materials available in absolute numbers, but that these resources are highly concentrated 
among a few countries. The current geopolitical environment focusses on strategic 
autonomy for which people and governments are willing to pay extra for raw materials. 
However, it remains to be seen whether this will also be the case on the longer term. 
This is essential for setting up a successful mining infrastructure.  
 
- Apply circular strategies to improve the efficiency in relation to all stages of critical raw 
materials.  
o Put more emphasis on the design and production phase. This requires the development of 
a coherent set of design and engineering tools. At its core, a circular economy is about 
keeping products and materials in the supply loop for as long as possible. This lowers the 
pressure on available raw material supplies and prevents waste from being generated and 
having to be incinerated. Policy efforts must shift to the design and production phase of 
products. It is important to take into consideration that possible requirements for the 
uptake of secondary raw materials should be sector specific, since there is no one-size-
fits-all approach. While recycled content provisions can act as a stimulus for the creation 
 
 
of a market for secondary raw materials in some sectors (considering there is a sufficient 
supply of high-quality recycled materials), it may negatively impact other markets (e.g., 
where recycling rates are already very high or the quality of recycled materials is low). 
o Commit to and formulate regulations for all strategies of the R-Ladder; 1) refuse and 
rethink, 2) reduce, 3) reuse, 4) repair, refurbish, remanufacturing, 5) recycle and 6) 
recover. This can simultaneously reduce demand, extend the life of products, and 
increase the supply of critical raw materials. In the field of circularity, there are many 
differences between member states. EU wide policy is desirable here.  
o Adjust the definition of waste to encourage the application of recycled materials. The 
Waste Framework Directive and the European Waste Shipment Regulation need to be 
revised. A generic framework is needed to give companies more legal certainty in a 
circular business model. The goal should be to shift the policy from 'something is waste 
unless...' to 'something is a resource unless...' The WFD definitions of waste, by-product, 
and end-of-waste status (Articles 3, 5 and 6) need to be adjusted, as it results in 
ambiguity in the licensing process for working with a waste/resource and prohibits the 
transportation of waste/resources across borders. This lack of clarity among both permit 
issuers and companies means that the application of recycled materials is 
underperforming, and waste/resources cannot reach specialised waste processing plants. 
o Retain and process as much CRM’s as possible. Currently, many valuable raw materials 
from the EU are exported to third countries such as scrap metal, e-waste and used 
consumer products for incineration. In the context of security of supply, it is a waste to 
export these raw materials. The goal should be to retain as much of these raw materials 
as possible. This can be encouraged by expanding processing capacity and considering 
how to curb exporting these products towards foreign dumping grounds. Existing raw 
material hotspots such as industrial clusters and harbours could play a facilitating role to 
get more grip on supply chains due to their central position.  
o Encourage R&D for technological developments and substitution. In addition to 
developing future industries such as robotics and AI, it is of utmost importance to look at 
established industries such as chemistry, metallurgy, and machine construction. With 
research on technological developments and substitution, the criticality of raw materials 
can be reduced. Possibilities to stimulate this are the inclusion of critical raw materials as 
a theme in new applications and grants and to connect companies with European 
initiatives such as the EIT Raw Materials and the IPCEIs.  
o Enable and support opportunities for greater control over and collaboration in supply 
chains. For example, some car manufacturers have direct contracts with the mining 
industry and have their own factories to produce batteries. This allows for more control 
over the supply chain. This is not feasible for most companies, but it is interesting to 
examine to what level this can be achieved, with or without government support, for 
some sectors. Where this is not possible, it is essential to encourage integral chain 
cooperation and transparency. 
o Encourage the circular economy by creating financial opportunities and by considering 
assigning CE-related projects of common European interests. Currently, applying CE 
strategies result in permitting problems and additional costs without additional benefits 
for businesses. Extracting CRMs from waste is an expensive progress, while the recycled 
materials do not have a high market value. Therefore, financial incentives should be 
created for businesses to create a viable business case and to accelerate the application of 
circularity. Different instruments such as investments, grants, contracts for difference, 
guarantees and flexible interest rates could be applied. Support is essential to maintain 
 
 
until the market is fully grown and can function on its own. Windfarms successfully 
used a similar route towards viability.  
 
- Improve the EU’s monitoring, risk management and governance, but prevent a top-down 
governance approach with far-reaching obligations for economic operators: 
o Public monitoring activities to inform companies about risk of supply chains 
disruption can be very helpful. However, as mentioned before, we are wary about any 
reporting obligations that might follow from public monitoring. Providing detailed 
information about company supply chains is risky since it results in sharing sensitive 
company information and can be an administrative burden. This would be 
counterproductive for achieving a secure supply chain. Therefore, we suggest 
applying public monitoring purely as a support mechanism.  
o Companies have the best oversight on their own supply chains, but the number of 
steps they can look back in their supply chains is often limited to 2 -3 steps. A clear 
view on supply chains is often especially challenging for SME’s. However, 
companies do have the most up-to-date information on prices, supply chain 
disruptions and their demands. Therefore, it is important to refrain from creating top-
down policies.  
o The amount of knowledge on (critical) raw materials differs a lot per member state. 
Some member states have dedicated agencies and national raw material strategies, 
while others only have ad hoc information on supply chains. To gather more 
knowledge on CRMs and supply chains it is important to connect the different 
European agencies and institutes with each other. Examples are the British "Critical 
Minerals Intelligence Centre", the German "Rohstoffagentur" (DERA) and European 
bodies such as the JRC. It is key that all public information in this field is made easily 
accessible for all entities in the EU in order to support not only the large companies 
but the SME’s as well. 
o Examine the impact of stockpiling. Stockpiling is a commonly cited policy option, but 
there are many different ways to apply stockpiling. For example, there is a significant 
difference between the Japanese model (corporate purchasing) and the U.S. model 
(government purchasing). Aside from financial and governance questions regarding 
stockpiling there is also a need for additional space. Therefore, spatial planning is an 
important aspect of stockpiling. The question is whether European stockpiling can be 
effective and, if so, which form should be preferred.  
 
Position on the non-legislative dimension and additional comments: 
In addition to the regulatory act that will be proposed based on Art. 114 of the Treaty on the 
Functioning of the EU, we would like to make the following suggestions in relation to the non-
legislative actions: 
- Employ the EU’s trade and investment policies and instruments to increase access to 
critical raw materials in third countries, while maintaining an open market: 
o Diversify suppliers of critical raw materials: Economic operators should be primarily in 
charge of diversifying supply chains, but governments can play an important role in 
diversifying suppliers of raw materials through trade agreements. Establishing trade 
agreements and strategic partnerships on a European level increases access to critical 
raw materials and reduces dependencies.  
 
 
o Deploy trade instruments for critical raw materials: In addition to trade agreements and 
strategic partnerships, trade instruments can be used to promote access to raw materials 
and internal processing. For example, import guarantees may contribute to securing raw 
materials as has been the case in Sweden, France, and Germany. 
o Cooperate with likeminded partners: Protectionist policies between like-minded 
countries should be avoided. The US Inflation Reduction Act prima facie discriminates 
against likeminded partners. While the EU should consider tax benefits (like the US), the 
EU should prevent protectionist policies. At the same time, the EU should assess means 
to ensure reciprocal market access and equal treatment regarding raw materials in 
foreign markets. 
 
- Create the required knowledge and skills in the labour market: 
o In addition to the physical plants for mining and processing raw materials, employees 
(both technicians as well as engineers and researchers) are needed to keep these plants 
running both on the short- and long-term. There is already a large shortage of technical 
personnel at present, and this will only increase due to the transitions. In that context, it 
is important to properly identify the short- and long-term personnel needs in Europe. 
This can then be translated into training requirements.
